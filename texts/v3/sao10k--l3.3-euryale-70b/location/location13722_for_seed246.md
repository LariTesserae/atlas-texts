This place is a strange, slimy realm of flowering fungi and silver moss on massive gray boulders, slick with dew and dirty water seeping from above. The sound of water running and pouring accompanies you at all times, growing louder as you approach walls that are bright with water soaking down through cracks. Air is warm but the ground is very cold below your skin. You can't see the sky — the air is thick and fog never fully clears, but daylight filters through well enough. Water is ever-present — condensation, wet clay, mud — but insofar as water "collects" here, it's in trickles down walls that stream in rivulets over the ground.

The general height of the boulders defines the size of the place: farther than you can throw a stone in most directions, though the mists make distances hard to judge. A few trees — twisted white birch and dayflower saplings — grow on piles of organic and precipitated material collected where boulders are arranged to block the full force of run-off. Plants also cling to rock surfaces, wherever water creates a medium for growth.

Trees appear to be cut at perhaps two human heights, as though the longest trunks broke (perhaps long ago). All forest growth seems to work within the large-scale form of the place.

No one ideas guide the definition and scope of this place. There are ancient, erosion-roughened stone ruins in some areas, but the idea and purpose of the intact structures is clear and well-defined.

Unlike many natural spaces, the place consists of separated masses with defined edges — making decisions about where one place ends and another begins is not hard — but in its larger-scale layout, it's quite integrated and enclosed. Except around where trickling seeps emerge, the ground is generally hard, severe impact will easily shatter the surface.

After a while in this valley, if I were to close my eyes and walk twenty steps in one direction, I think I could point to where the rest of the place lay despite the mist.

This place has been around as long as one could remember. No human contraptions or constructions are here. The air is always dim but not dark — it takes some of the light out. Once walking within the shape of this place, its oddness is what recurrs — for example, there are perhaps twenty separate areas where water constantly drizzles into the forest, but they are scattered across the place without any clear logic or pattern, most of them five to ten times as far from any other spot as they are from the wall.

The soundscape of the forest comes from constantly moving water: bubbly upwellings behind walls, that rush louder as the water emerges and thankfully softens as it flows away along the channeling surfaces of the boulders, to long after it has disappeared (or you have disappeared from view of it).
