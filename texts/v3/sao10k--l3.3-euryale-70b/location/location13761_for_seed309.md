Deep beneath the surface, a vast network of dystopian undercroft sprawls, a maze of industrial caverns, tunnels, and chambers filled with a strange, bioluminescent flora. The air is thick with the smell of damp earth, petroleum, and a sweet, floral fragrance from the plants that cover every surface. The water here is mostly condensation and underground seeps, though larger pools glimmer in low-lying areas.

The "walls" are living masses of interconnected, hollow tubes, like giant bamboo or a hive of organ pipes, providing structure and canopy to the place. Roots and tendrils spill across the ground, a tapestry of green, fleshy cords. In places, the tubes are as wide as houses; elsewhere, they're no thicker than a garden hose. The flora is almost everywhere, climbing up walls, hanging from the ceiling, carpeting the ground, so that the few pieces of machinery and infrastructure stick out starkly against the plant-wrapped and -encrusted buildings.

The fauna here is overwhelming. Large flocks of glowing, iridescent moths flit through the caverns. Also omnipresent are large, blind, subterranean worms — the top predator here — which burst from the ground to snatch prey. Scurrying around them are swarms of oversized insects and giant, burrowing shrews, while schools of bioluminescent fish dart through the pools and seeps.

Construction work is always underway. Train tracks etch broad paths through the plant masses, unseen trains rumbling through hidden tunnels. Cranes and dredges and trenchers jut from the "jungles," their tracks and cables disappearing into the gloom. In several areas, wide sections of the cavern "floor" have been dug away, creating broad, dimly luminescent snake pits and sunken gardens where suspension bridges and stepping-stone paths allow access to narrow, thickly planted islands.

Mist hangs thick in the air, contributing to the dim, although the plants' soft glow illuminates concourses and high-traffic areas. Here is an arrangement of carriage-style bicycles that sit empty, waiting for passengers on an elevated rail line.

Across this vast, complex system of caverns and buildings and engineered spaces, the soundscape is intense. The constant rumble of the trains, the screeching and whirring of heavy equipment always active somewhere, the subterranean worms bursting from the ground, the rushing, chattering insects, the hum of engines and the hissing of hydraulic systems, all join to create a loud, almost overwhelming mix. The atmosphere is active, with work going on always somewhere, and changing weather patterns influencing the complex interplay of environments.

There is a strange, almost impossible feeling to this place, as if it may shift or tip at any moment, or as if the plants have a consciousness that is considering whether to accept these intrusions. Certainly, the human inhabitants have entered a space that is not by nature their own, and for which no true permanence is possible. If they leave, the plants and the insects and the worms will reclaim this space, leaving signs of humanity solely in rubble and artifacts half-buried in the omnipresent roots and tubers.
