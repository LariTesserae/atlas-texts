Prepare for it the way you would prepare for a cold industrial mountain town, a wet labyrinth, and a low-grade reality anomaly all at once.

## First principle: do not visit casually

An ordinary human can probably survive and even enjoy this city, but only if you treat it as a place where:
- altitude matters,
- cold damp matters,
- infrastructure is physically dangerous,
- animals have right-of-way more often than you do,
- and geometry is reliable only by local standards.

This is not a “wander and see what happens” destination unless you are comfortable being stranded, misdirected, mildly hypoxic, soaked from condensation, and perhaps unable to prove that the street you entered is the same one you are leaving.

Go with patience, redundancy, and humility.

---

## What to bring

### Clothing
Dress for cold wet air, not dramatic storms.

Bring:
- a waterproof outer shell with a hood
- insulating midlayers that stay warm when damp
- sturdy waterproof boots with excellent grip
- extra socks, more than you think you need
- gloves that still allow dexterity
- a warm hat
- a scarf or neck gaiter for mineral fog and soot
- clothes in visible colors, but not reflective-flashy enough to confuse workers or attract animal attention

Important: this city wets you from surfaces, air, railings, walls, seats, cables, and fog. You may not notice yourself becoming damp until you are chilled through.

Avoid:
- smooth-soled shoes
- long trailing garments
- anything precious that rusts, stains, or mildews easily

### Navigation and safety gear
Locals carry simple practical tools because the city rewards them.

Bring:
- a paper map, and expect it to be partially obsolete
- a notebook and pencil for route marks and local corrections
- chalk or grease pencil
- a compact measuring tape or marked cord
- a small flashlight or headlamp
- spare batteries
- a whistle
- a length of line or reel cord
- a carabiner or belt clip for the line
- a basic compass, but do not rely on it alone
- a watch with a reliable timer
- a thermometer if you are detail-minded
- a small mirror

If possible, also carry:
- a local bell-tone guide or district sound index
- a transit chart issued no more than a few days earlier
- a local survey notice bulletin

The point is not to defeat the city’s strangeness. The point is to give yourself fixed reference habits.

### Health and comfort
Bring:
- altitude meds if you know you need them
- a basic first aid kit
- saline or eye drops for soot and mineral irritation
- a filtration mask or respirator for foundry districts and dust
- water bottle, ideally insulated
- electrolyte packets
- dense portable food
- any medication in duplicate, stored separately

Do not assume you can “just buy what you need” nearby. You usually can, but maybe not in the district you think you are in, and maybe not by the route you thought would take fifteen minutes.

### Electronics
Electronics are useful, but don’t let them become your reality anchor.

Bring:
- phone or device in waterproof protection
- battery bank
- offline maps if available
- camera if you want one

But expect:
- poor line-of-sight issues
- dead zones in cuttings and tunnels
- occasional confusing visual artifacts
- photos that may not clarify what you saw

Trust repeated physical checks over a single digital reading.

## What to learn before arrival

### Learn the local invariants
The locals teach children practical truths. Learn those.

At minimum:
- Follow water downhill.
- Trust shadow only at noon.
- If a dog refuses a street, take another.
- Count the windows before you enter and after you leave.
- Confirm intersections by more than sight: sound, smell, slope, wall color, drain direction.
- When in fog, do not assume apparent distance is real.
- If a route feels longer every day, believe the feeling.

These are not folklore in the dismissive sense. They are operational wisdom.

### Learn district names and tones
Street names may matter less than:
- color bands painted on retaining walls
- municipal bell tones
- rail line sounds
- vent smells
- local landmarks like tanks, chimneys, lift towers, and cistern courts

Before you go, memorize:
- your lodging district
- nearest funicular station
- nearest public bathhouse or clinic
- nearest guaranteed stable route to a lower basin
- nearest officially maintained shelter in fog events

### Learn animal etiquette
You are entering a place where animals are infrastructure-adjacent citizens.

You need to know:
- never get between herd animals and a habitual path
- do not feed scavenger birds near market waste or rooftops
- do not approach reservoir amphibians or overflow channels
- avoid warm catwalks at dusk unless you know they are clear
- if locals pause for a crossing, you pause too
- if people start looking upward, you look upward immediately

## Best way to arrive

Arrive in daylight, ideally late morning.

Do not arrive:
- after dark on your first visit
- in heavy fog
- during shift change if you can avoid it
- during a known topological revision period in upper districts

Your first approach should be with a local driver, guide, survey worker, or at least someone used to current routes.

The city is not best understood from a map first. It is best understood by:
- slope,
- sound,
- moisture,
- and the way routes braid around active industry and unfinished land.

If a local tells you “that way is shorter, but don’t take it today,” accept this immediately.

## Where to stay

For a first-time human traveler, stay in the Switchback Commons or another domestic district with:
- stable foot traffic
- active markets
- recent municipal updates
- nearby baths and food
- easy access to transit
- fewer industrial hazards

Avoid staying first in:
- outer survey camps
- extraction fields
- isolated ridge hostels
- cheap lodgings beside foundries
- any place advertised primarily by the view

Views are not worth much if the route back shifts in fog.

The Hanging Wards may be safe and stable if you have contacts there, but they can be disorienting, dim, and psychologically heavy for some visitors. The Foundry Steps are fascinating but rough on lungs, clothes, and attention.

## How to move through the city

### Never navigate by a single cue
Use at least three:
- slope direction
- painted color band
- sound source
- known building smell or heat signature
- number of stairs/landings
- presence and direction of runoff
- bell tone

If one cue disagrees with the others, stop.

### Favor maintained routes
Take:
- staffed funiculars
- marked enclosed bridges
- recently posted stairways
- roads with active drainage channels and current paint
- routes other commuters are using

Be cautious with:
- shortcuts
- service alleys
- abandoned rail cuts
- scenic upper catwalks
- foggy open ledges
- underpasses with pooled water

### Keep track physically
Good habits:
- note time between landmarks
- check your watch often
- glance back at intervals
- count doors, windows, landings
- notice whether the wall texture changes unexpectedly
- note whether your own footsteps sound right

If something seems off, do not push forward just to preserve pride.

### If geometry acts strangely
If you suspect a route has altered:
1. Stop moving.
2. Do not argue with what “should” be there.
3. Check your last fixed point.
4. Use your line if you have one.
5. Listen for bells, pumps, rails, or traffic.
6. Look for water flow.
7. Ask a local, a worker, or a municipal survey crew.
8. Backtrack before improvising.

Do not force a passage because a map says there is one.

## Weather preparation

The danger is not dramatic weather; it is cumulative exposure.

Expect:
- persistent dampness
- sudden fog banks
- cold metal everywhere
- drizzle-like condensation without rain
- slick stairs and rails
- soot mixed with moisture into paste

You can get chilled badly here while never feeling caught in “bad weather.”

Make habits of:
- changing socks midday
- drying gloves whenever possible
- warming up in baths, canteens, tea houses, or boiler-adjacent cafes
- checking for mildew and skin irritation
- airing your gear nightly

If snow comes while you are there, treat the city as temporarily more dangerous than usual. Snow outlines cables and edges beautifully, but it also obscures depth, changes animal movement, and makes already uncertain surfaces much less trustworthy.

## Food and water

The city is good at collecting water, but don’t assume every collected source is for casual drinking.

Best practice:
- drink from verified municipal sources
- ask locals which cisterns and taps are current
- avoid open standing water entirely
- treat reservoir overflow as contaminated
- do not interact with condensation basins unless invited or supervised

Food is probably one of the joys of the place:
- dense breads
- stews
- preserved meats
- hot teas
- broth
- rooftop livestock products
- heat-assisted greenhouse greens in some districts

Eat regularly. Altitude, cold, and climbing will burn more energy than you think.

## Social etiquette

The people sound practical, disciplined, and unsentimental about strangeness. Act accordingly.

### Good behavior
- ask for current route advice, not abstract directions
- respect work rhythms and shift traffic
- thank survey crews
- don’t mock local precautions
- don’t treat reality anomalies like entertainment in front of residents
- carry your own weight on stairs, lifts, and bridges
- stand clear when animals are being redirected
- obey bells, sirens, and temporary closures immediately

### Bad behavior
- insisting that maps “must be right”
- wandering into industrial zones for photos
- touching posted line reels, markers, or survey pegs
- feeding animals
- standing on active freight paths
- asking locals whether the city is “really moving” in a teasing tone

Remember: for locals, this is not a haunted attraction. It is a maintained home.

## Psychological preparation

This matters more than you might expect.

The city may produce:
- mild unease from low light and fog
- spatial fatigue
- irritation from repeated small uncertainties
- paranoia if you fixate on discrepancies
- dreamlike detachment after long walks

Countermeasures:
- keep routines
- eat and sleep on schedule
- check in with a companion
- write down what happened instead of obsessing over it
- take baths or sit somewhere warm when disoriented
- choose one or two trusted local landmarks and return to them often

Do not spend all day anomaly-hunting. The people who live there remain sane because they continue living: shopping, repairing, cooking, commuting.

Imitate them.

---

## Travel with a companion if possible

For an ordinary human, solo travel is possible but less wise.

A partner helps because:
- you can compare perceptions
- you can witness route inconsistencies
- you can maintain line contact in fog
- one person can seek help if the other is injured
- disagreement itself can be useful data

Best pairing rule: if both of you are confused, return to the last known stable point. Do not average your confusion into confidence.

## District-specific advice

### Foundry Steps
Prepare for:
- soot
- heat pockets amid cold air
- slippery metal surfaces
- heavy freight
- noise and poor visibility

Bring:
- respiratory protection
- eye protection if you are close to active work
- clothes you don’t mind staining

Do not linger where rails cross walking paths. The place carries momentum badly.

### Hanging Wards
Prepare for:
- dimness
- quiet bells
- enclosed bridges
- medicinal smells
- a more inward, watchful atmosphere

Be respectful. This district sounds like one where the sick, recovering, and dead are near each other in memory if not in space.

Do not joke about augury birds.

### Switchback Commons
This is your best acclimatization zone.
Expect:
- dense daily life
- market ramps
- rooftop animals
- children and elders navigating with confidence you do not yet have
- frequent small route adjustments

Watch what residents do with their feet, not just where they point.

### Outer extraction fields and survey camps
Not for a casual first visit.
These areas add:
- unstable routes
- heavy equipment
- fewer witnesses
- stronger geometric irregularities
- incomplete construction
- stranger exposed stone

Go only with:
- a guide
- proper safety gear
- permission
- and enough daylight to return early

## Animal-specific survival notes

### Cliff-herds
Beautiful, dangerous, and extremely sure-footed.
- Give them width.
- Never stand below a feeding line on loose slope.
- Watch for falling gravel before you see them.

### Scavenger birds
- Secure food.
- Cover cuts.
- Avoid roof edges and slaughter-adjacent spaces without guidance.
- If a flock lifts suddenly, something has disturbed them; pay attention.

### Reservoir amphibians
- Leave them alone.
- Assume water systems are ecologically active.
- Wash after contact with anything from the overflow network.

### Rust-colored dock mammals
Think “clever industrial vermin with teeth.”
- Protect food and bags.
- Don’t corner one.
- Check beneath benches and pallets.

### Ribbon-backed catwalk creatures
- Don’t handle them.
- Don’t use rails as handholds without looking first.
- Warm surfaces are habitat.

### Pale six-limbed grazers and other local crossings
If traffic stops for them, traffic stops for them.

Follow the locals’ lead without commentary.

## What not to do

Do not:
- chase a visual anomaly
- enter a tunnel that now ends in rock
- rely on reflected light as evidence
- trust pools, windows, or echoes to tell you where things are
- step off a known path to “check something”
- drink from atmospheric collection systems without asking
- walk upper fog districts alone at night
- trespass on quarry edges
- ignore your own mounting fatigue

This city punishes curiosity mostly by attrition and misplacement, not by cinematic disaster. That can be more dangerous because it tempts you to keep going.

---

## Signs you should stop for the day

End your excursion if:
- you can no longer estimate distances well
- you have started accepting contradictions without checking them
- you missed a bell or warning signal
- your companion’s account and yours diverge too much
- you find yourself in the same place more than once unintentionally
- a dog refuses your route
- locals begin asking where your line is
- fog makes the nearest handrail disappear
- you’re cold enough to stop caring

That last one is especially bad.

---

## If you get lost

1. Stop.
2. Shelter from exposure if possible.
3. Find the nearest inhabited, maintained, lit place.
4. Listen for bells, pumps, rail noise, public announcements.
5. Check water direction.
6. Use your line if deployed.
7. Ask for the nearest stable transit node, not your destination.
8. Descend toward denser, more maintained districts if safe to do so.
9. Contact municipal survey or transit staff if available.

Phrase your request simply:
- “I need a current route to a stable station.”
- “Has this lane revised recently?”
- “Which bell district is this?”
- “Is this stair complete today?”

That last one may save you embarrassment and trouble.

---

## Is it worth visiting?

Yes, if you are the kind of traveler who can appreciate maintenance as culture.

Do not go expecting sublime wilderness or flamboyant unreality. Go expecting:
- a city that survives by attention,
- an atmosphere of labor and damp endurance,
- odd beauty in steel, moss, fog, and repetition,
- people who have incorporated uncertainty into civic practice,
- and a mountain that is still, in some sense, becoming true.

The right mindset is not conquest, nor fear, nor thrill-seeking. It is respectful participation. You borrow certainty from the locals while you are there.

If you prepare properly, the city will probably not harm you. It may, however, permanently change your standards for what counts as normal urban competence. Afterward, most other places may feel strangely overfinished.
