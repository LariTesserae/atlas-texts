Prepare as if you are visiting a living machine disguised as an old neighborhood.

This is not a ruin, not a dungeon, and not a cave. It is an inhabited under-district with its own ecology, etiquette, and practical intelligence. The greatest mistake a traveler could make is to treat it as either picturesque decay or hostile underground wilderness. It is neither. It is domestic infrastructure aged into habitat. You are entering someone else’s managed weather.

## 1. Mental preparation

Arrive with these assumptions:

- **Everything has a use**, even if you cannot identify it.
- **Nothing is truly abandoned** unless a local says so.
- **Dampness matters. Drafts matter. Smells matter. Sound matters.**
- **Animals are part of the civic fabric**, not incidental livestock.
- **Your body will be noticed** by the place: your heat, your smell, your volume, your contamination risk, your clumsiness with low ceilings and shared air.

You should expect to feel, in sequence:

- curiosity
- sensory overload
- mild claustrophobia
- adaptation
- then, strangely, trust

That trust can be dangerous. Once the district starts to feel coherent, a visitor may forget how easy it is to foul water, block air, startle animals, slip on biofilm, brush against a hot pipe, or put weight on something that is not meant as a handhold.

Stay humble even after you begin to “get used to it.”

## 2. Clothing

Dress for **warm damp air, intermittent chill drafts, grime, low clearances, and constant brushing contact**.

Best choices:

- **Light, durable long sleeves**
- **Close-fitting trousers**
- **A vest or overshirt with many secure pockets**
- **Soft but sturdy boots with excellent grip**
- **A cap or close head covering**
- **A scarf or cloth wrap**
- **Thin work gloves**

Why:

- Long sleeves protect you from rough plaster, lime dust, insects, cordage burn, and incidental animal contact.
- Tight-ish clothing prevents snagging on pegs, baskets, shutters, hooks, and protruding repairs.
- Good boots matter more than almost anything. Floors will vary constantly: brick, damp wood, tile slick with condensation, gritty ash, packed lime, rung ladders, slatted walkways, wet stoneware rims, narrow ledges.
- A head covering saves you from dust, drips, feathers, soot, and low beams.
- A scarf helps with smoke, ammonia, compost odor, or a sudden cool vent draft.
- Gloves are useful, but not for all tasks; locals may handle many things barehanded for tactile reasons. Bring them, use them selectively.

Avoid:

- trailing coats
- loose hems
- jewelry that catches
- hard glossy fabrics that announce your movement
- high heels, smooth soles, or anything fashionable rather than practical
- bright white clothing, which will become filthy almost immediately and mark you as naive

Ideal color palette: earth, soot, reed, clay, olive, dark blue, faded red. You will blend better and worry less about stains.

## 3. What to carry on your body

Carry little. Bulk is your enemy underground.

Bring:

- **A personal light source**, warm and dimmable if possible
- **Backup light**
- **Water flask with a seal**
- **Small cloth towel**
- **Handkerchief or mask cloth**
- **A little soap**
- **String or cord**
- **Notebook and pencil**
- **Small gifts**
- **Basic first-aid kit**
- **A sealable pouch for valuables and documents**

Your light should not be aggressively bright. In a place with mixed low light, blasting every chamber with artificial brilliance is rude, disorienting, and may upset animals. A lantern-like light, shielded and directional, is better than a harsh beam.

A towel is invaluable: wiping condensation, drying hands before climbing, protecting a surface, wrapping a jar, kneeling.

A notebook matters because this is the sort of place whose logic only reveals itself gradually. Sketch paths. Mark vents. Note the smell changes. Write down names. If you are multiversal, also note any small infrastructural differences from your home baseline; they may matter.

Gifts should be useful and compact. Good options:

- sewing needles
- fine cord
- lamp wicks
- tough little knives
- waterproof containers
- quality salt
- fishhooks
- medicinal alcohol
- durable combs
- sharpeners
- small mirrors

Do not bring trinkets that are decorative but useless unless you are certain such exchange is welcome.

## 4. Health and bodily readiness

You should prepare for four main bodily stresses:

### A. Air quality
Expect a blend of:
- lamp smoke
- cooking smoke
- animal ammonia
- damp plaster smell
- mold spores
- warm organic density

If you have asthma, chemical sensitivity, mold allergies, or panic reactions to stale air, prepare seriously. Bring medications and do not assume airflow is poor everywhere; it may shift rapidly from stagnant to drafty.

### B. Microbial exposure
This is not filth exactly, but it is biologically active. You will be around:
- compost
- standing and flowing water
- animals
- fungus cultivation
- old organic matter
- confined humidity

You should be up to date on ordinary disease protections relevant to premodern-density environments: tetanus equivalents, gastrointestinal caution, skin infection prevention, respiratory prudence.

### C. Posture and movement fatigue
You will stoop, sidestep, duck, climb, pivot, and balance constantly. Prepare your body for:
- ladders
- narrow stairs
- uneven risers
- squatting
- moving in half-light
- standing in warm humid rooms

Stretch beforehand. Travelers who are fit in open landscapes can still become exhausted here.

### D. Sensory load
The layered noise can wear you down. If you are the sort who becomes irritable or disoriented under constant low-grade racket, bring discreet ear protection—but use it carefully. Hearing is safety here. Better to have something that dulls overload without muting warning sounds.

## 5. Water discipline

This place will teach you reverence for water very quickly.

You should behave as if every visible drop is already allocated.

That means:

- Do not wash casually in channels.
- Do not put your hands into cisterns or troughs without permission.
- Do not spill your own dirty water onto floors at random.
- Do not clean boots where runoff joins managed lines.
- Do not urinate anywhere except designated places.
- Do not assume “just a little soap” is harmless.
- Do not touch slatted lids, covered reservoirs, filter beds, settling jars, or ceramic runs unless invited.

If offered drinking water, accept it respectfully. It likely represents labor, judgment, and maintenance, not mere availability.

A traveler should bring a purification method for emergencies, but use it privately and tactfully. Publicly “correcting” local water habits is insulting unless there is actual danger and you have earned the right to speak.

## 6. Learn the etiquette of air

Most outsiders think first about not touching walls or not getting lost. More important may be **not interfering with ventilation**.

Watch for:

- shutters propped at deliberate angles
- vent doors tied open or closed
- hanging screens
- cloth baffles
- chimney throats
- shaft covers
- bellows or pump rooms

Do not:
- close something because it seems drafty
- open something because it seems stuffy
- lean gear against vents
- light smoky fuel in side chambers
- congregate too long in narrow air lanes

If someone says “leave that curtain,” leave it. It may be balancing temperatures between fungus, people, and stock.

In a highly tuned underbuilt place, airflow is part of governance.

---

## 7. Animal etiquette

You are entering a society where animals are not background scenery. They are food, labor, waste-processors, alarms, pests, companions, status markers, and climate indicators.

So:

- Do not feed anything without permission.
- Do not pet animals casually.
- Do not make high sudden sounds near birds.
- Do not stare too long at penned breeding stock.
- Do not block catwalks or feed chutes.
- Do not step over low barriers unless explicitly invited.
- Do not carry strong unfamiliar scents into sensitive rooms.
- Check your bags and clothes for small thieves.

Some inhabitants may judge character by how you move around animals. The ideal is calm, predictable, non-grasping.

Expect bites, pecks, thefts, and startled wingbursts if you are careless. That does not mean the place is dangerous; it means you are entering an old negotiated density and are currently the least adapted creature in it.

## 8. Light behavior

Because light is socially organized here, you should be careful with yours.

Rules of thumb:

- Don’t shine lights into sleeping alcoves.
- Don’t flare bright beams into nesting spaces, mushroom rooms, shrine niches, or watch posts.
- Don’t assume a dim room is unoccupied.
- Announce yourself before entering shadow.
- Let your eyes adjust instead of forcing the room to adjust to you.

A good habit: pause at thresholds.

This place is all thresholds—humidity thresholds, noise thresholds, odor thresholds, brightness thresholds, social thresholds. Crossing well is half the art of visiting.

## 9. Navigation and getting lost

You will probably get lost.

Not because it is a labyrinth by design, but because it is a district grown by accretion. Human navigation tends to rely on open sightlines and clean maps. This place offers neither. Instead it offers recurring patterns with meaningful differences: similar arches, similar tiles, similar pens, similar ladder wells, similar drain sounds.

To avoid real trouble:

- Never explore alone on first visit.
- Memorize by **functions**, not just turns.  
  Example: “Past the pigeon loft, left at the warm wall, down by the moss run, across the grated drain.”
- Note verticality. You may be above or below where you think you are.
- Track air and smell as much as landmarks.
- Learn the central cistern courts first. They are likely anchors.
- Ask what marks a “safe route” versus a “work route.”
- Mark your own path subtly only if local custom permits it.

Do not rely entirely on technology. In a damp, enclosed, old-built environment, sensors and locators may lie, fail, or become socially offensive.

## 10. Social preparation

The people here likely value:
- competence
- memory
- respect for process
- modest usefulness
- not making more work

A traveler gains trust fastest by proving they understand maintenance burden.

Good behaviors:

- ask where to stand
- ask what not to touch
- offer to carry, scrub, sort, fetch, or hold
- listen when someone explains a system
- remember names of spaces and tasks
- do not romanticize hardship aloud

Avoid saying things like:
- “How do you live like this?”
- “It’s amazing you made do.”
- “You should really open this up more.”
- “In my world we have a better way.”

Even if you truly do come from a world with superior plumbing, ventilation, and illumination, this place has survived by local intelligence. Offer knowledge only with humility and after understanding constraints.

The people who belong here probably possess astonishing situational literacy. A seep-master, vent-keeper, cistern cleaner, animal tender, or compost steward may know more practical ecology than many scholars.

Treat such people accordingly.

## 11. What to eat, what to decline

Expect a diet built from:
- fungi
- roots
- stored grains
- small livestock
- birds and eggs
- fish or tank-kept aquatic life
- preserved foods
- herbs grown in tiny controlled pockets
- things fermented, dried, smoked, pickled, or rendered

This can be excellent food if well managed, but a traveler should adjust cautiously.

Best practice:
- Eat cooked foods first.
- Be cautious with raw damp-grown items until you understand hygiene norms.
- Accept local staples if offered; refusing all unfamiliar food can offend.
- Ask quietly about allergens and fermentation strength.
- Do not mock texture, smell, or color.
- Be very careful with powerful preserved fats, fungal concentrates, or strongly ammoniac cured products if your digestion is not used to them.

Also: if they serve you fresh water, broth, or tea-like infusions, that may be a more significant courtesy than a rich plate.

## 12. Sleep and rest

Sleeping underground in such a place may feel odd even if you are physically comfortable.

Expect:
- never complete silence
- never complete darkness
- air that changes during the night
- distant animal calls
- drips, pumps, shifting cords, foot traffic, occasional doors
- the awareness of overhead mass

Bring:
- ear cloth or soft ear protection
- an extra layer for unexpected night draft
- a small familiar scent if that comforts you
- a sleep mask only if you are truly light-sensitive, though many chambers will already be dim

Do not expect privacy as an aboveground traveler might define it. Quiet exists, but it is porous. Rest will come easier if you accept that the district breathes audibly.

## 13. Safety hazards that are easy to underestimate

Not dramatic dangers—ordinary ones.

- slipping on algae, grease, ash, or worn tile
- banging your head
- putting your hand into a niche that is occupied
- stepping through an unlit threshold into a lower level
- leaning against hot masonry near kitchen galleries
- touching old lime or fresh plaster with bare skin
- disturbing insects in managed hives
- contaminating food or fungus rooms
- getting between a keeper and uneasy stock
- becoming disoriented from heat, smell, and dimness
- fainting from sudden cool draft after humid work
- taking the wrong ladder and emerging in a work shaft or private loft

The phrase “make a sound before entering shadow” is excellent advice. Follow it.

A soft knock, throat-clear, or spoken greeting prevents many bad interactions with both people and animals.

## 14. Best traveler skillset

If you can cultivate any practical competence before visiting, choose from:

- basic animal handling
- water sanitation awareness
- masonry or plaster literacy
- rope and knot use
- ladder confidence
- fermentation and food storage understanding
- fungal cultivation knowledge
- calm observation in busy environments
- repair-mindedness

This is a place where repair is culture. If you understand patched systems rather than ideal systems, you will understand it better.

## 15. Emotional orientation

The deepest preparation is emotional.

You should be ready for a place where:
- scarcity does not mean misery
- crowding does not mean chaos
- oldness does not mean decline
- animal smell does not mean neglect
- dimness does not mean ignorance

The district may feel poor to some travelers at first glance, especially those from cleaner, brighter, more spacious worlds. Resist that shallow reading. What you are seeing is a high-density survival craft refined over generations.

Its beauty is cumulative, not monumental.

You may come away with unexpected feelings:
- respect for maintenance labor
- tenderness toward improvised order
- relief at ordinary daylight
- sharpened gratitude for open sky
- and perhaps a strange admiration for any society that has learned not to fight its own necessary mess, but to guide it

## 16. If you want to be welcomed

Do these three things:

1. **Ask for the rules about water.**
2. **Ask how to announce yourself in dark rooms.**
3. **Ask what animals should not be approached.**

Those questions signal instantly that you understand the place is alive in specific ways.

If you want to go further, ask:
- Which walls are oldest?
- Which drains must never be blocked?
- How do you read weather from below?
- Which room smells wrong today?

That last one is especially good. In a place like this, to ask about smell is to ask about health.

## 17. Final practical summary

Bring:
- grippy boots
- fitted durable clothes
- scarf, gloves, cap
- dimmable light plus backup
- towel, soap, flask
- notebook
- first aid
- useful small gifts

Prepare for:
- warm humid air and cool drafts
- constant sound
- little open space
- lots of animals
- low ceilings and vertical movement
- strict water and airflow etiquette
- dense, lived-in biological complexity

Behave:
- quietly
- observantly
- respectfully
- usefully
- without touching things casually

And above all: **do not treat the place as “underground” in the simple sense.**  
Treat it as a neighborhood whose sky has been replaced by engineering, habit, and memory.
