## Footing and mobility

This city sounds deceptively walkable.

- subtle regrading
- surfaces that alter texture before rain
- floating or field-held pathways
- stairways that are not exactly where or how they were yesterday
- routes that become channels during precipitation
- plazas that may double as catchment systems
- “empty” ground that is actually reserved for hydrology, growth, or active substrate stabilization

So:
- wear grip-focused shoes
- step cautiously onto any unusual stone, glossy paving, or suspended slab until you understand local norms
- avoid standing in depressions if rain looks likely
- do not sit on retaining walls, membranes, reservoirs, or root-thickened parapets unless clearly meant for public use
- be careful at dusk, when wetness, dimness, and subtle geometry become harder to read

A walking stick or trekking pole is probably unnecessary for ordinary tourism, but a collapsible balance aid would be excellent if you are older, tired, or likely to explore edge zones.

## Water etiquette and safety

Water is important here in a way visitors may underestimate.

Do not treat visible water features casually. In this city, water may be:
- public utility
- climate battery
- irrigation reserve
- filtration zone
- data medium
- nutrient transport
- habitat
- legal commons
- sacred civic infrastructure

Therefore:
- do not touch reservoirs without invitation
- do not bathe, wash gear, or refill bottles from decorative-looking basins
- do not step into shallow channels unless you are certain they are pedestrian-safe
- during rain, move away from channels, folded paving, and catchment plazas unless locals are obviously using them
- after rain, assume “inactive” runoff systems may still pulse unexpectedly

Also: carry your own water container, but ask before using public refill systems. In a place where moisture is harvested with precision, wastefulness may read as barbaric.
