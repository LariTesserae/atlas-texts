Prepare for this place as if you are visiting:

1. a very high, very dry, very cold stone city,
2. with almost no accessible water,
3. where distances lie,
4. where architecture affects reality,
5. and where the environment is calm enough to trick you into underestimating it.

The main danger is not attack. It is attrition: dehydration, chill, exhaustion, altitude, disorientation, and gradual cognitive error caused by a landscape that is stable but not entirely honest.

## 1. Physical preparation

### Acclimatize for elevation
Treat this as a high-altitude destination even if the air doesn’t feel dramatically thin at first.

Bring:
- altitude medication if your physiology uses it safely
- pulse oximeter
- a very conservative movement plan
- trekking poles
- emergency oxygen if your multiversal travel practice allows practical carry

Assume:
- climbs will be longer than they look
- routes that seem adjacent may be separated by major vertical obstacles
- a “short walk” can become an all-day traverse

You want a body trained for:
- long, slow climbing
- balance on stairs and ledges
- cold endurance
- low-humidity respiratory stress

If you are not comfortable walking exposed stairs with no railing for long periods, do not improvise confidence on site.

### Prepare for extreme dryness
This may be the single biggest ordinary-human issue.

Bring more water than seems reasonable, and do not rely on finding any locally. Also bring:
- electrolyte salts
- oral rehydration packets
- lip balm
- heavy-duty skin barrier ointment
- saline eye drops
- nasal saline or moisturizing gel
- sealed water containers with redundancy

Expect:
- thirst may lag behind actual dehydration
- breathing will dry your throat quickly
- skin cracking, nosebleeds, eye irritation, and fatigue will arrive early

Also protect gear from dryness:
- rubber seals may stiffen
- some adhesives fail
- wood and leather may shrink
- food can desiccate oddly fast once opened

### Dress for conductive cold, not blizzard cold
This is not a snowy mountain. It is a place where warmth is scarce.

Wear:
- layered insulation
- wind-resistant outer shell
- gloves that still allow tactile use of railings, stone, tools
- insulated boots with excellent grip on dust, salt crust, and polished stone
- warm hat or hood
- thermal base layers

Bring:
- sit pad or insulated pad; stone will leech heat fast
- compact emergency bivy
- chemical hand warmers if your tech assumptions permit
- scarf or mask for cold dry air and dust fronts

The cold here sounds “mild” until you realize every surface collaborates with it. You may never feel dramatically frozen, but you can become steadily emptied of heat.

## 2. Navigation preparation

### Do not trust your eyes alone
This city seems specifically hostile to naïve visual judgment.

Bring:
- multiple independent navigation methods
- chalk, string, route markers, or other low-tech breadcrumb systems
- notebook for route logging
- altimeter
- laser rangefinder if it functions reliably in your travel context
- line of retreat marked physically, not just mentally

Do not depend exclusively on:
- GPS-like systems
- map apps
- line of sight
- memory of silhouettes
- “I can see the tower from here”

Because here:
- perspectives fail
- towers shift position
- arches may frame elsewhere
- stair counts change
- certain districts appear intermittently

You should navigate by a combination of:
- physical markers you placed yourself
- elevation changes
- distinctive material features
- repeated verification

If you travel in a group, keep visual and verbal contact at all times in unstable districts. A person turning one corner may not be one corner away anymore in the usual sense.

### Time budgeting
Multiply all route estimates. Then add more.

In this city:
- distance is negotiable
- stairs may temporarily stop functioning as stairs
- “downhill” may not be simpler than “uphill”
- crossing thresholds may alter traversal conditions

Set hard turn-back times. Do not push for one more plaza, one more ridge, one more gate.

## 3. Mental preparation

### Expect calm wrongness
This is not a screaming nightmare realm. It is bureaucratically impossible.

That makes it more dangerous to ordinary travelers, because your mind will keep trying to normalize it.

Prepare yourself to accept:
- miscounts
- repeated angles
- spaces larger inside than outside
- shifting visibility
- delayed footsteps
- altered perspective

Your job is not to solve the city. Your job is to remain functional in it.

A good mindset is:
- observant
- unhurried
- skeptical
- non-reactive

A bad mindset is:
- “I’m sure that was just my imagination”
- “If I stare long enough it will resolve”
- “I can force this route to behave”
- “I need to understand the rules before I can move safely”

You probably won’t understand the rules fully. Work with local practical truths instead.

### Keep a written reality log
This place sounds like one where memory drift is plausible even if full memory alteration never occurs.

Record:
- date and local light phase
- route taken
- number of thresholds crossed
- where objects were left
- how many people in party
- current physical symptoms
- any changes in architecture or visibility

If a courtyard, tower, or district is absent tomorrow, you want evidence of whether:
- it changed
- you changed
- your route changed
- or the city simply has cycles

Do not rely on recollection.

## 4. Threshold etiquette

This may be the most important intuitive advice: treat thresholds as active.

Doorframes, gates, archways, plazas, stair landings, aligned stones, and framed openings are probably not decorative. In a place where “doorways insist on passage,” crossing matters.

Practical rule:
- pause before entering any obvious frame
- observe from multiple angles
- mark your current position
- note what is behind you before crossing
- cross deliberately, not absentmindedly

Especially avoid casually passing through:
- solitary gates standing in open space
- arches that frame a different-looking light
- repetitive colonnades with unusual perspective
- plazas centered on shafts
- doorways with inscriptions you cannot focus on

You don’t need to be superstitious. Just be procedural.

If traveling with others:
- agree whether crossing is individual or simultaneous
- count people before and after
- do not let one person “just peek through”

## 5. Respect emptiness

In this city, empty space appears to do work.

That means you should be cautious about:
- camping in open plazas
- placing equipment in isolated central positions
- rearranging standing stones
- building cairns in formal spaces
- filling hollows, shafts, channels, or cistern-like voids with refuse or supplies

Your instinct as a traveler may be to use empty courts as convenient staging zones. That may be exactly wrong here.

Better camping choices:
- enclosed but ventilated spaces
- structurally stable side chambers
- partially sheltered terraces away from major formal axes
- locations that look lived-in or traversed rather than ceremonial

Worse choices:
- dead center of a plaza
- beneath a solitary gate
- at the focal point of aligned pillars
- on a bridge between high districts
- at the rim of a deep shaft

## 6. Don’t interfere with repetition

You were told repetition matters. Believe it.

If you see:
- rows of identical stones
- repeated niches
- rhythmic pillar arrays
- regularly spaced slabs
- matched markers along stairs
- a sequence of objects that seems formal

do not alter them lightly.

Do not:
- remove one as a souvenir
- use one as a seat if all others are untouched
- wedge a door open with one
- break the pattern for convenience

In a normal ruin this would be merely disrespectful. Here it may be mechanically unwise.

Similarly, if you set your own camp or route markers, make them:
- clearly yours
- reversible
- not confusable with local patterns

You do not want to accidentally complete a geometry the city knows how to use.

## 7. Supplies and gear

### Essential carry
- large water reserve
- electrolytes
- dust mask / respirator
- goggles or wraparound eye protection
- layered cold-weather clothing
- high-traction boots
- gloves
- headlamp plus backup lights
- low-tech map kit and notebook
- route markers
- first aid kit
- emergency shelter
- high-calorie dry food
- climbing helmet for rockfall and low stone clearances
- rope only if trained to use it in broken terrain

### Strongly recommended
- compact stove, if flame is safe in your travel protocols
- insulated flask
- portable shelter anchors suited to stone, not soil
- knee support / stair support if you’re prone to joint strain
- hearing protection for long exposure to faint tonal phenomena if that affects you neurologically
- mirror for indirect viewing if direct viewing of inscriptions or spaces proves difficult
- inert sample bags if collecting is permitted

### Food
Bring food that:
- tolerates cold and dryness
- can be eaten without much water
- provides salt and fat
- doesn’t become inedible powder instantly

Also remember: appetite often drops at altitude, while caloric need rises.

## 8. Shelter and sleeping

Choose sleeping places by four criteria:
1. structural stability
2. thermal moderation
3. low threshold activity
4. predictable geometry

Good signs:
- weathered use-wear
- side chambers with one clear entrance
- sheltered alcoves not centered on major axes
- modest scale
- ordinary-looking room plans

Bad signs:
- a room that feels much larger than the exterior suggests
- air movement with no visible source
- dry glitter or standing haze circulating indoors
- humming polished dead ends
- views that do not stay consistent when revisited
- spaces that feel “official”

I would avoid sleeping in anything that feels like a ceremonial, administrative, or highly formal part of the city. Sleep where a tired body would sleep, not where a city would make declarations.

Keep your gear physically attached to you or to fixed anchors. In a place where orientation and distance loosen, “I left it over there” may become fiction.

## 9. Interaction with local life

You may see very little life, which means every encounter matters.

Treat all organisms here as:
- fragile in their own way
- potentially chemically unfamiliar
- potentially defensive without obvious warning
- ecologically important despite low abundance

Do not touch:
- dark wall films
- chalky nodules
- dusk-unfurling membranes
- faceted watcher-like animals
- airborne forms
- tracks you don’t understand if preserving evidence matters

Also do not assume stillness means inertness. A roofline “statue” may be an animal. A mineral sheet may be a feeding organ. A wall sheen may be a colony.

Since there is no mention of obvious predators or aggressive swarms, your best defense is simple noninterference.

## 10. Air, dust, and gas hazards

This environment has several subtle atmospheric risks.

### Dust fronts
They sound frequent, organized, and fine-grained.

Prepare for:
- eye abrasion
- respiratory irritation
- route obscuration
- surface slipperiness on polished stone
- static-like particulate adherence

When a dust front enters a district:
- mask up early
- protect eyes
- avoid exposed edges
- stop if visibility and footing degrade
- do not assume it behaves like ordinary weather

### Cold dense gases
Some canyons collect gases that spill like invisible liquid.

That means:
- low spots may be dangerous
- shafts and enclosed hollows may be oxygen-poor or chemically wrong
- “still air” is not always safe air

If you carry a portable gas detector, do. If not, be suspicious of:
- hollows where candles fail strangely
- sudden heaviness of breath
- dizziness near basin floors or shafts
- visible flow-like distortions hugging the ground

Avoid descending into deep enclosed spaces alone.

## 11. Architecture-specific safety

This city is old stone, severe verticality, and impossible geometry layered together. That means ordinary ruin rules apply, plus extra ones.

### Ordinary ruin rules
- don’t trust edges
- don’t trust unsupported overhangs
- don’t walk under recently sheared crystalline growth
- helmet in unstable districts
- test steps
- watch for polished wear on sloping stone

### Extra rules for this city
- if a stair count changes, stop and reassess
- if perspective stops narrowing, do not charge ahead
- if a tower moves position when occluded, assume the route relation changed too
- if an arch frames another district under different light, treat it as possible displacement
- if a room exceeds exterior volume, don’t go deep without a fixed return line
- if your footsteps arrive before you step, slow down immediately

A lot of danger here sounds like “the built environment continues to function.” Do not explore it like a dead ruin.

## 12. Social / cultural posture

Even if no people are visible, act as though you are in a maintained civic place.

That means:
- don’t shout just to hear echoes
- don’t climb everything because it’s there
- don’t move markers or stones
- don’t leave trash
- don’t mark walls
- don’t camp in plazas
- don’t descend shafts out of curiosity alone
- don’t perform little experiments with thresholds unless that is the explicit purpose of your mission

If there are inhabitants—human, post-human, mineral, administrative, or otherwise—you are easiest to tolerate if you behave like a respectful visitor to a silent municipality.

The place gives the impression that procedure matters. So have procedures.

## 13. Team practices if not traveling alone

This is a very good place to have a disciplined buddy system.

Use:
- frequent headcounts
- verbal confirmations before and after threshold crossings
- designated rear and lead traveler
- fixed regroup points
- rope or visual tether only where physically safe and truly needed
- shared route notation

Agree ahead of time:
- what counts as “too strange, turn back”
- how long to wait if one person vanishes behind architecture
- whether anyone is allowed solo scouting
- what signs of altitude illness or perceptual drift require aborting the excursion

If someone becomes fixated on proving the city “makes sense,” they may be approaching danger.

## 14. What not to expect

Do not expect:
- wells
- foraging
- obvious shelter stocked by prior travelers
- warmth from the environment
- ordinary day/night cues
- clear astronomical navigation
- straightforward ruins tourism

Do not expect the city to announce danger dramatically. It seems to prefer understatement.

## 15. Best kind of traveler for this place

The ideal ordinary human visitor is:
- patient
- physically conservative
- comfortable with silence
- not dependent on comfort cues like greenery, firelight, or running water
- willing to leave mysteries unsolved
- methodical about thresholds and routes
- able to turn back without resentment

The worst visitor is:
- impulsive
- thirsty for “just one more look”
- dismissive of ritualized space
- overconfident in visual intuition
- underprepared for dry cold

## 16. Final intuitive advice

Do not visit this city as though it were a puzzle to beat or a ruin to conquer. Visit it the way you would enter an old courthouse still quietly in operation after its language has been forgotten.

Bring warmth.
Bring water.
Bring more patience than ambition.
Mark your route.
Count your companions.
Pause at every threshold.
Avoid the centers of formal emptiness.
Do not disturb repeated forms.
And leave before fatigue makes the city seem normal.

Because that, more than anything, feels like its trap: not terror, but acclimatization. Once its wrongness begins to feel orderly, you may stop defending yourself against it.
