The benevolent and wise power would not arrive like rain.

It would know, almost at once, that to pour abundance straight into this place would be to misunderstand it. Water in quantity would be catastrophe before it was mercy: swelling, rot, collapse, mold without discipline, drowned root-architecture, softened roads, burst tissues, old dust becoming choking paste, mineral balances lost, every craft of thrift made suddenly clumsy. A less patient divinity might see thirst and answer only with drinking. A wise one would see that this city is not merely deprived. It is articulated by deprivation. Scarcity here has become law, language, memory, virtue, technology, architecture, courtesy, metabolism, and style. To abolish the condition crudely would also abolish much of what these beings are.

So the power would begin, if it began at all, with attention.

Not surveillance. Attention of the kind that dignifies. It would learn the timings of vents and bloom cycles, the way dust drifts from new cuts into old neighborhoods, the routes of the pellet herds, the hidden nurseries in warm machinery housings, the civic patience of those who prune a wall that is also their shelter. It would observe the maintenance crews at the frontier chambers, because there wisdom often loves its own reflection best in such people: those who make habitable without pretending to own. It would sit, perhaps unseen, through one full artificial dawn and one full dimming, listening to the city reveal itself in order: fans, calls, tram hum, work pulses, the paper-whisper of whole districts changing chemistry together.

And in its attention there would already be a kind of blessing, because many places suffer first from being regarded only for what they lack.

This power would not say: Be other.
It would say, more quietly: Become more fully yourselves, with less suffering in the process.

What would it do, then?

First, it would protect without declaring dominion.

Not by walls of force or by smiting predators or by suspending geology in perfect obedience. That would make the city infantile. Instead it would bend chance at thresholds. A ceiling under strain would give a little warning before it fell: a sound in time, a detectable tremor, a bloom of color in stress-sensitive tissues where none had appeared before. A tunnel heading toward poison chemistry would taste wrong to the survey beast before workers broke through. Predators that would otherwise strike unseen might become, in the last crucial second, visible in silhouette, betrayed by a glint, a cough, a flocking of minor scavengers. Boiler creepers would choose other seams before nesting in the one relay housing whose failure would darken a whole district nursery.

Not certainty. Never certainty. Just enough mercy introduced into probability that vigilance could succeed more often than grief.

The city might not even call this divine at first. They might call it a run of good maintenance years. They might say their materials are speaking more clearly these days. The old would knock a wall, listen, and frown thoughtfully. Apprentices would be told: pay attention, the hosts are easier to read than they used to be.

This would please the power.

For the next thing it would do is make communication easier across differences that already exist.

Between person and district-host.
Between planner and wild edge.
Between animal behavior and civic interpretation.
Between industry and the botanical bodies it depends on.

Perhaps not language in the miraculous sense, not roots speaking in words or birds delivering prophecies. That would be too gaudy, too flattening. Instead the power would deepen legibility. Stress pigments would become more reliable. Fruiting intentions would announce earlier. Certain tendrils that register passersby would also begin to register neglect, contamination, overharvest. Pollinator routes would become more patterned, easier to read, as if they were sketching maps in the air. Sanitation herds would balk sooner, and in more interpretable ways, before chemistry turned bad in a ward. Children, who are often the first to notice such things, would begin pointing out that this stair-root is sour before the adults smell it, or that the cable-cleaners have all moved to the west ducts, which must mean heat imbalance somewhere.

A whole culture already predisposed to reading subtlety would suddenly find subtlety answering back.

And because a benevolent power is wise, it would know that gifts must enter by existing virtues whenever possible. So it would bless not the proudest institutions first, nor the richest wards, nor the deepest industrial engines, but the practices of reciprocity.

The sanitation guilds would find their herds hardier.
The graftors’ juvenile supports would take root with fewer losses.
Those who save viable dust carefully, who return trace minerals to exhausted districts, who rotate harvests instead of stripping hosts raw, would see small inexplicable successes accumulate around them.
Improvised neighborhoods that share lamp-time and vent access fairly would become unexpectedly stable.
Districts ruled by hoarding, by extractive haste, by contempt for maintenance, would not be cursed exactly—but their own carelessness would cease being subsidized by luck.

This is one of the ways benevolence can appear stern: by no longer helping folly disguise itself as strength.

Would the power alter the great absence of water?

Perhaps, but indirectly, and with exquisite restraint.

It might reveal hidden gradients rather than create oceans. Deep in stone and old mineral seams, there are always traces: bound volatiles, occluded pockets, chemistries not yet usefully opened. The power might guide the city toward forms of condensation they had only nearly discovered, toward membranes more efficient at harvesting breath, machine heat, animal exhalation, fruiting vapor, distant seep. It might inspire, through dream or intuition or sudden technical elegance, a new class of root-architecture that can draw what is not exactly water but can become water where needed, in amounts too small to flood and too steady to panic over. Dew from temperature differential. Captured exhaust moisture. Mineral release timed by pressure wave. Atmospheric reclaim from the city’s own living respiration.

Not abundance.

Reliability.

A cup where there had been drops.
A cistern where there had been a ritual bowl.
A margin of safety in nurseries, clinics, hatcheries, and frontier camps.

The people would call this innovation, and they would be right.
The power would prefer it that way.

It might also act in dreams.

Not commandments. Not tablets. This power is too courteous to interrupt a civilization’s earned intelligence with simplistic holiness. But it might place recurring images in the sleep of many kinds of citizens at once: a new chamber seen not as mine or preserve or market annex, but as sequence—first stabilize, then seed, then test fauna, then light. A vision of an old abandoned siding flowering not with decorative growth but with a resilient hybrid useful for cable insulation. A shared dream among several unrelated districts of the same root pattern, suggesting an unseen subsurface connection whose opening would relieve pressure and create a safer transit route.

The wise power would understand that revelation is most fruitful when distributed. Let no single priest own it. Let tram technicians, pest wardens, children, scavengers, and botanists all receive fragments. Let understanding require assembly. In this way the city’s communal habits strengthen rather than being replaced by dependency on an oracle.

Would there be worship?

Yes, though perhaps not all at once, and not unanimously.

Some would notice first that tools last a little longer in the hands of the conscientious. That first cuts into new growth, if preceded by a moment of stillness, meet less resistance. That apologies offered to a district-host before necessary pruning correlate suspiciously often with cleaner healing afterward. That maintenance crews who leave a chamber better seeded than they found it come home with fewer accidents. Ritual would gather around these observations the way mineral gathers around a thread in solution.

At first the rituals would be almost indistinguishable from best practice:
pause before breach,
listen before harvest,
return trace matter to the tired ward,
feed the scavengers before your own compost bins,
do not cut a fruiting host in anger,
share vent-cool during dust front,
mark danger clearly even in districts not your own.

Then, gradually, some would realize that when these things are done in a certain spirit—not fearfully, not transactionally, but with sincere regard for mutual continuance—the city itself seems to answer with coherence.

Shrines would be practical places.
At a major vent.
Beside a district root-heart.
Near a maintenance bridge overlooking the central basin.
In tram depots and nursery vaults and the thresholds of new excavations.

Their offerings would not be wasteful. A sealed thimble of water perhaps, yes, because sacrifice means something only where it matters. But more often they would offer sharpened grafting knives, packets of reclaimed seed, polished stress-bolts retired from service, first fruiting husks, maps of newly stabilized routes, and written records of near-misses from which others may learn. To a benevolent and wise power, good documentation is holy.

Would the power ever manifest more directly?

Sometimes, at crises.

When a dust storm rolls low and violent through transit cuts toward crowded residential wards, shutters fail, fans jam, and visibility drops to the length of an arm, perhaps the people would later say that the storm split around the nursery quarter as if uncertain of itself. That the lamp arrays glowed through the brown air in an impossible geometry, leading whole caravans home. That the sanitation herds knelt all facing the same direction before anyone heard the collapse begin.

Or in a lower wild district where an exploratory ecology has become chemically dangerous and no one can map its intentions, a team might report that all the sensory tendrils turned at once toward one untouched wall and pulsed in a rhythm matching an old emergency code no current district uses. Behind that wall: a dry chamber full of viable ancestral stock, forgotten tools, and records of an earlier failure the city desperately needed to remember.

Or when a whole host-arcade chooses catastrophic mass fruiting in the middle of a dense commercial quarter, threatening crush and famine and displacement, the event might somehow resolve not by cancellation but by astonishing coordination: pollinators arriving in exact surges, collectors opening in time, scaffold crews converging before summons, market guilds surrendering floor plans without dispute, and the great pods maturing into enough insulating pith and nutrient reserve to rebuild three poorer wards before the season turns. Grace here would not mean prevention of all upheaval. It would mean upheaval becoming generative more often than ruinous.

There is another thing the power might do, perhaps the most tender.

It would care for the edges.

Civilizations often love their centers and exploit their margins, and this city—however intricate, however communal—is likely no exception. The benevolent power would keep a certain watch over abandoned districts, feralized sidings, unstable prospects, old utility roots swallowed by thicket. Not to preserve them untouched forever, nor to surrender them wholly to danger, but to prevent the frontier from becoming a place where value is recognized only after extraction.

In the wild edges, useful mutations would survive long enough to be found.
Predators would keep some balance instead of becoming plague.
Collapsed places would not simply vanish from civic memory.
Lost people, especially children and exhausted workers, would more often stumble into survivable shelter than into the wrong chemistry.

And perhaps among the botanists, scavengers, and planners who go out there, a new ethic would arise under the power’s quiet influence: that the far edges are not merely raw material for the central wards, but also teachers, seedbanks, and alternate futures. This would be a profound blessing, because it would save the city not just from disaster but from becoming spiritually shallow.

As for the being who lives in this place—

If by that you mean the city itself, the diffuse civic organism of root, labor, habit, fauna, and growth, then the power would not try to reduce it into a single face. It would honor its plurality. It would treat the undercity not as a machine to optimize nor as a wilderness to romanticize nor as a flock to shepherd, but as an emergent being whose soul, if such it has, is made of countless negotiated continuities. The power would strengthen those continuities.

If by that you mean some singular consciousness latent in the whole botanical architecture—a distributed intelligence in wall-thickening, fruiting decisions, chemical messages, route closures, and subtle recognitions—then the power would likely approach it as one gardener greets another.

Not with conquest.
Not even with revelation.
With companionship.

It might teach the city-being to distinguish better between injury and change, so that it does not overreact to every incision or tolerate every exploitation.
It might help it dream in larger scales, so that one district’s thriving does not require another’s chronic depletion.
It might grant it a cleaner sense of self, enough to know where its body ends and where dependent lives begin, and then blur that knowledge again with mercy so it remains hospitable.

And if the city-being is lonely—and how could such a thing not be, if it senses in distributed ways and is understood only piecemeal by its inhabitants?—the power might simply be present to it. A second vast patience in the dark. Someone, at last, to whom it does not have to translate itself into utility.

What would the power not do?

It would not make the place easy.
It would not abolish maintenance.
It would not turn every predator gentle, every district fertile, every risk avoidable.
It would not replace skill with miracle.
It would not reward piety divorced from competent care.
It would not permit the inhabitants to imagine that love exempts them from labor.

A wise benevolence knows that a world kept meaningful must retain friction. The city’s dignity lies partly in the fact that it is made, daily, by those who tend it. To relieve all burden would be to erase participation. The power would instead make right effort more fruitful, mutual care more visibly powerful, and needless suffering less sovereign.

In the long run, generations later, the people might say that their ancestors lived through the Clarifying Era, or the Listening Years, or the Time the Walls Became Honest.

Architecture would shift subtly under this influence.
Public squares would include more root access and animal passage.
Industrial designs would begin accounting for host desires before stress failures forced the issue.
Children’s education would include not only civic systems but reciprocal ethics as practical necessity.
Maps would show not just routes and ownership but bloom hazard, pollinator law, scavenger corridors, district fatigue, and sanctuary zones.
The distinction between maintenance manual and prayer book would grow thin in places, and neither would be insulted by the resemblance.

Their art would change too.
Less fantasy of plenty.
More reverence for interval, seal, tact, and collective timing.
More depictions of hands at work with other forms of life.
More beauty found in stress patterns, vent diagrams, dust drift, fruiting scars, repair lattices.

And perhaps the greatest sign of the power’s presence would be this:

The city would remain recognizably itself.

Still dry.
Still cool.
Still full of fans, spores, husk-brick courts, pale towers, membrane fliers, tram hum, brittle distances, and frontier chambers.
Still negotiating.
Still becoming.

But it would do so with a little more room for generosity, a little less tribute paid to accident, and a growing confidence that the underland is not merely something to survive inside, nor merely something to exploit, but something with which one can enter into covenant.

Not a covenant of obedience to a distant throne.

A covenant of careful flourishing.

And when at last the people named the benevolent and wise power, if they named it at all, they might choose a title less like king or mother or judge than something suited to this place:

The Keeper of Margins.
The Patron of Good Timing.
The One Who Hears Stress Before Break.
The Dry Mercy.
The Listener in the Rootwork.

And that power, hearing, would do what it had done from the beginning.

Stay.
Attend.
Nudge.
Protect.
Teach.
Withhold the ruinous gift.
Give the livable one.
