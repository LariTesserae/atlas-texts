A benevolent and wise power, seeing this city and these people, would not mistake need for deficiency.

That is the first thing.

It would not arrive offended by the strangeness, nor seduced into romanticizing it. It would not look upon the frayed edges of space, the damp machinery, the looping stairs, the people with resin on their hands and maps in their muscles, and think: this must be corrected into cleanliness. Nor would it think: this is wondrous in its suffering, let it remain untouched for the sake of its character. Wisdom would refuse both cruelties—the cruelty of improvement without listening, and the cruelty of admiration without help.

It would watch first.

Not for a day. Not from a distance only. It would attend in the patient way the city itself has taught patience. It would stand in the factory groves where chimneys breathe through moss and see how labor and chlorophyll share the same weather. It would walk the broad terraces in rain and note where people pause instinctively before crossing. It would listen at reservoirs, in schoolyards, in graft halls, along freight lines, in the oldest ridges where instruments lose conviction. It would notice what hurts, but also what already works. It would learn the ethics already present in the place before introducing any ethic of its own.

And what it would find, I think, is not a civilization asking to be saved from itself. It would find a civilization carrying an immense and continuous burden with grace, ingenuity, fatigue, humor, and many hidden costs.

So it would help, but sideways.

Not by descending in spectacle. Not by tearing up the city’s roots and remaking it into some clarified paradise of dry foundations and obedient geometry. The plateau chose to root rather than spread; a wise power would honor that choice. It would understand that if you relieved such a place of all uncertainty at once, you might also strip away the forms of attention, solidarity, memory, and craft by which it lives. To cure too much would be to amputate culture. To cure nothing would be indifference disguised as reverence.

So it would ask, in whatever language powers use when they are being gentle: Where is the strain no one has time to name?

The answer would not be the obvious marvels. Not the rain-rings in plazas or the impossible groves. It would be the maintenance load. The endlessness. The attrition of small corrections. The way every livable thing depends on someone remembering one more sequence, one more scent, one more caution, one more temporary stabilization. The wise power would see tiredness in the city’s bones—not dramatic collapse, but civic exhaustion distributed so evenly it has become normal.

That is where it would begin.

It might lay no hand visibly upon the mountain at all. Instead it would make maintenance kinder.

Tools would keep their edge a little longer. Not miraculously forever—just enough that workers come home less ruined in the wrists. Ropes would mildew more slowly. Boiler seals would fail with warning rather than malice. Moss filters would reveal saturation before becoming dangerous. The flowers used to calibrate clocks would open more faithfully in difficult weather. Not always, not unnaturally, but enough to restore trust where vigilance had become punishing.

It would place small mercies into the interfaces.

A hand on a civic wall would receive clearer information: a coolness that means sound structure, a faint warmth that means hidden stress. Bridges whose resonance must be listened to would sing more distinctly to those paying attention. Hazardous fog pockets would smell slightly of bitter herb before they thicken into disorientation. Mold would show itself in colors easier to read before it enters the lungs or dreams. Root masses that bend time too sharply around schools and clinics would soften their field by a degree. Not gone—softened.

This is what a wise power often does: not abolish complexity, but improve legibility.

It would help the city understand itself without making it simpler than it is.

Perhaps the root-cartographers would begin, over a few years, to notice that their maps no longer aged into uselessness quite so quickly. Not because the roots stopped moving, but because movement acquired pattern. Contradictions would still occur, but they would cluster according to rhythms that could be learned rather than caprices that only punished. A lane that presently intersects previous Tuesday might still do so, but on damp weeks after southerly winds, and always with a certain echo beforehand. The old mountain would remain strange, yet become a little more willing to be in relationship.

And because the power is benevolent, it would not reserve this gift for elites or mystics. It would move first through ordinary competence.

The municipal pruners would find that branches under dangerous tension now shed a fine dust of silver-green spores before splitting, visible in lantern light. The water council would discover that reservoirs near contradiction-zones produce ripples in distinct geometries corresponding to specific distortions, allowing early intervention. Pollination keepers would realize certain tuned forks now calm swarm-bursts that previously had to be ridden out. Railway crews would develop signal songs whose harmonics are unexpectedly effective at stabilizing platform-space in fog. Children would be taught these things early, and call them common sense, as children should.

A wise power would be glad to become indistinguishable from improved procedure.

It would also tend, very quietly, to the city’s grief.

Because there would be grief there. Not merely death, which every city has, but the peculiar grief of places continuously altered by necessity. Streets lost into root. Houses regrown beyond recognition. Work so repetitive and essential that love has to disguise itself as routine. People missing not only the dead, but versions of places, versions of themselves, versions of certainty. A city built on adaptation accumulates unlamented transformations.

So perhaps in the rain-rings of certain plazas, alongside the older images of bare stone, timber, scaffold, and living facade, another thing would begin to happen. Names not publicly recorded would surface for a breath and then fade: the nickname of a vanished lane, the outline of a workshop wall before the collapse, the silhouette of a tree removed to save a district, the bench where two people always met before one was lost in the upper groves. Nothing accusatory. Nothing overwhelming. Just enough that memory would not have to live only in bent elders and private ache.

The power would make remembrance easier to share.

Not compulsory. It would never force revelation on a civic people who value function and privacy. But it might create places where what has been borne silently can be borne together. A retaining hedge into which memorial grafts take with unusual health. A public conservatory where lost-route maps and family cuttings can be housed side by side. A once-troublesome stair that now loops only for those who are fleeing grief and resolves for those ready to arrive. Benevolence is often very practical in its symbolism.

As for the oldest groves beyond the mastered city, the wise power would be especially careful there.

It would not tame them.

Those groves are not just danger or resource. They are one of the few remaining presences the city has not fully translated into use. They are measure, alterity, reminder. They are where people go to be repaired in ways policy cannot articulate. A benevolent and wise power would understand that every civilization needs some margin it does not entirely own.

So it would protect those groves not by fencing them off completely, nor by rendering them safe to the point of banality, but by making them more truthful.

Pilgrims would still lose certainty there, but less often themselves. Instruments might still falter, but not fatally all at once. Sound would still dull, moisture still hang luminous in air, but paths taken with humility would be more likely to return a traveler than paths taken in greed, haste, or extractive ambition. The groves would become clearer mirrors of intention. Research done in reverence might flourish. Logging schemes disguised as stewardship would become impossible to navigate. Those who entered to dominate would come home with blank notebooks and blunted tools, if they came home at all. Those who entered to listen might leave carrying seeds that germinate only where a district most needs patience.

This too would be a kind of law, but grown, not imposed.

If the power chose ever to reveal itself directly, I do not think it would do so as a blazing figure over the terraces. That would be too easy, too flattening. The city would immediately bureaucratize the apparition, assign jurisdiction, erect railings, issue commemorative route guidance. No. If it appeared, it would appear in forms this civilization already knows how to approach: as a maintenance anomaly that proves beneficial, as a worker no one remembers hiring but everyone trusts by the end of the season, as a visitor to the graft halls with impossible hands and ordinary boots.

Perhaps an elderly woman would arrive during a season of difficult re-centering, carrying tools of no identifiable district make. She would ask for work, accept poor tea, and spend three weeks helping crews stabilize a ward where roads have begun ending in old arguments. She would say little. She would tie knots no one has seen before, knots that teach vines and cables to share load instead of competing. She would show children how to hear the difference between a harmless echo and a hungry one. She would leave behind no doctrine, only techniques. Years later, whole sectors might still rely on “the mountain hitch” or “the patient splice,” never knowing whether these are human inventions or gifts.

Or perhaps the power would never incarnate at all, and instead enter the city through distributed intelligence: dreams shared among root-cartographers in separate districts, all of them waking with matching corrections to a dangerous map; pruners in distant wards independently developing the same chant, which turns out to prevent growth-spasm backlash; water flowers blooming in a previously unknown sequence that encodes a coming drought three months before any instrument can detect it. A wise power could work by consensus before language.

It would not demand worship as payment.

If anything, it would distrust becoming central in that way. The city already has enough central burdens. Better to become a background benevolence woven into custom. Let shrines appear if they must, but let them be practical shrines: a dry bench by a difficult stair, a lantern niche at a fog-crossing, a basin that always gathers clean water even in bad seasons, a public tool wall whose blades are somehow never all dull at once. Let devotion look like stewardship, because here stewardship is the sincerest theology.

And if prayers were offered, they would likely be specific:

Keep the west sluice honest.  
Let the wall declare its weakness before noon.  
Bring my brother back from the old ridge if he is willing to return.  
Spare the children this mold season.  
Show us where the avenue has gone.  
Teach my hands the cut that does not insult the branch.

Such prayers a benevolent power could answer often, though not always as requested.

Because wisdom is not indulgence. It would know that some difficulties are formative, some losses are already woven too deep to be erased without tearing wider fabric, and some ambiguities are the city’s very medium of thought. It would not make every route straight. It would not remove death from old age, nor labor from cultivation, nor all danger from weather. A city without resistance would become stupid quickly. A people no longer required to notice would stop noticing one another.

So the power’s greatest gift might be not ease, but margin.

Enough extra time in a day that workers can rest before disaster. Enough warning before failure that choices become possible. Enough civic abundance that districts can afford beauty not strictly tied to utility. Enough stability in the unstable places that children inherit skill rather than only stress. Enough successful seasons in succession that the culture can move from perpetual triage into reflection.

Then new things would happen.

Not immediate utopia. But perhaps the city would begin creating institutions not solely aimed at preventing loss. Archives would expand into schools of deep place-memory. Root-cartography would become partly an art, not just emergency science. Musicians would compose for resonance zones once considered too hazardous for public gathering. Public gardens grown to absorb contradiction might also become places of contemplation, where impossible geometries are appreciated rather than merely damped down. The people, granted a little less fear, might become more curious again.

That, I think, would delight the power.

A benevolent and wise power does not only want creatures to survive. It wants them to have enough steadiness to become more fully themselves.

And for this people, more fully themselves would not mean less rooted, less practical, less municipal. It would mean all those qualities ripening into something almost stately: a civilization not merely coping with entanglement but teaching from it. Delegations from elsewhere might come to learn not just botanical engineering, but how to live where systems leak into one another without collapsing into panic or domination. The plateau could become a place of instruction in reciprocity, maintenance ethics, and patient infrastructure. Its people, once defined by burden, might become exemplars of a hard and generous maturity.

Would the power cure the mountain of its deeper unreality? I think no.

Or not entirely.

Because perhaps that old condition in the mountain is not a disease but a pressure of memory, a geological metaphysic, some ancient refusal of complete singularity. The city has made a life there by learning overlap. To erase that would be to erase the ground of their particular wisdom. Instead the power would reconcile the relationship. It would make the mountain less adversarial, less prone to punishing confusion, more willing to be inhabited in honesty.

Maybe, over generations, the people would come to say that the plateau had become kinder.

Not safer in every respect. Not ordinary. Kinder.

Fog would still gather at corners, but seldom over cradles or operating tables. Streets might still alter their apparent length, but emergency routes would hold. Reflections in wet bark might still show absent branches, but also occasionally reveal where a lost object has truly gone. Trains would no longer be heard arriving after departure except in memorial weather. Stairways would still teach patience, but less often entrap the desperate. Time might still drift near ancient roots, though now with enough fragrance-change or floral cue to warn those who cannot afford to lose an hour.

In all this the power would remain characteristic: generous, restrained, exacting in mercy.

And one day, perhaps long after the first gifts had been folded into regulations and work songs, the city might realize that its whole civic philosophy had subtly changed. Not abandoned—changed.

Once, they might have understood themselves primarily as keepers against overrun, experts in compromise, survivors by vigilance. Afterward, without ceasing to be practical, they might begin to understand themselves also as participants in a living covenant: not merely holding chaos at bay, but helping an ancient place become habitable without humiliation. Their maintenance would acquire a clearer joy. Their laws would make more room for rest. Their festivals would celebrate not only successful containment, but successful listening.

And if anyone asked where this change had come from, the answers would vary.

A pruner might say the branches learned manners.  
A water councilor would say the mountain’s pressure equalized.  
A root-cartographer would say the city finally found a map with enough dimensions.  
An elder would say, no, a kindness began moving through the place, and people answered it.  
A child would say, of course the plateau helps us now. We help it too.

The wise power, hearing that, would ask for nothing further.

Because that is what it had wanted all along: not dependence, not awe, but relationship.

So what would it do?

It would bless their maintenance.
It would increase the readability of danger.
It would defend mystery without letting mystery become cruelty.
It would lighten repetitive burdens.
It would protect the old groves by making them morally responsive rather than inertly hostile.
It would turn private grief into shareable memory.
It would reveal itself, if at all, through usable knowledge.
It would give the city margin enough to become more than tired.
It would not uproot what they are.

And then it would remain—present not as a ruler above the plateau, but as a deepening tendency within it: toward warning instead of ambush, repair instead of attrition, reverence instead of conquest, and a kind of civic tenderness sturdy enough to live in iron, sap, fog, and time.

On some ordinary afternoon, years later, nothing would look apocalyptic or divine. The light would still be cool and plain. Railcars would move between orchards. Chimneys would breathe through vines. Bells would mark irrigation. Workers in oilskins would cross a stump-plaza under the continuous roar of leaves. But a woman hurrying home would pause at a wall and know, from the warmth under her palm, that a hidden support needs attention before nightfall. A child would smell bitter herb and take the alternate route around a forming fog knot. In the old grove, a pilgrim who has gone there to ask nothing but understanding would find the path home waiting when they turn.

And the mountain—still old, still strange, still more patient than stone—would, in its own way, be answering.
