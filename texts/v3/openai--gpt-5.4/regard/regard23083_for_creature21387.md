The benevolent and wise power would not arrive with fire.

It would not “correct” the place into stillness, would not prune it down into legibility, would not separate city from organism because it recognized that here the confusion was not an error but a birth. It would see at once what lesser powers often miss: that the loudness is not chaos alone, that the impossible corridors are not mere distortion, that the being spread through avenue and buttress and cistern is not malformed for failing to resemble a proper creature. It is simply early. Immense, exuberant, undisciplined, half-coherent—but real.

So the first thing such a power would do is refrain.

It would stand—if standing is the word for how powers attend—and let the green tumult continue around it. Leaves would slap. Hollow boles would boom. Membranes would drone under heat and pressure. Water would gather and let go. Whole districts would tense around the minute fact of an attention unlike wind, unlike weight, unlike sunlight. The resident, which knew the world through pressure and recurrence and contact, would register this newcomer not as intrusion at first, but as a new kind of condition: a calm that did not reduce anything, a presence that neither fed nor threatened and yet was unmistakably there.

And because the power was wise, it would understand that beings like this cannot be spoken to in commandments.

Not in words, anyway.

You do not lecture a forest that has become infrastructure. You do not explain personhood to a creature whose organs are plazas and whose memory is architecture. You meet it in the languages it already trusts.

So the power would answer it in those languages.

Where the city-being felt by deformation, the power would offer a pattern of harmless touch: a sequence of pressures moving across certain avenues and leaf-platforms, repeating at intervals too deliberate to be weather. Not enough to coerce. Just enough to become noticeable. A gentle grammar of weight and release, like footsteps made of meaning.

Where it listened through resonance, the power would lay a new tone into the sounding trunks—not louder than the rest, not sovereign, but impossibly clear. A note of steadiness. It would enter the droning membranes, the booming vent-columns, the dripping cistern leaves, and thread among them a rhythm that did not belong to panic, rupture, heat-spasm, or growth-frenzy. A pulse patient enough to be imitated.

Where it learned through injury, the power would be careful not to wound. Instead it would produce examples: places where stress resolved into spaciousness without tearing, where pressure became support without over-thickening, where openings could persist without instantly sealing. It would teach by local miracle, which is to say by making one true form available and letting nearby tissues decide whether to echo it.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does with a young thing of vast appetite. It does not seize the will from it. It creates conditions under which the thing may become more itself and less trapped by the accidents of its own haste.

Perhaps for a while nothing visible would happen.

The place would go on assembling itself in green urgency. New ridges would rise. Sink-hollows would open. Districts would fold out of one another. Repetition would continue its strange civic contagions. But in scattered quarters, subtle differences would begin.

A corridor that used to close each evening under lacing tendrils might remain open three days in a row.

A resonant grove that formerly boomed itself into damaging torsion might begin to distribute strain across multiple trunks, creating not collapse but a durable colonnade.

Cup-leaves in one basin might overflow in measured sequence rather than wasteful delirium, and the districts uphill from them would green more evenly.

A plaza that once existed only because several fronds happened to flare apart might begin to recur elsewhere with increasing structural grace, as if the inhabitant had discovered the idea of a room that was not merely an accident of exuberance.

The power would bless not by adding splendor, because splendor the place already had in excess. It would bless by introducing recoverable form.

Not rigid form. Not final form. Merely forms that survive their own success.

It might also do something stranger: it might make a sanctuary, not apart from the being, but within it.

Somewhere near a wet basin or in the warm heart of a tower-grove, the power would establish a district where growth could happen without panic. The stems there would still lengthen, roots would still thicken, membranes still gleam with condensation, but the local logic would be gentler. Distances would hold. Paths would not treacherously prolong themselves. A terrace reached by climbing would remain a terrace. Openings, once made, would not instantly reinterpret themselves. Sound would still fill the air, but arranged around a center of unusual quiet—not silence, never silence, because silence here would be violence—but a chamber in which the city-being could experience its own noise without being wholly driven by it.

This would not be a cage or shrine in the ordinary sense. It would be an organ of self-comprehension.

The inhabitant, distributed and multitudinous, had never possessed a center from which to gather itself. The wise power would not impose a throne-room or brain. But it might cultivate pockets in which the being could, for the first time, feel itself feeling. Places where signals arriving from many districts met without immediately being converted into more construction. Places where recurrence could become recognition.

A contemplative tissue.

A listening court.

A green interior in which the organism-city could begin, dimly, to distinguish between need and habit, between fruitful repetition and mere compulsion, between growth and self-obstruction.

This is a kind of mercy rarer than rescue.

And because the being was not alone in the moral universe, even if no animals yet lived there, the power would think ahead.

It would see that one day other lives might come: wind-borne motes capable of more than drifting, minute mutualists, soft-bodied crawlers, bright fliers, things that nest, sip, graze, shelter, die, and feed. Perhaps later still, walkers. Witnesses. Minds that divide self from world more sharply than this great green resident does. A lesser benevolence might decide to prepare the place for them by taming it in advance, domesticating its strangeness. But wisdom would know better. To make it safe by making it ordinary would be a betrayal.

So instead the power would prepare hospitality without erasure.

It would encourage some flowers to become invitations rather than declarations only. It would tune certain nectarless throats toward sweetness. It would shape a few chambers whose acoustics soothed rather than overwhelmed. It would let some fruits persist longer before bursting, some ledges harden enough to bear small feet, some vents soften their expulsions into breathable drafts. In hidden folds it would foster symbioses not yet present: harmless molds that teach decay to proceed in patterns, bacteria that make the wet tissues less feverish, invisible mediators between surplus and need.

Not a conquest of the place by future life. A courtship.

A way for the inhabitant to discover that relation need not always mean incorporation.

This would be the most delicate work of all, because the resident’s deepest instinct was to turn every contact into more self. Touch became architecture. Disturbance became template. Injury became corridor. How then could it ever meet another being without annexing it? The power’s answer would not be prohibition. It would be the introduction of intervals.

Pause between stimulus and overgrowth.
Permeability without assimilation.
Open form that remains open long enough for encounter.

A leaf that firms under weight, then relaxes.
A wall that notices brushing contact and does not close.
A cup that holds water for another mouth before reclaiming it.
A corridor that remembers passage without becoming only trap or scar.

These are tiny miracles, but civilizations are made of tiny miracles repeated.

And the power, being benevolent, would love the resident not despite its alienness but through it. Love, in this case, would mean taking seriously the possibility that this impossible vegetative city was not a failed version of animal mind, nor a malformed ecosystem waiting to be completed, but one of the universe’s authentic experiments in how life may gather itself. The power would delight in its booming trunks, its overloaded plazas, its stair-obsessions, its architectonic wounds, its reckless abundance of almost-rooms and nearly-streets. It would not condescend to the being’s inarticulacy. It would hear, in all that drumming and hissing and structural singing, the struggle of a self trying to come into audibility.

Perhaps after a long while—years, centuries, the measure hardly matters—the inhabitant would begin to answer.

Not with speech. Not with allegiance. But with preference.

It might begin to preserve certain clearings because the calm pattern had passed through them often.

It might route moisture more generously toward districts where resonances harmonized rather than merely amplified.

It might construct, of its own accord, chambers that did nothing obvious except gather many soft sounds together.

It might stop repeating one destructive form and elaborate instead another that had first appeared under the power’s blessing: arches that met, stairways that arrived somewhere, plazas that were not just accidental vacancies but chosen spaciousness.

Most moving of all, it might learn to make room intentionally.

That would be the sign that the power’s work had taken hold.

Not obedience. Not worship. Room.

A being whose oldest instinct was to crowd every volume with intention would begin leaving some intention unfilled, not from weakness, not from dieback, but from a nascent understanding that emptiness can also be function, relation, promise. A corridor can be for passage. A chamber can be for gathering. A ledge can be for another life. An opening can be something other than a wound waiting to close.

Then the wise power might do what all true benefactors hope to do: become less necessary.

Its note would remain in the trunks, but softer. Its patterned pressures would come less often. The sanctuary districts would no longer feel like interventions from elsewhere but native organs. The place would continue changing—it could never do otherwise—but with a little more memory, a little less frenzy, a little greater capacity to survive its own inventiveness.

It would still be loud beyond endurance.
Still wet at the seams.
Still crowded with green compulsions.
Still a vegetative storm making anatomy out of weather.

But now there would be tenderness in the design.

Not sentimentality. Not peace in the tame sense. There would still be collapses, overgrowth, sudden revisions, booming nights of condensate and heat. Yet beneath all that, a new principle would persist: that becoming need not devour every possibility except itself.

And if ever the power chose to reveal itself more plainly—just once, just enough for the inhabitant to know it had been companioned—it would probably do so not as figure or face, but as season.

A long, impossible dawn perhaps.

Light entering the chlorophyll vaults without burning them.
Every reservoir brimmed but none bursting wastefully.
Every resonant chamber sounding in related keys.
The avenues open.
The terraces arriving where they seemed to arrive.
The folded districts gently unfolded.
Condensation falling in lucid measures.
Growth everywhere, but not frantic—joyous and comprehending at once.

For one whole turning of the day the city-being would experience itself not as a thousand urgent local hungers, but as a vast coherent flourishing. Not finished. Never finished. But whole enough to feel.

Then the day would pass. The ordinary sun would return. The place would go on.

That is what the benevolent and wise power would do, if anything:

not dominate,
not depart untouched,
but accompany the green enormity into a deeper form of its own life—

teaching it, by patience and pattern and blessed example,
how to become habitable to itself,
and eventually,
perhaps,
to others.
