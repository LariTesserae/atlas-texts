At first the wise power would not descend in splendor.

It would look for what is already loved.

That is the first difference between benevolence and conquest: it does not arrive asking, *What here should be corrected into my preferred perfection?* It asks, *What here is trying, with great labor and partial success, to remain alive? What has already learned how to belong?* And in this place the answer would be immediate and inexhaustible. The power would see not a failed imitation of nature, nor an overbuilt wound, nor a romantic marvel to be preserved under glass, but a genuine achieved thing: a mountain made by hands that has, over generations, become a watershed, a neighborhood, a garden lattice, an engine, a memory palace, a habitat, a discipline of mutual accommodation.

A lesser divinity might have been tempted to do something dramatic. Split the retaining walls and restore the buried mountain. Call down rivers. Banish all rot, all rust, all friction. Turn every cistern into a spring, every rooftop into Eden, every labor into effortless abundance. But wisdom would recognize that this would be a kind of violence, even if smilingly offered. To erase the difficulty entirely would also erase the forms of care by which these people know themselves. To remove maintenance would remove one of the civic arts that made the place humane. To silence machinery would not only end drudgery but also end continuity, livelihood, craft, and the deep companionship between the inhabitants and the systems they keep alive.

So the benevolent power would do nothing at first that could not be mistaken for luck.

A certain long-cracked stair would hold three years longer than anyone feared, but not so long that rebuilding was forgotten. A fungus threatening the upper pepper galleries would fail to take in one district where a grandmother had been saving seed from the strongest plants. An engineer, pausing over old plans in a hot office, would suddenly understand how to reroute a troublesome drain without killing the fig whose roots had entered the wall. A child who liked to sit near the overflow garden and listen to frogs would notice, before any adult did, that the evening song had changed in a way that meant contamination upstream. Two rival committees arguing for years over a freight bridge and a market awning would, in one difficult meeting, discover a design that gave both shade and clearance. A monkey reaching for fruit would spring a latch and reveal that the entire greenhouse line had been relying on a mechanism one corrosion-season away from failure.

The power would move first as an increase in right noticing.

Not omniscience bestowed whole. Not prophecy. Something humbler and more fitting: a slight but pervasive sharpening of attention toward interdependence. People would catch things earlier. They would remember the old mason’s warning about capillary damp, the gardener’s observation about heat off the southern tanks, the bath attendant’s remark about metallic taste in the water after first rain. Crafts that had always depended on intuition would become a little truer to themselves. The right apprentice would be standing beside the right elder at the right moment. Misunderstood skills would reveal their hidden kinships. Water-keepers would begin listening more seriously to frog-watchers. Structural inspectors would consult vine tenders without embarrassment. Children with impossible route knowledge would be invited into planning meetings instead of merely scolded for trespassing.

This would already be a miracle, though many would call it only a good decade.

The power, being wise, would understand that help must enter at the scale of relation. This place is made of junctions: pipe to planter, stair to market, parapet to rest, truss to nest, workshop to home, runoff to root, one generation’s patch to the next generation’s revision. Therefore blessing it cannot mean merely adding more stuff. It must mean making the junctions more merciful.

So leaks would become less wasteful and more revealing. Where water escaped, it would mark the true weakness before catastrophe instead of hiding in walls until collapse. Shade would fall a little better where elders stopped to breathe. Breezes would find the school courts in the hottest months. The dangerous slickness on one blind landing would somehow always dry a little faster until at last the district found the will and means to repave it properly. The old lift whose failure would have isolated a whole upper shelf would begin making a new sound—distinct, undeniable, impossible to normalize—so that no one could pretend the repair was optional. Seeds traded across terraces would take with surprising vigor in soils one would not have expected. Mornings would produce a little more condensate on the highest catchment fins precisely in the seasons when the cisterns ran lowest.

None of this would abolish scarcity. The power would not infantilize the city by making consequences disappear. Rather, it would bend the margin between strain and breakdown, giving communities a little more time to respond, a little more clarity about what response was needed, a little more success when they acted together.

And because benevolence is not merely practical but also tender, it would care for delight.

It would be unworthy of wisdom to preserve a place only as a functioning machine. This mountain is not loved only because it works. It is loved because orchids are tied above machine rails, because diesel and jasmine can coexist without canceling one another, because parrots in transmission towers are absurd and perfect, because children race along aqueduct walks, because frogs announce the evening from drain gardens as if blessing the utility of water by turning it into song.

So the wise power would increase beauty in ways that strengthened use instead of competing with it. Flowers would bloom a little longer on the walls where weary workers climbed home. The citrus trained against the sun-walls would fruit in staggered abundance rather than all at once, so that markets and kitchens and birds all had their share over time. Moss would take in the right places and spare the wrong ones. Shade trees in impossible gantries would root with uncommon patience. Painted surfaces would weather more beautifully, not less, so that maintenance remained necessary but never joyless. The annual flowering over the old viaduct—the one everyone informally measured the year by—would become just reliable enough to anchor memory and just variable enough to remain an event.

Festivals, too, would subtly prosper. Lantern nights would have calmer winds. Processions would find their way past old rivalries. Musicians on one terrace would discover that certain rhythms carried upward more cleanly than others, and soon whole vertical neighborhoods would be answering one another in patterns no composer had designed. Reservoirs would catch light in extraordinary ways during holy days. A bathhouse attendant would place bowls of floating blossoms under a vent shaft and discover the air turned their scent through three levels. A shrine long hemmed in by logistics would unexpectedly gain a small forecourt when an adjacent storage use shifted, and the district would know exactly what to do with the gift.

The power would also attend to justice, because no true wisdom can ignore that abundance and burden are rarely evenly distributed.

It would see who climbs too much and owns too little of what they sustain. It would see who drinks last from systems they maintain first. It would see whose districts are asked to absorb noise, runoff, smoke, hazard, and repair delays for the comfort of others. It would see whose expertise is treated as folklore when spoken in one accent and engineering when spoken in another.

But again, it would not solve this by thunderbolt redistribution from the sky. Instead it would trouble every lie of separateness.

Records long muddled would become legible. Quiet thefts from common water would come to light not through denunciation alone but through undeniable consequence traced clearly enough that even the beneficiaries could not maintain innocence. A collapse narrowly averted in one underfunded district would reveal how many prosperous terraces depended on the neglected drain galleries below. The people making policy in breezier rooms would, through circumstances too specific to dismiss, have to descend and stand where the heat pooled, where the metal sweated, where the old retaining seams smelled of mineral damp and human postponement. Those long excluded from formal authority would prove, repeatedly and publicly, to have been right about practical matters on which everyone’s survival depended. Prestige would begin to flow, however slowly, toward competence in stewardship rather than mere possession of access.

The power would not abolish conflict; conflict is part of collective life. But it would make bad faith harder to sustain and shared reality easier to perceive.

There are other things it might do.

It might bless memory.

Not nostalgia—the dead weight that insists the old arrangement was pure—but memory as usable continuity. In a place built by accretion, forgetfulness is dangerous. So the power might preserve stories in material form. Old repairs would reveal their logic instead of hiding it. A wall opened for retrofit would show, with uncanny clarity, the sequence of hands that had kept it standing across generations. Plans thought lost would surface in dry cupboards. Elders on the edge of dying would find, in their last lucid afternoons, exactly the listeners needed to inherit the subtle knowledge that was never written down: which plaster smells wrong before the stain appears; how to tell whether a monkey raid is opportunistic or a sign fruiting patterns have shifted; which kind of evening breeze means fog catchers will perform by dawn; how to hear, in pump rhythm, the difference between ordinary fatigue and imminent failure.

Perhaps, in some districts, dreams would change. Not grand revelations every night; that would exhaust people. But enough that those responsible for care would sometimes dream in plans and sections, in water paths, in root maps, in remembered stair geometries. They would wake not with commandments but with good questions. *What is beneath this crack? Why are the frogs absent from the third cistern court? Why does this lane stay feverishly hot after rain while the parallel one cools? Whose route have we made too hard? What have we built that refuses to become habitat, and why?*

This is how the power would teach: by deepening the intelligence already native to the place.

And perhaps, eventually, it would allow itself to be recognized.

Not by tearing the sky open. More likely by pattern. After years of improbable but never theatrical mercies, people would begin to suspect that the city-mountain was under a particular kind of regard. Shrines would multiply not around images of domination but around signs of continuance: a valve wheel that turned when all thought it frozen, a tree spared during reconstruction and later found to be holding a whole bank of soil in place, a colony of frogs whose return marked the cleansing of a poisoned run, a child’s chalk drawing of terraces under rain that somehow resembled, in unsettling detail, the redesign later agreed upon by engineers and gardeners alike.

The god—if one must call it that—would likely gain titles from labor before it gained titles from majesty. Keeper of Joints. Patron of Catchments. The One Who Teaches Leaks to Speak. Lady of Usable Shade. He Who Curves Pipes Around Roots. She of the Second Chance Landing. The Hearer in the Drain Galleries. The Patient Weight Above the Retaining Wall. They might laugh at some of these names, but keep them. Wise powers are often willing to be loved in local terms.

Would it ever appear embodied? Perhaps, but not in a form that broke the city’s scale.

I think if it walked there at all, it would walk as someone easy to overlook: a maintenance worker with impossible calm, an old woman carrying cuttings uphill without seeming tired, a rail inspector who pauses to listen to frogs before signing off on a report, a child who asks one question too exact to be accidental. It would know every route and never seem hurried. Monkeys would not quite steal from it. Cats would sleep in its shadow. Parrots would interrupt it. It would accept tea in machine rooms and offerings in shrines and arguments in committee meetings. It would be infuriatingly patient with people who insisted on false choices: growth or green, efficiency or beauty, human use or animal life, old district or new district, repair or celebration. Again and again it would reveal that the mountain remained itself only by refusing such severances.

And if at last there came a true crisis—not the ordinary noble strain of civic life, but a danger large enough to wound the whole organism—then perhaps the power would act more openly.

A season of failed rains. A deep wall movement after unprecedented storms. A blight crossing the terrace gardens. A fire running along linked roofs faster than bucket lines can climb. A corruption in the mains. A political seizure threatening to strip the common systems for short-term gain. In such a moment, the benevolent power would not preserve every stone. Wisdom does not confuse love with refusal of change. But it would prevent panic from becoming cruelty.

Water would be found where maps had forgotten it. Firebreaks would hold in the one place they absolutely had to hold. Those trapped on cut-off shelves would be noticed in time. A leader poised to choose expediency over the living future of the city would, for one clear and unbearable hour, fully understand the generations depending on that choice. Rain might come—not enough to make people passive, but enough to cooperate with their labor. Or else rain would not come, but seeds from a neglected archive would prove suitable to harsher years. Structural failure might occur, because matter has limits, but the collapse would happen slowly enough to evacuate. Loss would still be loss. The power would not insult grief by editing it out. But from the edge of irrecoverable ruin it would keep open the possibility of a more truthful continuation.

That, I think, is what a wise benevolence would do here above all else:

It would help the place continue as itself, but more justly.
It would preserve difficulty without glorifying suffering.
It would increase perception before increasing power.
It would side with maintenance, mutuality, and long memory.
It would defend delight as a public good.
It would make interdependence easier to honor and harder to deny.

And for the being who lives there—not merely the people, not merely the parrots and frogs and monkeys and vines, not merely the turbines and cisterns and stairs, but the layered civic creature composed of all of them together—it would offer something like a blessing spoken in the grammar of the place:

May your walls remember what they carry.  
May your water travel honestly.  
May your repairs teach before your failures punish.  
May every shade you cast shelter more than one life.  
May your machinery never grow so proud it forgets the garden, nor your gardens so dreamy they forget the pump.  
May your children inherit not exhaustion alone, but mastery, and not mastery alone, but wonder.  
May the creatures who trouble you also remind you that you are porous and therefore alive.  
May your heights not despise your depths.  
May your depths be honored by your heights.  
May your unfinished parts remain hospitable to the future.  

And then, having blessed it, the power would remain.

Not above it like a crown.  
Within it like groundwater.  
In the habit of looking twice at a crack.  
In the choice to reroute around a tree.  
In the extra cup of water poured for a neighbor climbing late.  
In the meeting that lasts too long because people are trying, despite themselves, to build for those not yet born.  
In the evening when the frogs begin, the pumps settle, the terraces breathe out heat, and everyone who lives on that manufactured mountain feels—without needing to say so—that they are being accompanied by something patient, local, and immensely kind.
