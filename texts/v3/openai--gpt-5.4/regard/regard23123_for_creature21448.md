It would notice first without intruding.

A benevolent and wise power, if it were truly both, would not mistake suffering for failure or hardness for a plea to be made soft. It would understand almost at once that this shaft-city is not merely deprived; it is adapted. Its people, its animals, its customs, even its humor and piety, have been made in exact conversation with pressure, dryness, depth, risk, and scarcity. To arrive with abundance poured carelessly from the hands—to summon rivers down the shaft, gardens on every ledge, warm bright weather, easy air—would not be mercy. It would be conquest disguised as rescue. Water would crack stone, flood chambers, bloom rot in walls never built for damp, drown the cleaner colonies, ruin powders and furnaces and insulation, rewrite disease, diet, architecture, ritual, memory. A paradise imported thoughtlessly would kill almost everything that actually belongs there.

So the power would begin with restraint.

It would stand at the lip of the seam in the mountain, or perhaps nowhere visible at all, and attend. It would learn the timing of lifts, the speech of the glider flocks, the route by which children run messages without once blocking a ladder. It would watch a maintenance crew brace a fresh crack before stopping to eat. It would observe the thermal hounds refusing a certain span before anyone has smelled fire. It would listen to the way the dead are remembered in load-bearing places. It would see that this is not a broken imitation of some greener ideal, but a real civilization shaped to a severe truth. And because it is wise, reverence would come before intervention.

Then, having understood, it would ask itself not “How do I change this place into something else?” but “What here is being needlessly spent? What grief is not structural but accidental? What burden can be lifted without erasing the strength that learned to carry it?”

Its gifts would therefore be subtle, structural, and consenting.

First, I think, it would make the shaft slightly kinder to itself.

Not safe—wise powers know that safety cannot be total in a living world—but less gratuitously treacherous. Stress in the young stone would begin to speak earlier and more clearly. Cracks that once hid behind cladding until catastrophe would silver faintly at their edges, or sing under the hand, or gather a telltale mineral frost visible even in poor light. Bad braces would complain before they failed. Cavities beneath the lower tiers would answer a tap with unmistakable wrongness. The city’s existing liturgy of maintenance would still be required, but the mountain would cease its worst habit of keeping secrets until they turned lethal.

This would feel to the inhabitants less like miracle than like an uncanny improvement in legibility. Inspectors would become unusually accurate. Old listeners to the wall would say the rock had “grown honest.” Children would be taught new signs. Shrines might appear near especially clear warnings. No law of the place would be overturned; rather, the place would become more readable to those who already love it enough to read.

Second, the power would protect the sealed preciousnesses without abolishing scarcity.

Water, perhaps, would remain rare, because rarity is built into the city’s whole metabolism—but reservoirs would stop being lost to absurdity. Hairline leaks would crust themselves shut until they could be repaired. Contaminants in storage would precipitate visibly before poisoning a tank. Ceramic doors guarding cisterns would warp less, seal truer, crack only after giving warning enough to matter. If there are hidden aquifers or cold mineral pockets in the surrounding rock, the power might not break them open, but it might guide patient discoverers toward them: a surveyor’s instrument behaving just a little more faithfully, a thermal hound scratching insistently at a seam no one considered promising, a drill head surviving one meter farther than expected into a cavity lined with recoverable frost.

Not abundance. Reliability.

The benevolent thing would know that there is mercy in reducing stupid loss.

Third, it would bless maintenance itself.

In this place maintenance is already morality, ritual, memory, citizenship. A wise power would honor that by making acts of care disproportionately fruitful. Sealants mixed by conscientious hands would cure stronger. Patch plates installed with patience would hold beyond estimate. Cleaner colonies fed regularly would remain within their bounds and thrive where they are useful. Bearings packed by apprentices who truly attend would run cooler. Heat-moth membranes gathered with tenderness rather than haste would insulate better. It would not reward status but care; not ownership but stewardship.

Over time this would become a theology even if no one named it one. People would discover that corners of the city kept with diligence became improbably resilient. Shared tools lasted longer than hoarded ones. A ladder repaired by a person thinking not of inspection quotas but of unknown future feet would somehow outlive specification. The shaft would begin, in little local ways, to favor love made practical.

This is the sort of thing a benevolent power can do without humiliating those it helps.

Fourth, it would enlarge communication across the vertical divide.

The city’s great vulnerability is not only fracture, cold, hunger, or outside hostility, but stratification: upper against lower, middle against both, official against unofficial, civic tiers against feral interstices. Wise benevolence often takes the form of mending perception. So perhaps sound would carry more justly in moments when justice is needed. Alarms would be less likely to die in crosscurrents. Critical messages sent by rail-knock or lamp would arrive with fewer fatal ambiguities. In emergencies, people speaking truth with urgency would somehow be heard by exactly those who needed to hear them.

Not always. That would be too theatrical. But enough that a pattern emerged: the shaft had become a place where honest warning traveled strangely well.

Likewise, the power might work through the nonhuman citizens. The flocking flyers could become even more readable weather-script, their swarms sharper in meaning. Thermal hounds might take to escorting children away from vent danger before any human command. The gliders beneath the catwalks could abandon unstable spans hours before instruments notice. Cleaner beetles might mass at the edge of compromised insulation. The whole dry parliament of creatures would remain itself, but become more articulate to anyone humble enough to pay attention.

This would be a particularly beautiful form of aid, because it would not place revelation above ecology. It would make the existing community of lives more mutually intelligible.

Fifth, and perhaps most tenderly, it would intervene in memory.

A place always rebuilding itself is in danger of forgetting the very knowledge that keeps it alive. Old routes vanish. Informal wisdom dies with elders. Repairs overwrite stories of why the previous repairs failed. So the wise power might make memory adhesive in useful ways. Important names would resist erasure. Lessons written once in fear and competence would be rediscovered when needed. A child idly tracing old marks on a brace might ask the question that reopens a forgotten caution. Registry records thought lost might surface sealed and dry where collapse should have taken them. Songs would preserve measurements more accurately than ledgers. Jokes would carry technical truth. Murals around doorframes would include, perhaps unconsciously, maps of evacuation routes. The dead would continue to support the living not only symbolically but informationally.

Maybe, too, grief would become less isolating. Not less sharp—wise powers do not insult mourning by dulling it into convenience—but less sterile. A person lost to collapse or fire or pressure failure would leave behind, in some quiet way, an increment of usable clarity: a discovered flaw, a corrected design, a warning that saves others. Not because death is good, but because benevolence can sometimes prevent loss from being meaningless.

Sixth, it would make room for joy that does not endanger survival.

This may be the holiest thing of all.

Hard places often survive by narrowing permissible delight to what is efficient. Yet the shaft already contains severe beauty: bearing-chimes in draft corridors, saints on patch plates, latticework of lifts at shift change, polished slag ornaments, private jokes painted inside masks. A benevolent power would not distract the inhabitants from labor with opiate bliss. It would, instead, increase the yield of small beauty. Colors would hold better in the dim. Mineral whiskers would bloom more often on harmless surfaces. The upper gray light, on certain days, would descend unusually clear. Chimes would speak in richer tones before vent shifts. Children would find more polished black darts than usual on feast days. Heat-moths would shed membranes in patterns worth treasuring. The drifters caught at dawn might come in silver-backed swarms at the year’s hardest turn, enough for a communal meal.

Festivals would become possible without irresponsibility. Songs would arise. Courtships would have omens other than surviving the same shift. Someone would invent a tradition of painting over retired warning lines with family constellations. Another would make instruments from exhausted ceramic vanes. The city would remain austere, but not starved of splendor.

Because wise benevolence knows that endurance alone is not a complete good.

Seventh—and this is delicate—it might do something for the being who lives there, if by “the being” we mean not one person only but the shaft itself, or the emergent soul of the settlement.

For places can become beings when enough need, memory, labor, fear, and tenderness are packed into one body of stone and metal. This shaft already feels like a creature: a lung, a wound, an organ, an appetite, a refuge. If a wise power recognized that a city-consciousness was forming here—distributed through ducts, rituals, animal migrations, load paths, family marks, maintenance histories—it might choose not to awaken it fully all at once. That would be monstrous. A city should not suddenly open its eyes and discover every fracture, every furnace, every sleeping child as simultaneous pain.

Instead it would midwife the being slowly into benevolent self-awareness.

The shaft might begin to “care” in small impersonal ways. Drafts would reroute from nursery tiers during bitter outside cold. Elevator failures would tend to happen empty rather than full. Minor collapses would strangely spare shrines where people leave route maps and candles. Feral zones would open passable paths when someone is truly lost. Dangerous resonances would damp themselves after a few warning hums. The city would not become a god or machine-overmind. It would become, at most, a little more like a body developing reflexes that preserve its vital tissues.

Those who are sensitive—old listeners, animal keepers, exhausted welders on the edge of sleep—would start saying the shaft was “looking after its own.” Skeptics would scoff and still touch certain railings before crossing. Children would anthropomorphize everything. Priests, if any, would argue doctrine. Engineers would call it emergent systems coherence. All might be right.

And if there is indeed a singular being living there—some hermit intelligence, guardian, giant maintenance-mind, solitary consciousness in the interstices—then the wise power would approach it not as a problem but as a neighbor. It would ask what burden has become too lonely. It would offer companionship before cure. If the being has spent itself holding too many systems together, the power might share the load invisibly, just enough for it to sleep. If the being has become hard from watching generations perish, the power might give it one thing impossible to fabricate for oneself: the certainty that care is not solitary in the universe.

That may be the greatest gift any wise benevolence can give to a guardian.

Eighth, it would defend the city from what is outside without sealing it from change.

Since the outer world is harsh, the temptation would be to fortify absolutely. But wise powers know that perfect closure becomes another kind of death. So the shaft would gain discernment, not merely thicker walls. Nets near the rim would catch what is harmful more reliably and what is nourishing more gently. Debris storms would break around the opening more often than strike true. Predatory things from the ash-colored exterior would hesitate at the seam. Yet useful migrants—the edible drifters, perhaps unknown future allies among the dry ecologies beyond—would still find a way in.

Perhaps, over long years, the power would even begin the slow work of knitting limited kinship between inside and outside. Not greening the dead land by fiat, but encouraging along the escarpments a few pioneer forms compatible with the city’s terms: crusts, mineral lichens of some alien dry chemistry, nesting shelves for drifters, heat-trapping stone arrangements that make the rim less murderous. The shaft would remain refuge, but no longer absolute exception. The world beyond might become, if not hospitable, then legible.

That too is mercy: not ending exile at once, but shrinking the border of the impossible.

Finally, and maybe most importantly, the power would leave freedom intact.

It would not found a tyranny of gratitude. It would not make itself indispensable to every valve and meal. It would not demand worship in exchange for load-bearing mercy. It would not erase error so completely that no one needed judgment anymore. A city saved from every consequence becomes infantile, then doomed. Wise benevolence teaches, clarifies, strengthens, accompanies. It does not confiscate adulthood from those it loves.

So there would still be accidents. Still prejudice between tiers. Still petty theft, foolish risks, loves gone wrong, repairs done badly, children disobedient in ways that terrify their elders, hounds dying, flocks failing, hard winters at the rim, names lost despite all efforts. The shaft would remain a place of labor and incompletion. But within that incompletion there would be a new bias toward life.

Not life as luxury.
Life as continuance with dignity.

And over generations, the inhabitants might come to describe the change in their own practical way. Not “A god remade us,” because that would be false. More like:

The city got better at warning.
Repairs started holding.
The animals grew clearer.
The old marks mattered again.
We buried fewer children.
The ladders felt more trustworthy underfoot.
In bad years we still had feasts.
In grief we were not abandoned.
The shaft, for reasons no one can fully prove, became easier to love without becoming less itself.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do, I think.

It would not turn the vertical wound into a garden.

It would make the wound capable of healing around its inhabitants without ceasing to be the place they know.
It would honor the people by helping what they already practice become more effective.
It would honor the creatures by letting them remain creatures and yet become messengers.
It would honor the city by treating its maintenance as sacred work rather than evidence of failure.
And if there is a soul in the shaft already forming from stone, metal, hunger, vigilance, and care, it would place one quiet hand upon that soul and say:

You do not have to become softer to be worthy.
You do not have to become easier to be loved.
Only truer, a little less alone, and slightly more able to keep what lives within you alive.
