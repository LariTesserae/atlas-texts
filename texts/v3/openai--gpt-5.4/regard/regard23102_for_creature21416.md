It would not arrive as conquest, correction, or revelation.

A benevolent and wise power, looking upon such a place, would understand almost at once that it was not broken.

That is the first and most important thing. Many powers—especially those accustomed to light, air, fire, speed, and the visible signs of flourishing—would mistake this drowned mountain-city for deprivation. They would think: raise it. Warm it. Clarify it. Simplify it. Give it sky. They would hear the old continuous noise and call it unrest. They would see the darkness and call it lack. They would see the absence of abrupt change and call it stagnation. And in trying to bless it, they would ruin it.

But a truly wise benevolence would recognize maturity when it saw it.

It would perceive that this world is not waiting to become some other world. It is not an injured forest yearning toward sunlight, nor a ruined coast asking to be rebuilt, nor a dead civilization requiring rescue. It is a completed answer. Cold, dark, loud, patient, grown over itself into coherence. Its balance is not fragile in the ordinary sense, but it is intimate: every current taught by root and reed, every migration lane remembered by bodies, every collapse already entered into the mathematics of future growth. To intervene heavily would be to mistake age for infirmity.

So the first thing such a power would do is nothing visible.

It would stay its hand.

It would refrain from draining the waters, from summoning brightness, from driving out predators, from making the place easier, cleaner, warmer, more legible. It would not impose “improvement” according to the tastes of other realms. It would not separate organism from architecture where they have long since become one truth. It would not elevate one inhabitant into monarchy over the others. It would not seek to make history happen faster.

Its first gift would be respect.

That respect, however, would not be indifference. Wisdom is not mere noninterference; benevolence is not passive admiration. If this power acted—and I think it would—it would act as a guardian of conditions, not as a re-designer. It would preserve the possibility for the place to continue being itself.

Perhaps it would lay a vast, almost unnoticeable protection over the abyss-garden: not a wall, not a seal, but a law in the fabric of things. A prohibition against violating scales. Anything from outside—storm of heat, plague of hunger, extractive intelligence, celestial debris, invasive radiance, machines of simplification, gods of greed—would find the place difficult to grasp. Not impossible to enter, perhaps, but hard to dominate. Efforts to map it for exploitation would lose confidence in its dimensions. Weapons would misjudge distance and pressure. Predatory empires would find their appetite faltering in its halls. Those who came seeking to strip, convert, drain, mine, discipline, or harvest the mountain-city would become confused by its old self-consistency, unable to find the leverage points by which lesser ecosystems are often subdued.

Not because the power would lash out in anger, but because it would make violation cumbersome and unrewarding.

It might bless obscurity.

That would suit the place. Not invisibility exactly, but a kind of sacred misprision. Seen from above or from beyond, it would remain merely a difficult deep, a baroque and unpromising tangle, too old and too wet and too intricate to attract the schemes of those who only value what can be quickly understood. The benevolent power might let rumors about it proliferate in contradictory ways. Some would say there was nothing there. Some would say only ruins. Some only reefs. Some only trenches. Some an unending forest. All would be true enough to prevent the dangerous kind of certainty.

To those fit to visit—patient beings, listeners, creatures not bent on extraction—it might reveal itself more clearly. To others, it would remain optically and morally thick.

And within the place itself, the power might make gentler gifts.

Not transformation, but mercy at the margins.

Where a disease could arise that would not become one more fitted participant in the equilibrium but a crude unraveling—something too fast, too absolute, too sterile in its appetite—the power might dull it. Not erase death, because death is already woven here and useful, but prevent stupid death, flattening death, death that ends conversations rather than continuing them.

Where a lineage stood on the edge of vanishing not because its role had concluded but because of some accidental narrowing—an old nursery grove damaged in too many successive cycles, a current lane subtly shifted so eggs no longer reach the sheltering vaults that shaped them—the power might make minute adjustments. A current barely redirected. A blight slowed long enough for resistance to emerge. A drifting raft of fertile matter nudged toward a failing terrace. The sort of intervention that could be mistaken for luck by everyone within it.

That, I think, is how wisdom would work here: under the threshold of spectacle.

Perhaps the power would especially attend to memory.

This landscape already remembers physically. Its revisions are written in buttress and root-flange, in softened collapse, in terraces inherited across geological patience. But creatures die. Routes can be forgotten. Songs can thin. A benevolent power might deepen the mnemonic capacities of the world without ever turning them into script.

It could do this by making certain structures hold event more vividly.

A reed colonnade that carries not only current-sound but old patterns of drumming for generations longer than chance would permit.
A silver-moss that brightens slightly when touched by descendants of those who cultivated it long ago.
Vault chambers whose echoes preserve distinctions meaningful to the local intelligent beings, allowing them to hear history in resonance.
Root-cities where chemical traces of migration, spawning, danger, refuge, and kinship persist with unusual fidelity.

In such a place, memory would not become an archive apart from life. It would remain embodied and environmental. The power would strengthen the city’s ability to remember itself.

If there are persons there in the narrower sense—the silver-skinned keepers, the route-readers, the pruners of canopies, the tenders of resonant chambers—then the benevolent power would probably not appear before them as ruler or lawgiver. That would be too coarse. It would not demand worship in exchange for protection. It would not interrupt their civic participation by making them dependent on miracle.

Instead it might become available through attention.

Those who listened longest in the reed towers might begin to notice that some echoes answer not just with information but with counsel.
Those who tend failing bladder-forests might find, in moments of uncertainty, that their hands choose the right grafting place.
Those who grieve the collapse of an ancestral vault might dream of an older current path and wake knowing where support still lives in the hidden root.
Those who wander lost in the broad exposed basins might see a pulse-line of seed-pods awaken one after another, leading them home.

Not commandments. Orientations.

A wise power in such a world would teach by making understanding easier to receive, not by replacing understanding with decree.

And because it is benevolent, it would love not only the continuance of forms but the experience of beings within them. So it might create pockets of grace.

Not grand paradisal exceptions, but intervals of relief.

A nursery grove where predators, for a few hours of each cycle, feel no hunting urge.
A trench-garden where exhausted migrators can always find edible drift.
A root-catacomb in which the ancient pale eels, however fierce elsewhere, never strike the very young.
A set of open-water plazas where the huge filter-beasts always pass more gently, leaving currents soft enough for fragile larvae to survive.
Occasional blooms of rare luminous lichen in the darkest corridors, not enough to alter the ecology, only enough to give courage.

Mercy would take local form.

The power might also cherish the city’s music.

This matters more than it first seems. The place is acoustical as much as spatial. It knows itself through resonance. A benevolent intelligence, hearing organ-moans in reed towers, shell clatter in seams, baleen hum in the avenues, grazing rasp on bark-coral walls, drummed signals in resonant husks, would understand that this world’s self-expression is not ornament but function and fellowship both. So perhaps it would tune the whole region by infinitesimal degrees.

Nothing a visitor could call miraculous. But over centuries, dissonances that would have become dead zones resolve into fertile intervals. New chambers open where sound can travel between once-isolated populations. Long calls of the infrastructural giants carry farther and with less distortion. Intelligent inhabitants learn to converse across ridges that once cut them off. Seasonal-that-are-not-seasons become perceptible in harmonic shifts, aiding migrations and spawning. The city grows not larger, not brighter, but more mutually audible.

That would be a beautiful divine act: not to speak over the world, but to help the world hear itself.

If the power were very patient—and such a power would have to be—it might undertake works that take millennia to become visible. It might encourage the strengthening of deep foundations no creature now living remembers are load-bearing. It might guide the slow rise of new terraces from the accretion of dead generations, ensuring that when old canopies eventually fail, they fail into habitable forms. It might seed, at astonishing intervals, lineages capable of taking over forgotten ecological labor before a gap becomes a wound. It might preserve diversity not as abundance alone but as redundancy of wisdom: multiple ways to hold a wall, strain a bloom, prune a mat, carry a call, remember a route.

Such providence would look, from inside, like the world continuing to have answers.

And yet I do not think the power would only protect the place from harm. Benevolence also delights. Wisdom also blesses beauty for its own sake.

So perhaps, very rarely, it would give the abyss-garden festivals.

Not imposed celebrations, but convergences. Once in a great while, currents from distant ridges would align with the pulse-response of seed-pod orchards, the passing of filter leviathans, the spawning glow of silver skin-moss, the migrations of mirror-fish, the drumming of keepers in resonant vaults, and the soft release of luminous reproductive dust from high canopies. For a few hours or days, whole valleys would shimmer with traveling light, and the sounds of the city would enter unusual concord. Predators would still hunt, grazers would still browse, terrace walkers would still file through plazas of detritus, but everything would be touched by a surplus of coherence. Not utility alone: celebration.

No one would have to know a god had done it.

It would be enough that the old submerged world, which has finished becoming itself, would sometimes be allowed to feel that completion as joy.

There is one more thing such a power might do, and it may be the tenderest.

It might choose one being—or several across eras—not as prophet or king, but as witness.

A creature capable of loving the whole place more consciously than most. Not apart from it, not superior to it, but pierced by its totality. One who feels in the root-vaults, the hanging canopies, the broad dark basins, the reed-organ moans, the files of crustacean janitors, the pale eels in plumbing dark, the filter giants sounding avenues, something like sacredness. Such a being might spend a life moving through the city’s strata, tending here, listening there, never fully able to explain what it knows, yet carrying an unusual trust that this world is held in regard by something vast and kind.

That witness would not found a religion of escape. They would not preach dryness, sunlight, transcendence upward. They would teach fidelity to the cold dark made world. Care for route and grove. Courtesy toward the old structures. Restraint in harvest. Attention to resonance. Aid to the very young and the failing old. Reverence for continuities too large to possess.

In other words, the power might not tell the inhabitants, “You are mine.”
It might help some of them feel, quietly, “We are beloved.”

And if someday true danger came—danger not of ordinary predation or collapse, not of one species succeeding another in the normal civic revisions of the place, but a real unmaking from beyond—then perhaps the benevolent power would finally act openly.

Then the currents might rise against invasion.
The hanging forests might close their avenues.
The silver-moss on ancient columns might blaze bright enough to blind hostile senses.
Filter leviathans might abandon their placidity and become moving walls.
Root-cities might seal their young within pressure-hidden chambers.
The whole architecture of growth might remember, all at once, that it has always also been capable of defense.

But that would be last resort, done sorrowfully, and only to preserve the deeper peace.

Under ordinary circumstances, wisdom would prefer subtler forms of love.

So what would a benevolent and wise power do here?

It would understand before acting.
It would protect without colonizing.
It would preserve obscurity against exploitation.
It would make small repairs where accident threatened meaning.
It would strengthen memory, audibility, refuge, and resilience.
It would offer guidance as intuition rather than command.
It would delight the world with rare excesses of beauty.
It would call forth witnesses rather than subjects.
And above all, it would let this old loud submerged civilization remain gloriously itself.

Because the holiest response to some completed things is not to remake them.

It is to keep watch while they continue.
