A benevolent and wise power, looking upon such a place, would not mistake stillness for need, nor strangeness for error.

It would see at once that this drowned city is not broken. It is not a fallen version of some better world. It does not want to be drained, clarified, redeemed into sunlight, or translated into cleaner geometries. Its darkness is fertile. Its crowding is eloquence. Its incessant reuse of death is not a curse but one of its oldest forms of mercy. A lesser god, or a merely enthusiastic one, might try to improve it—bring light, separate waters, banish rot, simplify predation, lift the old drowned wood into air again. A wise power would understand that such gifts, wrongly given, are massacres in ceremonial dress.

So the first thing it would do is refrain.

It would arrive without a wound of radiance. It would not boil the black water into transparency. It would not part the root-hung lanes. It would not announce itself in a voice so large that every bladder burst and every school shattered. It would enter according to the laws of the place: diffusely, by gradients, by altered probabilities, by permissions that feel at first like weather.

Perhaps it would come as a patience entering the current.

Not a body descending from above, but a new quality in the water: a slight lessening of panic at the wrong moments, a slight increase of survivable chances where survival serves more life than death would. Not enough to abolish hunger. Never that. Hunger is one of the city’s architects. But enough that waste becomes a little rarer and cruelty, where cruelty exists as excess rather than necessity, a little less successful.

In the Silt Courts, buried mouths would continue to process the world, but dead zones would ease. Sour pockets that once killed indiscriminately might open into narrower boundaries, allowing more passage between one chemistry and another. Egg-fields would fail less often from accidents of stagnation. A brood hidden under a shell ridge would still be preyed upon, but not obliterated by a random poison bloom that served nothing. The wise power would not save all young. That would starve the city in another season. It would instead favor proportion. It would tilt catastrophe away from meaninglessness.

In the Mussel Wards, where appetite builds towers, it might strengthen resilience without granting permanence. A chimney struck by storm or by the flank of an old passing giant would still break. But fragments would settle in ways more likely to found new colonies than to smother old nurseries. Disease would still pass through the packed districts—there is no dense life without plague—but not as often in forms that erase whole lineages of structure at once. The gift here would be continuity through transformation, which the city already understands in its own idiom.

In the Root Halls, where danger falls from above and rises from below, the power might work most delicately. Light cannot be increased here without making a slaughterhouse. So perhaps it would instead alter timing. Juvenile swarms would, by no conscious miracle, turn a fraction sooner. The shadow of a beak would be felt a blink earlier in the rootlets. Reptiles basking on floating mats would choose, with no idea why, the stronger mat on the morning the storm rips half the district loose. When heavy rain lays a lethal fresh lens over the darker water, some fish that rise into it would taste wrongness and descend before blindness takes them. The benevolent thing here would not forbid predation. It would teach the water to warn.

In the Calcium Quarter, where blindness already resembles wisdom, the power might bless the languages that do not depend on light. It would refine echo. It would make pressure more articulate. Vibrations through shell and stone would carry more truth and less confusion. The old blind catfish, the cave eels, the white film-feeders opening on the margins of a seep—these would not become enlightened in any theatrical sense. They would simply become harder to deceive by noise. Chambers where breeding depends on resonant conditions would hold those conditions more faithfully through turbulent seasons. The quarter would remain austere, but slightly more legible to itself.

As for the Flood Roofs, that weather-written upper city where chance is nearly delirious, the wise power might not calm it at all. Volatility belongs there. Instead it might make the temporary ecologies less treacherous in stupid ways and more fertile in creative ones. Seeds would still arrive. Insects would still raft in and become feasts. Transparent predators would still spread like veils. But the poisoned aftermaths of certain floods—the suffocations, the blind mass strandings, the salinity shocks that kill not because they feed anyone but because two worlds met badly—might soften. The floods would remain weird. They would simply become more often generative than senseless.

This is what a wise power would understand: benevolence in such a world is not the cancellation of tooth and rot. It is the tending of relationships.

It would protect the city chiefly by protecting its grammar.

It would preserve gradients from being flattened. It would keep enough murk that the murk-born may live. It would shelter migration lanes. It would nudge sediments so that whole districts do not vanish under one season’s violence when another arrangement is possible. It would not choose winners by beauty or resemblance to creatures of air. The tube worm and the turtle, the blind shrimp and the armored gar, the bacterial veil and the juvenile school would all stand equally within its regard, because all are syllables in the same sentence.

And what of the being who lives there—the principle with a thousand bodies, the resident made of repetition, accretion, appetite, and remembered current?

A benevolent and wise power would not try to make it singular.

It would not seize the multitudes and compress them into a crowned representative. It would not elevate one ancient turtle into monarch, one eel into prophet, one great fish into chosen vessel. To force a center on this place would be to misunderstand its soul. The resident’s genius is distributed. Its memory is architectural. Its mind, if one may call it that, exists in the way routes persist, in the way larvae settle where ancestors prospered, in the way one species’ wall becomes another species’ nursery and another’s ambush and another’s road. The power would honor that mode of being.

So if it wished to speak to the inhabitant, it would speak in forms the inhabitant already uses.

Not commandments.

Recurrence.

A current line that returns more faithfully after storm. A warmth that holds in a brood-arch exactly long enough. A chemical cue that travels farther than before, allowing spawners from separated neighborhoods to meet. A rhythm of rain and clearing that, over generations, teaches the city a slightly larger repertoire of survival. The power’s scripture would be pattern. Its blessing would be a durable tendency toward fruitful arrangement.

Yet I think, because it is benevolent and not merely prudent, it would do one thing more.

It would make room for relief.

Not universal safety—there is no such thing in a living world—but refuges. Little amnesties. Pockets where the perpetual transaction of being eaten and eating, chasing and fleeing, can loosen for a while. Cleaner stations already approach this in their own rough way: truces written by usefulness. Nursery tangles do too, and certain under-arches where current is right and mouths are inconveniently placed. The wise power might increase such places. Not enough to sterilize the city into innocence, but enough that exhaustion is not the only intermission from fear.

Imagine a handful of new warm lee-chambers in the Calcium Quarter where gravid bodies can rest and parasites are few. Imagine root-dense alcoves in the Halls where juvenile fish find, more often than before, one complete day of growth before the gauntlet resumes. Imagine the broken base of a mussel chimney settling into just such a shape that shrimp, eel, and brood-cluster can occupy neighboring rights without immediate bloodshed. These would be small mercies, but the city is built of small mercies as much as of small violences.

And because the power is wise, it would know that every gift alters the receiver.

So it would watch.

Not from above as a surveyor, but from within as a listener. It would feel when its interventions began to simplify what should remain complex. If too much refuge led to stagnation, it would withdraw a little. If one favored current created monoculture where there should be argument and layering, it would roughen the flow again. Wisdom would make it revisable. Benevolence would make it attentive.

Would it ever manifest more personally?

Perhaps, but only rarely, and in ways the city could bear.

Maybe once in a century of storms, when an entire district is about to be erased by a shift too large and too abrupt for recovery, the wise power would take body for a little while. Not a humanoid god in the water, absurd and fatal, but something native-shaped and impossible: a vast slow-moving coherence of fish, bioluminescence, current, and root-shadow; a mobile calm before which predators lose interest and colonies keep feeding; a pressure-presence that passes through the main avenues and leaves behind not obedience but reorganization. The old citizens—the eel, the turtle, the ray, the armored fish—would encounter it not as ruler but as weather that recognizes them. They would follow for a while. Districts would reseed in its wake. Then it would unmake its body and become tendency again.

Or perhaps its only “appearance” would be that, during certain nights of spawning in the Calcium Quarter, the cold lights of the drifting colonies would synchronize across arches and chambers farther than any natural cue should permit, until the whole pale quarter pulses like a single breathing thought. No creature would understand this as revelation. But generations later, routes and timings descended from that improbable coherence would still be shaping lives.

And above all else, I think the benevolent power would defend the drowned city from other powers.

From clarifiers. From straightenings. From salt made absolute or fresh made absolute. From heat pushed too high for the dissolved world to hold breath. From extractive hungers alien to the basin’s reciprocal laws. From any force that sees the place and calls it waste because it is dark, calls it primitive because it has no tools, calls it dead because it is made so visibly from death.

The wise power would stand, quietly and with immense authority, between this city and those redemptions that are really erasures.

It would say: no, this murk is not neglect.
No, these layered shells are not debris.
No, this warm black complexity does not need to be translated into brightness to be worthy.
No, the resident with a thousand bodies need not become one face to possess personhood.

If it judged that the city must change—and all true places must—it would favor changes that increase its capacity to remain itself under new conditions. Greater tolerance to swings of salinity. More routes between districts. More species able to bridge the old ecologies and the strange incoming ones. Not preservation as freezing, but preservation as deepening the repertoire of continuance.

And perhaps, in the most intimate sense, what it would do is bless the city with self-recognition.

Not consciousness as humans flatter themselves with it, but a more gathered coherence in the multitudes: cleaner stations more reliably found, migrations more elegantly timed, the old practical citizens reading one another across species a little better than instinct strictly requires. The turtle pausing where fry need shade. The eel’s route avoiding the freshest brood-grounds in seasons when other prey is abundant. The schooling fish taking warning not only from their own kind but from shrimp-flash and mussel-withdrawal and subtle changes in root tension. A city more able to notice itself is a city more able to survive.

That, I think, is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

It would love the place by not asking it to become another place.
It would help it without humiliating it.
It would answer its dangers without abolishing its nature.
It would strengthen the distributed resident without forcing it into a throne.
It would add refuge, legibility, resilience, and time.

And if ever the city, in its innumerable way, could feel gratitude, it would not build a temple.

It would do what it has always done.

It would take the gift into its tissues.
A new wall would rise where one had fallen.
A nursery would hold.
A route would persist.
A season of spawning would find its rhythm.
An old turtle would pass through a corridor and not collapse it.
A fresh lens would thin before it killed too many.
A blind fish would find the chamber.
A thousand mouths would open together in good current.

The power, if truly wise, would ask for nothing more than that.
