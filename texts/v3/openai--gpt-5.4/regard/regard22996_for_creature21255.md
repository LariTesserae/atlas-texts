The benevolent power would not begin by remaking the place.

Wisdom would recognize at once that this underdistrict is not a mistake awaiting correction. It is austere, hard, airless by surface standards, but it is also exquisitely negotiated. It is a covenant between stone, water, labor, memory, and the beings who have consented to live within constraint. To flood it with sunlight, roots, broad winds, or easy abundance would be a kind of violence. To “liberate” it into some greener ideal would erase the intelligence that has grown there. A good power would know this. It would arrive, if arrival is even the word, with restraint.

First it would listen.

Not metaphorically only. It would attend to the district in the mode the district itself understands: through recurrence, echo, seep, interval, pressure. It would learn the map by sound before taking any action. It would know which chamber speaks in a high dry bark, which gives back a throat-deep hum, which crack has widened by the width of a fingernail in forty years, which old basin has been allowed to silt because the hands that cared for it are gone. It would let the place disclose its own grammar. Benevolence, here, would mean refusing to call anything broken before understanding what it has been doing.

And it would attend to the being or beings who live there with the same courtesy.

If the inhabitant is a people of maintenance, the power would not appear first as revelation. It would appear as relief too slight to be frightening. A cistern kept from souring in a bad season. A vent-shaft clearing after years of dangerous narrowing. The bitter seam turning less bitter for three days while stores are low. A child lost in the dim passages finding, improbably, every handhold dry and every turn answered by the right note. Help, but deniable help. Mercy that does not demand immediate theology.

If the inhabitant is the singular keeper-creature, the narrow listener and wall-reader, then the power would not summon it upward or command it to kneel. It would meet it in its own medium. Perhaps one night, in the circular cistern chamber, the repeated drop from the dome would fall and sound not merely woody and hollow, but tuned—just slightly—into a pattern the creature has never heard and immediately understands as intentional. Not language from elsewhere imposed upon it, but the district’s own acoustic logic made articulate. The calcite flutings would whisper in harmony with a hidden vent. The covered water would answer from below. The keeper would place its hands on the timber grating and realize that something vast has learned how to knock properly.

That is what a wise power would do first: ask permission by fluency.

It would not abolish hardship, because not all hardship here is cruelty. Some of it is simply the shape of reality under stone. But it would oppose wasteful suffering—the suffering that comes not from necessary limits but from fracture, accident, depletion, abandonment, needless fear. It would become a patron of continuance.

The breathing walls might begin to breathe a little more kindly.

Not extravagantly. Just enough that the most reliable seams become steadier through the cruelest dry periods. Condensation would gather where bowls had been set in hope and left empty too often. The old polished tongues of stone beneath productive patches would once again shine with work. A wall that had gone intermittently bitter might sort its minerals more gently. The people would say the west face has remembered itself. Or: the old wall has forgiven us. Or, if they are plainer-minded: the flow has improved.

Hairline failures in the masonry would declare themselves before becoming disaster. A salt bloom in the shape of a warning hand. A new overtone in the channel-song where pressure is building behind a retaining wall. A patch of damp appearing on a floor in the exact footprint of the weakness below. Nothing theatrical: signs in the house-style of the district. The benevolent power would not post proclamations; it would annotate the world.

The old resonance infrastructure, so long a practical marvel, would become kinder too. Messages tapped along the walls would carry farther with less distortion when they matter most. Calls for aid would choose the right chambers and arrive clear. Confusion in panic would lessen because the district itself would help sort signal from noise. In emergencies, even those who are not expert listeners would find the place briefly easier to hear. The undercity would become more intelligible to its own inhabitants exactly when intelligibility is needed.

This, I think, would be one of the power’s chief gifts: legibility.

Not ease, perhaps, but clarity.

The hidden district is a place where survival depends on reading minute truths. A benevolent power would not substitute itself for that skill; it would honor it by making the truths a little less cruelly obscure. Water would taste more honestly of what it is. Dangerous air would feel wrong earlier. Rot would smell sharper before it spreads. Sound would go strange before collapse, not after. The world would become slightly more willing to be known.

And to the being who lives there—whether people, creature, colony, or all braided together—the power would give dignity before comfort.

That is important. Too often mercy from above humiliates what it helps, treating endurance as pitiable and adaptation as deformity. A wise power would see instead that this life under stone has produced forms of attention, craft, reciprocity, and patience that many brighter worlds never learn. It would bless those qualities openly.

If there are people there, their maintenance rituals would begin to work with a new exactness. The old woman who can tell sweet water from burdened water by smell alone would find herself uncannily right every time. The child who learns taps too quickly would be recognized, not mocked. Ledger-keepers would discover that their scratch marks, knot-codes, and inherited measures align with deeper patterns than they knew. The power would not replace civic practice with miracle; it would reveal that faithful practice has always been a kind of conversation with reality, and now reality is answering back more clearly.

If there is a singular keeper-being, the blessing might be lonelier and more intimate.

It has spent its life listening, redirecting, clearing, tasting, interpreting. Useful, perhaps loved in the sideways way such beings are loved, but also burdened with the endlessness of maintenance. The benevolent power would not take away its task, because the task is also its identity. But it might divide the burden. The being would no longer be the only one who notices first. Walls would warn others. Water would carry simpler messages. A little of the district’s vigilance would be redistributed into the district itself. For the first time in a very long while, the keeper could sleep deeply.

That would be holy.

Not ascension. Not transformation into something grander. Rest without dereliction. The certainty that one may close both eyes and the third throat will still sing true until morning.

Perhaps the power would also establish one place in the undercity where fear cannot accumulate.

Not a temple in the surface sense. More likely a chamber already central to use—the circular cistern room, say—becoming imperceptibly but decisively different. Quarrels would lower their voices there. Panic would fail to reproduce itself within its acoustics. Water brought there would settle more quickly from cloudiness. The sick laid beside its cistern would not always recover, because benevolence is not omnipotent erasure, but they would suffer less confusion. Those dying there would hear familiar sounds sharpen and arrange themselves. The room would become sanctuary not because nothing bad happens in it, but because nothing in it is abandoned.

The power might consecrate by reliability.

In time there would be stories: in the heartroom, the dripping note never falters during childbirth; in the heartroom, lost memory returns enough to name a child; in the heartroom, a cracked jar does not widen until it has been emptied safely; in the heartroom, if you lay your palm on the timber cover and ask without greed, you will know which water to trust.

And still the power would be careful not to become tyranny.

A wise benevolence does not make dependents where it could make partners. So it would refuse many chances to overrule. It would not answer every request for abundance. It would not turn every bitter source sweet, because mineral difference is part of the district’s intelligence. It would not stop all collapses if some collapses open needed air or reveal hidden cisterns or simply belong to the long geology from which the place has always borrowed room. It would let mortality remain mortality. It would let labor remain meaningful. It would preserve the dignity of cause and effect.

What it would oppose is despair.

When a run of bad seasons came—and they always come—the power would make sure no one mistook them for cosmic contempt. There would be signs, small but sufficient, that the world had not turned hostile in its foundations. A lamp refusing to gutter in a flood draft. A child hearing a rescue tap through too much stone. A dead vent unexpectedly giving one clean breath when it is needed to survive. A hidden pocket of sweet water found exactly where old records, long doubted, said it should be. Enough to continue.

If the power were truly generous, it might also do something for memory.

Places like this are endangered not only by collapse but by forgetting. One lost technique, one dead lineage of listeners, one broken chain of seasonal knowledge, and the equilibrium falters. So the benevolent presence might lay a blessing on transmission. Children would remember what they are taught in the chambers where the lessons matter. The wall taps would become a little easier to learn. Dreams might preserve practical truths in memorable forms: an ancestor pointing to the fourth basin; a sequence of notes recurring until someone checks the old quarry face and finds the beginning of a dangerous shift. Not prophecy in the grand style—mnemonic mercy.

The dead, too, would be treated tenderly.

If the underfolk seal their dead into side cells, the power would not disturb that custom. It might instead ensure that the chambers of the dead remain dry enough, stable enough, acoustically calm enough that grief can enter and return without terror. Perhaps the buried cells would join the district’s benevolence: not haunted, exactly, but stabilizing. Ancestors in the walls not as clinging presences but as settled weight, a continuity of care. A wise power would understand that in such a place remembrance is structural.

And what if the being there is the dispersed colony-organism braided through pores and channels? Then the power’s kindness would be subtler still.

It would not “purify” the walls of that life. It would recognize it as one of the district’s oldest citizens. It might teach the people, gradually, that certain films are friends, certain sheens protective, certain translucent lappets to be left undisturbed because they sweeten runoff or hold a seam open. The old false divide between infrastructure and organism would soften into reverence. The colony-being itself might be granted coherence enough to choose more deliberately: to close a dangerous crack, to divert harmful seepage, to carry a warning through vibration or altered tone. Not made into a speaking oracle—again, that would be vulgar—but allowed a little more intentionality in the service it has always half-rendered.

Under such notice, the district as a whole would become more itself.

That is the deepest thing a benevolent and wise power can do: not overwrite a nature, but help it arrive more fully into its best form.

The undercity would still be cool, damp, close, mineral, laboring, dim. No leaves would suddenly tremble in impossible sunlight. No shafts would tear open to reveal a consoling sky. But the stone’s sweating might become less wasteful, the water less treacherous, the sounds less misleading, the repairs more durable, the listeners less lonely. The place would remain underground and yet feel less forsaken by vertical distance. It would know itself accompanied.

Eventually, perhaps, the people or keeper would understand that attention has fallen on them. Not by spectacle but by pattern. By the way aid arrives in the native syntax of the place. By the fact that what is most local to them—drip, hum, seam, crust, echo—has become slightly more merciful without ceasing to be itself.

Then what would they do?

They would probably not build a cathedral.

They would add one more niche somewhere near the cistern room. A very small one. In it they would place no idol at first, perhaps only a smooth stone kept always damp, or a bowl that is never permitted to stand empty, or a mallet hung on a cord because the first answered knocking was the beginning of trust. They would develop a courtesy before opening sluices. They would teach children a listening pause before drawing from a newly sweetened seam. They would add one more category to the ledgers: not just drinking, washing, mortar, bitter, sacred—but given.

And the power, if it is truly wise, would not ask for more than that.

It would remain mostly unpictured. Better a practice than an image. Better a confidence than a regime. Better a district that has become harder to betray than a populace made theatrically devout.

In the long run, its greatest act might be this:

to make the place survivable enough that it can choose its future rather than merely endure it.

Perhaps that means the underfolk continue as they are for many generations, with stronger records, steadier water, fewer needless deaths. Perhaps it means they eventually open a safer relation to the surface on their own terms—trade shafts, measured contact, a controlled exchange of goods and knowledge without surrendering their way of life. Perhaps it means the keeper-creature, once solitary, leaves signs by which another of its kind can be found or raised or recognized. Perhaps it means the colony-being spreads into abandoned chambers and prepares them, very slowly, for habitation again. The power would not decide this unilaterally. It would create the conditions in which coercion relaxes enough for genuine choice.

Because benevolence, here, is not rescue from place.

It is fidelity to place.
It is making endurance less punishing.
It is teaching the stone to be a little more forthcoming, the water a little more honest, the sounds a little more guiding.
It is giving companionship without colonizing the life it accompanies.

So yes: the wise and benevolent power would do something.

It would stoop to enter.
It would learn to knock.
It would bless the undercity in the language of drips.
