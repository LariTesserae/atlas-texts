The benevolent and wise power would not arrive as conquest, cure, or revelation.

It would understand almost at once that this is not a place suffering from mere defect. The city is not simply broken. The mountain is not simply wrong. The being that lives there—whether one calls it a young law, a settling principle, a half-formed local physics, a distributed mind in stone and interval—is not an enemy to be cast out. It is unfinished, yes. But unfinishedness is not the same as evil. Much of what is beautiful here depends on that distinction being honored.

So the wise power would begin with restraint.

It would stand, perhaps nowhere visible, perhaps everywhere lightly at once: in the sweating rails before dawn, in the long cistern courtyards, in the fog snagged on signal masts, in the bare quarry faces where fresh reality still shows its grain. It would watch the city doing what it already knows how to do—maintain, revise, adapt, instruct the world by repetition—and it would love it for that. Not sentimentally. Precisely. It would see the dignity of a people who do not ask the world to be easy before they agree to inhabit it.

And because it is benevolent, it would want to help.

And because it is wise, it would know that help given clumsily can destroy the very life it means to save.

It would not force the mountain to finish.

That would be the first temptation, and it would refuse it.

To fully stabilize the city in an instant—to lock every alley, fix every stair, decide every adjacency, silence every impossible echo, pin each reflection to its lawful weather, harden all the warm-setting geometry into obedient permanence—would certainly relieve suffering. Fewer falls. Fewer lost deliveries. Fewer vanished corridors. Fewer widows receiving notices revised after the fact. But it would also be a violence of a high order. It would take a place whose soul is collaborative maintenance and convert it into a solved problem. It would kill a becoming in order to grant convenience. Wise powers know that not all pain should be preserved, but neither should every wildness be flattened into safety.

It would not remove the being from the place either.

For the being is not lodged in the city the way a parasite lodges in flesh. The city and the being have grown each other. The people have shaped the instability into custom, architecture, humor, ethics, and care. The unfinished mountain has learned streets from their insistence, and they have learned patience from its hesitation. To separate them entirely would be like removing weather from a coast or memory from an old face. Mercy can mutilate, if it insists on cleanliness.

So what would it do?

It would make the place kinder without making it lesser.

It would choose not completion, but grace.

First, I think, it would speak to the being beneath the city—not in language, not in commands, but in the deeper form wise powers use with young realities: example, rhythm, permission.

It would descend into the unconsented stone, into the still-warm grammar of distance and surface, and show it something it has been trying to learn all along: that constancy need not mean rigidity. That a wall can remember itself without becoming dead. That a road can remain a road through seasons without forfeiting all capacity for growth. That continuity is not imprisonment. That form is not failure of possibility, but one of possibility’s mature expressions.

The mountain-being, if it can be called that, would not understand at once. It has been learning through pressure, repetition, and human insistence. It has been educated by rail lines and retaining walls, by footfall and drainage channels, by the civic stubbornness of those who repaint the same curb after every subtle rearrangement. The wise power would not interrupt that education. It would join it.

Where the people teach through maintenance, the power would teach through gentleness.

Places of recurrent danger would begin, little by little, to behave with greater mercy. Not fixed permanently, perhaps, but less treacherously. The extra stair landing that appears in fog would appear with a handrail. The alley that lengthens over months would do so with visible seams, so that the eye may notice what the body has not yet learned. The tunnel that opens onto blank rock would first give warning by a change in resonance, a deepening hum, a harmless shower of dust in time enough to turn back. Reflections in standing water would remain strange, but become less luring to grief. Echoes would still arrive early or late, but not in ways that draw the exhausted into hazard.

It would not abolish uncertainty. It would sand its sharpest edges.

And for the people, it would offer gifts so slight that many might call them coincidence.

Surveyors would find, on the worst mornings, that their plumb lines hang truer than expected.

Children learning invariants would remember them with unusual calm.

Workers crossing upper fog districts with line reels at their hips would feel, now and then, a subtle certainty in the hand—not enough to replace caution, but enough to reward it.

Dogs who refuse a dangerous street would do so more emphatically, and sooner.

The bells in the Hanging Wards would carry farther through the mist without seeming louder.

Moss on the safest retaining walls would grow in richer bands, while the walls under strain would pale or dry just enough to signal concern.

In the topological bureau, tired-eyed clerks would discover that revised maps disagree less often when drawn with patience rather than haste, as if the city has become more willing to be witnessed by those who attend it honestly.

That would be one kind of intervention: a blessing on legibility.

Not certainty—legibility.

The wise power would understand that these people do not need a world incapable of change nearly so much as they need a world willing, when it changes, to leave signs. The deepest cruelty of unstable reality is not movement itself but unreadable movement. So the benevolent thing would be to increase the readability of flux. To let the unfinished continue, but with better manners.

Then it would turn toward suffering more directly.

There are hospitals on the dim ledges, workers in foundries, children in stairwells, old women with polished sticks, mechanics under tractors while condensation drips from steel, herders on roofs, collectors in cistern chambers, all of them subject not only to strangeness but to labor, accident, cold, shortage, and fatigue. A wise power that loved only the mountain-being and not the human cost would be merely aesthetic. Benevolence must stoop.

So perhaps the reservoirs would slowly sweeten.

Not miraculously overflowing—this is not that kind of story—but becoming cleaner, less prone to hidden fouling, less burdened by subtle mineral sickness. Condensation systems would gather a little more than expected at the worst times of year. Leaks would announce themselves before becoming losses. The blind white amphibians in the overflow systems would thrive and spread where they are needed most, keeping pipework clearer than engineering alone can manage.

Perhaps the foundry heat would cease, in some districts, to linger so murderously in bad air. Ventilation would catch more efficiently. Boiler failures would happen on shifts with the most experienced hands present. A brake line would hold one minute longer; a weld would fail in inspection rather than under load; a cable would fray where it can be seen.

The benevolent power would have no contempt for small mercies. Small mercies are what such cities are built of.

And because it is wise, it would understand that institutions matter as much as miracles.

So it might bless the habits by which the city preserves itself. Not with visible halos, but with durability. The bureau of topological revision would become harder to defund, easier to trust, more resistant to corruption or despair. Apprentices would continue entering the trades that maintain shared life: map-printing, bell-tuning, line inspection, animal warding, drainage, bridge articulation, archive-keeping. The knowledge of old women, dog-handlers, roof-herders, and unofficial readers of the city would be less often dismissed by formal authority. The practical and the uncanny would learn a better cooperation.

This too is the work of wise benevolence: not replacing communal intelligence, but protecting it from erosion.

As for the animals, the power would not strip them of their animalness by making them symbols only. But it might deepen the channels by which they already participate in the city’s life. The cliff-herds’ routes would become slightly more predictive of coming shifts in the slopes. The scavenger birds would avoid unsafe vents before structural failures, and people would learn—perhaps have already long known—to trust the pattern. The ribbon-backed warm-catwalk things might gather where electrical strain is dangerous, making their bodies into inadvertent warning flags. The city already lives among signs; the benevolent power would tune them, not invent them from nothing.

And there would be consolation too, because wisdom knows that not every wound can be prevented.

This city must have its dead: workers lost to falls, to machinery, to exposure, to simple attrition in a hard place; children who miscounted; elders who knew too many revisions; animals struck, starved, tangled, or driven over edges by weather and fright. A benevolent and wise power would not merely optimize systems and call that love. It would attend mourning.

Maybe in this city the dead are hardest on the living because place itself is unreliable. Where exactly did it happen? Was this wall here then? Did the corridor once connect differently? Did the witness stand where the witness now remembers standing? Grief hates unstable coordinates. So the power might grant to mourning certain fixednesses unavailable elsewhere.

Memorial stairways would not change.

Places chosen for remembrance would hold.

Names inscribed in damp stone would remain legible.

When a family returns to a spot of loss, the route there would be straightforward that day. The bells would sound clearly. The fog would thin enough to let faces be seen. The dead would not be returned, but the living would be spared the indignity of searching for the location of their own sorrow.

This, too, is grace.

And perhaps, rarely, the power would act more openly.

Not often. Only when catastrophe threatened to exceed what collaboration could answer.

A whole terrace beginning to shear loose above sleeping blocks: the stone would hesitate.

A funicular cable on the point of failure over a drop into fog: something impossible but not violent would occur—friction where there should be none, time enough for brakes, a minute lent by mercy.

A topological convulsion in a school district during transit hours: every dog in the neighborhood would begin barking at once, every bell would ring itself one note out of sequence, every bird on the power lines would rise together, and the children, trained in invariants, would stop where they are and wait.

The wise power would not perform spectacles for belief. It would intervene as infrastructure does when it is functioning perfectly: decisively, almost anonymously, in time.

Would the people notice?

Of course.

But they would notice in their own way.

Some would call it a favorable cycle, a maturing of local conditions, a recent reduction in severe spatial malice. Some would attribute it to improved municipal methods, and they would not be entirely wrong. Some would say the mountain is settling at last. Some would insist the old warning customs are simply being obeyed better than before. Some in the Hanging Wards would whisper that the carrion birds have begun receiving clearer instructions. Children would tell each other that the city has learned to be careful with them. A few surveyors, exhausted and embarrassed by their own tenderness, might admit in private that the place sometimes feels as though it is trying not to hurt them.

That would be enough.

A wise power does not always require worship. It may prefer participation.

And if it did choose one further gift, one deepest gift, I think it would be this:

It would make possible a true conversation between the people and the being under the stone.

Not speech, exactly. Not some crude scene in which the mountain suddenly booms opinions through transit tunnels. That would be vulgar and probably useless. No: it would make the reciprocal nature of the place more consciously knowable. The people already teach the city through maintenance. The city already answers through adaptation. But this exchange is dim, inferred, half-superstitious. The wise power might brighten it just enough that custom becomes recognized as dialogue.

Engineers would begin to design not only against movement, but with a clearer sense of what kinds of repetition the mountain can keep.

Builders would learn which proportions soothe unstable geometry.

Bell patterns would be found to calm drift in certain districts.

Watercourses, carefully redirected, would help teach downhillness to newly risen slopes.

Certain plantings—moss, vine, reed, root—would prove not merely decorative or erosive but orienting, as though life itself can help persuade matter into memory.

And the being beneath it all, the young law, would begin to feel less beleaguered by human insistence and more accompanied by it. Less corrected, more raised.

This may be the wisest thing the power could do: transform coexistence from accidental endurance into mutual apprenticeship.

The city would still be hard.

People would still work until their backs ached and their sleeves were wet to the elbow.

Maps would still need revision.

Fog would still pool.

Animals would still interfere with timetables.

Children would still be taught to count windows before entering and after leaving.

The mountain would still not be finished.

But fewer losses would be meaningless. Fewer hazards would be mute. More of the instability would become expressive rather than cruel. The city would remain itself—cold, high, damp with withheld rain, full of steel, hoof, moss, resonance, and difficult grace—but it would lean, ever so slightly, toward mercy.

In the very long term, perhaps centuries, the wise power might hope for something greater than rescue.

Not a finalized city, and not an eternally unfinished one, but a mature relation.

A mountain that has learned enough form to be trustworthy.
A people who have kept enough humility to remain attentive.
A civic culture that remembers instability without fetishizing it.
A living law of place that can grow without devouring those who dwell within it.

The power would not hurry this. Haste is one of the great cruelties visited by the strong upon the becoming.

So it would remain.

Quietly.

In thresholds that hold.
In warnings that arrive in time.
In maps that age more slowly than fear.
In reservoirs that do not sour.
In bells that carry.
In dogs that balk.
In bereavements granted a fixed address.
In children who come home by routes that stay loyal long enough.
In stone that, under load and weather and patient human repetition, begins at last to understand the kindness of keeping its word.

And perhaps sometimes, at shift change, on an upper platform with the valley dim below and cranes half erased in cloud, someone would hear beneath the turbines and cart wheels and boiler hiss and cable-wind and dripping drums another note added to the city’s layered speech.

Not louder than the rest.

Just steadier.

As if, under all that burdened ascent, something vast and patient had laid a hand on the unfinished world and said:

You do not have to stop becoming.

But while they live here,
learn
to become more gently.
