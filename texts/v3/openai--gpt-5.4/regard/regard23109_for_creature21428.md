A benevolent and wise power, turning its attention here, would not begin with disgust.

It would not see filth where life had made a covenant with scarcity. It would not call the basin accursed simply because it was loud with scraping instead of birdsong, because its dwellers shone with dust instead of dew. Wisdom would recognize at once what the impatient miss: this place is not failed. It is completed according to hard terms. It is a kingdom of salvage, a difficult and exact peace assembled from dryness, pressure, age, and the stubborn afterlives of machines. Nothing here is ornamental. Nothing here is careless. Every creature is an answer.

So the first thing such a power would do is restrain itself.

That is important. Lesser beneficence blunders. It sees want and rushes to fill it. It sees harshness and tries to soften. It imagines that if only water were poured here, if only green things were planted, if only the metal were cleared away and the basin “restored,” then mercy would have been accomplished. But wisdom knows that help can be a form of violence when it arrives in the wrong shape. Rain, in abundance, might drown broods, rot nests, wake dormant chemistries, flood the powder warrens, turn archive-dust to suffocating paste. Sunlight broadened and warmed might cook what coolness has preserved. Cleansing this place might mean erasing it.

A wise power would understand that not every salvation is conversion.

It would kneel, if powers kneel. Or stoop its vastness into attention. It would learn the grammar of the basin before speaking into it. It would listen to the click before the lamp flickers, to the irregular grind beneath the oldest rubble, to the territorial drumming in hidden cavities, to the dry hiss of thousands of feet crossing mesh. It would watch traffic in the slag cracks. It would notice where one species never crosses bare ceramic, where another gathers around old grease ghosts, where the broad-backed migrators make their loop with priestly regularity. It would see that the swarm is not chaos but literature: a script of routes, prohibitions, timings, tolerances, truces.

And because it is benevolent, this attention would itself be a blessing.

Not the blessing of ownership. Not “You are mine now, and so you must become legible to my preferences.” Rather the blessing of being fully perceived and not condemned. There are worlds that perish because no greatness ever looked upon them except to use them. There are forms of life that endure forever under the sentence of ugliness. A wise and loving power would lift that sentence first. It would know these tiny plated bodies as souls of a sort—not souls like ours perhaps, but centers of appetite, fear, rhythm, inheritance, adaptation, effort. It would honor the long labor by which they made a biosphere from remnants.

Only then would it act.

And its action, I think, would be small in appearance and immense in consequence.

It would not remake the basin into paradise. It would make the basin more itself, and safer in being so.

Perhaps it would steady the buried machine.

Not restore it to full operation, not wake some ancient industrial purpose and let gears return with catastrophic force, but bless the failing heart beneath the sediment so that its intermittent turning becomes reliable enough not to betray those who have patterned their generations around it. The slow rotational grind that now stutters every few minutes would continue—not louder, not hotter, but with a deeper consistency, less liable to cease without warning. The warm trace around the unseen vent would remain warm. The vibrations that mark hunting times, brood safety, migration turns, emergence windows, and retreat signals would no longer vanish for whole fatal stretches. In human terms this is a very modest miracle. In the basin it would be comparable to making a climate trustworthy.

Or perhaps it would do something subtler still: it would reduce needless catastrophe.

Not predation; predation belongs here. Not hunger; hunger is one of the basin’s shaping truths. A wise power would not abolish the harsh economies that have made these creatures what they are. But there is a difference between hardship and waste. When an entire ceiling of compacted rust collapses for no reason beyond accumulated neglect, crushing broods indiscriminately, that is not law but excess. When toxic seepage from some old sealed chamber suddenly vents and sterilizes a quarter of the basin, that is not the food web speaking but history lashing out blindly. Benevolence might intervene there. Not continuously, not with a visible hand snatching every falling plate, but by minute mercies distributed through matter: a brace that holds one season longer; a crack that opens slowly instead of all at once; a dangerous bloom made inert; a trapped charge bled safely into harmless metal; a vent redirected by a hair’s breadth so that warmth spreads without poison.

Such miracles would be almost impossible to detect unless one knew what nearly happened.

That, too, feels right. The best care is often infrastructural.

A benevolent power might also increase the basin’s abundance, but only within its native logic. Not leaves. Not flowers. Not a sudden lushness that would replace the old order with another. Instead: trace organics carried more regularly on the air. Thin films appearing where there were none, enough to feed the scrapers and thereby everything above them. Mineral and microbial intermediaries encouraged in the dust so that waste becomes edible one step sooner. Condensation so slight it never beads, exactly as before, but just frequent enough in hidden seams to support brood survival during the harshest cycles. The basin would remain dry. It would simply become less cruelly exact.

If the power were especially tender, it might create refuges.

Tiny ones. Sanctuaries scaled correctly for thumb-joint creatures and seam-bound lives. A few additional hollows under stable plates. A few ceramic interiors altered so the ceramic-avoiders may cross by roughened mineral bridges. A few old cylinders made thermally gentler, so eggs need not all be wagered on a single warmth source. Narrow passages through compacted dust that allow prey species to escape total local exhaustion and predators to disperse without mass die-off. Hidden architecture, nothing grander than a new crack in the right place, but enough to reduce exterminating bottlenecks and make the ecosystem more resilient.

And because wisdom includes imagination, the power might preserve memory.

Not human memory imposed from outside, but basin-memory strengthened against oblivion. The dust is already archive. The routes are already inherited. Perhaps the blessing would deepen the fidelity with which successful paths, nest sites, warning cues, and seasonal pulses pass from generation to generation—not by conscious teaching, but through instinct clarified, signal sharpened, timing made truer. Whole lineages would waste less life relearning what the place has taught for ages. The swarm would become a little less vulnerable to senseless forgetting.

Would it make them more intelligent? Perhaps not in any dramatic sense. There is vanity in assuming that every grace must make a thing resemble us. But it might make them more lucid within their own mode: tremor-readers less likely to panic at harmless noise, drummers more precise in communication, burrowers more sensitive to unstable sediment, brood-tenders better able to distinguish viable eggs from dead ones, scavengers less drawn to poisoned residues. Wisdom refines fit.

And perhaps, because benevolence often carries beauty with it even where beauty was not requested, there would be one visible sign.

Not a beam from heaven. Not sudden verdure. Something native to the basin’s aesthetic: when the hooded lamp clicks on under its shield, it now flickers with a steadier rhythm, and for a heartbeat the dust motes in the air shine like filings of dull silver. Or the sulfide crusts, if they are indeed alive, take on a faint inner blue when the buried machine turns, a color so restrained it might be mistaken for angle and chance. Or certain shells begin, over generations, to carry a nacre-like sheen in the deepest seams of their plates, not bright enough to announce miracle, only enough to suggest that care has entered inheritance.

The wise power might also defend the basin from larger harms beyond itself.

If scavengers, miners, children, collectors, or empire were ever to break in—to strip the old metal, poison the hollows, flood the place, burn it clean, or classify it as waste to be erased—then benevolence could become fiercer. Not murderous, but unyielding. Tools fail. Maps prove inaccurate. Surveyors overlook the entrance. Plans are delayed. Those who come seeking extraction feel inexplicable disinterest. Those who come prepared to destroy decide, without knowing why, that this particular basin is not worth the trouble, or perhaps strangely deserving of being left alone. A blessing can take the form of administrative obscurity. Many fragile worlds have survived by slipping beneath the notice of appetites larger than themselves.

But let me say the most radical thing such a power might do:

It might love the swarm without demanding a representative.

Humans prefer singular residents. We want a queen, a guardian, a named beast, a face to speak with, a chosen vessel in whom meaning can gather. But the resident here is distributed. The basin’s “person,” if one can say that, is emergent: the totality of crossings, feedings, molts, predations, hatchings, vibrations, routings, and returns. A truly wise power would not force this multitude into monarchy just to make blessing easier. It would address the many as many. It would bless circulation, not merely any individual body. It would treat the swarm as a kind of plural creature and allow its soul—if soul is the word—to remain particulate.

That matters. Because to honor something truly, you must honor its form.

So perhaps the power’s prayer, if powers pray over what they protect, would sound nothing like our liturgies. It would be a distributed grace:
for the underside and the seam,
for the hidden route and the salvaged warmth,
for the shell that hardens after molt,
for the egg in the stamped recess,
for the feeder of rust and the eater of feeders,
for the warning carried through mesh,
for the machine that does not wholly fail,
for the dust that remembers.

And if there were speech in it, perhaps only this:
Continue.

Not “Become otherwise.”
Not “Ascend.”
Not “Be purified.”
Continue, and be spared what need not be suffered.

In time, the effects would become undeniable, though not theatrical. Population crashes would be less absolute. Local extinctions would reverse. New specializations would arise around the stabilized machine pulse. Predators would remain terrible but less often annihilating. Broods would survive harsh cycles in slightly greater numbers. The basin would sound fuller, but also calmer somehow—more layered than frantic. A deeper equilibrium would settle in, not gentle in the sentimental sense, but enduring.

And if one day an observer returned after many years, they might say: the place seems healthier, though nothing recognizable has been improved. It is still dry, still cool, still crowded with shells and powder and ruthless small lives. The old metal is older. The dust is thicker. The lamp still clicks. The buried heart still turns. Yet the whole basin carries the unmistakable quality of having been taken seriously by something vast and kind.

I think that is what a benevolent and wise power would do here.

Not rescue it from itself.
Not translate it into a prettier language.
Not spare it all pain.
Not crown one insect king and call the rest background.

It would protect the integrity of this hard-won world, ease the blindest forms of suffering, stabilize the precarious generosities already present, and confer the great blessing of all durable care: room to go on becoming what it already truly is.

And that might be the holiest possible act in such a place:
to let the basin remain a basin,
the machine remain a heart,
the swarm remain a people,
and the future arrive in increments small enough for tiny feet to survive.
