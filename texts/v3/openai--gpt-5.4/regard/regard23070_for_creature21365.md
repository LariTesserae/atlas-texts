The benevolent and wise power would not arrive as light.

It would know at once that light, in the ordinary triumphant sense, would be violence here. To flood this place with seeing would be to insult a civilization that has made truth out of touch, law out of resonance, tenderness out of dryness. It would not tear open the chamber to a sky. It would not pour rivers through streets that had learned holiness from a cupful of condensation. It would not “improve” the people by restoring what they had long ago ceased to need. Wisdom would begin with restraint.

So first it would listen.

Not metaphorically only, but truly: it would place its attention through the vaults and root-columns and suspended walkways the way one lowers a hand into deep water without stirring silt. It would learn the city in the native way. It would know the difference between load-creak and growth-creak, between a wind organ speaking weather and a tower beginning to fail at the core, between communal chant and a child lost two chambers over trying not to cry too loudly for shame. It would listen to the white tendrils recoiling from heat, to the seed vaults holding their breath against the long dry years, to the old ones laying their palms to structural bark and hearing fatigue before collapse has a sound.

And it would listen to the people as well: not merely to what they say in ceremony, but to what they permit themselves not to say because everyone here already knows it. The exhaustion carried privately by the tapkeepers. The fear hidden in the pruners’ discipline when a reservoir stem goes soft too early in the season. The grief of losing an elder whose body was also an archive. The small, hard pride of a people who have survived so long without spectacle that they no longer expect the universe to admire them. The power would understand that they do not need admiration. They need continuance.

Then, because it is benevolent, it would desire to help.

And because it is wise, it would ask what form of help does not unmake the helped.

A lesser mercy would bring abundance and call itself good. This one would see that abundance can be as destructive as famine if it breaks the disciplines by which meaning has been made. If water suddenly ran in channels, old law would fail overnight. Storage, exchange, kinship, ritual, architecture, all the patient ethics of rightful cutting and shared thirst would crack under the shock of miracle. If the city were made easy, the people might live and yet lose the shape of their humanity. A wise power does not confuse relief with goodness when relief arrives by erasing the soul of a place.

So it would not abolish hardship.

But neither would it leave everything unchanged out of some austere reverence for “authentic struggle.” That too would be vanity. Wisdom is not sentimental about suffering. There are burdens that form a people, and there are burdens that merely grind them down. A good power would learn the difference.

It would begin, I think, by acting at the margins of catastrophe.

Not a torrent, but a steadier rumor of moisture.

Some deep seam in the ancient mineral body, long sealed by salt or pressure, would be persuaded—never shattered—to give a little more than before. Not enough to become stream or pool. Never enough to transform the city into something wet and easy and unrecognizable. Just enough that condensation chambers produce certainty where once they produced chance. Just enough that a dry generation becomes survivable without the cruel arithmetic of deciding which district gets to remain fertile and which must sleep. Just enough that children are not taught, quite so early, the taste of civic panic.

Perhaps nothing visible would announce this intervention. Only the absorbent nets in the high vents would come down heavier more often. The acoustic bladders of the hydration plants would hold their tones longer into the lean season. A tapkeeper would put their ear to a reservoir stem and pause, frowning, because the pressure was slightly kinder than it ought to have been. The old would say that the city’s breath had deepened.

The power would strengthen memory, too—but again in native terms.

Not books of fire. Not inscriptions visible only to eyes. It would bless persistence in textures, in scents, in acoustic signatures. It might cause certain civic patterns to hold their legibility longer before wear smooths them. A threshold important to law would keep its scoring under generations of hands. A lineage braid underfoot would remain distinct despite dust and traffic. The voices of tuned chambers would drift less with age. The wind organs would fall out of calibration more slowly. In this way the city’s external memory—the living archive by which the people remain themselves—would become a little less fragile.

And perhaps, once in a long while, when an elder dies whose knowledge should not vanish yet cannot be spoken fast enough into apprentices before the roots reclaim them, the power would permit a transference of a very subtle kind. Not a ghost. Not a haunting. Rather a habit preserved in matter. The growth fed by that elder would thereafter answer the hand with unusual clarity. Lay a palm on a certain buttress and feel, somehow, the right moment to cut. Stand in a certain hollow tower and hear, somehow, the weather pattern that old person always predicted. The dead would not remain as personalities, but as refinements of civic intelligence distributed back into the city they had served.

This would fit the place. It would be mercy without spectacle, memory without idolatry.

The benevolent power might also address loneliness.

Not because the people are constantly miserable—one should not romanticize them into stoics nor pity them into children—but because to be the only moving intention in a rooted world carries a particular metaphysical weight. They have made companionship among themselves and with the city, yes. But there remains in such a civilization the vast surrounding dark, the old featureless burden of mineral time, the possibility that no one beyond their chambers has ever known or cared that they persisted at all.

A wise power would not answer this by appearing in overwhelming revelation. It would not demand worship through terror or splendor. Instead it might make itself knowable by recurrence and trust.

A certain harmless pressure-wave before a dangerous roof-shed.
A particular camphor-cold at the wall near chambers where a sleeping infant has stopped breathing.
A note in the wind organs that no tuning produces, always sounding before a hidden crack opens under a walkway.
A dream shared, rarely, among pollinators on the eve of an important season: not images, but tactile instructions too elegant to be mistaken for personal invention.

Over generations, the people would come to recognize that the city is not alone in being answerable. There is, somehow, a farther listener in the dark.

Would they call it a god? Perhaps. Or perhaps they would have a humbler, more exact term: the Hearer in the Dry, the Kindly Pressure, the One Who Keeps Seams from Failing, the Hand Beyond the Wall. Since their theology would arise from function as much as awe, they might not argue whether it created the world. More important would be that it can be relied upon never to waste their reverence by demanding lies. A true benevolence would allow doubt, because doubt is native to careful people. It would make itself known by reliability rather than domination.

And there are things it would forbid itself.

It would not choose favorites among districts.
It would not turn legal authority into prophetic monopoly by speaking only to rulers, chord-keepers, or custodians of ancestral growth.
It would not make dependence into devotion.
It would not solve every failure, because then no skill could remain meaningful.
It would not preserve every life, because finitude is part of the tenderness by which these people love one another and their city.
It would not end danger, but it would oppose wasteful devastation.

If some blight came—some slow rot in the vascular lattice, some mineral poison rising from below, some tectonic shift threatening to shear the whole basin’s ancient shell—the benevolent power would act more openly. Wisdom does not remain coy in the face of annihilation. Then perhaps whole districts would hear through the root-columns a new rhythm, guiding evacuation. The blind draft-seeking tendrils would all bend the same impossible direction at once, revealing an unguessed safe route. Seed vaults thought barren would prove unexpectedly viable. A collapse would stop at the width of one habitation instead of taking three generations of work with it. The power would not make the people passive under rescue; it would make their own capacities suddenly and decisively sufficient.

That is how a wise helper behaves: by giving creatures back to themselves at the moment they are about to be stolen by disaster.

Over a much longer span, I think the power would try to enlarge their future without violating their nature.

Perhaps there are other chambers in the old body of the world, sealed pockets where related ecologies have failed, or never flowered, or wait empty. The power might not command expansion, but it could make the possibility legible. Echoes would return oddly beyond a wall once thought solid. A vent-current would begin carrying unknown mineral bitterness from very far off. Explorers following a sequence of harmless signs might find not treasure in the vulgar sense but continuity: new hollows where old skills matter, where the city’s disciplines can be planted again before crowding or fatigue overburdens the ancestral basin.

This too is benevolence: not only saving what exists, but making room for its descendants to remain themselves elsewhere.

Would the power love them? Yes, but not sentimentally.

It would love their exactness. Their refusal to cut without answer. The way they greet walls as fellow residents. The fact that, in a universe where no eye is rewarded, they still make beauty in tuned chambers and patterned bark. It would love the children learning with their palms what other peoples learn with sight. It would love the old who become infrastructural memory. It would love that they have built a republic not of conquest but of maintenance. Such a power would find in them something profoundly worthy: a people who did not respond to scarcity by becoming predators, but by becoming careful.

And the people, in turn, might slowly become gentler because of it—not softer, but freer from the most punishing forms of fear. Law could remain strict without carrying so much desperation. Ritual could remember gratitude alongside caution. The moral imagination of the city might widen. If there is a benevolent listener beyond them, then perhaps not every future must be defended as if the universe were fundamentally indifferent. Hope, in such a place, would not be bright. It would be tensile. It would sound like a support column that still holds.

The power’s greatest gift might therefore be almost impossible to notice from outside.

Not light.
Not abundance.
Not revelation.

Confidence.

A deepening trust, distributed through generations, that carefulness is not futile.

That the next hand laid on the living wall is met not only by stored water, old growth, and civic memory, but by a further kindness woven invisibly through them. That endurance is not merely endured. That somewhere beyond the last dry vault and the oldest mineral fold, wisdom has heard this little loud city in the dark and judged it worth preserving.

So what would the benevolent and wise power do?

Very little, visibly.
Very much, truly.

It would enter the covenant the people had already made with life, and keep it with them.
