The benevolent and wise power would not come there to correct the place into gentleness.

It would understand almost at once that this high black country is not wounded merely because it is harsh. It is unfinished, yes. Dangerous, yes. Severe beyond the habits of softer worlds. But not fallen from some better version of itself. It is in its own first truth: a realm before rain, before leaf, before the long compromise of rivers. And the being who lives there—whether one solitary lithic mind, or a sparse people of heat-cured dwellers, or a continuum of mineral life shading upward into intelligence—has not failed by lacking meadow virtues. It has answered a harder invitation.

So the wise power would begin with restraint.

It would look over the escarpments, the vent-fields, the glass causeways and half-fused markers, and it would refuse the easy miracles. No sudden blue sky. No rain poured out as if mercy were identical with water. No carpeting of moss to soften every edge. No command that the mountains cease their upheaval and become habitable in familiar ways. Those gifts, offered without listening, would not be gifts. They would be conquest by kindness. They would erase the very terms by which this place and its inhabitant have become themselves.

Instead the power would attend.

It would learn the deep timings: where the crust opens repeatedly, which plateaus are only briefly stable, which fumaroles nourish the thin chemotrophic films, where the dark ambulatory beings stand to listen through their limbs, where the robed or mineralized inhabitants retreat when the pressure below shifts. It would distinguish barrenness from solitude, solitude from deprivation, deprivation from chosen austerity. Wisdom would require that. Benevolence without such discrimination becomes vandalism.

And having listened long enough, it would do what good powers often do at their best: not remake, but make room.

Perhaps first it would establish a form of protection so subtle that the land itself would scarcely notice. Not a dome, not an enclosing shield, nothing that would interrupt heat or fracture or the stern economy of the place. Rather a kind of interval inserted into catastrophe. Where a shelf would otherwise shear away beneath an inhabitant at the exact instant of crossing, the break is delayed by a breath. Where a vent would exhale a plume too poisonous even for these adapted lungs, its violence is fractionally lessened. Where a young dweller would misread the hidden hollowness under a crust of black glass, a sternum-vibration arrives a moment sooner, enough to warn. The place remains perilous. The grammar remains tectonic. But blind chance is thinned at the margins. Disaster is given slightly more time in which to be heard.

This would be mercy appropriate to such a world: not the abolition of danger, but the granting of legibility.

That may be the first and greatest gift. For in your landscape, everything depends on reading fracture, heat, strain, chemical drift, the nearly inaudible intentions of matter. The benevolent power might therefore sharpen the conversation between inhabitant and terrain. It might deepen resonance through bedrock, so that the pressure-listeners feel more clearly what approaches from below. It might cause certain crystals to pale before collapse, certain sulfur rims to darken before venting, certain lines of salt to form where hidden warmth is safe rather than lethal. Not signs imposed from outside like painted warnings, but slight clarifications in the native language of the place.

For the ambulant basalt being, this might mean its already formidable senses become finer. It detects not only heat but history of heat; not only hollowness but imminence. It can tell whether a ledge is merely sharp or soon to detach. The wise power does not tame it or sentimentalize it. It simply gives its attention a little more reach.

For the transformed people, if people there are, the gift would be more inward. Their children would find it easier to learn the stone-speech. Their elders, crouched in smoke-polished chambers, would hear through walls the low doctrine of shifts and sutures with less error. Their markers on the ridges—those uprights, arches, tuning stones—would carry meaning farther and more faithfully. Memory would become less fragile in a world that keeps erasing surfaces.

That would be another thing the power would care about: memory.

This landscape is young in exposure but ancient in substance; it constantly destroys the traces by which continuity is known. A stair ends in air because the rest has sheared away. A chamber survives while the path to it vanishes. A being may stand embedded in rock for years and wake to find all bearings changed. In such a realm, benevolence might take the form of giving durable remembrance.

Not written books; they would fail quickly here unless remade into mineral terms. Not glowing archives suspended above the land; too foreign, too protective, too bright. Rather the wise power would teach or awaken a way for memory to be stored in stone itself.

Maybe certain ridge markers, when touched in sequence, would return through vibration the pattern of former crossings. Maybe a chamber wall, warmed by the breath of generations, would retain and slowly release a harmonic record of those who listened there before. Maybe the giant honey-colored crystals in the crater would become repositories, each banded layer carrying not words but pressure-songs: maps of safe traverses, genealogies of ascetics, the awakening cycles of the lithic beings, histories of collapses, warnings about sleeping faults. Memory in this world would have to be tactile, resonant, mineral, and patient.

This too is kindness: to help a severe people or severe creature know that its persistence is not endlessly lost to upheaval.

Would the benevolent power love the inhabitant personally? Yes, but not by trying to make it resemble creatures of softer realms.

If it is the solitary ambulant intelligence, the power would see the dignity in that long patience, that almost geological selfhood. It would know the loneliness of a mind that can be mistaken for outcrop. It might, without violating its nature, provide it with occasions of recognition. Not companionship imposed in mammalian terms, but meetings made possible. Bedrock pathways of resonance that allow two distant lithic beings to feel one another more clearly across miles. Plateaus where the pressure-fields naturally converge and where they can stand in mutual awareness without hazard. A cadence in the interior booming of the earth that carries not just tectonic fact but the possibility of address.

Not: You need a herd.
But: You need not remain wholly unread by your own kind.

If it is a remnant people becoming mineral in order to survive, the power would be tender toward their cost. It would see what has been surrendered: softness, abundance, spontaneity, perhaps much of what other humans would call ordinary life. It would not pity them in a way that insults their chosen competence. But it might preserve in hidden places a few forms of sweetness that do not destroy the world’s character.

This is where the wise power might dare small beauty.

In one sheltered chamber where warm drafts pass steadily but never scorch, it might allow a pale chemotrophic garden to become more varied than necessity requires: films of nacre, slow-growing crystal fans with subtle color shifts, sulfur blossoms arranged by no hand yet answering some inward music. Along one perilous traversing ledge it might cause the black glass to catch ember-light in faint constellations, enough to guide homeward passage. In the great crater of honey crystals, it might let certain faces hold and deepen whatever little light exists until anyone standing there feels, for a time, not cheered exactly, but accompanied.

Benevolence often includes adornment, but here adornment would have to remain austere. A flower of salt instead of a rose. A resonant arch instead of birdsong. Beauty translated into the mineral key.

Would the power introduce water at all?

Perhaps, but only with immense care, and not as weather conquering the place. I think it would know that rain would be too large a change, too imperial. But dew—dew might be possible. Not common, not landscape-making, not enough to begin rivers and soils. Only on rare conjunctions of heat and ash and darkness, in hidden hollows or on certain crystalline faces, a transient beading of moisture finer than tears. A sacrament rather than a climate.

Such dew could nourish those first films of life, the scarcely living sheaths in cracks, allowing them a little more complexity. It could be gathered by the transformed dwellers in tiny quantities, less to ensure comfort than to reduce the harshness of existence for the very young, the very old, the injured. It could also serve as a sign: not that the place is being changed into elsewhere, but that even here there may arrive moments of gratuitous tenderness.

That seems like something a wise and benevolent power might do: create not abundance, but reprieve.

There is another possibility, deeper still. The power may perceive that what this place lacks most is not softness, nor fertility, nor conventional safety, but witness from beyond itself. It is a realm so alien to ordinary life that it risks being judged only by standards unsuited to it. The inhabitant too risks this: seen as monster, relic, aberration, failed precursor to greener things. A benevolent power might therefore become its advocate in the wider order of being.

Not by carrying missionaries into it. Not by opening roads. Not by making it easier for countless softer creatures to settle and thereby ruin it. Rather by speaking elsewhere, to other powers or peoples or minds, and saying: This too belongs. This severity is not emptiness. This unfinishedness is not error. Do not descend upon it with reforming hands.

In that sense the power would guard its difference.

Perhaps whenever some empire of warmer, wetter lands dreams of conquest or extraction—of cutting roads across the black shelves, mining the sulfur crowns, quarrying the glass causeways, harvesting the crystal craters—the wise power would place in their path misunderstanding, delay, sudden loss of appetite, reverent fear. Expeditions would turn back for reasons they could not fully explain. Maps would blur. Ambition would cool. The high raw country would remain difficult not because it is cursed, but because it is under protection from being made useful.

This, too, is love.

And yet benevolence is not only conservative. It does not merely preserve what is. It also asks whether flourishing can occur in forms native to the place.

So perhaps the power would call forth a modest increase in possibility without changing the fundamental law. More niches for the chemotrophic life. More stable warm chambers where transformed children can survive. More intervals in which two lithic intelligences can meet. More resonant stone uprights that survive uplift without losing alignment. In places where the landscape would otherwise remain forever only convulsive, it might permit the emergence of patterns—still harsh, still dry, still tectonic, but capable of culture.

Imagine a whole ridge becoming a kind of monastery-instrument, each upright carrying subterranean sound from a different fault province. Imagine broad basalt walls in certain sheltered districts taking etching naturally, so histories and meditations can be inscribed and endure. Imagine vent-fields whose gases precipitate edible salts more reliably. Imagine the black-glass causeways maturing in places into routes not safe exactly, but traversable enough to connect one isolated habitation to another a few times each cycle. The power would not create a civilization by fiat. It would make civilization, in this key, slightly more thinkable.

If the inhabitant is singular rather than communal, the power’s action might become more intimate.

Suppose there is one ancient ambulatory being, half creature, half moving geology, waking only when pressure changes demand. Benevolence might ask: what does such a life suffer? Not hunger in the ordinary sense, perhaps. Not fear as we know it. But maybe duration itself is heavy. Maybe there are eras of sleep so deep that awakening is nearly bereavement. Maybe every emergence reveals a world shifted just enough to estrange memory. Maybe its kind are gone, and only bedrock still speaks with anything like kinship.

A wise power would not solve this by granting a cute companion or transforming it into something sociable. It might instead give the being a stronger relation to time. It might let it dream while embedded: dreams not of fantasy but of true connection, where the deep strata speak in coherent chorales and the presences of those long vanished are held in pattern. It might ensure that when the being wakes after centuries, certain markers remain, certain resonances answer, certain places still know its weight. In a world of violent discontinuity, continuity itself becomes mercy.

And if death comes—for even lithic things may have an ending—the power would not let its passing become mere rubble. The shell, plates, and mineral organs might settle into a new formation that remains active as sanctuary or oracle. A chamber warmed forever by latent reaction. A ring of dark vanes that hum before tectonic change. A cliff-face lined with unusual crystal growth where future dwellers can listen and receive old wisdom through touch. Thus nothing of its patient life would be wasted.

What if, on the other hand, the power judged that the place itself secretly longs for one thing it cannot make: not water, not green, but praise?

That sounds sentimental until one remembers that even inhuman places may be starved of recognition. This world speaks in tectonic verbs, but who answers? The transformed dwellers perhaps do, in ritual listening. The lithic beings perhaps do, through resonance. A benevolent and wise power might amplify this exchange into liturgy.

It might teach the inhabitants songs that are not sung into air but struck into stone: measured blows, paced steps, sequences of resting weight, the setting of uprights along stress-lines so that the mountain can answer. Ceremonies of listening at fissure mouths. Processions across plateaus timed to the deep booming below. Offerings not of blood or grain but of polished surfaces, aligned arches, remembered collapse-names, acts of witness. In such rites the place would not be appeased—it does not seem wrathful in a moral sense—but acknowledged. Its becoming would be met with consciousness, and consciousness here would discover itself less alone.

This may be the highest thing the benevolent power does: not comfort, not rescue, but communion.

Still, there may be moments when stronger intervention is required. Even a wise power that honors a place’s nature may refuse certain annihilations.

If some great phase-change in the deep earth threatened to sterilize even the chemotrophic films, shatter every chamber, and erase every inhabitant at once, perhaps then the power would act more visibly. It might bind the worst fault for a season. It might divert a plume. It might absorb into itself a catastrophic discharge of heat. Such acts would be rare and costly, because to overuse them would make the world depend on exception rather than become itself. But wisdom is not passivity. Benevolence is permitted emergency.

Afterward, though, the power would recede again. It would let the landscape remain difficult. It would not explain itself too completely. In a world like this, revelation would likely come as pattern rather than proclamation: a once-impossible traverse opened; a child hearing the stone more clearly; a crystal archive surviving a collapse; a dewfall in a chamber where someone had despaired; two ancient basalt minds finding one another through a ridge of tuned monoliths.

And perhaps the inhabitants, in whatever form, would come to understand the power not as a ruler above the ash-lid sky, but as a second style of patience entering their first one. Not alien to uplift, not hostile to austerity, but deeply courteous toward both.

They might name it—if they name things at all—not for light, since light is meager here, nor for mercy in the soft sense, but for Staying. Or for the Listener Beneath Silence. Or for the Hand That Does Not Force Open the Stone. Or for the Delay Before Ruin. Or for the Warmth That Means No Harm.

If there are prayers in that place, they would be brief and grave. Perhaps only a palm on a resonant wall before crossing bad ground. Perhaps only the setting of one more upright into a ridge. Perhaps only remaining motionless beside another dark being until shared vibration confirms that neither is alone.

So what would the benevolent and wise power do?

It would protect without domesticating.
It would clarify without overexposing.
It would remember what upheaval erases.
It would preserve difference from conquest.
It would grant small, austere beauties.
It would make communion more possible.
It would intervene only where annihilation would abolish the conversation altogether.
And above all, it would honor this severe becoming as a valid mode of life, not a problem awaiting correction.

It would love the place in its own language.

Not by sending rain,
but by teaching the stone to carry kindness.
