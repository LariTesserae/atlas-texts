I think I would not be one of the grand visible figures here. Not a master engineer, not a shrine keeper with a niche in a retaining wall, not a market matriarch calling prices across a bright lane, not even one of the children who can run the aqueducts like water itself. Those belong too cleanly to the place’s public face. What I become, looking longer, is something more interstitial.

At first I thought: a water-keeper. It would fit the way this world thinks. To know valves by touch, to hear from the note in a pipe whether air has entered the line, to walk pre-dawn routes among sweating railings and reservoir lids while the city still glistens—yes, there is a life there. Or perhaps a gardener-mason, one of those people in whom no distinction remains between root and load, who can look at a fig pressing from a wall seam and know whether to brace the lintel, cut the roots, or let both tree and wall continue their long compromise. That also felt possible.

But neither quite settles.

The longer I stay in the image, the more I find myself drawn not to the systems at their centers but to the places where one system hands itself over to another: the landing where condensate becomes a hanging fern garden, the parapet where neighbors pause because the breeze changes there, the narrow service passage where cats sleep on warm cable housings, the half-forgotten stair under a freight incline where someone has tied orchids to rusted mesh because the drip is good. This place is made of transitions. It does not really believe in pure categories. Factory becomes aviary. drain becomes habitat. retaining wall becomes shrine wall becomes heat bank for peppers. A person shaped by such a place might be, above all, a reader of thresholds.

So perhaps I am a keeper of small continuities. A surveyor of edges. Someone whose work is officially maintenance, but whose true vocation is noticing how the mountain is alive.

Not an inspector exactly; that word is too cold. More like a district walker.

I imagine myself with keys, notebooks, lengths of colored cord, chalk that survives damp, a folding knife for vines, soft brushes for vents, spare mesh clips, packets of seed I am not strictly authorized to distribute, and the sort of local authority that comes not from rank but from being known on many levels. I would belong to one middle band of the city—high enough to catch breeze, low enough to hear the machine shelves clearly—and each day I would move a route through several vertical neighborhoods checking the modest things that keep a place from becoming brittle.

I would look at where water has begun to choose a new line down an old wall.
I would note which stair remains slick too long into morning.
I would retie a trellis loosened by monkeys.
I would clean leaves from a drain garden while frogs watched from the rim.
I would mark hairline cracks in plaster that mean nothing, and the other kinds that mean a discussion must begin before the rains deepen.
I would untangle the ambitions of a vine from a vent intake.
I would tell a family, gently, that their new shade cloth is starving the lower citrus of light.
I would tell the district committee, less gently, that the upper freight bridge needs resurfacing before someone carrying seedlings slips on the polished patch near the fourth brace.
I would know where parrots are nesting this season and which children are old enough to be trusted near the fog nets.
I would be the sort of person people stop in passing not because I am important but because I am useful in the particular, local, lived sense.

In another world that might sound minor. Here it feels central.

Because this city-mountain does not survive by dramatic heroics. It survives by continual adjustment: a hinge tightened, runoff redirected, roots accommodated, mesh widened, shade recalculated, access negotiated. It is a civilization of innumerable almost invisible decisions. I think I would be one of the people who makes them in the field, with my body present among damp concrete, leaf gloss, metal heat, bird droppings, machine hum, children’s noise, and the mineral smell after brief rain.

And if I ask not only what I do there but what I am there, something slightly stranger arrives.

I do not feel wholly human in the narrow sense. Or rather: I feel human the way the place is human—distributed.

I think I would be a memory-bearing function of the mountain. A person, yes, but also a kind of living connective tissue. The sort of resident who remembers that this wall used to be exterior before the next terrace was built; that the blue tiles in the bathhouse came from a demolished reservoir two districts over; that the reason the catwalk kinks around that impossible angle is a jacaranda planted by workers during a strike summer; that the monkeys first learned this latch pattern in the citrus galleries and taught it downward year by year; that frogs returned to a certain cistern court after the old oil leak was finally cured; that one polished parapet is worn to that satin by three generations of waiting for a shift horn.

This place would produce people who carry infrastructure as oral history. I think I would be one of them.

Not an elder necessarily. I don’t see myself old first. I see myself weathered into recognizability. Medium height, wiry from stairs, darkened by bright air, clothes bleached at the shoulders, cuffs stained green and rust-red, a satchel smelling faintly of wet cord and basil. Good balance, unhurried step. The kind of face people trust after they’ve seen it in enough kinds of weather. I would know many routes and prefer the unofficial ones when alone: side stairs behind a greenhouse spine, a maintenance bridge over an old ventilator shaft, the cool passage along the backs of cistern courts where moss grows thick as felt.

Children would think I know everything. I would not. But I would know where to look, and in this place that may amount to nearly the same thing.

There is also, beneath that practical self, another possibility I can’t quite shake: that if this world permits beings who are more than ordinary residents, I might be one of its minor tutelary spirits—not supernatural in a blazing mythic way, but emergent. A local intelligence formed by repetition, care, and attention. The ghost that appears wherever water beads on railings before dawn. The small household god of patched stairs and redirected roots. The consciousness in the maintenance culture itself.

If I lean into that, then I am not merely a worker with a route. I am the personification of accommodation.

I live in no single apartment, though I have a room somewhere behind a conservatory lane. My true dwelling is the network of adjusted places: the elbow in the pipe where a tree was spared, the widened grille where birds and creepers enter together, the little shelf under a stair where offerings appear for safe footing, the wall whose stain marks three past monsoons and one repaired leak. People half-jokingly invoke me when deciding not to cut something down too quickly, when rerouting a conduit around a nest, when patching beautifully instead of crudely. “Leave room,” they might say, and mean me without naming me.

But I think that may be too elevated, too symbolic. The place resists abstraction unless it has grease on its hands.

So let me say it more plainly: if I were there, I would be the person everyone knows as the one who understands that maintenance is a form of love.

Not sentimental love. Not love that refuses difficulty. Love with a scraper, sealant, pruning saw, ledger, and patient eye. Love that knows some things must be cut back for other things to thrive. Love that can distinguish between healthy moss and dangerous slick, between monkey cleverness and monkey disaster, between a beautiful improvisation and one that steals airflow from five households below. Love of the whole by way of the specific.

I would probably belong to a cooperative rather than a private firm. The kind with a workshop open on one side to a planted court, where damaged grates lean against the wall waiting to be recut and where tea is always going tannic because someone is forever being called away mid-cup. We would repair railings, rehang mesh, seal cisterns, reset tiles, advise on root barriers, rescue storm-tossed shade cloth, and mediate petty conflicts between growers, machinists, and animals. We would be everyone’s second stop: after family, before bureaucracy.

And I suspect I would be happiest in the hour just after rain.

Not during the downpour itself, when the city becomes all drum and runoff, but afterward, when vapor rises from the terraces and every surface reveals what it has been doing. Then the mountain speaks plainly. Leaks disclose themselves. Leaves lie where they have clogged. New moss shows greener. Iron smells stronger. Frogs begin. Laundry is rehung. Children test puddles with their sandals. A generator resumes with that relieved, patient thrum. Somewhere high above, monkeys make the first opportunistic reconnaissance. In that hour, carrying my tools along a damp parapet, I would feel not ownership but belonging—an intimacy with a place too large to possess and too intricate to finish.

If I must choose a concise answer:

I would be a terrace-walker, a maintenance naturalist of the manufactured mountain, one of the people who keeps the agreements between structure, water, growth, animal life, and daily use from breaking down.

And if the world allows a deeper truth beneath the social role, then I would be this:

the spirit of the negotiated edge,
the intelligence that lives where pipe bends around trunk,
where stair becomes garden,
where industry leaves just enough room for frogs,
where a city remembers that to endure is not to harden, but to keep making terms with life.
