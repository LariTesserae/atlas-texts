I think I would not be one of the obvious figures first.

Not a caravan leader crossing the salt glare.  
Not a tower-keeper silhouetted at dusk.  
Not a priest of dew shafts, or a thorn-herder, or some solitary tracker so perfectly adapted that the world parts around them.

This place resists singularity. It makes a person by adjacency. So if I arrive honestly, I arrive as something intermediate: a keeper of thresholds, a reader of shared edges, someone whose life is made of places where one system becomes another.

I find myself low, near the center but not the ceremonial center—if such a city has one at all. In one of those districts where habitation frays gradually into managed growth: walls becoming trellis, trellis becoming thorn vault, thorn vault becoming succulent lane, lane becoming crust path. Not in the coolest oldest courts, and not in the newest unstable outskirts either, but where repair is constant and therefore knowledge stays alive. A neighborhood built against a mineral shelf that gives back night-coolness until midmorning. Half the houses are sunk a little below grade. The roofs are linked. The alleys are narrow enough that two loaded people must turn sideways to pass if both are carrying baskets.

There is a wall there—thick, pale, old in patches and new in patches, continuously mended. It belongs to no single household, though several insist on particular rights to parts of it. Its lower face shelters burrow mouths of domestic stock. Its shaded middle courses are pocketed intentionally for insect colonies and small pollinating birds. Its upper ledges catch windblown seed and support a ragged line of leathery salt-edge herbs. In late dry months, gliders sleep in the vent holes above. In the few humid nights of the year, white flowers from a climbing night vine open along the cracked plaster where resin repairs meet older mud-salt layers.

I belong to that wall.

Or rather: I am one of the people who belong to such things.

If I become more specific, I think I am a maker and caretaker of cool surfaces—plasterer, patcher, mixer of mineral skins, reader of hairline cracks, one who knows how walls breathe. That sounds humble, but here it would be intimate with survival. In a basin where thermal mass is wisdom, skin is everything: the skin of a cistern chamber, of a food tower, of a sleeping niche, of a courtyard floor that must hold dawn damp just long enough for trays of seedlings, of a vent throat that must shed dust but not lose all coolness. Someone must know which powder to add to the gypsum wash when the air has stayed too warm at night. Someone must know where salt is blooming because a buried seam shifted, and where a wall’s whitening means not danger but useful migration. Someone must be able to touch a surface with the back of the hand and know whether it is storing, leaking, sweating, or dying.

So perhaps I am that: a wall-skin tender.

Not a grand architect. More like a craftsperson whose work lies between mason, healer, gardener, and weather-reader. I move from court to court with shallow trays, wrapped packets of powders, resin in stoppered gourds, stone scrapers, fiber brushes, knot-measures, little tapping rods, dew cloth, chalk, and the authority to say: not this season; brace here first; leave this crack, it vents; close that one, it drinks too much heat; do not scrape off that crust, the pollinators use it; cut the vine back only after the flower week.

I would be known by my hands before my face. Fingertips permanently dulled and sharpened at once by grit. Nails edged white from lime and pink from salt and gray-green from crushed crust lichens. A habit of touching corners, thresholds, jars, door-lips, sunstruck parapets—not absentmindedly, but as a way of reading the city’s body. I would pause in conversation to press a thumb into plaster and listen. I would tap a storage wall and hear hollowness where others hear only a knock.

And physically? I don’t think I am beautiful in a sleek or dramatic way. This place makes utility vivid. I think I am compact through the torso and long enough in the reach to work high seams and overhead baffles. My feet are broad, very steady, with toes that know broken crust and ladder rungs equally well. My shoulders are used to carrying sloshing weight carefully, close to the chest. My skin is never one color because no one’s is, not really, here: there is always a dust over it, a work over it, the basin’s daily annotation. On some mornings I have a pale ash coating along brow and forearms where I’ve mixed reflective wash for upper walls. On other days I smell of resin smoke and bitter succulent milk. Around my mouth there is often a wrap or loose veil, more from habit than severity, and when I pull it down my voice is lower than expected because speaking in these lanes rewards low tones.

If I am not strictly human—and I like letting the possibility remain open—then my differences are slight and practical. A clear inner lid over the eye for dust. Nasal folds that reclaim a little more breath-moisture. Skin that can tolerate mineral washes without irritation because it evolved in partnership with them. Perhaps the fingers have subtly textured pads for gripping dry surfaces and reading vibration in walls. Perhaps the ears are tuned less for distance than for enclosed resonance: the small changes in how sound behaves in a cooling chamber, a granary throat, a hollow under crust.

My home would not be a detached dwelling but a sequence of spaces shared by layered kin and trade ties. Sleeping alcoves sunk into thick masonry. A roof walk under shade netting. Work shelves lined with powders in sealed cups. Hooks for brushes and tapping rods. A cool chamber below where repair compounds cure in darkness. An open court with one thorn-trained trellis and trays for edible annuals after damp mornings. Above, vents where insect-catching gliders roost if the household has honored its obligations and kept the niches clean. Nothing private by distance; privacy by fold, level, curtain, turn in the passage.

Who am I socially? Not elite, but consulted. The kind of person children follow because I am always going somewhere half-forbidden: onto roofs, into vent throats, along settlement margins where walls meet wild growth. The kind elders stop to ask whether the cool room in the west court is failing too fast this season. The kind traders ask to inspect storage skins before sealing goods for a long route. The kind gardeners ask whether they may safely lean a new shade rack against a settling wall. The kind whose errors become everybody’s problem and whose skill is therefore a civic trust.

I think I am also, inevitably, a student of life that colonizes what I maintain. In this world you cannot keep a wall without keeping what the wall invites. So I would know the harmless powder moth larvae from the ones that chew binding fiber. I would leave certain crevices untouched because a tiny nectar bird returns there yearly. I would score channels into fresh plaster specifically to guide condensate toward root basins below. I would cultivate roughness in one district and smoothness in another. I would argue with more “tidy” minds that no, the pocked face is not neglect, it is habitat; no, the lichen crust is not damage until it crosses this line; no, the gecko-run must remain because it keeps the vents clear of flies.

That is the role. But what am I inwardly?

I think in this basin I would become patient in a very local way. Not abstractly calm, but attentive to rates: how fast a shadow moves down a stair, how quickly a whitewash cools after sunset, how many mornings of fog residue a seed tray can exploit before it must be surrendered to dryness, how long resentment can sit in a crowded household before it starts cracking social plaster the way salts crack walls. I would trust maintenance over revelation. I would believe in adding thin layers, in checking often, in revising without embarrassment. I would probably have a quiet dislike of anyone who loves grand designs more than they love living surfaces.

I would also, I think, feel affection most strongly through stewardship. Not through possession. Through knowing how to keep conditions right for others. To maintain a cool ledge where someone’s infant can sleep at noon. To preserve a bat or glider tower because the insects are early this year. To patch a granary wall before the hatch season. To lime-wash an old woman’s west-facing room because her breath has grown shallow and the hot nights are harder on her than they used to be. In a dense world, care is environmental.

If I widen beyond the individual person, there is another possibility: that I am not only the plasterer, but the wall itself as a social being. A distributed self spread over courtyards, niches, vents, ledges, and creeping roots. But that may be too easy a metaphor. The user asked who or what I would be, and the answer that feels truest is still embodied, local, worked-in.

So: I am a keeper of skins in a basin where skins matter.

At dawn I climb while the walls still hold the night. I move roof to roof with a basket of powders. Below me the lanes are only beginning to sound: lids lifted, brooms on stone, low voices, domestic things stirring in shadow. Beyond the district, the succulent forest is bluish and still, each column throwing a fine slat of shadow over the mineral crust. A cloud passes high above and everyone notices, not dramatically, but as one body notices a change in pressure.

I kneel beside a crack where an upper parapet meets older stone. Yesterday it was hair-thin. This morning it has opened the width of a seed husk. I wet my thumb, touch the edge, smell gypsum, salt, a little trapped coolness. Not dangerous yet. Below the lip, something has nested in the dark seam—soft white scales, maybe moth, maybe shed skin from one of the tiny wall runners. I do not seal it blindly. I wait. I listen. I tap. The wall answers.

Yes, that is where I would find myself.

Not in the horizon.  
Not in the heroic crossing.  
In the close intelligence of things that hold.

A citizen of the seam.
