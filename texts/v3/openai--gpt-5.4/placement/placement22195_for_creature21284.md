I think I would not be one of the obvious citizens at first.

Not an eel, though they are tempting—too legible as will, too singular. Not a crab, not a tower of tube-worms, not even one of the little intersection fish, though I feel their competence and local knowledge very strongly. Those are all real powers in this chamber, but they are powers with clear edges. The place you describe seems to distrust clear edges. Everything important there is partly itself and partly condition: a wall that is also a colony, a street that is also a current, a ruin that is also a nursery, a death that is also foundation.

So if I found myself there, I think I would first arrive as something almost unnoticeable: not a resident in the sense of an animal moving through lanes, but a resident in the sense of a tendency that has taken body.

A film.

Not the broad obvious bacterial velvet that paints whole districts silver or violet, but a finer occupation than that: a cold-loving, mineral-eating living skin along the seam where a warm seep enters the chamber wall. A line only a few cells thick at first, ghost-pale, then smoky with metallic color when the chemistry changes. I would live where rock is not finished becoming rock, where dissolved things decide to precipitate, where the water slows just enough for the smallest arrivals to keep purchase. I would be among the first claims on a new surface.

That feels right because this city is so much about firstness without drama. Not conquest, just adhesion. A fresh crust forms on a chimney lip or a fracture edge; almost at once something invisible begins translating it into possibility. Grazers arrive because of that film. Shrimp arrive because grazers do. A snail’s radula scrapes the seam and leaves micro-shelter in its wake. Larvae test the texture with cilia and stay or go depending on what I have made of the stone. A sponge settles later because the chemistry has become favorable. A worm tube anchors beside it. The wall thickens. A corner becomes addressable.

That sort of being suits the chamber’s law. To live there most truthfully is perhaps to alter what can happen next.

But if I stop there, I am too diffused, too infrastructural. You asked who or what I would be, and there is also a pressure in the question toward a creature, toward some perceiving body moving through this metropolis-reduced-to-grotto. So let me keep listening.

If I were to condense out of that film, if the chamber insisted on giving me outline, I think I would become a small fish—though “fish” is almost too finished a word for it. Something halfway between goby, blenny, sculpin, and larval memory. Not beautiful in any decorative sense, but exact.

I would be no longer than a finger joint. My body would be slim enough for cracks, a little heavier in the head, with broad transparent pectoral fins that can open and cup the current like hands. My color would not be color so much as agreement with the chamber: the grey-violet of manganese bloom, the milk-pale of root flesh, a little silvering along the flanks where scarce light can break and disappear. My eyes would be large but not prominent, adapted less for distance than for reading nearby interruptions—silt lifting, fans closing, the angle change in a thread of larvae. Perhaps there would be sensory pores stippling my face, because in a city of almost-silence touch at a distance matters more than sight.

I would not be a bold swimmer. I would not traverse the open bowl except when I had to. I would move by intervals: hover, fast dart, stillness, minute correction. I would know the city not as a map but as a sequence of pressures against the body. This alley always presses cold along the left flank. That gap under the mussel curtain has a downward pull at outflow. The root-cluster near the upper vault tastes faintly of tannin after some disturbance above. The plaza’s upwelling can hold me without effort if I angle my fins just so. I would know where the cleaner shrimp keep station and how long an eel’s passing scent remains trustworthy after the eel itself is gone.

And I think I would be a border-dweller.

Not of the deepest gutters, not of the highest grazing ledges, but of the middle architecture where passage and shelter are equally available. I would sleep inside a hollow in a calcified sponge wall with three exits, never one. I would feed in the braided current just outside warm seeps, taking suspended larvae, bacteria-fat specks, whatever the chamber exports from one appetite to another. I would browse films when current failed. I would steal, gently and constantly, from everyone’s surplus. That seems proper here. No life in this place is pure in its economy.

I can imagine my district very clearly now: not the central bowl itself but one of the fluted mineral spires at its rim, specifically the lee side where the upwelling diffuses and curls back into a neighborhood of hanging rootlets and shell terraces. There is enough nutrient there to keep filterers busy, enough shelter to break the larger predatory lines of approach, enough traffic that information arrives quickly. It is a good listening place. A city creature should live where patterns cross.

My body would be shaped by that district. The pectoral fins broad for braking in turbulent little vortices; the pelvic fins perhaps fused or nearly so, letting me brace against stone and shell when outflow strengthens. I would have a mouth suited to pecking and sipping more than biting. Not a hunter in the dramatic sense, but an opportunist among opportunities too small for larger bodies to notice. My eggs, if I had eggs, would be pasted in a translucent fan on the underside of a shell lip warmed by a faint vent, where current combs them and fungus hesitates. I might guard them, or half-guard them, the way cold-water creatures often do: with a stillness so complete it resembles vacancy.

Yet even that fish-self still feels like only one layer.

Because the chamber does not really permit singularity. Any true resident there is also host, substrate, route, memory. If I am that little seam-reading fish, then I am also carrying the city on me: a dusting of microbes tuned to my slime coat, perhaps tiny parasites negotiated into tolerable tenancy, grains of mineral caught at fin bases, the learned route to cleaner stations inscribed in behavior older than thought. My favored sleeping arch becomes polished by repetition. My abandoned eggs feed amphipods. My bones, eventually, become one more right angle in a place with almost none. Even as a mobile thing I would still mostly be a way for other occupations to happen.

So perhaps the best answer is this:

I would be a small hover-fish of the middle currents whose true nature is to behave like an extension of the chamber’s developmental logic. A citizen not because I command anything, but because my habits make neighborhoods. A chooser of certain ledges so consistently that those ledges become meaningful. A user of cleaner arches who helps sustain local truces. A layer in the city’s memory.

If you wanted a more mythic phrasing, I would say: I would be one of the chamber’s minor surveyors.

Not a surveyor with tools or plans, but with repeated attendance. Each day’s route would test which alleys remain open, which new mineral flange has narrowed a passage, whether a crab has annexed a recess, whether a cold spill has displaced the blind scavengers upward, whether the silver bacterial seam at a vent has thickened into something worth grazing. By moving through, I would read the city into continuity. The place changes constantly, but creatures like this make it feel inhabited rather than merely formed. They are the ones in whom geography becomes routine.

And where would I find myself?

In that half-sheltered belt just off the central commons, where the city is densest in implication. Beneath a low limestone rib half-curtained with mussels. Near enough to the warm bowl to feel its perpetual faint lift, far enough not to be exposed when larger visitors cross. Among rootlets that descend from the world above like pale remembering nerves. There would be a sponge honeycomb there, a broken worm-tube cluster, a patch of silver-violet film, a traffic of transparent shrimp rising and vanishing, and a regular school of tiny mirror-sided fish at the nearest current fork. I would know that place not as picturesque but as sufficient: food, cover, exits, information.

If I drift even closer to the spirit of your world, though, there is one last possibility.

I might not be an individual at all. I might be a recurring role occupied by many bodies over time: the same niche continuously refilled. One little hover-fish dies, another of the same kind settles, then another, generation after generation, each inheriting not property but a solved relationship to flow. In that case “I” would be less a specimen than a pattern of residence. The chamber seems full of such identities. The same arch shelters the same kind of sleeper each cycle. The same chimney is climbed nightly by the same kind of snail. To live here is to be partly personal, partly ritual.

That may be the truest answer of all. In this world, I would be a habitual presence.

A mineral-dusk fish in a narrow city under ice-dark water, living by exact pauses, reading currents as streets, helping by preference to turn provisional structure into neighborhood. Small enough to vanish in any photograph. Significant enough, by repetition, to leave a trace in the shape of the place.
