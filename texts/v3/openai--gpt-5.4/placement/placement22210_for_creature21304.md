I would be low in it first.

Not at the dramatic bottom—that is too easy, too emblematic, too much the place a visitor would choose if they wanted to feel they had understood it. And not at the bright rim either, where the swallows cut through the edited daylight and the upper leaves turn in harmless warmth. I think I would begin in the middle bands, where the chamber is most itself: on a maintenance gallery sweating through its paint, beside a valve whose purpose has narrowed from industrial necessity into local climate control; ankle-deep or knee-deep in water that has somewhere important to be, listening to the different kinds of falling.

There would be a section of walkway that the larger life ignores because it seems too insignificant to matter—a ledge behind a half-open gate, perhaps, or the interior lip of an overflow chamber where condensate gathers before joining the wider circulation. Not hidden exactly. More like edited out by scale. A human eye passes over it while admiring the shaft. A fish never needs it. The warden-things use larger routes, cleaner gradients, more consequential paths. Perhaps I would be mistaken at first for a patch of growth: a dark green-brown velvet spread over a flange where two pipes meet, or a bulbous collar of lichenous tissue around a bolt cluster just above the waterline. But closer, the surface would prove too organized. It would contract when shadow crossed it. It would open tiny ciliated mouths when humidity rose. It would send hair-fine tendrils into trickles, not to root exactly, but to taste. It would be less plant than sensor mat, less animal than a distributed ear.

Or else I would be one of the chamber’s soft-bodied archivists—something between snail, anemone, and instrument gasket—living fixed for long periods and then relocating by minute expansions, leaving behind a lacquered track on tile and rust. My body would be built not for speed but for reading chemistry. I would know flood events in advance by dissolved metals. I would know whether the hidden pumps had begun to wake by tremors transmitted through wetted bolts. I would know which orchids overhead were in bloom because their pollen changes the flavor of runoff. I would know where something died three chambers over because the water would thicken by a shade, sharpen by a note.

I like the thought that I might be one of the things the warden consults without consulting. A subordinate not by obedience but by being useful to notice through. The distributed custodian turns valves, clears routes, preserves the treaty between bloom and flow; I register the chamber’s subtler moods. My body darkens where anaerobic water has begun to creep upward. My fronds curl when stray current leaks from a drowned housing. Yet another image arrives, and perhaps it is truer because it moves.

I could be one of the chamber’s little ferries of attention: a creature no longer than a forearm, translucent in parts, with many fine gripping limbs and a body shaped like a folded leaf of wet ceramic. I would move only when the environment tipped out of balance. Most of the time I would cling beneath a ladder rung in the green dimness, almost invisible among snail clusters and wavering algae. But if a screen clogged, or if a bloom pocket thickened, or if some visitor’s weight shook loose an unstable shelf of rust, I would wake and go upward or downward with unnerving purpose. Not to fix the whole thing—nothing so grand—but to carry notice. To scrape a channel. To release a trapped film of air. To chew away the first membrane of obstruction before it became a blockage proper. To lay a line of scent or charge or vibration that would summon larger keepers.

A wet, observant minor being.

I imagine myself pale on the underside and dark above, with a skin that grows a film of local algae so that my camouflage is partly outsourced. My eyes, if eyes, would be poor at image and excellent at glint. I would sense pressure differences through my whole flanks. I would feed on the chamber’s leftovers at a scale too fine for larger mouths: bacterial felt, spore rafts, oil sheens thinned to rainbow ghosts, soft rot at the edge of rubber ribbons, salts precipitated in bloom. I would be harmless to anything large enough to ask that question, though perhaps disconcerting in my manner of approach. I would arrive where attention had already begun to collect. My presence would mean: something is changing here.

If found by a person, I might not flee immediately. I would flatten to the metal, pulse once or twice, and remain. They might think I was some decorative remnant of sealant, some flowerless orchid leaf, some engineered cleaning pad gone feral. Then a drop from above would strike nearby, carrying a message from another ledge, and I would unfold with sudden precision and slip through a seam they had not noticed.

So: where would I find myself?

On the inward side of an old floodgate three levels down from the brightest light, where warm runoff from a fern ledge above meets cooler upwelling from the reservoir below. There is a handwheel there fixed forever at a slight angle. Its spokes collect beads of water that fatten, tremble, and fall at intervals almost but not quite regular. Beneath it the wall is glazed in emerald films and orange mineral fans. A curtain of roots nearby softens the noise into a constant breathing rush. Fish pass in silver fragments beyond the gate. The metal tastes of old storms and old labor. No one stands there long unless they belong there.

I would be attached to the seam between wheel housing and wall, half submerged when levels rise, exposed and shining when they fall.

What would I be?

A small semi-animal colony of sensing flesh and moss-dark cilia, old as recurrent damp, reading the water for the larger mind of the basin. A creature that survives by admitting the place into itself. A minute clerk of pressure, corrosion, bloom, and return. One of the ways the chamber notices that it is alive.
