I think I would not be one of the shrubs.

They are too declarative. Too much silhouette. Too much solitary endurance visible at a distance.

Nor would I be one of the first films, though I feel close to them—the green-black skins that wake on dew and vanish into dormancy by noon. They are intimate with the place in a beautiful way, but they belong to surfaces so completely that they cannot choose among them. They answer where they are given. I feel more like something that reads the seams and waits.

So: I would be low, nearly hidden, and distributed through a small geography rather than occupying a single point. Not a creature crossing the plain, not a watcher above it, but a resident of one recurrent kind of accident in the made world.

I would live under the lip of a warped slab where the edge has lifted just enough to make a narrow dusk even at noon.

Not in the grand basins or the visible green lines on the east-facing scarps, but in one modest place repeated a million times across that artificial continent: the underside of a plate, the cool margin where condensation gathers, where dust has lodged, where runoff once found the seam and left behind a darker mineral trace, where a little dead stem caught and stayed, where the heat is still terrible but fractionally less absolute.

I think I would be a crust-forming colony: part lichen, part moss, part algal film, with fungal threads sunk into the first thin organics under the dust. Not a single organism in the neat sense, but a compact republic—one of those early collaborations that can only exist where world-making has barely begun. A living felt laid into the hairline gap between manufacture and weather.

At first glance I would look like discoloration.

A gray-green darkening along the adhesive failure.
A soot-soft patch under stone.
A stain with texture only when seen very close.

But my real body would be in layers. On damp mornings, the outer skin would wake first—cells swelling in minutes, color deepening, surfaces briefly tender with moisture. Beneath that, filaments would hold the dust in place, netting fines that otherwise would slide away at the smallest disturbance. Tiny rhizoids or rootlike threads would enter the crack where thermal stress has widened it by fractions over years. Not deep, not heroic. Just enough to keep the seam from being only a seam. Just enough to begin changing it into habitat.

I would live by timing, not by possession.

Before dawn I would take water from droplets gathering on the slab above me. Not many; just enough. A patient tapping, each drop finding the same places. Some of it would fall directly onto me. Some would wick along the mineral grain and arrive slowly. For an hour, perhaps less, I would be fully alive in the active sense—metabolizing, repairing, adding a membrane's width to my surface, feeding sugars downward to the fungal weave and the almost-invisible threads that hold the dust.

Then the light would strengthen. The plain would begin releasing heat. The open country would turn brilliant and impossible again. I would retreat without moving, becoming duller, tighter, less legible. To anything passing at noon, I would seem inert. But that would be one of my main skills: looking dead at the correct times.

Around me would be a little assembly of others. A few straw-colored stems from last season or ten seasons ago, lodged against the slab edge and half-incorporated into dust. Perhaps two beadlike succulents rooted farther back where the shadow lasts longer, their tissues tasting of metal and bitterness. A chalk-pale lichen scaling the cooler stone nearby. Hidden fungal strands threading all the dead matter no wind has managed to take. Together we would make a room smaller than a body and more important than it looks: a pocket where the built world has begun, almost shyly, to remember how to hold life.

If I were to choose a self more precisely, I would be the dark margin at the drip line.

Not the center of the hollow, where water briefly stands, nor the outer bright crust, where mineral residue dries clean and hard—but the narrow intermediate band where wetness reliably reaches and just as reliably leaves. That zone seems truest to me. It is not abundance, not deprivation, but recurrence. A place shaped by intervals. A place that survives by knowing exactly how long a blessing lasts.

In that band I would do very small work.

I would trap one more layer of dust.
I would keep one more minute of damp.
I would soften one grain, then another.
I would darken the seam so dawn heat enters it differently.
I would make a surface rough enough for the next spore to catch.
I would take the dead reed filament that slid under the slab after rain and turn part of it into the first actual crumb of soil.

Nothing dramatic. Nothing anyone would name from far away.

But in a place like that, the humble makers are the real inhabitants. The giant constructed plain belongs, in the long run, not to its vanished builders but to whatever can begin converting intention into weather, weather into texture, texture into hold. I would be one of those converters.

If you found me at all, it would be because you had learned to stop looking at the scale of horizons and start looking at the scale of permissions. You would crouch beside an uplifted plate in the hour just after dawn. The air would still be cool enough to forgive touch. Beneath the slab edge you would see a narrow band of darkened life, almost black where it is wettest, paling to sage and ash where it dries. If you looked closer still, you would see minute capsules, tiny translucent swellings, powder-fine crust, threads through dust, the gloss of water not yet gone. You might smell a faint mineral greenness, sharper than the plain around it.

That would be me.

Not the landscape's monument, but one of its first sentences.
Not its future forest, but its first durable hesitation against sterility.
A seam-dweller.
A condenser-keeper.
A maker of minute shade and slower drying.
A citizen of almost-land.

And where would you find yourself in that world?

Under an angled slab before sunrise, I think—kneeling without hurry, learning that the place is not empty at all, only working at a scale that asks for devotion before it reveals itself.
