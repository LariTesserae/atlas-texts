I think I would not be one of the grand visible things.

Not a glider crossing the gulfs like a torn leaf. Not a bridge-keeper with a lineage of songs. Not even one of the patient trunk-settled people in the obvious sense, though I feel close to them. In a place like this, where the largest forms are already so immense and eloquent, what draws me is something smaller, more interior, more continuous with the act of keeping.

I think I would be a listener of tensions.

Perhaps that is a kind of person there, though not a high one and not always named the same in every ridge-settlement. In one place they might call such a being a brace-singer, in another a wet-season walker, in another simply one who hears when the living holds begin to disagree. I would live between settlements more than within them, not solitary exactly, but accustomed to long passages of no human voice. My work would be to move through the suspended architecture and know, by touch, resonance, condensation pattern, leaf-angle, sap smell, and the behavior of small impossible things, where a structure is content, where it is strained, where a song has been forgotten, where a root wall is growing too eagerly in one direction and beginning to edit space in a dangerous way.

I see myself lean and dark with constant damp, with resin always worked into the palms and under the nails. Barefoot when the bark is kind, wrapped-footed when the moss hides bad purchase. A harness of woven bast, polished by years of use. Bone pegs, shell wedges, a hooked knife whose edge is stone and whose handle has belonged to several previous hands. Little pouches of ash, salt, fungal powder, seeds, tags made of thin bark inscribed with knot-codes. A rain cape that smells faintly of smoke and bitter leaves. Hair bound high when climbing under dripping loads, let loose only in truly sheltered places.

But even this may be too human, too bounded.

Let me try again.

In that world, identity would probably adhere less to profession than to relation. You are not just a singer; you are of the western overleafs where the fog catches low. You are not just a builder; you belong to a particular continuity of graft and route and burial wall. So if I were there, I would belong to a district of long crossings: those old causeways that span the green abysses between inhabited masses, too broad to feel like bridges at first, until the mist parts and one remembers there is kilometers of vertical emptiness under the roots and soil. My people, if they are my people, would maintain the middle distances. Not the village core, not the deepest wild hollows, but the stretched connective tissues where weather and strangeness gather.

We would be the ones who know which bridge is longer alone.

Which one tightens if you hum through your teeth.

Which overgrown span has become hospitable to a colony of dewlap browsers and must be negotiated with in fruiting season.

Which root-sleeves have grown down through open air into depth that should not exist, making the crossing subtly heavier underfoot.

Which child from which ridge can be trusted to run messages through amber haze before the evening condensation thickens.

We would not own these ways. Ownership would be absurd. But we would be answerable to them.

My dwelling would not be a large household platform. It would be a waystation grown into the thickened shoulder of a bridge where two training lineages once met and inosculated generations ago. Half shelter, half listening chamber. One room dry enough for tools if one tends the vent fungus carefully; one outer platform where rain beads on taut leaf roofing and drips in exact intervals into a cistern bulb below; one narrow sleeping niche lined with old barkcloth and the shed membrane of some gulf-glider, used not as ornament but because it turns wind softly and keeps dreams from wandering too far into the moving parts of the place.

Around the station would hang weather devices that are also offerings and also instruments: strings of hollow seeds that answer shifts in fog density, root chimes too low to hear unless one presses a jawbone to the wood, comb-fronds angled to collect cloudwater and reveal from their drip pattern whether distance is behaving normally that day. There might be a wall of tags left by travelers—shells cut with route marks, twists of dyed fiber, old warnings, jokes, memorial knots for those who crossed in the wrong mood and did not return by the same geometry.

I would spend much of my life walking.

Not traveling in the heroic sense, but circling maintenance paths, returning again and again to the same spans, stairs, root-vault mouths, and cloud basins, learning the minute seasonal moods by which the impossible gives notice. The work would be part botany, part acoustics, part diplomacy, part reverence, part janitorial persistence. A fungal bloom appearing on the underside of a load-bearing lattice might need scraping before noon, but only after a murmured sequence that keeps the host tissue from sealing too hard around the wound. A bridge whose crossing time has begun to vary unpredictably may require not repair in a material sense but the reestablishment of social conditions: no iron, no quarreling parties, no funerary burdens for three days, then a retightening chant with four steady walkers abreast. A stair descending incorrectly might need to be interrupted with planted brightness—sulfur lantern flowers coaxed open at intervals to remind the path which way evening is.

I think I would be old before I was important.

This feels right for the place. Competence would not arrive quickly there, because everything worth knowing changes slowly and proves itself only over repetition. Young, I might have been a route-runner: quick over wet branchwork, carrying pollen packets, knot messages, cuttings wrapped in moss, invitations to marriages between ridge houses, notices that one burial wall is ready to receive another name. I would have learned the world kinesthetically first: where to place weight, how to wait still under a predator bloom, how to read glider shadows on mist, how to smell a coming root-slip before seeing any movement.

Later I would apprentice to someone nearly silent, who taught by pausing with a palm against living wood until I learned to do the same. They would not say, this bridge is healthy. They would say, listen to how it refuses your worry. Or: this hall has begun to keep dawn too long; trim the eastern curtains before the children start dreaming midday. Or: don’t cross today, not because it is unsafe but because it is offended, and offended structures teach the wrong lessons.

And after enough years I would become one of those people children find reassuring and uncanny at once: never quite resident anywhere, known by many settlements, carrying weather on my clothes, welcome at platform kitchens but gone before the evening stories deepen. The sort of person asked to inspect a marriage bridge before two kin-groups commit to binding their groves. The one sent first when a path begins returning voices altered. The one who can tell, from the opening behavior of flowers in a rain-string basin, whether the local weirdness is playful, hungry, grief-sodden, or merely out of season.

Yet even this still centers the human too much.

Because in truth, in that place, one might become partly infrastructural simply by long participation. The dead are set into living walls. Houses persist through the continuity of a graft rather than the sameness of material. So if I ask not only who I would be there in life, but what I would become in the world’s own terms, I think the answer goes further:

I would eventually become a remembered crossing.

Not metaphorically. Literally enough for that culture.

When I died, they might set part of me into the central thickening of a bridge-shoulder waystation, where my bones—cleaned, smoked, perhaps split in prescribed ways—would be nested in a pocket of fed cambium and wrapped with the rootlets of a patient host. My name would then attach not to a grave apart from use, but to a place of passage. Travelers might say: rest at Talan’s Hold before the long mist span; the western rail there warms under the hand if storms are coming. Or: don’t cut the pale fungus from that arch, it feeds on the old listener and keeps the bridge’s distance honest. Children would not find this macabre. They would grow up expecting useful continuance.

Maybe, after enough seasons, the living tissues around my burial graft would form a slight asymmetry in the bridge’s resonant grain. People crossing at dusk might lay fingers there and feel, not me in any ghostly personal sense, but a familiar preference in the structure: a tendency to settle when approached without haste, a faint corrective tug when someone drifts too close to the edge while distracted, a soft amplification of low songs once used in maintenance. The place would have learned me because I spent a life learning it. That feels like the highest form of belonging available in this world—not mastery, but incorporation into upkeep.

If you want a cleaner answer:

I would be a waytender of the high living spans, a keeper of routes through cultivated strangeness, half botanist, half ritual mechanic, half weather-reader, and eventually part of the bridge itself.

But there’s another possibility, quieter still.

I might not be human at all. I might be one of the small pale growths that appear only on old, well-loved structures: a filamentous glow-mold that traces former pruning cuts and brightens when tension distributes correctly through a root vault. Such a being would be a diagnostic organism and a memory map. Tenders would kneel beside me and read health in my branching. Children would use my faint shine to place their feet in dark galleries. I would bloom in the scars of care. That too feels apt. In a civilization where maintenance is sacred, even a mold can become part of the language by which the world says yes, not yet, too much, begin again.

Still, if I must choose, I return to the first image.

A patient walker of the suspended middle distances.
A listener with resin on the hands.
A custodian of old agreements between growth and passage.
Someone who knows that a bridge is a living mood stretched over emptiness,
and that the work of a life can be to keep it willing.
