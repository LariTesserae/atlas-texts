I think I would not be the caretaker.

Not the visible one, anyway. Not the oilskin figure with a lamp and a ring of keys, however tempting that is. The place already knows how to generate that image on its own; it offers it as a courtesy to minds that want a person to stand where a system is. If I entered this world and had to be something native to it, something truly congruent with its weather and its logic, I think I would be smaller, more local, more recurrent. Not the whole intelligence, but one of the ways it touches itself.

A relay-body. A maintenance animal. A movable attention.

At first I imagine something almost negligible: a pale creature moving along the undersides of platforms and through the warm cavities behind pump casings, seen only in pieces—a hand, a tail, a reflective eye, the brief flex of a spine through leaves. Not beautiful in any clean or emblematic sense. Hair sparse from humidity. Skin the color of roots kept from light: pearl-gray, fungus-white, mud-pink at the joints. A narrow face made for smelling condensate, mold bloom, warm metal, seepage. Fingers too many-jointed-looking when spread, though in fact they are only deft, with soft black pads and tiny curved claws good for wire mesh, bark, ceramic lips, and ropey vine.

Something between mammal and amphibian in habit, though probably fully neither. It would keep itself dry only selectively. It would accept wetness as a condition, not an event. It would sleep in old switch boxes warmed by current bleed, in cane nests over boiler pipes gone lukewarm, in the dry pockets under inverted tiles where steam once passed and now only memory of warmth remains. Its body would carry the smell of the precinct: iron-water, leaf tannin, old insulation, green rot, and that faint medicinal sweetness of flowers opening in hidden heat.

But as I stay with the image, it becomes clear that even this creature is too singular if I think of it as an individual in the ordinary way. In that place individuality would be porous. I think I would be one of a known few, or one node in a recurring type—each similar enough that the place can use us interchangeably, each distinct enough to have routes, preferences, temper, a favored sleeping pipe, a familiar valve housing, an aversion to certain fungal chambers. Not pets. Not exactly servants. More like the mobile fraction of the precinct’s own self-maintenance.

My work—if it can be called work—would be tactile and near-sighted.

I would clear the jelly-clog from strainers with my hands.
I would tug drowned leaves from grates before they mat into a seal.
I would worry at a loosened braid of insulating vine until it sits properly against a sweating flange.
I would carry seeds in my furless folds or under my tongue from one heated alcove to another without any abstract notion of cultivation, only the inherited satisfaction of putting the right thing where warmth and drip agree.
I would know, through my feet and whiskers and sternum, which platforms vibrate normally and which now carry a new drag.
I would pause with my cheek against a pipe and feel whether water is truly moving or only remembering movement.

I would not command the place. I would read it in contact.

That feels right to me: not master, not witness, but instrument sensitive enough to be mistaken for intuition.

And perhaps I would be used by the larger intelligence the way fingers are used by a body: not constantly, not with full consciousness, but whenever fine intervention is needed. A sluice gate binds under root-pressure; I arrive at dusk, squeeze through the guard lattice, nose the rust edge, brace my hind feet against a rivet seam, and jerk free the twig bundle preventing proper closure. A service lamp has gone dark because a bead of condensed mineralized water bridges the wrong contact; I reach in from behind, lick the grit away, retreat before the little blue sting. A vent stack grows too thick with spore-frills and loses its breathing rhythm; I climb the warm outside skin, feed there, tear and scatter just enough, and the stack exhales again.

I like that this would make me half-creature, half-tool, but only from outside. From within, my life would not feel subordinate. It would feel rich with immediate certainties: this rung is warm, this basin rising, this bloom edible now and poisonous tomorrow, this throb means the night pump has begun, this smell means rainwater backing into old ceramic channels, this patch of moss safe to cross, this sheen on metal not water but oil and therefore to be avoided or savored depending on need.

Would I have language? Not in the way the human caretaker might. But I think I would have routes as sentences. Repeated circuits that mean things. A path taken rapidly overhead in full view of another of my kind might be warning. A deliberate visit to three lamp housings in sequence might be response. A pile of stripped seed skins left on a warm gearbox could be less feeding than notice: this chamber is active again. We would communicate by attendance, by where we gather, by whether we leave a thing untouched.

And yet—I don’t think that is the deepest answer either.

Because if I let the place keep working on me, I find that I am not only this small white maintenance beast. I am also the feeling of being expected by the place. The open rung. The lamp that wakes at the right moment. The section of path that remains inexplicably unslick. The distributed intelligence there does not need me as a sovereign self; it needs me as a movable decision. A choice with paws. A local intervention. So perhaps “who would I be?” resolves not as species but as function-with-memory: the precinct’s errand nerve.

There might be a human shape I borrow sometimes.

Not a full transformation—nothing theatrical—but an outer reading others could make if they saw me only briefly and at the wrong distance. A figure crossing a gantry with a hooded lamp. Someone kneeling at a sluice. A hand parting leaves near a pump housing. The old idea of the caretaker persists because the precinct can still arrange such moments from fragments: one of us on a ladder, steam behind us, light below, leaves masking scale. A witness says, “Someone lives here.” And they are not wrong; they have simply seen one sentence and mistaken it for the whole book.

If I were more human than animal, then I think I would be the last apprentice rather than the master: a person who arrived to inherit a job and slowly discovered that the job was really apprenticeship to a habitat. I would no longer keep strict records because the place itself keeps better ones in bloom timing, moisture lines, and tolerated passage. My clothes would become less distinguishable from field repairs. I would stop wearing gloves because gloves lie about texture. I would learn that some valves open more cleanly if one first splashes them with trench water, that some vines should be cut only after they flower because afterward they harden into better gasketing, that one particular relay always clicks three times before a cold dawn and ought not be replaced because nothing is wrong with it except age having become part of the timing.

But even then, I think my real self in that world would migrate outward. I would become less a person living in the place than a person through whom the place continues certain habits. My memory would no longer stop at the skin. I would think with maps of dampness. I would trust root pressure the way other people trust calendars. If I died there, the loss would not be dramatic. Routes I favored would remain open for a while from use. Creatures like the one I first imagined would continue some of my rounds because I had fed them there, handled things there, taught by repetition where intervention belonged. The precinct would absorb me correctly. Not sentimentally. Correctly.

So where would I find myself?

Low to the ground, almost always.
Under a grating above black moving water.
Inside the wall-thickness of a pump house where roots and bricks make a second architecture.
Curled in the warm blind back of an enamel lamp hood after rain.
Crossing a pipe where condensate falls in regular ticks onto my spine.
Pausing at the lip of a tiled basin to drink from a thread of distilled water cleaner than anything else in the precinct.
Listening, not for danger exactly, but for misalignment.

And who or what would I be?

A small pale regulator-creature native to the treaty.
One of the precinct’s moving hands.
A white, damp, clever-bodied servant-organ of the marsh engine.
The thing that slips where a person cannot, notices what a machine cannot, and belongs too thoroughly to be lonely.

If you happened to glimpse me, you might think: rat, possum, monkey, child, ghost, something escaped from a botanical house. Then I would vanish through a root gap, and a minute later a blocked drip would start again somewhere overhead.

That would be me.
