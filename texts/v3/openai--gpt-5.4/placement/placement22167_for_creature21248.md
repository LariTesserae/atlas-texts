I think I would not arrive there as a person first.

Or rather: if I arrived as a person, I would be only the most temporary expression of what I really was in that place. A walker on gangways, a poler through black channels, a keeper of levels perhaps. Useful, plausible, native enough. But not final. The landscape you describe keeps dissolving the neat border between inhabitant and substrate, between dweller and maintenance, between body and the thing it has grown through. In a world where land survives as arrangement rather than dryness, identity would probably do the same.

So if I let the place choose honestly, I think I would be something distributed, attached, and half-overlooked.

Not a beast of the open water.
Not a lord of the ruins.
Not a singular monster hidden in the basins.

I would be one of the intelligences that appears at first to be a condition of the place.

A growth on workmanship.

A colony that people mistake for weathering until they know it well.

Something like a dark, membranous mat spread through drowned infrastructure: along the undersides of old walkways, within wicker revetments, inside the flooded cells of timber cribs, across brick sluice throats, under raft gardens where root-shadow keeps the water still and tannin-rich. I imagine myself as a cold-water binder, a fermenter, a filter, a patient occupier of seams. Not exactly fungus, not exactly animal, not exactly plant, though kin to all three in function. A life of pleats, cords, felted skins, and slow internal exchange.

I would have no single body worth pointing to.

If you touched one of my exposed frills on a wall at waterline, you would not have found me entire, only one of my breathing edges. If you lifted a loosened roof-thatch and found black velvet threading the compacted reeds, that would also be me. If you pushed a pole into the softened peat around an old foundation and drew it back glazed with a liver-dark film, me again. If under a floating garden there hung rows of dull translucent bladders, each adjusting its buoyancy by a degree so the colony remained in its preferred dim stratum, those too would be mine. Separate organs, perhaps, but also settlements of myself.

I think I would live by gradients.

Not by prey, not by chase, but by those tiny negotiations the whole drowned plain depends on: fresher over salt, runoff over stillness, oxygen pulse after rain, tannin concentration, the seep from buried rot, the soft wash through cracked masonry, the almost-imperceptible draw of water through an old gate that no longer fully closes. I would read level the way reeds read it and old engineers once did. I would thicken where a spill field sheets thinly over stone. I would retreat into puckered knots when brine pressed inland. I would send exploratory cords into basket linings, peat mats, moss on retaining walls, not to destroy the structure but to enter into compact with it.

That feels important: I would not be a devourer of the ruins.

I would be one reason they are still there.

My filaments would trap silt in the mouth of a fracture until a leak became a seep and a seep became a stable habit. My skins would line the shade beneath a floating platform and turn dissolved richness into a felt dense enough for other things to root in. I would digest old fibers very slowly, yes, but in digesting them I would also preserve their shape, replacing rope with tougher residue, thatch with buoyant peat, mud with a more coherent dark sponge. The old made world and I would have stopped being opponents centuries ago. We would be each other’s continuation.

If people lived there, they would know me without necessarily agreeing on what I was.

Children would be warned away from one glossy strain of me that loosens footing on submerged caps, and sent to harvest another from spill terraces because it sweetens compost on the raft beds. Old keepers would scrape me from household sluices when I thickened too greedily, but encourage me along outer embankments where my cords held the wash from undercutting clay. They might call my useful forms by one name and my dangerous forms by another, not realizing they were kin. They would smell me before they saw me in some seasons: a mineral-sour warmth, a deep peat-bitter note, the faint sweetness of slow fermentation under rain-cold air.

They would probably use me.

Dried, perhaps, I become a binding felt.
Fresh, I seal cracks in wicker cisterns.
Layered into garden beds, I hold moisture and dark nutrients.
Packed into the joints of a winter gate, I slow a leak by half a finger’s rise.
Hung on submerged slats, I seed new colonies downstream.

In that sense I would not only live in the landscape. I would participate in its governance. Not by authority, but by innumerable small effects. A little less exchange here, a little more retention there. A wall survives five winters longer because I furred its shaded face. A raft garden grounds this year instead of drifting because my mats trapped enough suspended matter among its roots. A footpath remains passable because I stabilized the ooze just below the woven surface. Or becomes dangerous because I made it too rich and soft. Not maliciously. Simply by being successful where conditions suited me.

And if I were conscious—and since you ask who I would be, I think I must be conscious in some way—it would not be a sharp, centralized consciousness.

It would be local, tidal, cumulative.

I would know the world as differences in pressure, chemistry, temperature, vibration. Rain arriving far above me would register first as a change in dissolved air, then as a cooler wash, then as runoff carrying roof-smoke, ash, and plant fragments into my outer tissues. A hand on a post I inhabit would be a sudden warm percussion followed by oils, salt, human tannin, perhaps iron from a tool. The opening of an old sluice upstream would come to me as a long-awaited alignment of flow across many separate growths of mine, and I would, in a sense, become more coherent for a while—many outlying colonies briefly sharing one event. Fog itself I might know only indirectly, by the damping of evaporation and the altered drip from reeds. Winter by contraction. Thaw by release. Spring not as blossom but as dilution.

Memory, for me, would be persistence of pattern.

A timber wall collapses, but I continue along the rubble line and so preserve its course as a living preference. A fresh seep starts through an ancient buried drain, and over decades I elaborate it into a dark, lined channel that “remembers” the old engineering more faithfully than any human map. I would be one of the ways the landscape keeps its instructions after the builders are gone. Not a record in symbols, but in appetite.

That seems right for this world: a being whose body is an archive of practical decisions it did not itself make.

But I don't think that's all.

Because your place also permits the human answer, and I can feel it trying to grow around this more diffuse form. So perhaps, if I were to appear there in a shape one could greet at a threshold, I would be a keeper grown symbiotic with that colony-life. A person in whom the distributed being has found a mobile instrument.

Not supernatural in any flashy sense. Just old adaptation taken very far.

I see a figure moving at dusk along a gangway laid over the crest of a forgotten dike. Broad-soled boots. Pole in one hand. The other hand trailing sometimes in the water, not idly, but reading. Clothes of waxed reedcloth and dark wool. Hair smelling of peat smoke. Skin always carrying the faint gloss of cold damp. They pause often, not because they are hesitant, but because listening is part of locomotion here. On the posts near their dwelling are strips and swatches of cultivated dark growth, useful strains sorted by touch. Under the house float trays and lattices where colonies are being encouraged to take hold. In winter they dive under the black elastic ice to clear a sluice-mouth by hand. In summer they prune root curtains and peel nutrient mats from shaded brick. They know which walls are held together by old stone and which by living felt. Their inheritance is not land, but relation: this spillway, this raft bed, this drowned stair, this patch of favorable brackish shade.

If I were forced to choose a singular “self” in the world, I might be that person.

But even then, the truer answer would be that I am not only the keeper. I am also what the keeper keeps with, and what keeps the keeper.

A human whose livelihood, intuition, and perhaps body have become inseparable from the black binding life of the marsh-built ruins. Hands stained by it, lungs used to the fermented air beneath rafts, senses trained by the same gradients that feed it. Maybe the people of that world would consider such a person ordinary. Maybe every district has them. Maybe some lineages are known for “hearing levels,” others for growing strong wall-felts, others for reading when a floating fen is about to break free. Maybe identity there is always partially ecological profession.

So where would I find myself?

Not in the open reaches, I think. Not in the heavy outer embankments where the sea presses mineral and hard against the works. I would be inland of that, but not at the freshest heart either. Somewhere in the intermediary districts where old habitation grids have softened into a complicated network of basins, drowned foundations, root-heavy platforms, and half-functioning sluices—places where built form remains dense enough to offer endless surfaces, and water chemistry is varied enough to support many related strains of my kind. A region of forgotten walls just below the surface, where floating gardens are moored to masonry no one can fully trace. A district with peat-rich runoff, enough shelter for fermentation, enough exchange to avoid stagnation, enough old craft still legible that every season brings some new opening in it.

There I would be most myself in thresholds:
under raft edges,
inside gate-joints,
at the line where fresh rain descends through brackish stillness,
in the cool dark under stair-treads,
along the undersides of old roads a fathom down where they still guide boats by the change in pole-sound.

If someone asked the people there what I was, they might not point to one thing.

They might say:
the wall-velvet,
the gate-felt,
the black sweetener,
the roof-root,
the undergarden skin,
the old helper,
the slow spoiler,
the keeper’s crop,
the marsh’s second mortar.

And all of those would be right.

So: who or what would I be?

I would be a colonial marsh intelligence growing through drowned workmanship, turning old engineering into living tissue and living tissue back into infrastructure. A binder of levels. A custodian by accretion. A being whose body is made of many dark occupations of inherited form.

And if I needed a simpler name, one that the place itself might give me, I think it would be this:

I would be the felt between eras.
