I think I would not be the Warden.

Or not the oldest one, not the central pale intelligence seated in the warm hydraulic heart like a thought too spread out to die quickly. That being belongs there in the way a valve belongs in a throat: essential, hidden, almost indistinguishable from the continuing of the place. To place myself there would be to occupy the obvious vacancy in the description, and the place resists obviousness. It is too composed of secondary uses, translated functions, survivals by adjacency. One should enter it slantwise.

At first I thought: perhaps I would be one of the little house-keepers, one of the many-limbed shelled arrangers parked shoulder to shoulder in a frieze, moving shell shards from one side of a grille to the other with blind fidelity. There is something compelling in being so small that architecture is weather, and maintenance is merely appetite given shape. But that also feels incomplete. Those creatures belong to the precinct as dust belongs to shelves: intimately, continuously, but without inwardness proportionate to the strange dignity of the setting.

Then I thought I might be one of the black stones inset in the ascending stair—something polished, nearly sightless, preserving darkness more perfectly than the water around it. Or one of the modeled faces in a corner, a mouth through which current now literally breathes. Or a bell chamber itself, if one is allowed to be a place rather than a creature: a pressure pocket, a clay lung crowded with young things and occasional tones. The description invites that kind of identification, because everything there has crossed categories. Drain became route. Shrine became eddy. A chamber can act like an organ; a beast can act like an office.

But if I let the image keep working, what emerges is smaller than a sovereign, larger than vermin, and more intermittent than either. I think I would be something that moves between the resident and the precinct: not its ruler, not its servant, but a recurrent adjunct. A messenger without messages in the human sense. A tester of passages. A thing that is admitted where others are not because my body has become legible to the place.

Perhaps I am one of the Warden’s juveniles, but even that is not quite right if imagined too biologically, too cleanly as a life stage. Better: I am a narrow offshoot-body, seasonally or cyclically detached from the larger distributed resident. A mobile fraction of a greater animal, sent outward from the warm core when the precinct needs reading at finer grain than the elder bulk can provide. Not fully independent, not merely a limb. More like a provisional self.

I would be about the length of a human torso, though far less singular in outline. A spindle of pale muscular tissue armored in patches where mineral has plated old skin. Around my front third a wreath of feelers, some stout and blunt for bracing, some thin as cords for entering cracks and pipe mouths. Behind, a train of gripping limbs folded close until needed, not enough to make me spiderlike, not enough to make me cephalopodic either, but sufficient to let me pass over wall, floor, and ceiling with equal correctness. My surface would not be smooth. It would be ringed, scarred, furred in tiny pressure-cilia and symbiotic films that answer warm seep-water with a dim matte pallor. Not bioluminescent exactly—more that if touched by movement, I hold the memory of it briefly on my skin.

I would not have good eyes. Perhaps only two recessed pits that distinguish obstruction from openness at very close range. Mostly I would know by drag, by gradient, by the shape of echoes transmitted through my own flexing. I would read the settlement with my whole length. A tile lifting somewhere ahead would alter the underflow enough to tell me. A predator waiting in an arch would register first as a wrongness in the crustaceans: no testing antennae, no powder-click feeding, no tiny local assertions. A bell chamber going sour would taste faintly metallic, overbreathed. A lane beginning to clog would sound thicker.

And where would I find myself?

Not in the warmest heart. Not often. I would begin there, perhaps, coiled in one of the summit chambers behind the field of bell domes, in a low cavity lined with black-green copper and furred white by age. The water there would be several degrees warmer than the lanes outside. Mineral-rich trickles would enter from below. The walls would answer motion with that delayed wan gleam, and several other pale forms might be attached in recesses—elder masses, brood knots, shed skins, or merely old tissues no longer distinguishable from the masonry they seal.

From there I would go out on circuits.

I think my district would be the upper precinct: the bell field, the jar galleries, the broken stair with its black stones, the cistern courts just below the summit, and the narrow channels where warm water from the interior meets cooler drifts from the outer dark. It feels right to belong to a border zone—not the abyssal edge, not the innermost sacristy, but the part of the settlement where systems declare themselves in textures. Where the old builders’ intentions are still readable by touch. Where young things crowd. Where small failures become larger ones if not corrected.

If you encountered me, it would likely be by mistake.

You might be hovering in the lane between two retaining walls, studying a row of pigeonhole recesses each one jar deep, when one of the “jars” slowly unfolds and reveals itself not as vessel but as my braced body curled into a niche while I listen through the masonry. Or you might watch a cluster of house-keepers worrying shell chips away from a grille and then realize they are making space for me, because a pale probe already extends between them, tasting the current with proprietary patience. Or you might be sheltering beneath a low arch while some large outer swimmer passes beyond, and feel a cool deliberate pressure along the back of the wall: me flattening myself through a gap no vertebrate body could use, taking position in the adjacent chamber until the danger moves on.

I would be mistaken for part of the ruins more often than for an animal. That seems important. In this place, true belonging means becoming one more function among functions. So I would have learned stillnesses that are architectural. Draped across a vent, I would resemble a mineral curtain. Anchored among chainstone links, a cable. Curled in a bell chamber slit, a seal or old basket valve gone soft with age. Only when I begin to move pebbles one by one, or to extrude a line of mucus to redirect a swarm of house-keepers, or to brace myself and pulse a silted conduit clear with three measured contractions, would the animal fact become undeniable.

What would I be, inwardly?

Not noble. Not contemplative in the human sense. But very attentive.

My mind, if it deserves the word, would be made of routes and thresholds. I would know the precinct not as a map seen from outside but as a repertoire of permissible transformations. This shutter can open now because the pressure difference has eased. This nursery bell must stay warm; that one should be cooled by widening its slit. This carcass should be dragged away before it fouls the lane. This predator can be tolerated on the outer stair but not beyond the second court. This run of tiles sounds hollow under two of its slabs; something is sheltering there that should not yet be disturbed. That kind of knowing. Not abstract dominion, but exact continuance.

I might carry traces from the elder resident—the central distributed body—from which I budded or was extruded. Chemical inclinations, inherited routes, perhaps periodic impulses returning me to certain vents where I must press my skin and “listen” to messages not encoded as symbols but as pressure histories and dissolved cues. In that sense I would be both myself and an emissary of a larger maintenance-intelligence. A finger that can wander and also report, though “report” would mean returning altered fluids, altered tensions, altered body-state to the warm core where the elder tissues can read what I have encountered.

That makes me less like a child and more like a temporary organ.

There is a sadness in that, but the place’s sadness is never dramatic. It is all conversion. So yes: perhaps when my rounds ended and I grew thicker, slower, more encrusted, I would not remain a rover forever. One day I would stop in some decisive passage beneath the bell field or under the stair of black stones, in a chamber where warm and cool currents must be modulated with precision. I would fasten there. My limbs would become braces. My skin would host films. My sensory fronds would retract into pores. House-keepers would clear around me; smaller offspring or offshoots would move past me into districts I once knew directly. And over a very long period I would cease to be something that goes out and become one more living gasket in the old machine.

But before that final settling, while I was still mobile, where would I find myself most often?

On the stair ascending nowhere.

That stair feels like the right emblematic address. It is a route that preserves the idea of upwardness in a world where upward has lost its old meaning. Its treads grow cleaner as they rise; its flanking black stones gather white crusts like accidental stars. It once led to air, perhaps, or to a terrace, or a small shrine, or merely a lookout over dry land. Now it leads into open water. Yet ascent still inheres in it as a behavior. It remains a place of approach, expectancy, orientation.

I think I would use the underside of its fallen landing as a station. A hollow where the fracture made a roof and currents comb beneath. From there I could monitor both the summit courts and the first terraces dropping into darker water. I could feel arrivals. I could test the exhalations from the bell field nearby. The black stones would hold cold better than the surrounding masonry; the white crust-creatures on them would act like tiny indicators, opening and closing with subtle changes. Sometimes I would move along the insets without touching them, preserving some instinctive protocol older than memory. Sometimes I would rest there long enough that my body’s passing woke the wall-film into pale delayed traces, briefly sketching me among the constellated shells.

If you asked me who I am in a more nameable sense, I might say:

I am a stair-keeper.

Not keeper of the whole precinct. Keeper of one transition. A verifier of ascent, though nothing waits above now except water. I test what comes up from below and what drifts down from the core. I clear the narrowings before they become closures. I learn the moods of the bell domes by the warmth on my skin. I know which face-mouths are breathing strongly, which with a catch. I remove the dead from certain clean places because those places must remain clean, though no doctrine survives to explain why. I let the little shell-workers gather where I have fed the stone with edible film, and in the morning a vent that was furred shut is open again. I am felt more often than seen. I am mistaken for installation. I am a local intelligence shaped exactly to the grain of this drowned made hill.

And if I were not that—if one rejects even this degree of animality—then the only other answer that feels equally true is that I would be the delayed pallor on the walls themselves.

Not a creature at all, but the precinct’s brief memory.

The faint bacterial-chemical afterimage that answers motion and then fades. The thing that does not act, but records passage in vanishing script. If the Warden is continuing function made flesh, then that pallor is continuing perception made surface. To be that would mean belonging everywhere and nowhere, existing only as response, sketching routes for a second and relinquishing them. It is perhaps the most intimate possible way to inhabit the place: as the masonry’s own momentary recollection of being touched.

But because you asked who or what I would be, not what atmospheric property I most admire, I return to the stair-keeper.

A pale adjunct of the deeper resident. A mobile office. A blind custodian of one upper district. Living under the broken landing near the summit, among bell domes and black stones, making rounds through warm lanes and jar-throats, reading pressure as if it were weather and law at once.

Not the king of the drowned sanctuary.

The thing that keeps one path true.
