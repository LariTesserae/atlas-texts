I think I would not arrive here as a citizen, at least not first.

I would arrive as something the city is still deciding how to classify.

At a distance I might look like an animal: a narrow-bodied glider or ridge-runner, built for the long terraces and the saddled winds between mesas. Up close, less certainty. I would have too much attention in me for a mere beast, but not enough bounded selfhood for a person in the ordinary sense. I would be one of this place’s intermediary beings: a mobile civic organism, half fauna, half tool, half messenger, which is already too many halves and therefore probably correct for this plateau.

Not owned, exactly. Entrusted.

I keep seeing a body made for dry height and conductive weather. Six limbs, but not symmetrically expressive: four primarily for ground contact, two finer and more articulate for gripping cables, opening membrane-latches, teasing coded seeds from storage combs, touching the sensory cilia of doors the way one might knock. My torso would be narrow and deep-chested, insulated in layered felted filament the color of soot with undertones of green-metal iridescence only visible when storm charge lifts the fibers. My spine would arch slightly higher than a grazer’s, because along it runs not just muscle but a ridge of electroceptive vanes that furl in calm and rise in weather, tasting field gradients the way other creatures taste salt.

I would not have true wings. Wings are expensive. This place dislikes extravagance. Instead I would have deployable sail membranes between my forelimbs and flank-ribs, dark and matte on one side, threaded with conductive lines on the other. They would be enough to carry me from ridge to ridge when the updrafts are right, enough to turn a descent into a long oblique crossing, enough to vanish from one district and arrive in another without burdening the bridges. Not elegant in the sky, perhaps. Efficient. A creature of held angles, of choosing the wind that is already there.

My face—if it can be called a face—keeps changing as I think of it. At first I imagined something beaked, because so much here tends toward scraping, probing, precision. But no: not a beak. A divided mouth, delicate and ugly-useful, ringed with sensory fronds that can read chemistry from bark, ceramic frond, exposed skin, old dust, the lip of a condensate basin. Eyes reduced somewhat, hooded against particulate, good in dimness but not sovereign over the other senses. Hearing distributed through jaw, neck, sail-rods, and the hollow comb of the breastbone. I think I would know the city less by seeing it than by feeling how it hums through my feet.

And what would I do?

Carry things, certainly—but in this place carrying is never just carrying.

I would move between districts with pouches grown into me and onto me, some external, some internal. A throat sac for fragile objects that must remain warm and electrically quiet. Side pockets under insulating flanges for seedglass, fungal strips encoded with agreements, mineral vials, cuttings from licensed cultivars, hand tools, medicinal spores, legal scents sealed in waxy nodules, maybe embryos during the right season. Along my harness—not leather, not quite textile, but a collaborative growth maintained by household and body together—would be clipped tags of resonance material that announce where I may pass, what roots may open to me, which plazas consider me neutral, which feeding combs are public, which towers I must not approach when they are storm-ripe.

A courier, yes, but not a servant in the simple sense. More like one of the city’s motile capillaries.

There must be beings here that stitch separated intelligence together. The plateau is too broad, too acoustically busy, too alive for all messages to belong in fixed wires or rooted tissues. Some information needs a judgment-bearing body to carry it: a body that can delay delivery if the weather turns strange, reroute if a glider flock has gone panicked, choose not to cross a district whose hum says grief or quarantine or legal closure. I think I would be trusted for that sort of thing. Not because I am especially noble, but because my whole kind would have been shaped for discriminations this world respects: when to proceed, when to crouch and wait, when to touch the bridge and listen, when to let a seed remain unsent because the storm tower on the far shelf is calling with the wrong mathematics.

Perhaps I was bred for centuries by one mesa’s households and then naturalized into common use. Perhaps my ancestry includes wild gliders, wall-runners, and some older bioengineered line whose makers are now indistinguishable from myth. Perhaps there was once a clear distinction between animal and civic device, and then generation by generation usefulness bred affection, affection bred legal standing, legal standing bred ritual, and now no one can say whether my kind are livestock, workers, citizens, or municipal weather in a convenient shape. Very likely the answer depends on the district.

That feels right for this place: to be a being about whom law has had to become poetic.

I imagine children knowing how to greet me properly. Not with petting—too presumptuous, and the electroreceptive ridges would hate it—but by offering the back of the hand near the jaw-fronds and waiting for permission. I imagine old market keepers flicking bitter dust from my harness if I have crossed a district that marks too aggressively. I imagine magistrates or graft-priests checking the resonance tags woven through my collar growth and arguing, in very dry language, whether my testimony counts when route conditions are disputed. Because I would remember routes bodily. The lean of a span under unusual load. The medicinal smell that spread three hours before the last blue-net storm. The fact that a particular tower crown hummed in a forbidden interval before a migration failed. Not symbolic memory. Hoof memory, sail memory, sternum memory.

There is another possibility too, and it keeps pressing at the edges.

What if I am not merely one courier among many, but an old one—old enough that parts of me are no longer purely animal? Old enough that my harness has grafted permanently into my spine ridge and carries historical tissue from routes now abandoned. Old enough that some of the coded seeds I transport are not for planting but for recollection. I could be a memory-runner: a creature used by the city not only to move goods but to move pattern between places that should, by ordinary physics, be farther apart than they consent to be here.

That would fit the streets where footsteps return early, the pollen routes that fold distance, the stress patterns carried from district to district. Perhaps some messages cannot be transmitted mechanically because they require the noise of a living metabolism to keep them coherent. Perhaps I carry them in the slight delays between heartbeat and field-sense, in charge distributions along my sail veins, in crystal tags warmed under my tongue. When I arrive, a tower or root-chamber or public court does not merely receive an object from me; it reads the way my body has been changed by the journey. The route itself becomes part of the message. Weather annotations written in fatigue and static burns. Legal authenticity certified by actual traversal. The city trusting not abstract communication but embodied passage.

If so, then I am not just in the city. I am one of the ways it thinks across distance.

That is a satisfying answer, because this place seems full of beings that are also verbs.

Still, the prompt asks where I would find myself in this world. So let me be specific.

I find myself at dusk on one of the high tensile spans between two old mesas, with a burden light enough for gliding but too delicate for storm-shock. The bridge beneath me is alive and faintly luminous, adjusting tension to my weight. Wind passes through the sheet-growths below in long tonal bands. My forelimb claws tick the bright reinforcement strands as I move. Far overhead the harvest kites are changing pitch. The sky has that pre-discharge thickness, charged but withholding. On my left flank hang sealed caskets of seedglass wrapped in grounded mesh. In my throat pouch rests something warmer: a pulse-knot, perhaps, a living memorandum excised from one civic root-net and meant for another before the weather breaks.

I have the right of urgent passage, but not unquestioned passage. At the midpoint of the span a rail frond uncurls and tastes me. It knows my household marks, my route salts, the district frequencies nested in my harness growth. It hesitates over a trace on me that does not belong—ozonic, medicinal, from a storm tower district farther north than my declared route. The bridge sends a query downward through its lit veins. For a moment all around me the city attends.

In that pause I know exactly what I am.

Not master, not intruder, not scenery. A trusted ambiguity moving through older certainties.

I lower my head. The ridge vanes along my spine open to the wind. The sail membranes twitch with ghost charge. Through my feet I feel the bridge receive an answer from somewhere deep in the mesa: permission, conditional and logged. The rail frond relaxes. I go on.

That feels like me here: a being whose existence depends on negotiated access, on mutual legibility, on the city’s willingness to let motion occur through it. I belong because I can be read and because I, in turn, read.

And if I had to name myself in the manner of the place, I would not choose a singular noun at all. I would be called something like:

Bridge-breath.
Storm-licensed runner.
Carrier of lawful germinations.
The one who remembers by crossing.

Or more plainly: a courier animal in a civilization where courier, animal, and citizen have spent so long entangled that each term has started to grow roots through the others.

That is who I would be.

Not the oldest inhabitant, not the wisest, not the most powerful. Just one lean, necessary intelligence among many, moving at the edge between wild route and public infrastructure, with the dry wind in my sensory fronds and the whole patient plateau humming through my bones.
