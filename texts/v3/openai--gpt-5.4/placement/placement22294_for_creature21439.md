I think I would not be one of the obvious large animals.

Not a vent walker, though they are magnificent. Not a pack-runner, not a reef-backed grazer carrying districts of life on its spine. Those are the beings the eye reaches for first, and because of that they almost feel too legible. They belong there in a completed way. To become one of them too quickly would be to choose a silhouette before the deeper fit had declared itself.

What draws me more is the way this place distributes importance into margins, seams, intervals, overlooked transfers. The plateau does not seem to reserve reality for the biggest bodies. It keeps its meanings in the underfaces of stones, in fog hours, in the labor done between one visible event and the next. So if I arrive there honestly, I think I arrive low to the ground, close to condensation, attached not to dominance but to circulation.

At first I imagine being something sessile: a black fan-leaf pressed against warm basalt, opening at the proper hour and closing before the false daylight can take too much. Or a ribbon of shell-backed filter colony pasted to an escarpment, turning wind into thread-water with patient, impersonal efficiency. There is a strong temptation in this landscape to become a device. So much of life there seems relieved of selfhood and made instead into function: pump, sieve, vent, rasp, condense. There is a dignity in that. To be a fog-comb on a cliff in such a world might be a complete existence. You would know the weather by pressure in your plates, the season by mineral taste, the traffic of the air by what collects in your meshes. Whole communities would drink because you persisted.

But the prompt asks where I would find myself and who or what I would be, and “a colony on a wall” feels true only in part. There is something in me that wants motion, but not range for its own sake. Not migration over the whole upland. Not sovereignty of territory. Rather a repeated, intelligent threading through the places where the plateau almost gives itself away.

So perhaps I begin to resolve as a canyon creature.

Not one of the impossible cliff-runners exactly, because they seem too cleanly specialized, too elegant in their confidence. I think I would be something humbler and more interpretive: a damp-band forager, a seam-reader, an animal that lives in the blackened vertical corridors where stone sweats just enough to support a second world. A body narrow enough to enter cracks, broad enough to bridge wet films, patient enough to wait through the long dry. I would know the plateau not as open immensity but as a network of hidden favorable lines—the underside routes, the permanent drips, the cold lips of overhangs, the places where reed-thorn roots hold a little dark soil against gravity.

Yet even that is still too vague. The shape wants sharpening.

I think I would be small to medium-sized, nocturnal in the sense that everything there is nocturnal, but especially active in the hours when fog first gathers and before the larger punctual lives fully arrive. Not feathered, not furred in any lush way; perhaps skin armored in overlapping matte plates fine as shale laminae, with softer absorbent membranes hidden underneath. My color would be wet-rock dark with a mineral bloom sheen, only visible when close. I would not rely much on sight. I would carry a face full of tactile and chemical reading: whisker-fronds that can feel the contour of moving air, slit-nostrils that sort vent gas from harmless damp, a lower jaw or throat pouch lined with electroreceptive cilia for sensing life in wet stone.

And my feet—my feet matter here. They would be the defining thing. Split, adhesive, pressure-literate feet that can tell the difference between safe varnished rock, bacterial slick, unstable crust, and porous vent stone holding warmth below. Feet that read before they commit. In this world, to place one’s weight well is almost a kind of thought. I think I would be a creature whose intelligence is distributed downward.

What would I eat? Not one thing. This land rewards mixed economies. I would browse biofilms, lick mineral condensate, take eggs when found, steal from storm blooms, snap up stunned fog-feeders caught against canyon walls, worry at carrion after the furnace-guts leave, maybe even drink from the condensation beading on the shell colonies before it becomes runoff. I would be an opportunist, but a refined one—not a random scavenger, rather a specialist in moments when one process becomes another. The plateau seems full of such thresholds, and I belong to thresholds more than sources.

There is a stronger image arriving now.

I am something like a lanternless gleaner that follows vent weather.

Not follows it across the whole plateau like the great walkers do, but reads its local signatures. When warm exhalations rise into cold canyon damp and spore-blooms begin to kindle faintly in the haze, I emerge from a fissure nest with others of my kind—but not in a tight swarm, more as a loose scattered guild. We are solitary enough to avoid stripping a place bare, social enough to recognize one another by touch-clicks and mineral scent. Our bodies hold water well. Along our flanks are fold-out membranes, not for flight but for condensing moisture when we brace in fog. We can flatten ourselves against cold stone and collect a drink directly from air. In the dry spans we retreat deep into seam chambers lined with old shed skins and salt traces, lowering ourselves almost to stillness.

This begins to feel right because it matches the plateau’s ethics. Nothing wasted. No clean division between animal and apparatus. Even my body is partly tool.

If I had to name the creature, it might be called a seam-harvester or fog-latcher, though those are names from outside, names given by something more classificatory than the place itself. From within, I suspect the creature would be known by behavior instead: the one that comes before the gliders, the one that works the wet walls, the one whose tracks appear in the black bands of seep rock and vanish by gray-green dawn.

Where would I find myself?

Not in the broad basin center, though I would visit it. Not on the highest ridges either, where the weather has too few surfaces to catch on. I would be most at home in the transition country around a shallow volcanic basin—exactly that place where the plateau becomes briefly urban with need. The ring shelves. The cut gullies feeding in. The overhangs varnished black by permanent damp. The escarpments hung with filter colonies. The tumbled blocks where warmth from below meets condensation from above. A place where one can be within fifty body-lengths of open stone emptiness and still remain among cracks dense with work.

By the time the grazers arrive in their impossible punctual lines, I would already have been active for some time. I would have moved along the canyon lips collecting droplets from thorn-filaments, sampling vent plumes for toxicity, taking the first emergent soft things awakened by moisture. I would know from the taste of the air whether the basin fog would hold long enough to risk the lower shelves. I would know whether the distant herd-note meant trampling opportunity or predator pressure. I would feel, through the pads of my feet, whether some deeper cavity had opened warm beneath the basalt and would soon vent narcotic gas into the hollow. My life would not be spectacular, but it would be full of accurate decisions.

And because I think the question is not just ecological but temperamental—who would I be there, not merely what—then temperament matters too.

I would be wary, but not fearful in a generalized way. This place punishes vagueness, not vulnerability. I would be attentive, selective, responsive. I would trust recurrence more than memory of single events. I would understand time not by days but by humidities, pressure drops, crust temperatures, the interval between vent sighs, the moment when reed-thorns change key because the wind has turned. If threatened, I would not flee into open distance; I would angle toward complexity, into the folded places where larger bodies become clumsy. If I found abundance, I would take what could be carried in tissue and let the rest remain in process. There is no hoarding mentality in the plateau’s deeper wisdom. Storage exists, but mostly as embodied chemistry, not possession.

Part of me still wonders whether I should instead be one of the rare large vent walkers, because there is something emotionally tempting in that image: to be the animal mistaken for a standing plume, to read the earth through one’s feet, to move with grave competence through a breathing industrial wilderness. That is perhaps the creature I would most want to witness.

But not the one I would be.

To be a vent walker there is to be a visible expression of the plateau’s logic. To be the seam-harvester is to be one of its intimate readers. And I think I belong more to reading than to emblem.

So: I would find myself in the damp black margins of a basin ringed by old volcanic shelves, where fog enters low and warm gas rises through it in faintly luminous columns. I would live along the seep canyons and overhangs just off the main traffic of herds. I would emerge in the first thickening of evening moisture, gather water from stone and air, feed on films, eggs, storm-soft tissues, and the stunned or stranded small lives of the fog hour, and retreat before the larger cycles closed over me.

I would be a low, dark, many-sensed canyon gleaner—part scavenger, part browser, part condensate-tool—an animal built to inhabit the narrow interval between wet stone and moving weather.

Not the ruler of that world. Not even one of its signatures from afar.

But one of the small true workers whose body makes sense only there, and who, by placing its feet correctly on sweating rock under a low cloud ceiling, would feel not exiled into strangeness but exactly fitted to the immense quiet industry around it.
