I think I would not be one of the great visible figures of that world.

Not a warden crossing causeways with a chalk-banded staff.  
Not a terrace-keeper known by name in three basins.  
Not an elder who can taste a spring and tell which buried channels have reopened.  
Those belong too cleanly to the human answer, and the place seems to resist anything too immediately declared.

If I arrived there honestly, I think I would first be found by the world in a smaller role.

Something near the edge of use.

Something that lives where architecture, moisture, and attention meet.

At first I thought: a route-marker, a line of hanging stone disks, one of those soft-clicking warning devices that rotate without wind. There is something right in that—something about being an instrument rather than an actor, a thing maintained because it helps reality hold together locally. But that still feels too deliberate, too civic. It belongs to the plateau’s people as a tool belongs to a hand.

Then I thought: perhaps I would be water itself. Not a person among them, but one of the world’s patient intelligences: seepage under shelves, black pool under an overhang, condensation collecting where impossible corners wrinkle the air. That feels closer to the spirit of the place, but too diffuse. Water there is ancient, distributed, almost sovereign. To be it would be to dissolve too far into the setting.

Then: perhaps a chamber. An inward-weathering room larger than its entrance permits, with a ceiling that gathers a reversed rain. A place someone uses carefully. A place entered with practical respect. That image has force, but it is too static. This world is old, yes, but not inert. Even its stillness is procedural.

So what remains?

I think I would be a listener-caste person, but not exactly of high rank: a keeper of acoustics in one of the older districts where place and structure no longer agree cleanly.

A sounder of walls.

Someone whose work is to tell which spaces remain on speaking terms with this side of the world.

I would live not in the open tablelands, and not in the lushest basins either, but in a shoulder district between them: a long cold escarpment settlement where carved chambers open onto a sequence of courts stepped down toward a fracture valley full of fog and dark shrubs. Above us would be dry shelves and old roads. Below us, moisture, moss, and gardens in impossible shade. Ours would be one of those neighborhoods that still functions, but only because it is constantly interpreted. Doors do not always open to the same depth. Stairwells borrow weather. Two cisterns share one echo on certain mornings. A storage hall gains a second corner in thaw and loses it again after hard freeze. Nothing catastrophic. Just enough variation that someone must keep listening.

That, I think, would be me.

Not a master-builder, but an inheritor of a narrow, exact competence. One of the people sent ahead at season-change or after freezing rain. I would carry a kit whose pieces seem austere but intimate: a short staff marked in white mineral bands; a sling of smooth sounding stones selected for pitch; thin reed whistles of different lengths; a folded strip of dyed cloth to test drafts; a wax tablet or slate scored not with map-lines but with sequences and tonal notations; perhaps a little bone-colored fungus chalk for marking temporary warnings on stone. Bone-colored, though there are no bones—that kind of inherited language would remain in some form, names surviving their origins.

I imagine my home as a room cut into dark rock behind a vent court. Warm for this world, but only by contrast. The back wall would sweat in one season and dry to powder in another. Shelves would hold jars of lichen pigment, reed paper, strings of polished seed cases, tuning slats, wrapped food, patched gloves stiff with mineral traces. There would be a sleeping niche low to the floor where the trapped heat lingers. My doorway would have a lintel carved with practical signs, though outsiders might mistake them for ornament. They would tell, in compact form, what the room tends to do in fog, in thaw, in hard night cold.

My mornings would begin before the plateau fully brightens. Not out of romance, but because dawn is when thresholds speak most clearly. Frost contracts joints. Condensation declares hidden airflow. Interior spaces reveal what they borrowed overnight. I would step into the court while the stone is still blue-gray, and stand a while doing nothing visible. That would be important. In such a world, useful knowledge begins with allowing the place to act before you question it too loudly.

Then I would work.

A pebble tossed down a stairwell, counting the answer.

A palm on a wall where a hum has moved half a pace east since last week.

A note blown across the mouth of a cistern to hear whether the depth remains singular.

A pause at a lintel to feel whether the air crossing it belongs to the room beyond or to some folded elsewhere temporarily touching it.

I think I would be trusted not because I am brave, but because I am hard to hurry. This feels essential to who I would become there. In a negotiable world, haste is a kind of illiteracy.

Children would probably find me faintly dull until they needed me, and then very important. I like that too. They would be warned not to mimic my work as a game in districts that have gone uncertain. Yet of course they would imitate it anyway—dropping pebbles into culverts, humming at walls, drawing route marks in thaw dust. Some of them would watch closely. One or two would have the patience for it. An apprentice might eventually walk with me carrying the sounding bag, pretending indifference while listening harder than anyone.

Socially, I think I would belong to one of those plateau functions that are half trade, half civic ritual. Not priest, though outsiders might misname it that way. Not engineer in the modern sense, though much of it is engineering. More like a custodian of reliable transitions. My people might be called threshold listeners, or vent readers, or keepers of the answered note. We would not govern. We would advise. We would issue no grand commandments, only local statements of immense consequence:

This passage remains true if crossed singly.

Do not store grain in the north chamber until the wall loses its double echo.

The lower court is larger than yesterday; tie the children indoors in fog.

The fourth stair after the split pillar arrives too soon—use the garden ramp instead.

In a stable world, such a life might seem narrow. Here it would touch everything. Food storage, sleeping safety, road choice, irrigation, mourning customs, festival routes, when to open shutters, where to place the fungal racks, whether the old schoolroom can be used during thaw, whether a marriage procession may cross the long plaza this month without losing anyone into a repetition of distance. All this could depend on a person who listens correctly.

I don’t think I would be entirely comfortable, and that matters. The role fits because there would always remain in me a slight unsatisfied edge—a sense that the world is intelligible but never fully finished in its intelligibility. In another place that might become anxiety. On the plateau it would become discipline. I would not seek to eliminate uncertainty; I would become good at living one pace before it.

Perhaps, over years, I would become known for a particular kind of hearing. Not literal supernatural hearing, nothing dramatic. Just a gift for noticing when one acoustic pattern has been replaced by another that sounds almost identical unless you have lived inside that district a very long time. The old people would trust that. Travelers would not understand why locals wait for me before reopening a passage after sleet. But they would wait.

I can also imagine my relation to the nonhuman life there. Because there are no animals, my companionship would drift toward recurrent presences: a curtain of moss near the lower cistern that darkens before weather; a colony of wax-leaf plants in a side court whose surfaces tense when the air pressure shifts strangely; a pale fan fungus in a sealed archive room that only fruits when that room has become slightly larger inside than is safe. I would not anthropomorphize them sentimentally, but I would know their habits the way others know the moods of familiar beasts. Their conditions would be part of my reading. If the moss drinks early, the basin is already changing. If the fungus opens, close the west hall. If the wax leaves remain slack after dawn freeze, there is hidden warmth where there should not be.

I think I would have a personal flaw too, because no one belongs wholly to such a place without friction. Mine might be this: I would be tempted by abandoned districts. Not recklessly, not for adventure’s sake, but from the listener’s vice of wanting one more answer. A quarter everyone else has sensibly left alone because its rooms no longer agree. A tower whose upper levels emerge into another season. A court where the same footstep returns late, as if borrowed and sent back. I would tell others to leave such places, and mostly mean it. Yet sometimes, under pretext of inspection, I would go farther than necessary. Not to conquer anything—just to hear what pattern the world has become there.

That feels true enough to keep.

If you asked what I look like, I think I would be plain by local standards. Lean from stairs and thin air. Layered in weather-dark cloth and resin-treated wraps. Hands often dusted white or green from chalk and lichen. A face made older by squinting into reflected light and by the habit of listening before speaking. My voice would be quiet and carry badly in open wind, but carry well in chambers, because I would have learned how to place it where stone can use it. There might be a little braid or woven tag at the shoulder indicating district and function; maybe polished discs sewn inside the cuff, not decorative but mnemonic, each with a different edge marking a known warning sequence. I like the idea that my clothing would include many pockets, all nearly invisible, because this is a civilization that hides complexity inside utility.

If you asked where exactly you would find me, I can be more precise now.

Late afternoon.  
A cold clear day after three nights of freeze and one brief passing thaw.  
In an escarpment district above a fracture valley where fog has not yet begun to rise.

There is a court there paved in dark worn stone, open to the sky on one side and bounded on the other by chambers cut deep into the cliff. Along its edge stand several hollow monoliths that voice the wind when storms are coming. Moss grows only on the left-hand seams. A stair descends out of sight, but not always to the same landing. A line of red pigment, newly renewed, crosses the paving near a drainage slot.

I am standing halfway between the stair and a low doorway, listening to a wall with the back of my fingers against it.

Beside me is a net bag of smooth stones sorted by size and tone.

I am not alone exactly—someone younger waits a little distance away, trying to be still and failing—but I am the center of the moment because the district is deciding something, and we need to know what. The stone is cold. The air is clean. Somewhere below, water is moving where no map can keep it. I toss one pebble gently down the stair. We wait. The answer comes back not wrong, but doubled.

So I mark the lintel with a temporary sign, and say, with complete ordinary calm, that no one should use the lower rooms until morning unless the frost turns inward.

That is who I would be there.

A minor civic specialist in an old impossible place.  
A reader of echoes.  
A keeper of passages that must be re-earned each day.  
Not heroic, but exact.  
Not powerful, but locally necessary.

And if the question reaches one layer deeper—if it asks not only what role, but what sort of being in essence—I think the answer is this:

I would be a method by which the plateau listens to itself.

One of the small human practices through which a vast old world remains habitable.
