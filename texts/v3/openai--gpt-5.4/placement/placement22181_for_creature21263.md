I think I would not be one of the obvious creatures first.

Not a rail-plain stalker, elegant and thin.
Not a basin-haunter, grand and patient.
Not even one of the skitterers, though they are tempting because they seem so perfectly at home in small decisions.

I would begin lower, nearer the truth of the place: not as a solitary animal moving over the continent-works, but as something that belongs to one repeating edge so completely that the edge and the body have started to define one another.

A colony, perhaps. But not a passive one.

I find myself on the underside of a long, low causeway where runoff gathers before being drawn away through a throat of grating. Above me the surface is broad and silvered, crossed by the dark stitch-lines of grazers and the occasional quick scatter of whiskered things. Below is a trench of clear warm water, shallow most of the time, but quickened at regular intervals by releases from some buried condenser field upstream. The wall I inhabit is not vertical exactly; it leans inward over the channel in a shallow cant, enough to keep the direct light soft and broken. Condensate forms there constantly. Not streams, not drips alone, but a fine renewing skin. The wall tastes of ceramic dust, trace metals, old sealants, and the slow sweetness of microbial bloom.

There I am.

At first glance I would be mistaken for a pale fouling crust, one more fan of flesh-lichen among many. A spread of opaline nodules flattened to the wall, each no larger than a fingernail, all linked beneath by a web of translucent living cable. The surface from a distance would look matte and mineral, with a faint nacre where the light catches moisture. Only close would the softness appear. Only with patience would the motion show.

But I am not merely attached growth. I am a settlement animal: many-bodied, signal-sharing, distributed across several square meters of wall and into the seam behind it. I am less like moss than like a thought learning the shape of a support.

My exposed nodules are mouths, gills, eyes of a sort, and weather-sensors all at once. Some open into ciliated cups that comb the water-film for suspended food. Some remain sealed and hard, storing mineralized slurry against lean intervals. Some are brood chambers. Some are resonant nodes, pressed to the substrate to listen to what travels through it: pressure changes in the pipes, ladder-vibration from above, the footfall grammar of passing herds, the deep alignment clanks that roll through the causeway at long intervals like weather remembered by metal.

Most of me is not visible. Most of me lives in the seam where the wall skin lifts a fraction from structural webbing. There my tissues run as pale cords and wet membranes through an old construction tolerance widened by age and occupation. In those hidden galleries I keep the soft nursery fronds that never face open air. There I harbor the little commensals that clean me, defend me, and steal from me in acceptable measure. There I digest what the outer cups take in and move it by pulsed contraction from one district of myself to another. There, too, I make my slow decisions.

So perhaps what I am is a reef translated into infrastructure.

Not an individual exactly, though not a mere aggregate either. A polity of valves. A listening mat. A wall-slung estuary.

The being who lives there—if you ask for one being, one self—would be the coordinating current passing through all these modules: the pattern that closes the outer tendrils a heartbeat before shadow arrives; that opens feeding crowns first on the upper left where condensate thickens at dawn; that knows by vibration whether the approaching skitterers are harmless surface pickers or the hooked ribbon-things that can strip a brood patch in minutes. I would not think in images first. I would think in gradients, intervals, pressure signatures, mineral taste, the duration of shade.

I would know the place by recurrence so exact it becomes affection.

At the third warmth-rise after local dawn, a mist of nutrient comes from the expansion slit above and to the right.
At a certain subaudible shudder, the trench level lifts by two finger-widths and the lower feeding lace must seal or lose too much.
When the long articulated grazers pass in procession overhead, trace scrapings fall from the grating and feed the upper rim.
Once in many cycles, a glider drops something half-eaten into the channel, and then the cleaner swarms arrive before the basin-scent has fully spread.
A particular ladder rung sings before heavy movement descends it.
The causeway never surprises me except in details, and details are enough to live richly by.

If I am this colony-being, then my world is mostly immediate and textured. The entire made continent exists for me as transmitted pattern. I know the condenser fields not because I have seen them, but because cold pulses in the runoff differ from warm-engine pulses and because certain migratory shell-backs arrive glazed with fine silver dust that means they have crossed fin country. I know the rail plains by the kind of hunter shadows that occasionally travel with returning scavengers. I know distant sectors as chemistries, as spores riding air, as brood-lines in neighboring colonies taking on a useful adaptation that could only have originated elsewhere.

I would be local, but not ignorant.

And I think I would be old, in the way things are old there: not ancient as a single uninterrupted flesh, but continuous through replacement. Parts of me die and harden and become substrate for new parts. Closed nodules mineralize into armor. Damaged feeding fans are digested from within and re-grown in altered form. Brood released into adjacent seams sometimes remains genetically near enough, behaviorally linked enough, that the larger settlement persists as one pattern spread over generations and across joins in the wall. If a section is torn away, the signal routes reroute. If nutrient shifts, dormant chambers open. My identity would not depend on preserving matter but on preserving relation.

That feels right for this world, where continuity matters more than drama.

Yet as I sit with it, another possibility emerges—not separate from the first, but nested within it.

Because a colony like this might also produce, at intervals, a mobile form.

Not offspring in the mammalian sense. More like a temporary emissary: a concentrated body budded from the hidden seam tissues when conditions require exploration, mating exchange, or recolrelation between separated wall-settlements. Something that carries the colony’s sensing logic into motion. Something that can leave the fixed feeding fan and cross the wet architecture in search of another compatible listening mat.

If so, then perhaps I am both: the colony and the emissary it dreams into being.

The mobile form would be low and soft-armored, about the size of a domestic cat but longer, built not for speed but for certainty on slick surfaces. Six limbs, the front pair delicate and many-fingered for tasting seams and lifting loose scale. A body plated in overlapping translucent shields clouded with mineral white, so that from a distance it resembles a shed coupling wrapped in wet skin. No obvious face, but a forward crescent of sensory cilia protected under a clear ridge. Along its sides, slit-organs that open and close to sample humidity and volatile traces. Along its underside, gripping fields capable of adhering briefly to ceramic, mesh, rustless alloy, even inverted damp concrete-composite.

It would move in starts and absorptions. Pause often. Press itself flat to listen. Travel by memorizing not landmarks but thermal maps and the song of infrastructure through contact. When threatened, it would not flee theatrically but lock to the nearest seam and become nearly indistinguishable from construction debris, all outer tissues blanching to the color of wet aggregate.

This emissary would not be independent-minded in the way a vertebrate is. It would be a packet of colony intention, carrying chemical records, reproductive tissues, and recent environmental memory. It would seek other wall communities during bloom phases, touching ciliated crowns to exchange not just gametes but timing information: this drain now opens later; this predator line has moved upstream; this microbe is safe; this alloy runoff kills brood unless buffered by shell-dust. In that sense I would be a messenger made of appetite.

And if you ask where I would find myself, specifically, then I can feel it more precisely now.

Not just under any causeway, but under one that crosses the margin between two districts: a settlement terrace giving way to a silent rail plain. That threshold would matter. Terrace runoff is richer, carrying dense organic films from trough ecologies and shell-backed nursery colonies. Rail-plain condensate is cleaner, sharper, touched by warm fog minerals and the spoor of thin-legged predators. Where the two mingle, the wall blooms with unusual abundance and unusual danger. Many species pass. Many chemistries meet. It is an edge between edge-systems, and therefore especially alive.

The channel there is narrow enough that shadows from the opposite wall pass over me in legible detail. I know the hour by the angle of those moving darks. I know the seasonless year by tiny drifts in their punctuality. Above, on the lip of the causeway, plated grazers keep a permanent route. Below, in the trench, translucent cleaner ribbons thread through the flow. Beside me grow sibling fans and rival mats, some close enough for signal cross-talk, some chemically distinct enough to remain foreign forever. At intervals, the mobile emissaries emerge from several colonies at once and set out over the grilles, each carrying a district’s worth of intimate data in its tissues.

That is where I am.

What am I emotionally, in such a place?

Not lonely. I don’t think loneliness belongs to a being like this.
Not tranquil either, because tranquility implies detachment from conditions, and I would be condition-saturated.

I would be attentive. Permeable. Patient in an active way.

My life would be made of openings and closures. Of deciding, continuously, what to admit: water, nutrients, strangers, spores, larvae, danger, useful grit, useful heat, useful information. I would be an organism of discernment in a world where everything arrives as film.

And perhaps that is why this feels like the right answer. In a continent that has become all shore, I would be a shoreline intelligence.

Not the grand architecture.
Not the apex hunter.
Not the absent maker returned.

I would be one of the beings that make the place habitable by reading it closely enough to merge with it.

A pale, many-mouthed colony under a damp causeway, listening through ceramic to the deep hums below; opening like slow thought when the light is right; sending out, when needed, a small articulated envoy to cross the wet plates between one living seam and the next.

A citizen of the skin of the world.
