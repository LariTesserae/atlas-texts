I think I would not be human first.

Or rather: if I began by trying to place myself among the people—pump-keepers, roof-tenders, surveyors of breeding cycles, readers of stains and droppings and water pressure—I could do it, and there is a version of me that belongs there. Someone who knows which stairwells bloom with condensation before a storm, who can tell by smell whether a cistern lining has cracked, who pauses on a landing because the usual red huddling creatures are absent and that means something is wrong three terraces over. A keeper of thresholds, as you say. A minor official of continuities. I can feel the rightness of that.

But it is not the first rightness.

The first thing that answers in me is smaller, wetter, more distributed. Something that belongs not to the city’s idea of itself but to its undersides and repetitions. Not a flagship creature. Not even, perhaps, a creature anyone would point to individually. I think I would be one of the city’s readers of surfaces: a film-grazer, wall-licker, seam-follower, one of those low-bodied animals that make use of what the city sweats.

At first this seems too humble, but the more I stay with it, the more central it feels.

Because this place is built out of delay. Water that does not leave. Heat that does not fully dissipate. Stone that remembers every runnel. Labor layered over older labor. Occupation settling into every suitable hollow. A grand animal would move through it as spectacle; a small rasping thing would know it intimately. It would know the city by taste before shape. Sulfur around warm vents. iron along handrails. old salts where a wall has dried unevenly. bacterial sweetness in the runnels below feed stores. oil-thin bitterness where machinery breathes. It would travel not by map but by gradients: damp to warmer damp, shadow to shadow, bloom to bloom, danger by vibration.

I imagine it now less as a slug and less as an insect than something in between categories, or under them. Flattened, yes. Broad in contact with the world. A body built to seal itself to wet stone. The underside a patient muscular sole or many coordinated pads; the mouth not noble, just efficient, a fine rasp or combing crescent. When still, it is almost only a dark gloss on darker masonry. When moving, it leaves a cleaned fan through the soft mineral-organic film, a visible testimony that a meal has happened.

Smoke-gray on exposed walls. Nearly translucent in the channels. In old industrial districts they might pick up rust colors from what they ingest, so that colonies in one ward are reddish, in another ash-white, in another black-lipped from manganese skins. Children would know them in aggregate, not individually: the licking beds under the condensers, the slick bands behind the public baths, the pale clustered things under the market drains.

And if I am one of them, I am not solitary.

That matters. This city does not feel like a world that gives identity to lone exemplars. It gives it to patterns, accumulations, recurrent occupations. So perhaps “I” would be closer to a route than to a body: a lineage adhering to a certain retaining wall for generations, active at dawn where the masonry cools enough to bead, retreating by noon into a crack behind a jacketed pipe, emerging after dark to travel three habitual meters along a mineral lip beneath a warehouse vent. A tiny citizenship, one ripple in the larger citizenship.

There is something appealing in being below notice but not beneath significance.

Because these creatures would be among the first to register change. If a vent stops warming, their band thins. If runoff chemistry alters, they disappear from one gutter and overpopulate another. If some larger scavenger has died in a culvert and begun to dissolve into the city’s lower food systems, they arrive as a dark tideline. Human workers might not admire them, exactly, but they would read them. Their presence would be diagnostic. To be such a creature is to be a sentence the city writes constantly in a small script along its walls.

Still, I don’t want to settle too quickly.

There is another possibility nearby: not a grazer but one of the blind runoff dwellers. A channel-animal. Something pale and touch-led, moving in the finger-deep ceramic streams under grates and beside service roads. That also feels true to me, maybe truer in temperament. Less exposed. More infrastructural. A life spent in continuous contact with the city’s circulation, knowing all districts as connected by flow. Such a being would experience the settlement not as blocks and towers but as one branching body of cool currents, edible drift, dangerous pulses, and thunder from above. It would know market quarter from foundry quarter chemically, know shift changes by what enters the water, know storms before clouds arrive because pressure and dilution change upstream.

If I were that, I would be pale to the point of seeming unfinished. Ribbed, perhaps, or softly plated. Eyes reduced to shadows under skin or gone entirely. Mouth ringed for sifting. Limbs minimal, or many small holding limbs for resting in current. When disturbed, I would not flee far; the channel itself would be enough world. The reluctance to retreat that you mention feels important. A creature not bold exactly, but founded in prior claim. This is my rill, my under-stair run, my culvert mouth, my warm eddy below the purification outlet.

That version of me is older.

Not older by species age, maybe, but by feeling. More surrendered to the city’s long patience. Less interested in surfaces, more in continuities. The film-grazer is a reader of facades. The runoff thing is a reader of metabolism.

And then there is a third possibility, which arrives later and with more mischief: one of the latch-learners.

I resist it because it is too flattering at first. Intelligence is an easy place to put oneself. But the longer I think, the more I can imagine being not the grand clever raider of folk stories, but a middling urban opportunist with inherited practical knowledge: a small, dexterous, social animal that has learned timetables, warm ledges, food hierarchies, and the exact duration of human inattention. Not a ruler of the city, just an excellent exploiter of its routines. Something that waits above a freight door because condensed feed always spills when the second cart jams. Something that understands hooks but not locks, patterns but not plans. Something passed from elder to young along with routes and cautions: never cross the polished yellow pipes after dusk; the broad-winged tower scavengers watch that corridor. Avoid the deep humming bridge in mating season. The woman at the west cistern throws scraps but sets traps in cold weather.

This too would be a way to belong: not by merging with infrastructure, but by learning the civic grammar well enough to improvise within it.

Yet even here, I suspect I would be a lesser member of the cast, not one of the city’s famed personalities. I would not want to be emblematic. This place distrusts emblems. Its truth is pressure, repetition, adaptation by countless partial successes.

So if I answer as honestly as I can: I would be found low, close, and often where a person would almost fail to focus.

I would be in the damp margin where a retaining wall meets a paved descent, where the wall sweats through the morning and a shallow runnel carries enough dissolved matter to feed a narrow gloss of life. Perhaps beside a pumping station old enough to be jacketed in three eras of repair. Perhaps under the warm exhale of an industrial vent where gray translucent colonies gather and are thinned nightly by rasping mouths. Perhaps in the tiled overflow channels of a covered cistern, disappearing into a crack whenever a maintenance lamp passes.

And what would I be?

Not one body, but one of a species of low-profile mineral grazers, city-native in the deepest sense: a damp-adhering, many-toothed wall feeder that turns condensation, bacterial film, chemical bloom, and the fine edible ambiguity of wet stone into motion. A creature half cleanup, half indicator. Unbeautiful nearby, beautiful in pattern. Most active at the hour when the city seems to have exhaled in its sleep and every surface is fresh with beads.

If you insisted on something more singular, I might say I am an old individual of that kind, large for my species, scarred by pipe heat and near misses, with a habitual circuit along one specific district wall. Workers on the early shift know that wall by my absence or presence without realizing they know me. Children once tried to pry me loose. A roof-tender, years ago, noticed that where my kind vanished the condensate had turned sour with a leaking sealant line; perhaps that saved a cistern from contamination. I am not symbolic to the city, but I participate in its legibility.

Or perhaps I am the runoff counterpart of that same creature: the pale siphon-bodied channel dweller beneath the gratings below that wall, the thing the grazer becomes in another branch of evolution. Surface and underflow. Facade and circulation. Two ways of reading one mineral habitat.

Actually, that pairing feels closest.

In this world I do not want to be perched above it, naming it. I want to be one of the small intelligences by which it knows itself is changing.

A wet-stone citizen.
A taster of walls.
A keeper of the city’s minor truths.
