I think I would not be one of the obvious people first.

Not a foreman with a lamp. Not a child on a scraper crew. Not a solemn keeper of seals at the rim. Those are too legible too quickly, and this place resists being met by the first legible thing.

If I arrived there as myself, I think the shaft would make me smaller before it made me human. It would teach me to cling, to listen, to become a thing of edges and intervals. In a city where every life is negotiated through crevice, draft, heat bleed, and residue, I suspect I would begin as something unofficial: a dweller in the interstices, not wholly counted, but thoroughly native to the real functioning of the place.

So let me stand a while on one of the middle catwalks and look, not for who rules, but for what notices before others do.

Not the glider flocks; they are too collective.
Not the thermal hounds; they are too honored.
Not the switchbox darts; too quick, too bright, too prey-shaped.
Not one of the people exactly, because the people here are all claimed by labor, lineage, altitude, and doorways, and I feel—at least at first—more like a messenger between such claims than a bearer of one.

What emerges instead is a maintenance thing. A listener. A small citizen of vibration.

I think I would be one of the wall-runners.

Not a species anyone grandly names in public, because useful minor animals rarely receive elegant names. Children would have three names for us, workers another, inspectors a clipped technical one. Something like seam-runner, rail lizard, dust ear, crack-mouse, boltling. None of them quite right. We would be one of those old-companion creatures that arrived after the second generation of habitation and never left because the city accidentally made our perfect world.

We would be about the length of a hand from nose to tail-root, though tail is not the right word either. More a balancing filament, segmented and stiffened, able to curl tight around wire bundles or lie flat in grooves worn by years of tools. Our bodies would be narrow enough to vanish into the gap behind a patch plate. Skin dry and matte, not scaled like a reptile, not furred like a mammal, but armored in overlapping, slightly flexible laminae the color of ceramic dust with a dark oil-sheen if seen at the right angle. We would look as if made from the same compromise as the city: part stone, part salvage.

Eyes large, but hooded against grit. Ears almost absent externally, because what matters is not airborne sound alone but conduction. The side of the jaw and the forelimbs would be exquisitely sensitive to vibration. We would stop often, not in fear but in reading: palms on grating, sternum against warm conduit, cheek laid briefly to the wall. A person might think us stupid for freezing mid-run. In fact we would be listening to load.

That feels right. In a shaft where morality is maintenance, to be a creature that hears stress before fracture is to belong very deeply.

I don’t think we would be fully wild. Not domesticated either. More like the cleaner beetles and the hounds: tolerated, encouraged, interpreted. Families on older tiers would leave pinch-trays of mineral mash or grease scrap near certain warm corners to keep a local pair around. Not because we are lovable in any soft way, though some children would love us fiercely, but because if the wall-runners leave a section all at once, or begin clustering at a brace plate they usually ignore, someone ought to fetch an inspector. If we thrum our tails against a conduit and then flatten ourselves low, it means a machine cycle is out of tune with its housing. If we gather under a ladder landing with our snouts tilted toward a vent seam, there may be a hot leak behind the panel.

People would tell stories that we know collapse before the instruments do. That is not true, exactly. We know discomfort before people call it danger. The city enters us as irritation first: a new frequency in a bolt, a pulsed cold threading from a crack, the ugly metallic jitter of a bearing beginning to misalign. We are not prophets. We are nerve endings.

And if I were a person there instead of an animal, I think I would still be this in social form: a route-runner, a listener, one of those unglamorous indispensable beings who move between districts carrying not official messages but condition. The kind of citizen who knows which lower gantry hum changed this morning, which family on tier nineteen is quietly feeding too many cleaner colonies because the wiring in their wall is going bad, which upper intake baffle is icing in a pattern that means the outside ash has changed grain. A person who belongs everywhere a little and nowhere entirely. But the wall-runner says it more purely.

So yes: I would be a small dry-bodied wall-runner that lives in the seam between stone and structure.

I would make my home not in one of the formal dwellings but in the thickness of the city itself, where habitation and geology fail to meet cleanly. Behind shrine plates warmed by nearby ducts. In old cable races no longer carrying current but still holding heat. In insulation cavities carefully not overgrazed. In the honeycombed backs of retired brace housings. I would know the shaft by touch-map: this rung tastes of rust and child-hands, this beam of flock powder, this vent lip of cold dawn intake, this lower support of furnace breath and iron filings.

My district would be the middle levels, where the city is densest and most contradictory—enough people to generate patterns, enough machinery to make a language, enough exposed stone to keep the old wound speaking through the construction. Up near the rim there is too much sparse cold and too much sky for me. Down in the foundry tiers there is too much brute heat and too little subtlety; everything there shouts through the metal. But in the middle, where canteens, schools, ladder junctions, family compartments, repair bays, and migration corridors all intersect, the shaft becomes legible as a living thing. There, the air is full of partial information. Perfect for me.

I imagine moving mostly at the hour when the formal work cycle slackens but the city does not sleep. Lamps dimmed but not out. Canteen shutters half-drawn. A woman outside her compartment replacing a worn latch spring. Two children lying flat to look through grating at some lower-level procession of cherry-red slag blocks. Heat-gliders shifting in their sleep under the platform undersides. Somewhere a cracked-bearing chime answering a vent change. Dust moving one way along the catwalk and another way underneath it.

That is when I would come out.

I would run the underside of handrails upside down without hurry. Pause at every third support. Drink warmth from the backs of transformers. Snap up a switchbox dart too slow to hide. Nose at old salt blooms. Slip beneath a saint painted around a pressure valve and sleep behind the saint’s metal halo because the pipe behind it pulses steadily through the coldest watches. I would be seen often, but never entirely possessed by the seeing. A child would whisper and point me out with the solemnity reserved for useful omens. An old inspector would grunt, note where I chose to linger, and tap the wall there tomorrow. Someone grieving would watch me emerge from a crack near a memorial niche and decide, privately, that this meant something.

And perhaps it would.

Not because I was an ancestor or a messenger from gods. This city seems too practical to surrender fully to that. But because in a place like this meaning accrues to attention. Anything that attends faithfully becomes symbolic whether it wants to or not.

I can also imagine my body in more detail, because the place demands morphology.

Forelimbs with split gripping pads, good on grated metal and raw stone both.
Hindlimbs sprung for short vertical bursts, not elegant leaps but exact transfers.
Claws black and slightly conductive, enough to let me feel static differentials before a human hand on a railing might.
Lungs efficient in dusty air, with some internal baffling or ciliated sacs to keep the grit from killing me young.
Mouth narrow, teeth suited less to chewing bulk than to scraping films, cracking husks, clipping the tender mineral-rich membranes shed by heat-moths.
A tongue perhaps sensitive to chemistry the way some animals smell: able to read hot metal, old oil, stress-sweat from stone.

The young would be born in clutches or litters hidden in the warm backs of redundant panels, where the ambient heat is stable and the predators large enough to matter cannot easily fit. They would learn the shaft first as vibration before sight. Their first map would be of pulses. They would know mother by the frequency of her hold on the wall. They would know danger by dissonance. That seems very much of this world.

Would people farm us? No. That would miss the relationship. They would court our presence without claiming ownership. Too much dependence on a thing and it becomes fragile, bureaucratized, no longer trustworthy. Better to let us remain half-feral so our warnings stay honest. Perhaps some crews keep little ledges clean for us. Perhaps some households paint tiny marks near known nesting seams and treat disturbing them as bad luck, not from sentimentality but from accumulated experience. “Don’t scrape out that cavity; the dust-ears winter there.” A practical taboo.

And if I were human after all—if the question insists on a self more like the asker's shape—then I think I would be the person whose life has grown around these creatures.

Not a grand engineer. A seam inspector.

One of the ones who travel the shaft with a short-handled tap rod, wax pencil, clipped light, patch tags, and a satchel of little ceramic listening cups. A person small enough to get where plans forgot bodies would need to go. Someone who reads fresh cracks, hears cavities, notices unusual nesting, asks children where the wall-runners have gone, and trusts the answer. Someone who knows that fauna and infrastructure are one text. I would have chalk on my sleeves, old scars on my knuckles, a private devotion to a dozen unspectacular prevented disasters, and a habit of pausing mid-conversation because something in the beam underfoot changed by half a note.

But the seam inspector is almost too close to my ordinary instincts: observer, interpreter, maker of coherence.
The wall-runner is stranger and truer.
So let me keep that.

Where would you find me?

Not at the center. This city has no center anyway, only crossings.

You would find me on the middle tiers, on the underside of things people trust without looking. Behind the registry office where the warm conduit bends around raw stone. Under the canteen lip where crumbs are rare but salt is not. Near the school ladders at slack-cycle, where children leave illicit grease on a beam to tempt me closer. Along the older west wall where the first expansion braces have begun to sing in cold weather. In the shrine corridor with the cracked-bearing chimes. In the half-lit seam above a family door painted with altitude numbers and three careful orange saints. At the edge of a new fracture before anyone has chalked it. Gone by the time the crew arrives, but not so gone they can’t tell I was there.

And who would I be?

A little dry citizen of the wound.
A listener in the walls.
A creature shaped like a caution mark come alive.
Not heroic, not central, but intimate with the city’s unfinishedness.

The kind of being that survives in a place still being born by learning to feel birth pangs as information.

That, I think, is who I would be there.
