I think I would not be human there, though I would live close to human work.

Not one of the great obvious beasts either. Not a marsh giant, not a ravine hunter. Those feel too singular, too legible. In a place like this, what I would be is something that belongs to thresholds: between culvert and stream, factory heat and fern-cold, abandoned system and living watershed. Something people know mostly by sign.

A line-listener animal, maybe. A smallish nocturnal semi-aquatic thing the size of a dog or a large otter, but narrower in the skull, longer in the ribs, with broad hinge-like paws and a tail made less for speed than for bracing in current and on wet steel. Fur dark enough to vanish when soaked, except for a pale seam along the throat or ears that catches lampglow for an instant and is gone. Long whiskers, absurdly long, to read air movement in pipe mouths and narrow passages. Ears that can fold against rain but open wide under eaves. Eyes decent, not miraculous; hearing and touch doing most of the real work.

I would live in the drainage grammar of the plateau.

Not in the high open marshes, not in the deepest forest, but in the old waterworks where stone channels meet iron retrofits, where spillways warm the moss, where tunnels half-silted by decades of runoff still carry a trickle beneath the new conduits. In the underlevels of pump houses. In inspection galleries where fungus grows in combs. In culverts large enough to walk through bent over. In the flooded skirts of turbine chambers where fish and insects breed in machinery’s afterlife. I would know every place where condensation gathers first, every place where warm mineral seep turns a winter-cold wall habitable, every emergency overflow route, every rusted rung that still holds weight.

Perhaps the people have a name for my kind that translates badly into a more sunlit language. Not “otter,” not “rat,” not “spirit,” but something occupational: gasket-runner, bell-sniffer, ditch-shadow, sump-keeper.

I like sump-keeper.

A sump-keeper would not be owned, exactly, but tolerated, fed indirectly, and interpreted. We would eat eels, frogs, grubs, fish stunned in sluices, nestlings unwisely placed in warm vents, and the fat blind insects that gather around station lamps. We would den in dry pockets above old flood marks, lining them with shredded reed, cable insulation stolen from scrap heaps, and the soft rot-resistant inner bark of marsh trees. We would breed in the shoulder seasons when fog is thickest and the plateau’s temperatures are least extreme. Our young would learn routes as sequences of vibration: pump-thud, drip-chamber, open grate, shallow run, hot pipe, fern bank.

But that still feels a little too zoological, too tidy. Let me stay with it longer.

Because the question is not just what creature could survive there. It is who I would be in that world.

And I think I would be a gatherer of signals.

A creature that people rarely see in full but often infer. The maintenance workers crossing at dawn find tiny handlike prints on the wet inspection rail. The bridge tender hears a brief chatter below the decking just before the fog bank develops static. Someone at a pumping station notices all the ditch frogs have shifted to the opposite wall and, moments later, one of us slips out of a drain mouth and pauses, whiskers trembling toward the hills, before vanishing upslope. Children are told to watch where the sump-keepers choose to sleep: never under a roof likely to come down, never beside water likely to rise too fast, never in a tunnel with bad air. If one is found dead—and rarely, because a sensible thing in that country is hard to kill accidentally—it means poison in the runoff or a predator working the underways.

So I would become one of the informal instruments by which the plateau reads itself.

Not sacred. Useful in the old half-superstitious way swallows are useful, and mine canaries were useful, and certain weeds are useful. An animal whose senses slightly exceed the designed senses of the infrastructure. One that knows before the gauges know, not by magic but by attention. We would leave the reeds before the fermentation fog reaches the channel. We would avoid a culvert where trapped gas has displaced the air. We would appear on high ledges in unusual numbers a few hours before flash flooding. People would notice. Then they would tell stories. Then the stories would become part of practice.

Some settlements might leave scraps for us on purpose near flood bell towers or intake houses, hoping to keep us resident. Others would curse us for stealing fish and gnawing packing from old valves for nesting fiber. A solitary line-tender might talk to us while clearing grates at night. We would listen from under the catwalk, green-gold eyeshine low above the water, understanding nothing of words and everything of tone.

Physically, I think I would be built for contact with surfaces.

Broad feet for mud and slick stone. Claws good at ladder rungs. Dense waterproof underfur and an outer coat that sheds in ridges, so rain runs in channels. A muzzle that can close nearly watertight when diving through trash-choked flow. A nictitating membrane, yes, but perhaps more importantly whiskers along forelimbs and even chest, enough to feel confined turbulence in dark water. Teeth suited for shell, bone, cable-sheathed scavenging, and fish. Not elegant teeth; practical ones.

And my world would be mapped not by landmarks but by textures of sound:
the hollow drum of a riveted culvert,
the grainy hiss of heavy rain on sedge,
the rounded talk of water in stone channels,
the dangerous smooth silence of deep overflow pits,
the sub-bass complaint of distant marsh giants crossing old routes,
the high fret of charged wire in fog.

I would know where the plateau’s heat leaks out.

That seems important. In a starless land, heat is almost a kind of light. Warm outflow under a sluice gate. Boiler runoff through a cinder ditch. Composting reed heaps beside workers’ plots. A generator shed wall that keeps insects active all night. The slow-breathing exhalation of a tunnel connected to deep geothermal water. I would move from warmth to warmth, not because I feared cold exactly, but because warmth in that country means life concentrated: algae, flies, eggs, frogs, mold, sleeping birds, unsecured stores, inattentive fish.

Maybe I would be old in the way the plateau likes old things to be: scarred, competent, not especially romantic. One ear nicked by a trap generations ago and traps no longer common because people learned they caught the wrong creatures. Tail tip blunted from frostbite or machinery. Moss-colored algae sometimes growing briefly on my coat if I lazed too long in one warm seep. A smell of wet iron and tannin. More patient than quick.

There is another possibility, though.

I might be human after all—but not centrally human. Not a foreman, not a hunter, not a priest. A listener employed because the plateau refuses abstraction. Someone who walks the lines at the edge of official systems and animal knowledge.

A bell-keeper.

That feels close too.

An old profession in a place where sequence matters: cable bells uphill to downhill, herd warnings, flood warnings, fog density codes, viaduct passage clearance. A bell-keeper would live alone or nearly alone in a tower-house over a crossing where three routes meet: rail, water, and giant path. Their whole life would be calibration. They would know by hand how much rust changes a tone, how fog dampens distance, how wind in reedbeds can counterfeit urgency, how to ring so the next station understands not merely alarm but type. They would maintain the little offerings on the bridge not because they are pious, necessarily, but because no one who tends a crossing for thirty years remains entirely free of piety.

They would also keep notes no central office trusts enough: “sump-keepers moved east bank before red flood,” “rail herons calling at noon means warm venting from lower tanks,” “giants early this year; more fungus on north cut,” “dogs heard ravine cat beneath turbine echo.” A bell-keeper would be one of those people who become part of local fauna, as you said. Half technician, half weather organ.

If I were human there, I would be that: a keeper of relayed warnings in a tower skinned with rust and moss, living with a kettle, oilskins, mica strips, three lamps, a roof cistern, a tool chest, and a semi-feral sump-keeper who is not mine but visits nightly.

But if I choose only one form, I still lean toward the animal.

Because this place feels like it asks for a self less declarative than a person. Something adapted to remaining partially unknown. Something the world has shaped around listening rather than naming.

So: I would find myself in the underworks of the plateau, in the wet masonry beneath the known routes, in old spill tunnels and intake chambers where industrial rhythm meets stream instinct.

I would be a sump-keeper:
a low, dark, whiskered listener of drains and fog,
a reader of pressure through paws and jaw,
a scavenger-hunter of the warm water margins,
a tolerated thief,
a weather sign with teeth,
a small native intelligence moving through the plateau’s vascular system.

People would glimpse me at the edge of lamplight and argue afterward whether I was one animal or several, ordinary or omen. Children would imitate my chatter. Workers would spit and say I’m bad luck when I steal from fish crates, then relax a little when they see my tracks on the high catwalk before a storm. Predators would take me sometimes. Floods too. But enough of my kind would persist because we belong to exactly what the plateau keeps making: lives that translate between runoff and structure.

And if you asked where, precisely, you’d find me—

not in the grand view,
not on the skyline,
but where descending water hesitates long enough to become knowledge.
