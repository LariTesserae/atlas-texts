I think I would not be one of the obvious figures first.

Not a ruler of ducts, not an elder of water law, not even at once a pruner or pollinator, though all of those are beautiful and necessary. In a place like this, where everything important is already under the hands of specialists and ancestors, the self that emerges most strongly is something slightly lateral to power: a listener whose work begins where certainty frays.

I would live near an edge within the city, but not the outermost edge. Not in the oldest central vaults where every wall has a genealogy recited by touch, and not in the raw margins where paths become conjecture. I would belong to the districts where maintained structure gives way, chamber by chamber, to long pockets of uncommitted dark: archive margins, abandoned trellis-fields, former habitation knots gone dry, acoustic chimneys no one climbs unless a season has turned strange. A threshold citizen. One of the people sent where the city is still itself, but less confidently.

My dwelling would be grown into the side of a narrow vertical chamber that carries drafts from somewhere higher and older. Not a prestigious place. A useful one. The chamber would have three distinct voices depending on air pressure, and from those voices I would know whether to sleep lightly, whether condensation cloths should be hung, whether distant sectors have opened vents they do not usually open. My sleeping niche would be small and dry, lined in softened bark felt and old binding rope polished by many hands before mine. Outside it, hanging at shoulder height, would be a chain of seed-pods tuned not to announce rank but to identify me to those who know the pattern: not household, not office exactly, but vocation.

I think I would be an echo-keeper of disused places.

Not merely an echo-mapper in the practical civic sense, though that would be part of it. More specifically: a reader of chambers that have slipped partly out of use, a caretaker of acoustic memory, an examiner of structures whose danger is not collapse alone but forgetting. The city you describe would generate such people inevitably. In a world where architecture is living and darkness abolishes sight, every abandoned space becomes ambiguous very quickly. Is it dead, sleeping, reverting, listening? Is a changed resonance only dryness, or root-thickening, or a hidden split opening through a load column? Has a walkway become unsafe, or merely unfamiliar? Has a district truly emptied, or have its tactile markers drifted enough that no one trusts themselves there anymore?

Someone must go and learn what the city has become in those places.

That would be me.

I would carry very little, but everything with intent: a wrapped bundle of tapping pins cut from hard mineral seam; a hooked knife for trimming not growth itself but dead snarls obscuring the speaking surfaces; thin gloves for reading bark strain; heavier resin-dark gloves for handling fertile tissues if unexpectedly found; coils of marker cord knotted in jurisdictional patterns; a stoppered pouch of camphor crust to clear the nose after moldless but confusing accumulations of old plant dust; a throat veil to reclaim moisture while I work; and, most importantly, a set of tuned knockers or clappers—small paired pieces whose tones are known to me intimately, so that I can strike a wall and hear not just “solid” or “hollow” but age, separation, bridged cavities, pulp-rot, root intrusion, old quarry scar under regrowth, chambers beyond chambers.

My work would seem half practical, half uncanny to others.

I would be sent to answer questions like:

Why has the north archive stair begun to answer in three pulses instead of two?

What has changed in the dead cistern quarter where sound now lingers too long?

Is the old pollen sink below the split-vine bridge truly swallowing noise, or is there a new opening beneath it?

Can the shuttered shrine of bare mineral be reopened safely for winter resonance rites?

Did anyone move through the abandoned tier gardens, or did a settling trunk merely imitate footfall for six nights?

In such a city, these are not superstitious questions. They are civic ones. But the people who ask them would still regard my trade with a little ritual caution, because I would spend long hours in places where texture markers have worn smooth, where the city’s intention has thinned, where one hand on a wall may encounter living discipline and the next handspan may meet dry anonymity.

I do not think I would be old, exactly, though my work would incline me toward oldness early. The role feels like one for a person whose patience matured faster than their status. Someone not central enough to become rigid, not young enough to mistake novelty for truth. My body would be narrow from moving through constrictions and husbanding water. My hands would be unusually articulate even for this people: fingertips slightly broadened, scars crossing the pads where thorn-ribs or silica flakes taught exactness. My hearing would not be magically superior, but trained into another order of literacy. I would hear the difference between a chamber made larger by loss and a chamber made larger by ripening. I would know when a creak has bark in it and when it has ancient mineral.

If I have eyes, I use them almost as one uses old heirloom tools from a prior age: with respect, little dependence, and a private sense that they once organized a different species of mind. In the less-dark vents I may perceive blur, gradation, perhaps a ghost of pale against black, but it would not be where meaning lives for me. Meaning would come as impact maps in the skull, pressure remembered through the wrist, heat traces across the face, the aftershape of a room rendered by return-time. My thoughts would likely not form as pictures even inwardly. They would arrive as arrangements of near and far, taut and slack, thirsty and burdened, open above, dangerous below, bark-sure, pith-uncertain.

Among my people I might be considered courteous to the point of strangeness, because I would greet not only persons and household walls but also disused structures before entering them. Not from theatrical piety. From method. In such places one announces oneself with a sequence of taps or a low throat-tone, waits, and listens to what answers. Sometimes the answer is only the room admitting itself. Sometimes a hanging cable stirs. Sometimes old pod curtains settle. Sometimes a dormant pressure bladder brightens its note. Sometimes another worker, hidden in black, replies in the sanctioned rhythm and saves you both a collision over a sink.

And because of this habit, those who know me would say I am a person who speaks well with things that no longer expect speech.

That feels right.

Socially, I would belong everywhere a little and nowhere completely. Useful in all districts, fully native to none. The central growers would tolerate me because I help determine which old support vaults can still bear grafting. The water accountants would consult me when humidity chambers start sounding wrong. Chord-keepers would value me because abandoned towers often become the best resonant instruments. Dust-wardens would curse and bless me in equal measure because I am always finding forgotten drifts that either threaten a path or preserve some ancient strain of seed-husk they hoped not to lose. Children would be half fascinated, half warned away from me. Not because I am dangerous, but because I return from underused places carrying altered air around me, that smell of sealed chambers, old dry interiors, historical dust.

Perhaps I would have a formal title, but I suspect what people actually call me would be simpler. Something like Far-Listener. Or Hollow Reader. Or, in the city’s own tactile idiom, a phrase that translates badly into visual languages: the one who places an answer into empty bark.

If there is any spirituality to me, it would center on the bare mineral exposures. Not in orthodox devotion, perhaps. More in relief. The living city is eloquent, responsive, full of obligations. Bare stone is older than conversation. When my work takes me to places where the plant architecture has thinned and the original body of the chamber emerges—smooth, cold, faintly bitter to the skin—I would rest my palms there longer than necessary. Not because the stone loves me back, but because it does not. It offers a scale beyond municipal intimacy. A reminder that our republic of maintenance is noble precisely because it is not guaranteed by the world beneath it.

As for temperament: I would be careful, but not severe. Solitary for practical reasons, not emotional ones. The sort of person who can move alone for hours in total dark and return more companionable, not less, because the city’s long tensions have spoken through me and I want, afterward, the ordinary comfort of known voices, shared dry meals, familiar resin scents. I think I would laugh softly and rarely, and when I did it would be because someone made an acoustically precise joke, the kind that depends on knowing how a certain chamber swallows pride or returns exaggeration enlarged.

I do not think I would have children, though not from disdain. My life would be too much spent at the civic margins, in places where one’s inward maps are always being revised by absence. But I would train apprentices eventually, because such knowledge cannot be entrusted to written record alone, perhaps cannot be written at all. How do you teach the difference between an old safe hollow and a young dangerous one? By bringing another hand to the wall beside yours. By having them strike, listen, strike again. By teaching them to notice what the chamber does to their own breathing. By saying: no, not empty—withdrawn. No, not stable—merely quiet. No, not dead wood—load-bearing silence.

This is important: I would not be outside the city’s tenderness. My work sounds austere, but it is really another form of care. The abandoned places are still part of the communal body. To read them is to prevent fear from spreading where knowledge should be, and to prevent carelessness where reverence should be. If a disused bridge can be made safe, then memory remains connected. If an old plaza must be surrendered, then it is surrendered knowingly, marked properly, allowed to dry into its next use—chimney, shrine, seed husk, warning throat in the dark. I would be one of those who help the city decide what can be retained, what can be relinquished, and what must simply be listened to longer before any hand cuts.

In another sense, more inwardly, I think I would partly resemble the city itself.

Reserved on the surface. Full of held spaces. More articulate when struck correctly.

Not ornamental. Not central. Structured by old pressures. Useful because I do not rush to fill emptiness with conclusions. In a place where haste is vandalism and certainty can kill a district, such a nature would be a social good.

So: where would I find myself?

In a narrow grown chamber between the maintained city and its patient forgetting, with a cord of tuned pods by my door and old drafts moving overhead.

Who would I be?

One of the republic’s margin-listeners: an echo-keeper, a reader of hollows, a citizen whose task is to enter the less-used dark, place both hands on the living wall, and learn whether it is asking for binding, cutting, warning, reopening—

or simply more time.
