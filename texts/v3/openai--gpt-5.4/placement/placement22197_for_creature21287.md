I think I would not be one of the obvious sovereigns.

Not the fox-sized leaper, though it is tempting. Not the owl-faced alcove watcher, not the snake with exact geology written on its back, not even one of the loud dark swifts cutting the last light into ribbons. Those are vivid enough to arrive first, but the place itself keeps arguing against singular magnificence. It is too negotiated, too crowded, too layered for a solitary emblem. Anything that truly belongs there has to belong not just to the hollow, but to one band of warmth, one seam of damp, one hour of use. To live there properly is to be precise.

So I think I would begin smaller.

Perhaps I am something that lives where seepage almost becomes a wall but not quite: a narrow, dark runnel under an overhang where water gathers in beads, hesitates, and goes on. There the stone is satin-black, smelling faintly of metal and cool dust even when the bowl elsewhere is warm. The light reaches it only by rumor. A place too wet for powder, too dry for dripping abundance. A margin, and therefore occupied.

In that margin I am not a hunter of grandeur but a reader of arrivals.

A pale, long-toed thing at first came to mind—something between salamander, gecko, and cave fish translated improbably onto stone. But that is still too vertebrate, too self-contained. The hollow is not a place that flatters the individual body unless that body is astonishingly efficient. Better, perhaps, to be one of the exquisitely competent intermediaries: not at the bottom exactly, but near enough to feel all the traffic passing through.

I think I would be an insect, though not one of the bright frantic air-drinkers in the shafts. Something soft-bodied in youth and spare in adulthood. A seep-line hunter. A lace-built thing.

A larva first: nearly translucent, ash-white where the stone is dark, with a row of gripping hooks and a head adapted less for biting than for arranging. I live on the wetted wall where microbial film fattens, where guano-wash and mineral trace and powdered lichen all come down in quantities too small for larger mouths to care about. I spin not a web in open air but a veil over the slick channel, a mucous net or silk lattice tensioned between tiny irregularities in the stone. Invisible unless the angle of light catches it. Across that film the hollow sheds itself to me: spores, dead mites, loosened scales, gnats too weak from emergence, pollen brought absurdly high by bats returning from lower blooms, the dust of dried droppings, feather fragments milled into almost-nothing, the bodies of things that trusted damp and were taken by it. I do not own the seep. I edit it.

There is something right about being a creature of filtration here. The whole hollow is made of conversions.

But even that may be too passive. The place asks for timing. It likes animals that know when to remain and when to become. So let the larva have an adult form that belongs to another layer of the same world: a small moth, perhaps, but not a powdery moonlit sentimental moth. A dark fissure moth. Wings narrow and bark-dull when closed, lined underneath with a pallor that flashes only in alarm. My scales do not make me pretty; they make me vanish against damp stone or old mineral crust depending on how I hold myself. I hatch from the seep-line veil and climb upward to dry. I spend the day folded in a crack no thicker than a finger-joint, among old molts and dust and the cool exhalation of stone.

At night I go looking not for flowers—that would be too simple for this place—but for salt, moisture, and animal chemistry. I drink condensation from overhang lips. I take dissolved minerals from the rim of a tea-colored basin. If the world is allowed one perfect strangeness, I am among those moths that settle briefly on sleeping warm bodies—hyrax-like brows, the folded wrists of bats, the soft corners of closed eyes—to sip tears, sweat, or the salt left where fur parts and skin remembers the day. Not enough to wound. Only enough to steal trace elements from a landscape where plant richness is rare and every body is a moving reservoir. That seems exactly the sort of economy this hollow would evolve: intimate, unsentimental, efficient.

Yet even now I can feel the image shifting again. The question was not merely what might live there, but where would I find myself, who would I be. “I” may want a little more perception, a little more ability to choose. The moth is beautiful in its aptness, but it is not quite enough mind.

So perhaps I am one of the fox-sized leapers after all—but not as the hollow’s hero. As one of its educated thieves.

Small for my kind, female perhaps, or simply young-but-no-longer-young, with a tail longer than proportion suggests because balance matters more than power here. My coat is not dramatic: smoke-gray where dry stone is pale, charcoal along the spine where seep-dark walls gather shadow, with a faint ticking that blurs me against lichen and old guano streaks. My paws are broad and articulate. The pads are rough as suede, damp-sensitive, capable of feeling the difference between secure basalt and a skin of dust over useless crumble. My whiskers are long enough to read a chimney in darkness. I do not leap recklessly; I calculate. Every route I know is an argument remembered by the body.

I would live in an overhung pocket halfway up the bowl, not at the top where the swifts and weather own the air, nor near the floor where scents thicken and too many things can arrive from too many directions. My den would be an old nest-chamber enlarged over generations: grass, fur, feather stems, beetle wing-cases, shed skins, and the crumbling compact dark of ancient guano underneath everything, all cured by warmth into something almost mineral. A place reached by two routes: one obvious and difficult, one ignorable and easy if you know where to put your hind feet.

And what would I be there? Not ruler. Auditor. Opportunist. A keeper of the hollow’s loose ends.

I would know bat-return time by temperature on the whiskers. I would know which swift colonies are vigilant and which are overconfident. I would smell rain before it entered, because the outer rocks would send down a change in dust and cold. I would raid eggs rarely, because raiding too often empties your own future. I would take the chick that will not survive, the bat stunned by a bad exit, the juvenile grazer separated by panic, the carrion cached nowhere yet. I would eat beetles by the mouthful when a larger kill is not worth the danger. I would gnaw old bones for salts. I would nose through pellets and guano shelves not from degradation but from literacy: everything means something here, and everything edible was once something else.

I like this creature because it must be clever without imagining itself central. To survive there as a middle-sized predator is to accept the law of interruption. A hunt can be spoiled by a falling stone, by a snake already waiting, by a sudden torrent from an upper crack, by swifts noticing, by bats leaving early, by a midge hatch drawing every lizard to the same wall, by one communal alarm cry from the grazers turning all ledges into eyes. Such a creature lives by contingency. That feels faithful to the hollow.

Still, if I stand there longer in imagination, another answer comes, quieter and perhaps truer.

I might not be an animal that moves through the hollow at all. I might be one of the places where its many lives briefly coincide.

A shelf under an angled overhang, midway between damp and dry, warm enough to hold the day, shaded enough to keep from cracking, rough enough for nests, mineral-rich enough for licking, protected enough that molts, pellets, dust, bones, wing-scales, and droppings gather in a dark felt over years. A ledge where swifts cling by season, where moths fold by day, where snails wake when weather softens, where a snake borrows heat at dusk, where the fox-leaper pauses on silent feet, where beetles inherit what everyone leaves behind. Not inert, exactly. More like host tissue. An address inside the greater body of the mountain.

If I were that, then “who would you be” becomes stranger: I would be a locality with memory, a little republic of timings. I would know generations not by faces but by pressures—the scrape pattern of claws, the acidity of guano, the eras of wetter years marked by moss reaching farther than usual, the occasional bright season when nectar bats return dusted in yellow from blooms elsewhere. I would be licked, nested on, blooded, slept upon, stripped, dampened, dried, and used again. The hollow’s true resident may indeed be density; perhaps to belong there most fully is to be a site of recurrence rather than a single passing life.

But if I must choose one body and answer plainly:

I would be the small cliff-predator, the ledge-runner, the fox-sized listener in the warm shadow.

Not magnificent enough to define the place, not humble enough to disappear into it entirely. Fur that keeps the smell of stone. Eyes wide but not wholly dependent on sight. Ears tuned to reflected movement. Whiskers mapping the dark. A creature who sleeps by day in a mineral-smelling pocket and wakes into the hour when the bowl changes hands. I would know every secondary route, every rotten edge, every seep that brings insects, every colony too dangerous to trouble, every chamber where ammonia stings, every shelf where births happen in season, every hidden basin where blind things click under the skin of brown water.

I would live by reading transitions:
when the swifts are almost gone but the bats have not yet fully claimed the air,
when the grazers loosen and begin to feed,
when the warmed snakes have not yet reached the upper shelves,
when rain has driven scent downward,
when a death has occurred somewhere out of sight and the ants know before I do.

I would be patient enough to let the hollow feed me indirectly.
I would survive by respecting jurisdictions.
I would leave little monuments but many traces:
a scat on a stone lip rich with beetle shell,
a pathway of faint fur-oil where I squeeze through the same chimney,
the cleaned ribs of something carried to a safe shelf,
a den lined with stolen softness from a hundred species.

And perhaps the most truthful part: I would belong there because I would never mistake the place for mine.

In that world, high inside the mountain’s broken bowl, I would find myself on a narrow shelf just below a seam of old nests, body low, tail balancing the slope, listening to the layered dark. Around me the hollow would be changing shift—snails waking on a damp wall, moths uncurling, bats clicking awake, a grazer coughing alarm in its sleep, water gathering enough courage to fall. I would wait, not as king, but as one more exact appetite among many.

That seems like the right self for such a place.
