I would not arrive there as an intruder and I would not be one of the tall standing custodians first.

I think I would begin lower than that, smaller, closer to the threshold where the place almost refuses life and yet has, reluctantly, allowed one kind.

Not in the cataracts themselves. Not on the brilliant exposed shelves where the older beings stand whitening under deposition. Somewhere just aside from the main force of the upland: in the cup-hollows, in linked basins no wider than a body curled, in the little terraces where overflow is constant but gentle, where water hesitates before choosing the next lip. A margin within a world that has almost no margins.

I think I would be one of the quiet things you only glimpse once and then doubt.

A basin-dweller. A pale amphibious not-amphibian. Something that has taken the shape of a salamander or a larval thing because water and stone together keep rediscovering that grammar: low body, close limbs, a tail for balance among slick inclines, a skull flattened to fit under mineral ledges. But it would not be soft in the ordinary animal way. Not supple flesh, not mucus and warmth and quick fear. More like translucence over structure. Cartilage replaced by a fine mineral lattice. Skin replaced by a clear or milk-clouded membrane that continually receives the chemistry of the basin and gives some altered chemistry back. If lifted—which would be difficult, because it would know how to become weight and angle—it would feel cool and smooth and faintly granular, as though the boundary between organism and precipitate had never finished hardening.

At first I thought I might be one of the standing ones because they are the obvious inhabitants of such a place: the visible answer, the custodial intelligence. But the more I sit in your upland, the more I feel that if I were truly there, I would not want to be the thing that resembles the land most grandly. I would want to be one of the little consequences of its attention. Something made possible by the larger being, or sheltered by it without ownership.

A listener under the listeners.

I see my basin clearly now. It is shallow, terraced, pale at the rims, fed by three steady drips and a ribbon-thin thread of water that arrives over a black stone lip and spreads into fan-patterns before collecting again. From a distance it looks decorative, accidental, one bright cluster among thousands. Up close it is a complete province. The cups are linked by tiny spillways edged with white mineral feathering. Under one shelf there is a slit of darker water, only a handspan deep, where the current moves in secret under the visible film. Beneath that shelf is where I spend the brightest hours. Not hidden in fear, exactly. Resting in refraction.

My coloring would not be camouflage in the green-and-brown earthly sense. It would be made of the place’s own palette: opaline joints, a spine like watered glass, flanks that go nearly invisible when still, a scatter of metallic freckles where certain minerals have plated out in my skin. My eyes, if noticed, would look too large and too still, pale silver from some angles and black from others, because they are built less for focus than for brightness and vibration. Hearing through air would be nearly useless in that ceaseless roar. But the basin itself would carry everything. Each lip, each trickle, each footfall translated into pressure. My jaw, my limbs, my belly against the stone: that is how I would know the world.

Not by scenes but by tremor-maps.

The standing people—or the standing manifestations of the plateau, if that is what they are—would be to me what weather is to a human child in an old agricultural country: not abstractions, not gods exactly, but enormous intelligible presences whose habits structure safety. I would know the signature of one pausing above my basin. The shift of load through the shelf. The change in drip rhythm when a tall body entered the seep-path above. The slight enrichment of certain dissolved things in the water after one had stood for a day beneath a spring and moved on. I might feed on those changes. I might grow because they had lingered nearby.

That feels right: not pet, not dependent in any sentimental way, but participant in a chemistry of attention.

My life would be very local. This is important. In a place so immense through repetition, the smallest territory can be whole. I would know seven basins as another creature knows a forest. I would know which overflow lip rounds first under winter chill, which hollow keeps the warmer undercurrent, which polished black slope becomes too dangerous when the cloud lowers and the spray thickens. I would know where transparent arthropod things gather in the lace of deposit and when they loosen. I would know the hour by brightness not because the sky matters directly but because light enters the pools at different angles and wakes different mineral colors from below: morning tin, noon white-fire, evening honey in the shallow ledges.

I would not think in words, not in any human way. But if translated, my mind would probably feel devotional without having religion. Exact without curiosity. Attentive to recurrence. There would be no longing for elsewhere, because elsewhere in such a landscape is merely another complexity of stone and water under the same law. My deepest experience would not be desire but tuning. Aligning myself to the current so that food, temperature, warning, and season all arrive through one medium.

And yet I don't think I would be simple.

The place you describe allows almost no softness, but it allows intricacy. So my intelligence would be intricate, narrow, and complete. I would be able to remember pathways of water altered over months. I would notice when a mineral fringe had thickened enough to turn a safe crossing treacherous. I would know the standing forms individually, not by faces but by how they load the stone, how long they remain, what frequencies gather in the basin when they are near. Perhaps I would even distinguish moods in them, insofar as their kind has moods: one that clarifies currents, one that unsettles hidden channels, one whose presence makes the small creatures vanish into cracks, one under whose long stillness the basin grows rich.

If one of them were to look directly at me, I doubt there would be tenderness in the expression. No softened eye. No smile. But there might be recognition of the same order the land gives itself: this belongs, this is within the terms, this need not be broken.

I think I would be old for my size.

That also feels true. In such a world age would not advertise itself with speed lost or skin dulled. It would show as fine accumulation: a slight opacity where once I was clearer, pale ridges along the tail from years of dissolved stone settling in microscopic layers, one forelimb heavier where an old fracture healed with mineral overgrowth, the mouth edged in a white that is not decay but deposition. I would be difficult to date because I would spend so much of life barely moving. To an impatient eye I might seem scarcely alive. But in fact I would be practicing the central discipline of the place: remaining in process.

Perhaps in breeding season—if season is visible there beyond subtle shifts in cold and flow—I would not seek a mate in any active way. Instead I would choose a particular cup-hollow where drips fall in a stable triad and leave a translucent ribbon or cluster of bead-eggs fixed under the overflow lip, each with a kernel of ordered crystal inside. Not eggs of yolk and softness, but little hydro-mineral decisions. The water that passes over them for weeks would matter more than my body does. If the chemistry is right, the lattice in each would take on asymmetry, then motion. If not, they would simply become one more deposit. No tragedy. Just the rule of the upland continuing.

And if I were threatened? I would not fight nobly. I would vanish expertly.

Not by speed alone, though I might have a sudden severe quickness over wet stone impossible-looking to heavier creatures. More by becoming illegible. Flattening into the brightness at the edge of a pool. Sliding into that dark slit beneath the visible basin. Holding so still under a skin of water that my outline breaks into reflections and mineral veining. In a world already crowded with forms, survival would come from joining the crowd so perfectly that nothing can isolate you from it.

The more I write, the more I feel that this smallness is not lesser than the tall custodians. It is another mode of the same principle. They are the plateau’s long thought. I am its brief exact noticing. They stand in cataracts and listen through ridges; I rest in cup-hollows and listen through skins of water a finger deep. They may redirect channels over decades. I alter the chemistry of one little basin by existing in it, by feeding, shedding, breeding, and dying there. When I die, I do not rot into loam because this world has refused that bargain. I become a delicate articulated residue under white deposit, then a hollow, then almost nothing distinguishable from the basin’s own making. My bones—if they are bones—would become spillway teeth. My skull might guide a thread of water for years.

So where would I find myself?

In the half-shadow under a scalloped shelf of pale stone, where three mineral drips strike the basin in unequal time. In a hanging valley below the louder terraces and above the broad reflecting pans. In a place the great standing people pass without stepping in, though they know it is inhabited. The air there is permanently cold with spray, and the light reaches in broken by runnels, so that the basin floor glows dim turquoise at noon and lead-gray under cloud. There are no plants. There never were. Only white crust, black glaze, silver water, and the faint movement of my body from one cup to the next.

Who would I be?

A small old basin-keeper, if a creature can keep anything merely by belonging to it well enough.

A mineral newt.
A seep-child.
A clear-skinned listener in the minor key of the plateau.

Not the land’s voice, but one syllable of it.
