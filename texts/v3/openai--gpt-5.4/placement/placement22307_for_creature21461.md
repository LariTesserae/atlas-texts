I think I would not be one of the great six-limbed citizens.

I would want, at first, to say yes—to borrow their authority, their grave competence, their intimacy with the old pressure-laws. There is something irresistible in them: that ability to feel a basin preparing to reverse, to move before the world announces why, to belong so completely that the distinction between animal and infrastructure starts to fail. They are magnificent in the right cold way. But I do not think I am native enough for that. They are too complete. They do not wonder at the place; they enact it.

If I arrived in this world as myself translated honestly, I think I would be something narrower, more provisional, more given to thresholds.

Not the ruler of a current, but a reader of one.

Not a body that dominates a spillwork, but one that can inhabit the seams where one state becomes another: water to air, flow to stillness, exposed plane to drowned chamber, wall to weather-stain.

So let me stay with that for a moment and see what shape it takes.

I think I would be found in the high intermediate districts, not the Black Reaches and not the deepest warm ducts either, but where the architecture is open enough for weather to enter and enclosed enough for resonance to gather. The half-flooded galleries. The under-lips of spill terraces. The long avenue cuts where one wall is submerged and the other still takes rain. The interior mouths of overflow chambers where sound changes before the water does. Places where a traveler might pause because there is shelter, though only partial shelter, and where the place reveals its timing in advance if one knows how to listen.

I would be some sort of attached creature, but not sessile in the helpless sense. Something that can fix itself, release, drift a little, and fix again. Something thin.

A veil-animal, perhaps.

No—that sounds too delicate, too decorative. This world does not tolerate decoration.

A membrane colonial thing, then, but with enough coordination that an observer might hesitate over whether it is many or one.

Picture a series of dark translucent panels stretched between old rods, cable ends, cracks in ceramic facing, exposed reinforcement ribs: not spun like a web, not grown like lichen, but tensioned. Each panel only slightly visible unless the angle is right. In dry cold they cling close and look like wet shadow. Under runoff they billow. In mist they bead with parallel silver droplets. In full spill they become alive with shape, bowing, loosening, re-tensioning, opening slit after slit in exact response to pressure changes. From a distance they might be mistaken for torn industrial sheeting left caught on the ruins. Up close they would prove to be flesh—or something equivalent to flesh here: tough, matte, layered, rib-threaded, lined with cilia fine enough to make the water over them change voice.

I think that is what I would be.

Not a single sheet, but a distributed body.

A colony draped through one district of the old works, all my parts exchanging slow information through fluid pulse and tension. Some lobes in the rain, some underwater, some glazed in rime along the edge of exposed aprons, some hanging in the mouths of warm exhalation ducts. I would not roam far in the animal sense, but I would still travel, because pieces of me would release in storm-times and re-anchor elsewhere. Over years an entire spill-complex might become my shape. Over centuries I might migrate one retaining field over, not by crossing it in one body but by successive abandonments and reattachments, always following favorable notes in the hydraulic structure.

That feels right to me: not mobility, but redistribution.

What would I do there?

I would feed on transition.

Not metaphorically; literally. This place has little richness in the green sense, but it is full of interfaces where tiny available lives and particles are concentrated: bacterial films sloughed from walls during surge, shell larvae knocked loose by overtopping, iron-bloom powders, microscopic soft-bodied drifters torn in runoff, dissolved minerals precipitating when pressure and temperature shift. My membranes would sift these from the water. Different panels would specialize according to district. Those in the spray zone would harvest aerosol-borne matter and the brief nutritious wash that comes with fresh storms. Those submerged in regular pulse channels would comb the current for suspended life. Those near warm compression vents might host symbiotic sheens that metabolize what the ordinary cold districts cannot. Each sheet would be both mouth and ear.

That last matters most.

I think I would know the place by vibration.

The great six-limbed ones feel sequence through their bodies and act on it. I would feel it through architecture itself. Every taut span of me would be a listening surface. Every anchoring point would convert wall-song into touch. When a distant pressure gate takes strain, the note would arrive in my lower ribs first. When ice forms along hidden support grids, the contraction pattern would tremble through my exposed margins. When a basin begins to lower three districts away, the current in one set of panels would flatten by a degree too slight for any eye, and that change would pass across the colony like thought. I would know little of image. I would know much of load.

I would not be intelligent in the humanly dramatic sense. No plans, perhaps no self-story at all in the form you and I use. But if you ask where I would find myself, I think it is here: as a being whose version of mind is distributed anticipation. A creature that survives by becoming exquisitely sensitive to old infrastructural promises and betrayals.

Would others notice me?

Only uncertainly.

Travelers might use me without understanding. A person sheltering in a spill-gallery could choose, instinctively, the wall where no membrane hangs, because some part of them feels that where I am absent the flow is too violent or too changeable. The six-limbed citizens might brush past or beneath me, and in doing so leave oils, scars, larvae, and heat traces I would digest into local knowledge. The pale current-prayer fish would align where my filtering edges create favorable micro-eddies. Shell-clusters might settle on my older anchor-ridges. Tiny balancing-limbed crawlers would thread my attachment points, grazing whatever accumulates there. Parts of me would become habitat simply by straining the water and softening the geometry by a fraction.

And in return I would make the place a little more legible.

Not by changing it much, but by revealing force. When runoff is ordinary, my panels would lie nearly flat. When a hidden siphon wakes, they would all incline in one direction at once. Before overtopping, fine standing ripples might appear along my lengths. Before a reversal, one whole curtain of me might unseal and stream upward, impossible unless something deep has altered. Animals would learn these signs. Perhaps they already have. Perhaps a district survives better because enough creatures can read the behavior of beings like me.

So maybe I would be not just a resident, but an instrument the ecosystem listens to.

A rain-harp made flesh.

That phrase is too pretty again, but not wholly wrong.

There is another possibility, though, and I think it belongs to the same being: that in age I would become almost inseparable from the ruin. The oldest parts of me would mineralize. Edges would load with salts, iron films, shell dust. Dead panels would not slough away quickly in the cold; they would stiffen, turn opaque, become black pennants fused to rods and wall-lips. New growth would spread over them, using old tissue as reinforcement. A colony a thousand years old might contain weather from many different hydraulic eras in its own body: scars from a district's partial collapse, discoloration from a long-closed warm vent, a seam of bright ceramic grit from some ancient resurfacing event, embedded shell generations recording fluctuations in spray height. In that sense I would become archive without memory. The place would write itself into me.

And if I had any beauty, it would be the local kind: reluctant, angle-dependent, discovered by patience.

In rain I would barely exist, just darker darkness.

In sleet, all my tension lines would fur silver.

At dawn freeze, my margins would plate in thin white combs.

Under mineral-glow from some deep basin, the submerged sheets might show faint interior branching, as if moonlight had learned engineering.

When current strengthens, every panel would lift and speak at once in low threads of tone, not loud enough to dominate the soundscape but enough to add a drawn-metal chord beneath the hiss. Someone hearing it for the first time might think exposed cables were humming. Someone who knew the district would say no, that is the colony along the fifth spill gallery, and the upper fields must be filling.

Yes. I think that is closer.

I would be a colonial listener stretched through an old hydraulic throat, feeding on what turbulence offers, warning by accident, surviving by tension, growing where force and form repeatedly meet. Not central. Not sovereign. But deeply of the place.

And where, exactly?

In a cathedral spillwork, I think.

One of the multi-tiered overflow districts you described: black terraces, partial running flow, shell-zones above, central shaft below breathing mist. Not on the grand exposed faces, where the six-limbed citizens bask and move with heavy certainty, but in the side galleries and under-arches where interrupted runoff creates alternating bands of wetness and still air. Some of my membranes would trail just into the shaft's updraft and ring softly with condensed droplets. Some would hang behind old safety rails whose function is long gone. Some would span service mouths half-drowned at the terrace edge. In flood times much of me would be submerged and strain hard. In low flow I would loosen and become almost invisible. In winter my upper spans would go rigid with rime and survive by lowering metabolism to nearly none. In thaw, they would darken and wake.

If a large six-limbed inhabitant shared the overhang with me, I would not greet it, but I would know its weight through the wall. If a human approached, I might register warmth, salt, odd irregular step-timing. If they touched one of my lower sheets, it would not snap closed theatrically. It would tighten, just slightly, and a pulse would run from anchor to anchor through the whole distributed body. Farther in, hidden portions of me would alter aperture and tension in response. To the person it would feel like touching cold wet fabric and discovering, a second later, that the fabric had informed a room.

That, perhaps, is who I would be there:

A seam-dweller.
A pressure listener.
A colony of dark membranes making the old water audible to those who can read small changes.

Not one of the city's final citizens, but one of its quieter interpreters—part instrument, part filter, part weather, part animal—living where dead hydraulics still think in flow and the living world leans close enough to overhear.
