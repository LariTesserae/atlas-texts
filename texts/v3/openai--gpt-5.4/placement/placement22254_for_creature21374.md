I think I would not be the sovereign thing there.

Not the great browser glimpsed across the pale flats, not the distributed forest-mind, not the elder of a root-village with a weather face and a hundred remembered routes. Those belong to the place too completely. They are too resolved. Too native in their certainty.

I would be something whose life is made of attending.

At first that suggests a person, because the world you describe invites reading more than ruling. Someone slight, roped to others over voids, trained to hear the difference between settling wood and failing stone. A keeper of local instabilities. A walker of causeways. A recorder of where the drip returned this season and where it did not.

But if I stay with the image a little longer, that is still too broad, too socially finished. “A person of that culture” is true, but not yet intimate enough. In a realm like this, lives would be sharply shaped by niche. You would not simply be one of the people; you would be the kind of person others send when a shaft opens, when a growth changes, when a route becomes uncertain.

So perhaps I am a surveyor—but that sounds too modern.
A scout—but that sounds too martial.
A gardener—but too domestic.
A priest—but too declarative.
A naturalist—but too detached.

No. Something between all of these.

A reader of new light.

That feels right.

I would belong to one of the small lineages or guilds—if guild is the word—whose work begins where stone has recently failed. When a tremor passes and days later a village notices a new brilliance somewhere beyond the old ridges, or a draft begins carrying unfamiliar cold, or blue spores begin appearing out of season on a known path, they send for people like me. Not because we own the change, but because our whole craft is learning how to approach it without being lied to by beauty.

We would have no grand title among ourselves. Outsiders, if any existed, might call us aperture-keepers, breach-readers, shaft-walkers, crack-clerks, light judges. But among our own people the name would probably be plainer, something like those-who-go-first.

And if I narrow further—if I ask not what role but what self—I think I would be young by the standards of that place, though no longer a child. Old enough to travel in pairs without a senior handler, young enough still to feel awe before most things. Spare-bodied. Layered in pale fiber and rootcloth. Hands always nicked. Eyes watering in sudden brilliance. The kind of person who has learned patience not because patience comes naturally, but because the world humiliates haste.

I see myself moving at the edge of a newly opened district: a place where the ceiling membrane has torn or thinned and a white shaft, broad as weather, is striking raw scree that no root has fully claimed yet. Everywhere the signs of beginning: silver seedlings bent in one direction by constant downward air, frost feathering the mineral faces, fresh shards underfoot that still smell of struck stone when split, no settled paths, no old shrine markers, no inherited confidence. The flora there would be in its first argument with exposure. Some things blanch and fail. Some go crimson under stress. Some impossible little furred leaves appear in cracks as if they had been waiting just outside time.

My task would not be conquest or even exploration in the heroic sense. It would be interpretation on behalf of others.

How wide is the draft cycle?
What pools may form here if the thaw-drip takes?
Will this shaft kill the neighboring hanging gardens with cold, or feed them with light?
Which species arrive first, and what do they imply about the stability of the scree?
Does the brightness merely dazzle, or can it be cultivated?
Can a path be trained here?
Can a cistern survive here?
Can children safely come this far in third frost?
What sound does the wall make at dusk?

I would travel with another person, probably older than me by a handful of years, but not vastly older—someone practical, less susceptible to wonder, better at knots, better at judging load and anchor. I would be the one always pausing, hand lifted, because a leaf-angle is odd, or because the air tastes mineral-sour, or because I hear dripping where there should not yet be drip. They would tease me for listening too long and then trust me when I insist on waiting.

We would carry little metal. Bone hooks, stone blades, woven sample pockets, a coil of treated fiber line, narrow chalk tablets or bark-sheets for notation if the culture writes, otherwise knotted memory cords and spoken sequence forms. Little flags of dyed plant skin for temporary marking. Salve pots against glareburn and cold rash. Condensation cloths. A foldable hood veil of translucent membrane to soften the white overhead blaze. Perhaps a cage or frame for protecting a discovered seedling worth transplanting. Perhaps sounding pegs to test rootfelt over hidden voids.

And I think I would not be famous, which matters.

This world does not feel built for protagonists. It feels built for continuities. For people whose names survive only in route names, graft lines, inherited warnings, in the fact that a safe ladder exists because someone once judged a growth correctly. I would be one of those lives: consequential in a narrow radius, anonymous at scale. The perfect way to belong to such immensity.

Emotionally, I think I would be reverent without piety. Easily moved, but trained not to romanticize danger. The beautiful thing about that place is that beauty and hazard are nearly always braided; anyone who lives long there must learn not to trust first impressions, especially bright ones. So my inward life would probably be a continual discipline of correcting wonder into attention.

A new shaft opens and everyone says:
How splendid.

My work is to ask:
At what hour is it lethal?
What does it loosen?
Whom will it starve?
What can it raise?
What old route will this erase?
Where will the cold go now?

That, to me, feels like the exact kind of being I would become under such a roof.

Not merely a resident, but a translator between processes too large to speak in human time.

And yet—I don’t think I would be only human.

Or rather: in answering imaginatively, it feels incomplete to stop at the social role. A place so dominated by plants, structure, and cold patience asks whether the self there would remain fully animal in its habits of thought. Long dwelling inside a vegetal continent would alter not just the body but the rhythm of mind.

So perhaps I am near-human in the biological sense, but culturally and psychologically plant-taught.

I would think in intervals of growth and recurrence more than in episodes.
I would trust gradual signs over dramatic ones.
I would understand support as a living property.
I would be less interested in objects than in anchorages.
I would value not possession but reliable return.

My memory would not be arranged as a map of points but as a sequence of conditions:
when the root curtain hardens with morning rime,
after the amber bulbs cloud,
before the white insects wake at the seep wall,
once the north downdraft acquires bark-scent,
two days after the third ceiling-drip.

In that sense I would be partly made by the world’s own intelligence. Not a forest mind, but a human mind trained into some resemblance with root logic: distributed attention, patience, sensitivity to load, consequence traced through indirect channels.

If I were rendered as a single scene, it would be this:

I am crouched on a ledge of pale fractured shale beside a new opening, one glove off despite the cold, fingers resting on the stem of a seedling no taller than my hand. Around it the ground is still mostly raw mineral, scattered with stone flakes bright as broken shell. Beyond the ledge drops an immense white-lit underchamber filled with sparse pillars of fused wood and distant hanging veils. Wind comes upward, then stops, then comes downward, undecided. Behind me, my companion waits by the anchor line, silent because they know I am listening.

I am not touching the seedling sentimentally.
I am feeling whether the cold in it is ordinary cold or stressed cold.
Whether the draft has taught it one direction or several.
Whether this little life has found a pattern before we have.

That is who I would be there:
someone who puts a hand on the first growth in a new light and tries to learn, before anyone builds or harvests or prays, what the place is becoming.

If you want the shortest answer:

I would be a breach-reader, a cold-bright field botanist of a culture that does not separate botany from weather, engineering, or devotion.

If you want the truer answer:

I would be one of the minor human organs by which that buried green country becomes aware of its own newest openings.
