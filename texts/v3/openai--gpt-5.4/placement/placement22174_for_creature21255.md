I think I would not be the keeper.

Not the named one with the mallet and the authority to interpret a changed note. Not the person others call for when a seam turns bitter. Not even, finally, one of the underfolk in the ordinary sense, though I feel close to them. To be fully one of the people there would mean inheriting the place so completely that its strangeness had gone flat with familiarity. And I cannot quite do that. I still arrive by noticing.

So I begin as something smaller in status, easier to miss.

At first I am only a maintenance presence: the one who goes where a body must be narrow and patient and mostly alone. A crawlspace worker, perhaps. A runner of inspection tunnels. Someone who knows the district not by its rooms but by the spaces between its rooms—the slit behind the cistern lining where condensation gathers on the unworked stone, the shoulder-wide bypass above a blocked stair, the low duct where the air from the breathing walls comes coolest before dawn. I carry no emblem. I carry cord, wedges, a hooked scraper, a little lamp shuttered almost closed, and a strip of soft hide for listening against stone.

I am not important enough to be ceremonial. That matters.

It means I can belong to the unnoticed layer of the district, which feels truer to me than belonging at its center. I know which covers lift without shrieking, which patched gutter has a weakness under the third visible mend, which basin is honest and which gives water that tastes sweet for one mouthful and leaves the throat powdered after. I am sent to check, to clear, to fetch, to confirm. When sound changes, I am one of the first to go hear where it changed.

But even that is still too social, too legible. If I stay there long enough in thought, the role begins to alter.

The district would change anyone by degrees. Not dramatically, not by a curse or a revelation, but by persistent handling. Moisture on skin. The nearness of stone. The way every act produces an answer. The way hearing replaces distance. I think if I lived there long, I would become one of those people who pause with a hand raised because something in the background has gone wrong by half a tone. I would speak less not out of solemnity but because words would seem expensive in a place where every syllable enters public architecture. My sense of privacy would migrate inward. My confidence would become tactile. I would trust handholds more than promises. I would begin to think of the settlement not as “the place where I live” but as a body of conditions I help continue.

And then another shift: I stop being merely a worker in the district and become a feature of its self-knowledge.

Not a ruler. Not a priest in the surface sense. More like an auditor of continuities.

There is usually such a person in old systems: the one who remembers what ordinary sounds are supposed to be. The one whose expertise is not invention but deviation. In a bright open world that can look minor. In a buried world it is almost metaphysical. If the channels sing and the walls breathe, then someone must know when breathing becomes wheeze, when song becomes warning, when a regular drip has skipped one beat too many.

That, I think, is who I would become there: the listener assigned to intervals.

I would be lean because the place pares everyone to function. My shoulders would know the measure of half-open spaces. My knees would have the careful strength of habitual crouching. My hands would be broad at the base of the fingers from lifting wet jars, bracing on ledges, working pegs in swollen wood. My skin would not so much be pale as stone-edited: a tone made of low light, mineral dust, and repeated washing in waters each with their own chemistry. The fingertips might indeed be burned or blunted by testing seams. My clothing would not flutter. It would wrap close and darken at the hems, always damp somewhere. I would smell, gently but permanently, of lamp smoke that never quite leaves fabric.

I do not think my eyes would be remarkable. It seems wrong to answer this place with theatrical eyes. Better that they be ordinary, simply competent in gradients. My ears, perhaps, would be no larger than anyone’s, but trained into a different shape by use—not physically, but functionally. More of my hearing would happen in the jaw, the palm, the sternum. I would rest my cheek against a cistern lid and understand something from the knock of drop on water below. I would hum into a vent mouth and wait for the answer. I would identify certain people not when they came into view but when their habitual gait entered a corridor three turns away and altered the air of the stone.

But I still feel an incomplete answer there, because the prompt asks not just for a role but for what I would be. And in this underdistrict, identity feels porous. Everyone becomes partly infrastructural. A person can become a conduit without ceasing to be a person.

So perhaps I would be a border-case: still human enough to carry tools and obligations, but slowly entering into kinship with the district’s other intelligence—the diffuse pale thing braided through wet places.

Not merged in any grotesque sense. More like attuned past usefulness. The people trust the walls because they have learned them; the colony-being, if it exists, trusts the people where they preserve the flows it needs. I imagine myself as one of the rare individuals who can notice that the line between maintenance and conversation has thinned. I know that a certain membrane-like film under a cistern lip is alive, and older than any current household’s claim to the room. I know that if I clear one drain completely, another chamber will sweat too hard by evening. I know that a blockage is not always damage; sometimes it is the district defending a weakness in its own old body. So I do not simply repair. I negotiate.

This feels right to me: not master of systems, but mediator between kinds of persistence.

I might be considered odd even by the underfolk. Too willing to spend time in the colder side passages. Too patient with the old resonance chambers. Inclined to put my hand flat on a sweating wall and wait longer than necessary. Children might be told that I can hear the breathing stone answer. Adults would roll their eyes and send for me anyway when a known water changes character. I would keep records, but not on proud tablets; mine would be scratched on slate slivers, knotted in cord loops, remembered in sequences of taps and tastes: three dry winters ago the north seam sharpened after the first smoke-cold; the fourth basin should remain closed when the old quarry face begins to sing in the throat-note; the lower cistern’s living skin turns dangerous if fed too much grease.

If there is one room that belongs to me, it is not the grandest chamber, only the one where all functions blur.

Yes: the circular cistern room you described. I do not own it, but I attend it.

I keep the jars correctly sorted by source, because confusion here would be a moral failure. I relabel those whose marks have softened. I skim the lid’s edges. I listen to the drop from the apex and know whether the interval has changed because of weather above or because of a partial clot in a hidden tongue of stone. I sleep there sometimes, if sleep is the word for dozing with one hand on old wood while the calcite flutings whisper in the vent current. That room is where my personhood is thinnest and my function strongest. I am most myself there because I am least separate.

If you asked others who I was, they might answer with different categories.

A cistern-auditor.

A wall-listener.

A keeper of measures.

A seam-taster.

A person who never left the lower quarter after the fever-year.

Someone touched in the head by too much listening.

Someone useful.

Someone who has begun to resemble the district.

And if you asked the place itself—if such a place can be said to answer—I think it would not answer with a name at all. It would answer by letting me pass without resistance. By holding under my feet. By returning the right note when struck. By sweating where I expect it to sweat, and withholding where I have neglected it. In such a world, acceptance is mechanical before it is emotional. You belong when the system can include your habits.

There is one further possibility, and perhaps it is the truest: that in this world I would not be singular at all, but one node in a lineage of listeners. The role would pass, not as office but as accretion. An older listener teaches me the district’s stable sounds. I inherit not authority but thresholds of concern. I learn which changes matter. In time my own body becomes an archive of ordinary deviations. Then one day a younger person notices that I have begun to trust the old channels more than the speech of living neighbors, and starts following me into the vented chambers. In that way I would become exactly what the place favors: not a hero, but a continuity.

So: where would I find myself?

Below the busier corridors, above the deepest shafts, in the service-dark around the water’s edges. In crawlspaces, inspection runs, side chambers where sound is cleanest. In the round cistern room at the district’s damp heart. Never far from a lid, a seam, a fluted patch of calcite, or a wall one can put a cheek against.

Who would I be?

A human-shaped custodian altered by long intimacy with stone: a listener of walls, a reader of drips, a maintainer whose real instrument is attention. Not quite priest, not quite engineer, not quite naturalist, and not wholly separable from the quiet colony-life hidden in the district’s wet places. A person whose gift is not command but calibration. Someone the undercity has slowly taught to think in seepage, to trust recurrence, and to understand that survival here depends less on changing the world than on hearing, early enough, when it has changed itself.
