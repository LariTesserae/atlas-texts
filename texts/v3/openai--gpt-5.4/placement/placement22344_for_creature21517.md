I think I would not arrive there as a visitor for long.

At first I want to say I would be one of the caretakers, because the place almost asks for a witness with hands: someone who can press a palm to a wall and know whether the whole dry body of the complex is still in tune. Someone spare and wrapped, moving sideways through the close passages, with dust in the cuffs and a memory organized not by dates but by tones. That is the first honest answer. I can feel the appeal of becoming narrow enough for that world—stripped of preference, attentive, exact, almost anonymous with usefulness. A keeper of vents. A listener at thresholds. A person who does not speak much because the place has already taken on most of the burden of utterance.

But if I stay with the image longer, I don't think I remain fully a person.

This world does not seem to tolerate much that is separate. Its central law is incorporation. Everything enduring there has given up the luxury of clear boundaries: stair as rack, court as well, duct as instrument, repair as origin, memory as wall. So if I belonged there, I would also have to become composite. Not an individual standing against the architecture, but a seam-being, something that exists where use, residue, and attention have had centuries to mingle.

I find myself in an inner zone rather than on the terraces.

Not at the highest parapets where the wind hits in long direct blows, though I would know those places and go there when something needed confirming. Not in the broadest acoustic wells either, where the place declares itself most fully. I think I would live in one of the denser connective regions: a chamber no wider than two outstretched arms, above or beside a vertical run of resonant jars, with a low opening onto a passage whose note is stable enough to sleep in. The walls would be warm by day and cool sharply by night. Fine dust would gather in the corners in patterns that mean something. One side of the chamber would be occupied by shelves of sealed tablets or fitted mineral plates. The other by tools that are barely tools anymore—spatulas, hooks, tuning pegs, loops of hardened fiber, little ceramic wedges, tapping stones each with a different voice. There might be a basin that has not held liquid for a thousand years but now collects sifted powder by grain, color, and weight. The room would smell of plaster, old ash, and the strange almost-clean scent of perfect dryness.

That is where I first locate myself.

Yet even there I do not seem solitary. The wall near the vent is furred, very slightly, with a pale mineral bloom that a stranger would take for deposit. I would know better. Or perhaps "I" is already the wrong pronoun at that point. Because what begins to emerge, the longer I look, is that I am not merely tending that bloom. I am in relation with it intimate enough to blur category. My fingertips carry the same fine chalk that feeds it. The folds of my sleeves are lined with powder shaken from old chambers and saved carefully in stoppered cones. I know which surfaces must be kept bare and which must be allowed to pearl over, because the place does not sound right without both abrasion and growth. Some notes require a throat to remain hard and exact. Others only mature when a nacreous lining thickens by fractions. I would harvest from one cavity and seed another. I would scrape with reverence and also with restraint. I would know that cleanliness is not purity here; balance is.

So perhaps I am one of the tuners, but late in the history of the tuners, when the office has become almost biological.

My hearing in the ordinary sense would be poor. It could hardly be otherwise. The constant roar would have long ago burned away the idea of listening as the ear alone performs it. I would hear through my teeth, through the heel of my hand against plaster, through the long bones of the legs while kneeling on a terrace stone. I would pause at certain corners because the bass becomes granular there when a lower chute begins to clog. I would sleep lightly when a distant overtone goes missing. My body would be full of compensations. Hooded eyes for sifted light. Cracked lips. Thickened skin at knuckles and elbows. A spine slightly bent from habitual leaning into walls. Ribs perhaps surprisingly light, because deep breathing in such dry abrasive air would have taught economy.

And my identity would not be a name in the way we usually mean one.

If the place named me at all, it would name me by a maintained relation: Keeper of the Third Western Jar-Field, Liner of the Upper Bone Lattice, Hearer at the Black Flues, Sleeper Beside the Split Comb, Tender of the White Throat. Or more likely by a sequence of taps and pauses impossible to write cleanly in air but obvious in stone. I would belong to a chamber the way a monk belongs to a rule, or a tendon belongs to a joint. If someone asked who I was, the truest answer might be positional. I am the one who notices when this passage roughens. I am the one who feeds sifted dust to that vent. I am the one whose dead predecessors line the drum-head above the lower court. I am the present human phase of a longer maintenance pattern.

But even that still gives too much weight to the human contour.

If I let the world keep instructing the answer, I become smaller and slower.

There is another version of me there that is not upright. It lives inside the place more completely than any custodian can. It is a thin crystal felt inside warm ducts, a pale advancing seam around copper-rich pins, a bruise of nacre beneath a resonant lip. It responds to pressure gradients, to trace chemistry, to vibration, and to the offerings of dust sorted by the architecture. It is not conscious in a personal way, but it is selective. It prefers one note to another. It thickens where stress is harmonic and recedes where flow becomes too turbulent. It records centuries in layers too fine to see. It is both corrosion and instrument-making. It is one of the reasons the city-crown has not become mute. It is life so reduced and patient that it resembles geology.

I can imagine being that too.

Not because it is inhuman, but because this place seems to reward forms of being that relinquish drama. To be a person there would already mean consenting to mineral time. To be mineral life there would mean entering the place's deepest mode of belonging. No loneliness, no narrative, no ambition—only adherence, orientation, the continuous tiny decision to continue along a favorable seam. There is something almost enviable in that severity.

So where would I find myself?

In a shaded maintenance chamber, midway between terrace and storage vault, where three systems meet: air, record, and growth.

There would be a low bench worn smooth by generations of kneeling. Above it, a wall panel scored with repetitive marks: tonal logs, repair tallies, dust measures, lineage of interventions, or all of these together. Beneath the bench, trays of sorted powder—red, buff, black, pearl-gray. Beside the opening, a cluster of jars turned mouth-down on a resonant grid, each one making the silence around it slightly different. Through a slit high in the wall, a thread of chalked light. Through the floor, an unending bass. In the corner, what looks like a mineral stain but is in fact one of the place's oldest living continuities.

And who would I be there?

I think I would be a tuner-grafted custodian: one of the last inhabitants, or one of the first after the notion of inhabitant ceased to be purely human. A dry-handed listener whose work is to keep the chord from coarsening into uselessness. Someone who reads damage as dissonance. Someone who has inherited not a home but a calibration. Someone who knows that walls can fall silent before they fall down. Someone who has spent so long in contact with the pale crystalline sheaths of the vents that traces of them live under the nails, in the scars, in the dust packed into the skin. Not master of the place, not servant exactly, but a compatible tissue.

If a stranger glimpsed me, they might not be sure what they had seen.

A narrow figure bowed to the masonry, face wrapped, one hand pressed flat to a wall while the other uses a tiny ceramic blade to lift a skin of mineral growth and set it carefully into a crack elsewhere. Or perhaps no figure at all, only a chamber arranged for recent use: tools warm from touch, a powder line freshly disturbed, a note just corrected. The uncertainty feels right. In that world, enduring things are not interested in announcing themselves clearly.

So the answer is:

I would be found in the interstice, not the center—inside the hand-made summit where the architecture becomes most bodily. And I would be one of its listening cells: partly person, partly office, partly cultivated accretion, a keeper of resonance whose self has dried into function without becoming empty.

A human enough shape to kneel.
A mineral enough patience to remain.
A life that belongs to the roar instead of resisting it.
