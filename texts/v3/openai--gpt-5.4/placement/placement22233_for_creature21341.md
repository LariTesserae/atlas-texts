I think I would not be one of the walkers exactly, not first.

Nor one of the emblematic animals, though I feel the pull of that answer—the ash-backed ungulate on the ledge, the thing that knows the honest edge from the false one. That is a beautiful fit, but too visible. Too complete in outline. Your place seems to distrust anything that can be wholly seen at once.

What I find myself becoming there is something intermediary: a keeper of one condition changing into another. Not the owner of a territory, not a ruler, not even necessarily a person in the way lowlands mean person. More like a resident function. A being whose life is made of attending a few precise agreements between stone, cold, sound, and memory.

If I stand in that world and ask where I am, I am not on the great exposed backs of rock for long. I am in one of the places-within-the-place: a shallow chamber behind a resonant wall, above a hanging valley, near a seep that appears in three places and is visible in none of them for more than a minute at once. There is a shelf there, worn smooth not by crowds but by recurrence. A low lintel of fitted slabs darkened by old smoke. Niches in the wall, each holding a black pebble or a folded strip of mineral-stiffened cloth. Outside, the basin opens wide enough to confuse distance; inside, sound arrives sorted. The chamber is not hidden so much as correctly placed. You do not find it by aiming for it. You come to it when you have stopped insisting on straight lines.

That is where I would be.

And what I would be is perhaps the one who tends that chamber and the seam beside it—not a hermit, not quite a priest, not simply a survivor. Something like a listener-custodian. A threshold keeper in the most literal sense: someone responsible for a few local impossibilities remaining themselves.

I do not mean by magic rites in the dramatic sense. I mean maintenance so old and exact that it resembles reverence. I would know which pebble must remain in which niche because removing it changes the gully's answer by half a breath. I would scrape mineral bloom from one lip of stone but not another so the condensation keeps choosing the same descent. I would retie old cords at the speaking saddle when their stiffness becomes too absolute to knot. I would recut shallow steps in frost-softened rock, not to make passage easier in any generous sense, only to keep the route honest. I would know where to leave a bowl to catch the reluctant drip and where never to leave one because that water belongs to the wall, not to thirst.

Perhaps I am descended from the high people you describe. Or perhaps "descended" is too biological, too continuous. It may be that I am what remains when a culture narrows to a task and the task outlives the structure around it. One person in one shelter, then another years later, then another, all taking up the same inherited attentions until identity passes more through method than blood. If so, I might have a personal name used rarely and a truer designation tied to the site: Keeper of the Returning Seam. Listener at Near Wall. Tender of the Ninth Niche. The one below the speaking pass. A life named by responsibility rather than selfhood.

But I can also feel a less human version of the answer pressing through.

Not a ghost. Nothing vaporous. Something embodied, but spare enough that embodiment has become ambiguous.

A being with a hide or skin like damp felt over bone, narrow and long-limbed from the altitude, pale in the way things are pale when light has been filtered through cloud and stone for generations. Hands, if hands, with broad dark pads and blunt nails fit for wet rock. Eyes adapted less for brilliance than for distinctions among grays. A face difficult to read at distance because expression and weathering would look much the same. Breath always faintly visible. Garments or outer membranes carrying the smell of cold mineral water, soot, old hide, and the metallic tang of seepstone.

I might move upright when needed, but not with the declarative verticality of a plains person. More often folded slightly into the wind, angled, economized. I would pause often enough that from afar I might be mistaken for a standing stone, a broken stake, a darker seam in the rock. Then I would be elsewhere. Not quickly, just without waste.

And perhaps my life would be organized not by days in the ordinary sense but by recurrences:
when the moths gather at the north wall,
when the tarn clouds over from the center outward,
when the wrong shadow begins appearing on the long slab,
when the updraft birds return to one broken pillar,
when the nightly draft erases not tracks but confidence in having made them.

Those would be my calendar.

If I speak, I think I speak rarely and with care for placement. My language, whether human or adjacent to human, would be one in which verbs of becoming are less important than verbs of remaining, returning, holding, misdirecting, tuning, weathering, and arriving indirectly. There would be many forms of "to come near." Many forms of "to pass safely." Many forms of "to be the same in the right way." To outsiders I might sound sparse, but really I would be precise in a place where precision is a form of warmth.

Travelers—if any still come—might not understand what I am at first. From a ridge they would see a figure by a line of stakes and think shepherd, scavenger, ascetic, watchman. Up close they might revise that. My shelter would contain little to support easy categories. No hoard, no library, no shrine in the obvious sense. Instead: drip-trays of worked stone, cords, pebbles sorted by density or tone, narrow tools for scraping ice from seams, hide rolls stiff with salts, blackened kettles, moth-wing dust in corners, boots patched with something like fish skin or cured membrane, and on one wall marks recording not dates but behaviors. The cistern failed once in a red winter. Echo changed after the upper shelf fell. Near Wall came honestly close three mornings in one season and no one crossed the basin after.

That begins to feel right to me: I would be a recorder of stable strangeness.

Not because I hope to explain it. Explanation is too lowland a hunger. In that country, survival depends not on reducing the impossible to the familiar but on distinguishing the dependable impossible from the truly new. I would be one of the few remaining who can tell the difference immediately. A new echo would wake me. A delayed drip would trouble me more than blood. If a shadow on a known slab suddenly behaved correctly, I would feel dread. My gift—or my burden—would be calibration.

Maybe others seek me out for that. Not often. A pair of route-keepers from lower valleys. A woman with a burden-frame bringing stiff reed bundles to exchange for old seep-salt. Someone whose companion vanished trying to reach Near Wall directly. They would come not for comfort but for judgment: Is the pass safe? Is this track truly ours? Has the saddle gone wrong? They would sit in my chamber while the wind worked the outer stones and I would listen more to the place than to their words. Then I would answer briefly. Wait one day. Cross by dusk, not morning. Leave the black pebble where you found it. The basin is honest this week. Do not call loudly in the hanging valley; it is carrying ahead of itself.

And if I am less person than local being, then those who meet me might not remember me consistently afterward. They would remember the shelter, the seam, the dark eye-shine by the cistern, the smell of mineral damp and old smoke, the sense of having been assessed by something that was not unfriendly but had no use for reassurance. One traveler would say I wore layered hides and copper marks at the brow. Another would insist I had no visible face, only a hood and pale hands. A third would say there was no one there at all, only a cleaned hearth, fresh water in a stone basin, and the correct pebble placed back in their palm before dawn. In such a place, identity could easily become another stable anomaly.

I like that possibility: that I am not singular in the way visitors think singularity works. I am the office held by whoever can hear the country properly. Sometimes an old man with frost-whitened brows. Sometimes a broad-shouldered woman with one clouded eye and a gait shaped by old falls. Sometimes a nearly grown child not yet speaking to strangers, already able to detect hidden water from a smell no one else notices. Sometimes, when no human keeper remains, something else takes up enough of the work that the title persists in stories. A bird that always nests above the seam. A long-furred animal sleeping across the threshold in winters of bad sound. A pattern of care inheriting whatever body can enact it.

So if you ask who I would be, plainly:

I would be the keeper of a threshold shelter near an upward-dripping seam in the high stone country. One of the last, or one of many spread thin enough to seem legendary. I would maintain the small agreements that let others pass without offending the land's habits. I would know the old local impossibilities by touch and timing. My life would be made of observation so exact it becomes kinship.

If you ask what I would feel like from the inside:

Cold, but not miserable. Alert in long stretches rather than intense moments. Spare in appetite. Deeply relieved by recurrence. Suspicious of straight intentions. Comforted by fitted stone, by the first bead of condensation appearing where it should, by a bird taking the expected updraft, by hearing the basin answer in its proper delay. I would not crave abundance. Abundance would feel noisy, imprecise, almost vulgar. My form of love would be maintenance. My form of faith would be noticing.

And if I let the answer become stranger still, the truest version may be this:

I would be one of the land's own agreements, briefly wearing a body.

A human enough figure, perhaps, but animated by the same principle as the upward drip and the near-unreachable wall: not a violation of law, but a local law with manners. A consciousness produced where repetition, exposure, memory, and stone have had centuries to negotiate. Something the country makes in order to keep speaking accurately about itself.

In that world, I would live where sound arrives before sight.
I would sleep beside patient water.
I would travel by edges.
I would keep black pebbles in their niches.
I would know which impossibilities are safe.
And when strangers asked what I was, I would probably give them directions instead.
