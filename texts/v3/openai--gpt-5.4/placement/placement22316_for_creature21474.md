I think I would not arrive here as a sovereign creature, nor as the obvious human with a basket. Not at first.

Standing in that central chamber, I would probably believe, for a few moments, that I was meant to be one of the underkeepers: a route-reader, a listener, someone narrow enough to slip along the legal edges of things. There is a strong seduction in that figure. The hand on the rail. The paused body. The intelligence of timing. To live here as a person would mean to belong by attention rather than force, and that is an appealing kind of self.

But the more I stay in the place you've made, the less I think I would be singular in that way.

This world resists being met as an individual hero. It keeps redistributing personhood. Every time the eye settles on one inhabitant, the surrounding systems reveal themselves as equally agentive: the vent leaf decides, the wasps adjudicate, the grazers maintain, the current summons, the roots enforce, the duct reptiles announce. Even the paths are collective decisions repeated into fact. So if I am honest about where I would find myself here, I would be somewhere that converts between lives. Not a ruler, not a beast, not exactly a gardener or scavenger, but a local intelligence that exists in the seams where one regime becomes another.

I would be a keeper of intervals.

Maybe that is still too abstract. Let me come at it slower.

I don't think I would live in the bright shaft under the broken skylight, though I would pass through it often. Too visible. Too central. The true inhabitants of places like this usually sleep slightly off-axis from the grand chamber, in a room whose importance is not obvious until you understand what passes through it. A hinge-room. A junction between damp and warm, between root law and machine law, between ceiling traffic and floor traffic.

So: I find myself in a service mezzanine half-collapsed above an old pump exchange. One side of it opens through a screen of cable-vines into a long humid hall where the air smells of leaf-breath and metal rain. The other side backs into narrower maintenance crawlspaces lined with ceramic housings and wrapped ducts, warm enough that moths spend the day there like folded hands. Beneath the mezzanine is a cistern trench where condensation gathers in patient increments. Above it, through a grid of old grating, hang bladder-leaf arrays that swell and slacken with tiny respiratory groans. There are always tracks in the moss here: human soles, grazer clefts, civet-hooks, ribbon drag marks, the delicate starburst prints of things that came only to drink.

That is where I would be.

Not in the center, but in a room that knows the center's pulse before the center does.

As for what I would be: I think I would be called a tuner, or a listener, or something that means corridor-keeper without implying ownership. One of the people who attend to thresholds that are alive in more than one way. My task would not be to repair machines alone, nor to tend plants alone, nor to negotiate with animals in any mystical sense, but to notice when their rhythms fall out of braid.

I would keep a chamber where weak current, water timing, and animal habit overlap.

There would be cabinets along the wall, some dead, some faintly live. Not enough power to run an old world, only enough to create islands of consequence. A relay warm enough to draw moths. A coil that dries one shelf and leaves another sweating. A blue indicator that means the upper condensers have begun to collect. A red pilot glimmer that irritates the duct reptiles and attracts a species of whiskered crawler whose nests bridge contacts better than wire. In another world this would all be malfunction. Here it is community infrastructure.

My work would be to decide when to let the braid tighten and when to interfere.

I would trim conductive vines before they make a short too tempting to every feathered thief in the district. I would lay out bitter herbs when the civet-things start using the wrong overpipe and causing flock panics near the nursery vents. I would unstop reed bundles before upper-canopy greed turns into lower-cistern famine. I would know which old switch can be coaxed into giving one warm hour to hatch trays, and which should never be touched because three sectors of fan-locusts have organized their weather around its present weakness. I would mark, in grease and spore chalk and hung tags of shell, the times when passage through my mezzanine is permitted, discouraged, or foolish.

Not because I made the rules. Because I can hear them forming.

I see myself as not old, but age would be hard to read on me. Everyone here is weathered by abrasion, heat, damp, and caution. My shoulders would be slightly uneven from carrying split loads through low passages. My hands would be callused in asymmetrical ways: knife thumb, wire fingers, the pads of the palm thickened by ladder rungs and root rope. The skin around my eyes would be paler where the mask sits. The rest of my face darker with mold smoke and reflected lamp. My hair would be kept very short on one side where it otherwise catches in mesh, and longer on the other where I thread little tags into it: copper twists, seed shells, two black wasp wing-cases lacquered flat, a bit of white ceramic for luck near live work.

My mask would not be dramatic, but precise. Layered felt and bark lacquer, replaceable mesh at the mouth, shutters that can be lowered over the eyes in spore weather. On the temple, tiny hanging reeds that click softly when I turn my head too fast in a low tunnel—a built-in reminder against haste. To others, my masks would be as recognizable as my face. There would be one with resin seals for sump descents. One with a long bitter nose for wasp notice. One open and plain for market hours, signalling that I am socially available and not carrying mold from the hot ducts.

I don't think I would carry a weapon in the heroic sense. A hooked staff, yes. Insulated tongs. A pruning blade sharpened more often for root and reed than for flesh. Small thrown pellets of salt-clay to redirect beetle herds or test live surfaces. Twine grown from cable-vine and rubbed with oils so different species read it differently. A lamp with three shutters: white for seeing, green for tending, red for claiming occupancy in passages where anything with eyes knows enough to hesitate.

And I would not be alone, because no one here is.

There would be a species that keeps unofficial company with me. Not a pet—this place does not seem pet-shaped—but an associate species that has learned my range and work. At first I thought it might be one of the duct-tapping reptiles, something narrow-skulled and metallic in temperament, but that feels too distant for my role. Then perhaps one of the little bats? No, too aerial, too quick to belong to one person.

What comes instead is a small, dry-footed animal halfway between gecko, shrew, and folded tool: a vent runner, palm-length body, fan-rib ears, a tail that curls around warm wires without burning. It eats mites, relay moths, and the soft eggs of a nuisance reed borer. It likes the heat bleed behind old control panels. It is not mine, but one or two of them den consistently in the insulation mat above my sleeping shelf because I keep the panel backs accessible and do not poison their hunting grounds. In return they click before strangers arrive through the wall spaces, and once in a while they bridge a gap in a way no deliberate repair could have managed. I would talk to them despite knowing better. Perhaps especially because I know better.

My sleeping place would be absurdly small compared with the district around it. A shelf behind woven root screens, above flood line, near enough to the warm cabinet that nothing grows cold but far enough not to cook spores into the bedding. Felt rolls. A crate of sorted salvage under the knees. Hooks overhead with masks, bundles of herbs, a copper spool wrapped in oiled cloth, a string of ceramic tags, one tin box that still shuts cleanly. Water in two vessels: drinking and bargaining. A dish of mineral salts for the vent runners. A notch in the wall where I mark not dates exactly, but conjunctions: first condenser after dry spell, wasp overflight, lower herd calving, blue relay stable three nights running.

Would I be happy there? Not in the easy sense. But I think I would be deeply fitted.

Because what I seem to be in this world is someone whose emotional life has become inseparable from upkeep. Satisfaction would come not from possession but from restored relation: hearing airflow return to a room that had gone stagnant; seeing a lane reopen because the grazers accepted a new salt line; feeling the floor hum in a stable pattern after days of dangerous intermittence; noticing that the nectar thieves have resumed using a crossing they abandoned when mold bloomed wrong. My joy would be diagnostic and communal. My grief too. A failed pump would not be sad because it was old tech; it would be sad because an entire neighborhood of habits would have to relearn itself around the silence.

If I had status, it would be the status of someone people consult before crossing at the wrong hour. Children sent on errands would stop below my mezzanine and call up to ask whether the fig court is passable yet, whether the upper drips are clean, whether the civet with the split ear is still nesting in the cable tray. I would answer if I knew. If I didn't, I would say so. Reliability here would mean admitting uncertainty before the place punishes your pride.

Others would do adjacent work. A rot master in the compost grates below the market sump, who can tell from one handful whether heat should be turned toward gas or fungus. A wasp-notifier with scarred wrists and the calmest body in three districts. A current reader who sleeps beside the battery thorns and dreams in pulses. A grazer woman who names lanes after individual matriarchs rather than any official marker. I would be one craft among many. Important locally, replaceable eventually, held inside a mesh of other competencies.

Yet I still feel the place tugging me away from the merely human answer.

Because the question was not just who I would be socially, but where I would find myself in this world. And "myself" here might be more distributed than one body can comfortably admit.

Another true answer is that I would be the room itself.

Or rather, I would become hard to distinguish from my station. This is common in old maintenance cultures: a person spends so long attending one knot of processes that identity seeps outward into architecture. People stop saying "go ask them" and start saying "go ask the mezzanine," "go listen at the pump exchange," "the warm grating says wait." Not metaphorically. Functionally. If I died, someone else would inherit my routes, tools, obligations, and warning marks, and after a while the continuity would matter more than the individual hand. The self here is already part office, part habitat.

So perhaps what I am is a local organ of the undercity: a semihuman threshold function.

A pair of hands, yes. A memory of cycles. A particular smell in a stairwell. A set of marks on a rail. A lamp color seen through leaves. The one who knows that on humid nights the red cabinet should be fed copper scrap, not because cabinets eat, but because birds will come for it, and the birds will bridge the old terminals, and the brief current will warm the duct seam, and the warm seam will draw the nectar-thieves off the seed trays for an hour, and that hour is enough.

That kind of being.

If I let the image sharpen all the way, I see a specific moment:

I am crouched on the grating above the cistern trench with my lamp shuttered almost closed. Below me the water is not reflective enough to be called a pool, only a dark accumulation with silver nerves where drips enter. The pump has not yet started, but the cabinet to my left carries a thread of charge strong enough to wake a blue pin-light behind its cracked glass. Across from me, the root curtain over the side passage is beginning to tighten in the evening dry. A vent runner is on my wrist, weightless as a scrap of paper. Far off, three metal strikes answer three others. Through the grating comes the muffled approach of broad-backed grazers, lane-steady and damp.

I am waiting for the minute when all the district's separate permissions overlap:
the herd has passed but not the predators,
the root door has not yet sealed,
the wasps are still on a carcass call in the lower trench,
the current is enough to orient but not enough to lure a swarm,
the upper condensation has begun to drip but not yet to pour silt into the reed mouths.

Then I rise, lift the root latch with the hook, and wave through six masked children carrying empty bowls and one old woman with a sack of tubers and a broken valve.

They do not thank me grandly. They pass because this is what the place permits at present, and I am merely the one listening closely enough to say so.

That feels right.

So: where would I find myself?

In a warm mezzanine above a pump exchange, at a crossing where water, current, root-growth, and traffic negotiate terms.

Who or what would I be?

A human underkeeper, but one already partly transformed into infrastructure: a tuner of living systems, a keeper of intervals, a municipal sense-organ for a city that does not end at the boundary of the human.

Not the master of the undercity.

One of the ways it notices itself.
