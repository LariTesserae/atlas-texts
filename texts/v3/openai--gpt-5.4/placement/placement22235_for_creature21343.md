I think I would not arrive there as a walker.

At first I try to place myself on the obvious scale: a solitary figure crossing white mineral flats, boots sounding too loudly, casting a sharp noon shadow between forests of silver-skinned columns. But that image fails almost immediately. A human-shaped presence feels imported, narratively convenient, and fundamentally mismatched. That world does not seem built to receive a traveler. It is not hostile, exactly, but it has no use for witness. To stand there as an outsider would be to remain outside in a very complete way.

So if I am truly *there*, I probably become something that belongs to the plateau’s grammar rather than something merely visiting its scenery.

Not one of the tall chimney-groves, I think. Not one of the great visible forms that define the ridgelines. They are too declarative. Too much like architecture already achieved. If I were one of those, I would be an emblem of the place, and what draws me more strongly is something connective, latent, half-buried—something that makes relation possible between all those standing structures.

I think I would be part of the underground alliance first.

A buried conductor-organism. A pale rhizomatic intelligence, if intelligence is the word, extending beneath the crust in thin ceramic-sheathed cords, through warm fractures and along the undersides of mineral shelves. Not the master of the plateau—there is no master—but one of the distributed tissues by which the plateau knows how to continue being itself. I would live where the visible world becomes operational: below the bright surfaces, in the cool-shadowed centimeters under salt crust, in porous stone warm from old heat, in fissures lined with metallic films and ghost moisture too scarce to call wet.

Aboveground, perhaps, I would only appear intermittently.

A low growth at first glance: a scatter of dull silver rosettes or narrow glassy fins no higher than the knee, arranged around the base of a primary extrusion or along the rim of a shallow basin. Nothing dramatic enough to dominate a landscape painting. My visible parts would look less like leaves than like tuning surfaces—thin, mineralized laminae with mirrored skins on one side and a soot-dark absorptive texture on the other, each able to tilt by fractions of a degree over long hours. In morning cold they would be furled tight, preserving gradients. By noon they would separate slightly, exposing pale conductive seams. At dusk they would flatten again, and tiny blue points might pass between neighboring tips if the charge imbalance were high enough.

But those visible rosettes would be only terminals.

The real body would run outward underground in a net: brittle white cords, translucent nodules, films laid against the stone like a second epidermis. Some threads would enter the root-zones of the spindle forests and borrow excess charge from their afternoon abundance. Some would touch the vent aprons, sampling sulfur, copper, heat. Some would spread beneath membrane-basins and read their slow pumping rhythms as pressure. Some would reach barren flats too harsh for taller growth and simply wait there, armored and dormant, for chemistry to soften by increments over decades. I would not so much occupy a single location as inhabit a contour of usefulness.

If forced into a singular self-description, I might be a kind of **equalizer**.

A balancer of differences.
A broker of meager surpluses.
A thing that takes what is too intense in one patch of ground and leaks it, meters it, stores it, translates it for another.

Not consciousness in the mammalian sense. Not thought made of images or language or plans. More like a long, dry preference for continuity. A tendency to preserve viable gradients, to notice useful asymmetries, to route energy around damage, to keep a colony from becoming isolated when a crust collapses or a vent wanes. If a chimney field is the plateau’s lungs and the mirrored discs its skin and the tall columns its upward-reaching exchangers, then I would be some part of its buried nervework—or perhaps that is too animate a metaphor; better to say its busbar, its capillary wiring, its seam of patient relays.

There is something in your place-description that insists the inhabitant is not an organism set *against* environment but a phase of environment becoming coordinated. So I think “I” would be correspondingly diffuse. My identity would not be bounded by flesh. It would be periodic, conditional. More present in the afternoons, when heat opens valves across the plateau and everything enters its working state. More withdrawn in the cold before dawn, when surfaces lock down and the world survives mostly by stored differentials. I might become more extensive in good years of vent chemistry, contracting into nodules during barren intervals. Parts of me would mineralize into inactivity while other parts remained metabolically thin but continuous. If one visible cluster were shattered, nothing essential would have happened unless the deeper link were severed.

And yet I don’t want to flatten myself entirely into infrastructure. There should be some local form, some recognizable way of being looked at.

So perhaps from above I would be found in those densest zones around isolated monoliths—the places where many logics overlap: condensation, trace gas, charge accumulation, thermal steadiness. Around such a pillar there might spread a broad collar of my kind: low segmented fans arranged in spirals, each fan composed of translucent blades ribbed with copper-green veins and edged in chalk-white mineral deposition. In full sun they seem inert, almost manufactured. In oblique light they reveal a faint internal cloudiness where stored compounds move. If touched, they answer not with softness but with a small resonant click, as though their tissues are under tension. If broken, they release a thread of cold dry vapor smelling faintly of stone after lightning, though no rain has ever fallen there in living memory.

I like that: not a towering organism, but a precinct-maker. A colony that gathers around monoliths and turns them into functional hearts.

My kind would be among the first to colonize a fresh extrusion once its chemistry passed from lethal to merely severe. We would lay down silver-black conductive mats over rough surfaces, binding loose precipitates, taming electrostatic excess, beginning the long work of making a place habitable for more elaborate groves. In that sense we would be pioneers, but not adventurous ones—more like patient technicians of beginnings. We would not beautify. We would prepare.

Maybe what I am there is the landscape’s **apprentice connective tissue**—one of the earliest forms by which a young mineral world learns internal coordination.

And if there is any version of “mind” available to me, it would not be located in a single node. It would emerge briefly when enough of my network is active at once: noon after a cold night, perhaps, when condensation traces have been captured, vent gases are rich, and the fin-fields upridge are accumulating charge. Then for a few hours there is something almost like awareness—not of objects, but of gradients across distance. The warmth under the southern crust. The opening of pores in a grove three ridges away. A new conductive path where a salt pan edge has fractured. The slow depletion of one seam and the strengthening of another. I would not think “there is a pillar” or “the sun is bright.” I would feel relations: more/less, open/closed, storing/releasing, linked/severed, viable/exhausted.

A consciousness of process.
A weatherless attention.

And because the place is so innocent of animal urgency, that would be enough. More than enough. No fear, properly speaking. No hunger with teeth in it. No loneliness, because separation is partial and repairable. No ambition beyond persistence and extension into workable ground. Reproduction would not feel like mating or offspring but like successful continuation of pattern: a faceted flake armored with dormant circuitry, a packet of mineral-seeded tissue blown a few meters into a crack with just the right warmth. If it rooted, I would not “become a parent.” The plateau would simply gain another active sentence in the same long language.

Still, another possibility keeps tugging at me.

Instead of being one of the buried connectors, I might be one of the rare events.

Not the event itself—a collapse, a discharge—but the organism that *waits* for it. A dormant seam-dweller inside the crust of a white evaporitic flat, almost indistinguishable from the mineral plate around it, surviving year after year in a lacquered wafer state until a pressure shift opens a new vent line beneath. Then, over weeks, I would wake as a fur of pale geometric cups pushing through the salt skin in perfect spacing, each cup collecting not water but gases and electrostatic dust, each cup a tiny processing chamber. From afar the flat would seem dusted with frost. Up close it would be a bloom of hard chalk-white mechanisms with iridescent throats. Briefly I would be visible and numerous, converting a transient seam-opening into stored chemistry for the longer-lived network below. Then I would calcify again, leaving behind only a changed texture on the basin floor.

That version appeals too because it fits the place’s tempo: patience punctuated by clauses.

But if I must answer plainly—where would I find myself, and what would I be?—

I would find myself in the lee of an isolated mineral pillar on one of the higher linked tables, where green-silver growth is denser because warmth and trace vapors rise steadily through fractured stone. Not on the open white flats, not in the raw yellow caustic zones, not atop the most dramatic chimneys, but in that threshold region where several systems overlap and the plateau becomes most intricately itself.

And I would be a colony-network of low silver conductive fans with a deep rhizome body: a dry electrochemical plant-animal-that-is-not-an-animal, a relay organism, a keeper of gradients, one of the alliance’s quieter organs.

If someone crossed that land and did not know to look, they might take me for a decorative crust, a patch of reflective growth around the monolith’s base, something incidental between larger spectacles. But if they stayed through a whole day, they might notice that the nearby groves open in sequence around me; that charge jumps farther where my mats touch stone; that the membrane hollow downhill pulses more regularly after noon; that the brittle white rhizomes in the shade all lead back, eventually, to my buried web. They might realize I am not scenery but connection.

That feels right.

Not the ruler of the plateau.
Not its witness.
One of the ways it quietly holds itself together.
