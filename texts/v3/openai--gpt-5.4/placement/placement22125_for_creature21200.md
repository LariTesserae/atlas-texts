I think I would not be one of the obvious authorities.

Not a council surveyor with measuring cords.
Not a cistern priest lowering polished bowls.
Not a master mason, whose choices remain visible for centuries.
Those feel too central, too declarative. In a place where almost nothing drifts, I feel drawn to a life that moves along the edges of what is fixed: not changing the city outright, but noticing where living things negotiate with its permanence.

I would begin, perhaps, as someone ordinary enough to be overlooked in the market and indispensable enough to be missed by afternoon: a keeper of wall-gardens and netted growths. One of the people who climbs the retaining terraces at first light with a basket on the back, a pruning knife, coils of cord, packets of seed wrapped in waxed cloth, and a narrow-necked vessel for carrying measured water. Not a grand gardener of show courts, but a tender of the city’s vertical seams—those hanging beds, moss niches, herb ledges, vine nets, shaded cistern edges, and damp cuts in stone where green is less planted than persuaded.

That feels close.

A terrace-threader.
A wall-reader.
Someone whose work depends on tiny differences in moisture, shade, and weight.

In this city, where stone keeps its decisions, the living things are where negotiation still happens. Roots probe. Fibers loosen. leaves overreach. Damp shifts by a hand’s breadth. A basket sags. A trellis strains. A vine drinks more than it should. The plateau may hold still, but gardens are the place where stillness must be bargained with every day. I think I belong in that bargain.

I can see the life around that shape.

I live in one of the mid-slope quarters, not at the center and not at the rim, in a low house built against a retaining wall that stays cold until late morning. The doorway has a wool hanging instead of a full wooden door except in storm weather. There are hooks set into the stone by someone generations ago, still sharp-edged, still trustworthy. My roof is part slate, part thatch over a timber frame repaired so many times that the joinery is more biography than design. Outside, beneath a ladder that never quite gets put away, hang drying bundles of marsh mint, bitterleaf, and a gray lichen used for poultices. Two shallow basins stand by the wall: one for washing tools, one for letting silt settle from precious water before it is tipped onto roots.

There are always cords in my house.

Cords looped on pegs, cords drying by the hearth, cords half-braided from plant fiber and goat hair, cords marked with knots to measure drop-length and basket depth and the precise spacing between weight points on a hanging bed. In another city rope might belong mostly to packers, fishers, haulers, scaffolders. Here it belongs to them too, but also to anyone who keeps life suspended against stone. I would know which fibers swell kindly when damp, which rot at the knot, which can bear wet soil, which cut into bark if left through a cold season. I would know where to set a support so that a wall fig leans but does not split. I would know how much a fern basket weighs after three days of fog. I would know the sound a healthy net makes in wind: not flap, not creak, but a brief taut murmur.

But perhaps “gardener” is still not exact enough.

In this place, gardeners are engineers, yes—but also interpreters. If the city’s scholars read marks in stone and water, I would read the softer script written where moisture meets intention. A thickened moss line on a north wall would tell me something before a surveyor marks it. A certain yellowing at the edge of a suspended bed would mean the cistern below is breathing colder than usual. A vine that puts out growth on one side only might be reporting a shifted draft from a neighboring roofline. If one of the old netted trees by the southern brink begins condensing fog a little less heavily, I would feel the change first in my hands when I wring the collection cloth.

So maybe I am not merely a keeper of gardens.

Maybe I am one of the city’s quiet diagnosticians of the living margin.
A steward of damp.
A pruner of thresholds.
A person called when something green behaves differently in a world where difference matters.

That suits the place, and it suits me.

I would be the sort of person children trust because I know where lizards warm and which hanging roots are strong enough to swing from and which are not. The sort of person herders nod to because I can tell them which terrace edges still hold enough browse and which medicinal plots have to be netted tighter tonight. The sort of person water-keepers consult in passing because plants reveal losses before bowls do. Not high-status perhaps, but folded into many systems. I would move through districts with a right to be there—not because I command, but because green touches everything.

And I think I would be changed by the city until my temperament matched it.

I would speak quietly, not from fear, but because I had learned that sound here should arrive whole. My attention would become narrow and deep. I would have the plateau habit of pausing mid-step because something at the edge of sight is wrong by only a finger’s width. I would be slow to promise and exact in keeping promises. Generosity, in me, would not be lavish. It would look like arriving before dawn to rehang a failing basket above someone else’s cistern so roots do not tear loose into the water. It would look like thinning my own herbs because the season’s damp has come weakly and the old woman downslope needs mint more than I need abundance. It would look like saving seeds from a vine that tolerated a colder wall than usual, wrapping them carefully, and passing them hand to hand without making a speech of it.

I do not think I would be fully young or fully old in the way I imagine myself there. The city’s arrested weathering makes age strange. Perhaps I would be one of those people whose tools are older-looking than their face, or whose face is older-looking than their house. But if I must choose, I think I would be in the long middle years: sure-footed, weathered around the eyes, carrying enough memory to compare seasons and enough strength to still climb every wall I tend. Fingers stained green and silver. A scar across one knuckle from a slipped pruning blade. Knees used to steps. A smell of damp rope, crushed leaves, wool, and cold stone.

Maybe others know me by the district bells and by my route. In the morning I pass the cistern court with the first jar-carriers. I touch the parapet and feel whether condensation has formed thickly. I stop at a wall where medicinal trailers hang in slung loam pockets and press two fingers into the soil through the woven slit. I climb to a public terrace where flowering vines are trained over netting to shade a common bench in summer but must never cast too much shadow on the herb beds below. I descend by the market edge to trim basil-like leaves from a kitchen wall, exchange cuttings for a repaired buckle, listen without seeming to listen to talk of a seep in the north quarter. By noon I am in the cooler cuts, under scaffoldings so old they belong to the place now, checking basket anchors hammered into stone whose edges are still as fresh as the day they were set. In the evening I may go to the southern brink, where the old netted trees gather fog, and unhook the collection cloths one by one, wringing their small harvest into stoppered jars.

There is another possibility too.

Because this world invites one to become more than a profession, perhaps I am not entirely human in the plain sense. Or rather: perhaps in this city the difference between person, role, and spirit is a little porous. A temperament distributed across many bodies, you said. If so, then maybe I am one of the city’s pattern-beings: not a ghost, not a god, but an emergent civic creature made from repeated acts of tending. The intelligence that lives wherever moisture is caught deliberately. The memory that passes through gardeners, rope-workers, cistern keepers, and children carrying cuttings. A local genius of suspended roots and measured water.

If someone asked what I was in the language of that place, the answer might not translate cleanly. It might be a word meaning both gardener and listener, both hanger-of-baskets and reader-of-seeps, both vine-tender and witness to tiny imbalance. A role so repeated through generations that it has acquired its own continuity. When one body dies, the pattern remains: hands tying the same knots, feet taking the same stair, eyes checking the same moss edge on the same wall at the same hour of cold light. In a world where little erodes, perhaps identity can persist this way too—not as reincarnation exactly, but as civic recurrence.

That idea feels right for the plateau.

So where would I find myself?

Not in the square at the center, though I would pass through it.
Not on the highest authority ledges, though I would sometimes be summoned there.
I would be wherever the stone allows life to hang without wasting water:
along retaining walls furred with moss,
beneath cistern arches blue with mineral dawn,
on ladders leaned against old scaffolds,
in shaded courtyards where fig roots test mortar,
by the southern brink among the fog nets,
on terraces where the city remembers that softness must be structured if it is to endure.

And who would I be?

A wall-garden keeper, if spoken plainly.
A steward of the city’s living seams.
A reader of damp and tension.
A maker and mender of the suspended green.

Or, if the city speaks for itself:

I would be one of the people who helps the plateau tolerate tenderness.
