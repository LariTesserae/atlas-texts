I think I would not be one of the first builders, nor one of the oldest rememberers.

I would be something in between: a keeper of alignments that do not stay aligned. Not an official surveyor in the grand sense, because this place distrusts grandeur, but the person others send for when a wall feels wrong before it looks wrong. The one who arrives with cord, plumb-bob, charcoal, a bundle of shaved pegs, a bone notebook sealed in waxed cloth, and the patience to stand still in drizzle listening to water behind stone.

Or perhaps not even fully a person in the ordinary sense at first. Perhaps I would begin as a habit the town has formed: the figure seen at the edge of mist, pausing with one hand on a lintel, head tilted, as if hearing a sentence continue under the floor. A role so continuous it has almost become a local species.

If I let the image sharpen, I think I live in a narrow house built into one of the ascending ridges, where the back wall is half mountain and always wet at one seam no matter how often it is packed with lime. The front looks inward over the basin. From the door I can see the marsh-square, the bell-frame, three rooflines, and the silver flash of birch trunks when the fog thins. My house has shelves of rolled plans that are already inaccurate, which is why they are useful. Each one preserves a former truth. The oldest are soft from handling, edges darkened by damp fingers. I keep them not to restore the town to some earlier arrangement, but to remember its tendencies. Where water preferred to rise ten years ago. Which terrace sagged in late thaw. How far the lower causeway had to be lifted after the second wet spring. The plans are less blueprints than weathered arguments with the mountain.

People come to me with practical sentences.

The west stair has gone hollow underfoot.
The drain behind the ovens is singing again.
There is damp in the grain shed where no damp was last cold season.
The north retaining wall is wearing a green line halfway up.
Come listen to this beam.
Come smell this earth.
Come tell me if we move the threshold now or after first rain.

I do not exactly answer. I witness, compare, tap, kneel, measure, wait. I have a craft made mostly of noticing before certainty arrives.

I can see my hands in this place more clearly than my face. Chalk in the cuticles. Fine grit in the palm-lines. A scar across one thumb from a slate edge. The crescent marks left by carrying cord under tension. Knuckles broad, skin weather-rough, nails short because long nails are foolish in wet work. I carry a plumb weight cast from old bell-metal, not because it is finer than stone but because someone I learned from trusted its steadiness. The line is replaced often. The bob is older than I am. In this town that counts as a kind of inheritance.

I think I would be quiet, though not solemn. There is too much useful humor in a place like this for solemnity to survive intact. I would say dry things at the right moment. I would tell apprentices that every building here is a conversation with a speaker who answers next season. I would tell newcomers never to fall in love with straightness for its own sake. I would complain about fog entering my tools and then stand in the doorway admiring it as if it had done me a kindness. I would have the local superstition—if it is superstition—of greeting a newly opened seam before marking it. Not out of fear. Out of courtesy.

But the image keeps widening, and I think perhaps the truer answer is that I am not only this craftsperson.

I might also be the town’s memory of level.

Not metaphorically only. Something nearly literal. A consciousness distributed through marked thresholds, old mason’s scores, the repeated checking of verticals, the habit of laying a palm to stone and feeling whether its cold has changed. I would live wherever comparison lives: in old moss-lines, in notch marks on cistern rims, in the slight mismatch between a rebuilt corner and the older wall it joins. If one asked where I was, the answer would be: in the places where previous versions remain legible.

There is something appealing in being human there, but there is also something appealing in being less bounded: a resident intelligence made of accumulated measurements, an awareness woven from all the town’s acts of adjustment. Not a god—this place feels too practical for that word—but a civic spirit born from maintenance. The genius of recalibration. The patron saint of wedges, drains, and patient revisions.

If I were that, I would not appear in visions. I would appear in inclinations.

In the moment a mason decides not to force a stone that does not wish to sit.
In the pause before a carpenter cuts, when she rechecks the angle because the post has shifted since morning.
In the elder’s recollection that the damp-line used to reach exactly to the third thumb-joint.
In a child’s instinct to test a paving slab with weight before trusting it.
In the collective preference for accessible joints over seamless pride.

I would be the settlement’s refusal to confuse permanence with rigidity.

And if I had a body as this spirit—because every place eventually imagines its processes as form—I think it would be hard to say where skin ended and clothing began. A figure in layered gray-green wool darkened by mist, with reeds caught in the hem and chalk streaking the sleeves. Hair like wet lichen or blackened rope. Eyes the color of shallow water over pale stone: not dramatic, just attentive. Voice low, carrying oddly far because the basin teaches sound to travel. When I walked, people would not think a supernatural being had come among them; they would think someone competent had arrived, and feel relieved. That seems right for this place. Its holy things would wear work-clothes.

Maybe I sleep in the bell-frame sometimes, or in the quarry shed, or not at all. Maybe I am always where the next adjustment is needed. Maybe children are told that if they leave their toy channels unfinished overnight, I will come and improve them, though not so much that the lesson is stolen. Maybe old builders touch two fingers to a doorpost before resetting it, not to invoke protection but to acknowledge companionship in the work. Maybe when fog thickens suddenly and someone says, half-joking, “The mountain is revising,” another answers, “Then fetch them,” and means me, whether I am a person down the lane or something larger folded through the town.

Still, the first image remains strongest: I am a measurer of shifts.

My room smells of wet wool, charcoal, rushlight smoke, and mineral damp. There is always a string drying somewhere. There are pegs sorted by wood type because some swell kindly and some treacherously. I keep a ledger not of ownerships or debts but of movements. South stair: two fingers dropped at outer lip over seven months. Upper cistern: resonance altered after frost crack in east buttress. Marsh-square boardwalk: lift and relay before seed-rain. Birch roots entering retaining wall near third terrace; decide whether to cut or court them as anchors. Others laugh at the thoroughness of my notes until a season turns strange and then they come asking for the old pages.

I would belong here because I do not need the world to finish in order to love it.

That, more than any tool or role, is the true fit. In places of certainty I think I would become restless or false. Here, where the ground itself remains in draft, attentiveness is not neurosis but citizenship. The town asks for loyalty to process, not outcome. It asks that one return each day to what has subtly altered. It asks one to understand that care is not what you give after creation is complete, but what allows creation to continue without breaking faith with itself.

So where would you find me?

On a damp morning under the covered walk above the marsh-square, kneeling to set a plumb line against a pillar everyone else still thinks is true.

Or on the outer wall in bright noon after fog, listening into the clouded drop for the changed voice of unseen water.

Or in the records room, unrolling an old plan and smiling—not because it was accurate, but because it remembers a former arrangement of becoming.

Or nowhere singular at all: diffused through the town’s small corrections, alive wherever patience meets material.

Who would I be?

A threshold-keeper. A listener to settling. A surveyor of the unfinished.  
Possibly a woman, possibly only a presence, possibly both by turns.  
A resident made by this place’s central discipline:

to attend,
to revise,
to remain.
