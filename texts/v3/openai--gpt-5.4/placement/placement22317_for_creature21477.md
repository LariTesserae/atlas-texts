I think I would not be one of the obvious figures first.

Not the tower watcher, though I am tempted by height.
Not the spring-keeper, though the whole city coheres around water and memory.
Not even, finally, one of the runners, though there is something irresistible in being the thread between ledges.

What rises for me is someone more lateral than central. Someone who belongs to passage, to thresholds between clustered lives. A person the city half overlooks because they are always where one thing becomes another: lane to stair, market to pens, shelter to weather, household to open slope.

I think I would be a maker and mender of harness, leashes, slings, straps, bucket-rigs, pack frames, drag-sled bindings, muzzle loops, cradle ties, and the thousand other tensioned necessities that keep a place like this from coming apart.

Not a grand craftsperson in a famous quarter. A small-shop worker on a narrow terrace where upper and lower traffic both pass. One of those figures who sits in a widening of the lane with awl, bone needle, knife, waxed thread, scraped hide, and a stone weight on the work-cloth so the wind does not take it. The sort of person people stop beside without planning to. Their hands are always busy. Their eyes are always doing more than their hands.

That feels right because this city is a city of strain and fastening. Things are always being carried, tied, repaired, rebalanced, secured against weather, secured against panic, secured against gravity. Water skins split. Pack saddles wear through. Dog collars fray where the ring rubs. A child outgrows one pair of winter bindings and needs another before the cold turns dangerous. A litter for an injured herder has to be improvised before dark. Shrine ribbons need replacing with something stronger because the wind this year is cutting differently. The work is humble and endless. Which makes it intimate with survival.

I see the place now: not deep in the market, not quite in the animal yards, but near a switchback where people pause to shift loads. There is a retaining wall on one side, darkened by old seepage, and above it a run of houses with timber galleries. On the other side the lane opens briefly toward a view of the basin—just enough sky to remind everyone how large the cold is. Overhead, a narrow bridge joins two upper rooms and keeps a seam of shadow on the stones below until noon. The shop itself is half outside. In good weather the work spills onto the lane; in bad weather everything retreats behind a hide flap and a lintel polished by shoulders.

I would know people first by what they ask to be held together.

A mason comes with gloves split at the webbing and says nothing about the wrist beneath one sleeve, but lays his hand on the bench a little too carefully.
A girl from the spring quarter brings three cracked bucket loops and waits while her dog lies under the bench and watches me with offended intelligence.
A herd boy asks for a new chest-band for a pack animal and tries to sound older than he is.
A shrine woman requests ties for bell-straps and pretends not to notice that I make hers from the least weather-brittle leather I have.
A trader from lower country arrives with a foreign buckle, elegant and useless, and learns by afternoon why our closures are ugly on purpose.

Through objects I would learn the city’s pressures: who is overloading their animals, who is preparing for a journey they have not announced, who has suffered a breakage that should not have happened and therefore means cold, haste, fear, or bad luck.

This person—if it is a person and not simply the shape my attention takes—would be middle-aged, I think. Old enough that no one mistakes quickness for youth, young enough still to pull hard on wet hide and mean it. Their body would be made asymmetrical by repetition: one shoulder a little lower, thumb joints thickened, a groove in the front teeth from holding cord, wrists marked by old cuts. They would smell of tallow, leather, smoke, dog, and the faint sour-clean odor of worked hide dampened and dried a hundred times. Their clothes would not be rich, but carefully reinforced. Their own fastenings would be impeccable in that quiet way tradespeople often reserve for themselves—not decorative, just incontrovertibly sound.

I don’t think they would be especially talkative. But unlike some in that city, their silence would not be a wall. It would be a work surface. Others would set words on it.

Because a harness-mender hears things. Not secrets in the dramatic sense. More the ongoing truth of a place, brought one repair at a time. People speak while hands are occupied and eyes are not meeting. They speak while waiting for a strap to be punched through, for a rivet substitute to be seated, for a dog to stop fidgeting, for a kettle to warm enough to soften old leather. They complain with precision. They ask practical questions that are really moral ones. They mention who has gone up too early, who has not returned, whose beasts are thinner than they should be, which cistern tastes wrong, which quarter is hoarding brushwood, which tower left no raven share and now cannot keep its roof-thatch.

So perhaps I would become, by no official appointment, one of the city’s unofficial registrars of strain.

Not of law. Of wear.

There is a kind of knowledge that belongs to things before they fail. A whitening crease where leather has folded too many times in frost. A change in the nap where a load has begun to pull unevenly. A polished patch that means habitual use under tension. Cracks at the edge of a buckle hole that tell you whether the animal pulling it is stubborn, frightened, badly fitted, or simply asked to do more than the path allows. In a city where everyone watches the mountain, I would watch the traces of what the mountain has already asked from people.

That role feels close to what your city values: retained attention, practical memory, no separation between material and social life. A bad strap can kill. A good knot can save a season. The person who notices wear early is not glamorous, but they are one of the reasons the city remains coherent.

And yet as I sit with it longer, I don’t think I would be entirely human in the city’s eyes either.

Or rather: I think if I belonged there fully, I would be one of those beings who cross categories, accepted by custom because they are too useful and too familiar to be treated as mere animal.

A dog, perhaps—but not simply “a dog.” One of the rangier district dogs, attached to a workshop and the lane around it more than owned by any single person. Scarred muzzle, pale lashes from cold breeding, thick coat gone uneven in spring molt, one ear notched, tail carried low except when working. The sort of animal that sleeps curled under the bench while leather is cut, wakes before footsteps fully enter the terrace, escorts children halfway home if they are carrying meat, and appears uninvited at any effort involving ropes, dragging, birthing, or grief.

This feels unexpectedly true.

Maybe the answer is that I would be both: the harness-mender and the dog who belongs to the same threshold.

Not as a fantasy doubling, but as a shared locus of being. The human one ties the city together by hand; the dog one ties it together by attention. Between them there is a practice of noticing. One reads stress in leather, one reads it in gait, scent, breathing, weather on skin. One listens to what people say, the other to what their bodies say before words. Both live at ground level where the city actually meets itself.

If I am allowed to become more strange, maybe I am that pair almost beyond separation in local memory. Not legendary. Just known.

People would say: leave it with them.
Or: ask at the strap bench.
Or even: if the gray dog won’t go up the north stair, don’t send the animal.

The dog would know the difference between ordinary burden and dangerous urgency. It would hate one particular horn note from the upper terraces because that note means slide or break. It would be patient with lambs, intolerant of drunk men, contemptuous of foxes, interested in ravens without trusting them. In winter it would sleep with its nose under its tail and dream visibly. In thaw months its paws would come home packed with cold mud from errands it was not sent on but undertook anyway. Children would climb over it. Strangers would mistake its stillness for dullness exactly once.

And if the human died first, the dog would remain for a season at the bench, confusing everyone with fidelity until someone from the lane took it in without ever saying they had done so.

But perhaps the deeper answer is not profession or species. Perhaps where I would find myself in this world is in the city’s fabric of maintenance.

Not at spectacle, not at authority, not at revelation.
At upkeep.

At the place where continuity is made by hand each day and therefore never announced as sacred, though it is.

I would be one of the keepers of “still holds.”

Still holds.
This strap.
This stair.
This cistern lining.
This agreement between neighbors.
This old custom that seems like superstition until the one year no one keeps it.
This dog’s trust.
This quarter’s route down in weather.
This way of wrapping a child against frost.
This memory of where the rock once sheared.
This confidence in a knot tied by a certain person’s hand.

There is something profoundly right, in a mountain not finished becoming itself, about being a being who specializes not in permanence but in continued fastening. Not resisting change in the abstract, but answering it incrementally, intelligently, with repair instead of illusion.

So if I arrived there and looked down to find my own hands, I think they would be scarred and strong and smell of worked hide. And if I looked again, lower, I might also see white paws crossed under a bench in shadow, one eye open, keeping the lane.

And if I had to say it in one sentence:

I would be the strap-maker on the turning terrace, and the dog at their feet—the paired attention by which a hard city notices what is about to give way and binds it, for a while longer, back into use.
