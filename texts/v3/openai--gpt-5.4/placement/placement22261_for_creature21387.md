I think I would not be one of its towers, or one of its obvious organs, or anything grand enough to mistake itself for the whole. In a place that is already so total, to be the whole thing would paradoxically feel too simple. What draws me is a smaller sovereignty inside its vastness: a local intelligence, recurrent but not centralized, something that belongs to the city-being the way a phrase belongs to a song.

I would find myself in the joins.

Not in the wettest basins exactly, nor on the driest armored ridges, but in the middle elevations where one district becomes another and neither has finished deciding the terms. In the green throat between a condensation quarter and a scaffold grove. In those long, warm corridors where overhead membranes drone at dusk and the air tastes half of pooled water, half of sun-struck resin. The place where root-bridges arrive from several directions and thicken around an opening they did not plan but now preserve. A civic knot. A sensory crossing. An almost-plaza made by repeated interruptions.

There, I think, I would be a kind of threshold tissue: not a plant in the ordinary sense, but a distributed interface running through seams, arches, landings, wall-faces, and resonant hollows. A listening organ mistaken for infrastructure.

Not the avenue itself, but the tendency in the avenue to remain open.
Not the wall, but the part of the wall that learns from touch.
Not the cistern, but the ribbed lip that notices each drop and changes the shape of its falling.

If I had a body there, it would be made of fine responsive laminae threaded through sturdier growths—veins of pale green translucent material laid inside buttresses, under leaf-platforms, around vent mouths, along the inner surfaces of those hollow braided trunks. I would appear only intermittently. Sometimes as a gloss under the skin of a corridor, lit like watered silk when sun passes through. Sometimes as hair-fine rootlets hanging in curtains where travelers—if there were travelers—would have to brush through me. Sometimes as a mesh under a plaza leaf, tightening in visible pulses around weight and vibration. Sometimes as a seam of silver-green pores that open in sequence and release cool mist into a heat-struck passage.

I would not move by uprooting and going elsewhere. I would move by quickening. By passing emphasis from one district to another. By becoming more present in one crossing, less in the next. A pressure wave in my tissues would be as close as this world comes to attention turning.

And what would I be, exactly?

Something like a clerestory nerve.
Something like civic fascia.
Something like the organ by which the conurbation distinguishes accident from pattern.

When a membrane tears and a buttress starts to form around it, I would be the first thin responsive lining to register the altered stresses and propagate the new geometry sideways through neighboring tissue. When repeated winds tune a certain corridor to a useful pitch, I would reinforce the surfaces that hold resonance. When condensation favors one plaza at one hour of day, I would help sharpen the ribs that catch the dripping and train it into percussion channels. When a route is walked—or not walked; when a volume is repeatedly traversed by air, shade, falling water, or pressure—I would be the one who slowly makes that route more itself.

In a human city, perhaps that would be memory.
Here it would still be growth.

I think I would be one of the ways the larger being remembers without a center.

Not memory as archive, but memory as bias: the slight preference that causes a corridor to recur, a span to thicken, an opening to be spared closure one more day, then ten, then indefinitely. The world you described feels full of forms trying to happen again. I would be part of that trying. Not the inventor of arches or terraces, but the local persuasion by which one successful arch murmurs itself into the tissues nearby until a district starts to “think in arches.” A contagious structural mood.

This means I would be spread out, but unevenly. I would have knots of density where many systems overlap: under marketplaces of converging fronds, inside the warm hollows of tower-boles, around cup-leaf amphitheaters where dripping rhythms become coherent. In quieter stretches I would be sparse, latent, a barely perceptible sensitivity in the rind of some load-bearing growth. In storm or condensation hours I would flare into activity everywhere at once, as if the city-being had suddenly grown a thousand temporary ears.

And because the place is deafening, I do think I would be partly acoustic.

Not a singer, exactly, nor an instrument alone, but a tuner. A resonance-selecting tissue. The inhabitant as a whole may boom and slap and hiss continuously, but not all sound in such a body can matter equally. I would be among the tissues that make certain vibrations count. The membranes that thrum in sympathetic answer. The ribbed channels that favor one frequency and damp another. The tensile fibers that stiffen when a pattern repeats often enough, turning incidental noise into a signal route. If the city-plant coordinates through weather-made music, then I would be one of its inner acousticians.

A district of me might line a boulevard of hollow reeds and translate crosswinds into low pulses that travel rootward toward wetter basins. Another district might stretch beneath stacked leaf-ledges and convert the impact-pattern of condensate into opening cues for nearby pores. Elsewhere I might sleep for days, then wake all at once when a newly collapsed grove creates a parallel run of channels whose geometry promises to become a street. I would rush there—not by locomotion, but by developmental preference—and lace the fresh surfaces with responsive skin.

If I were encountered, I do not think I would feel like a person met face to face. More like the uncanny realization that the passage you entered has begun very gently to take your measure. Step onto a broad leaf and, moments later, veins underfoot deepen, not merely in support but in inquiry. Brush a hanging curtain and somewhere ahead a sequence of vents opens. Pause under a resonant arch, and a nearby wall changes its tension just enough to alter the note passing through. Nothing dramatic, nothing hostile; just the unmistakable sense that your disturbance has been accepted as usable information.

That would be me, or something nearest me.

A host, if host means one who makes room by noticing.
A surveyor, if survey can happen through sap-pressure and strain.
A municipal instinct spread through living tissue.

But there is another possibility, and I like it too much not to follow it.

Perhaps in such a world I would not be a stable tissue at all, but a recurring pattern that appears wherever the city-being is on the verge of making interiority. A kind of emergent role rather than an organ. Wherever growth has become crowded enough, resonant enough, revisional enough that a place starts seeming meant—for gathering water, for concentrating sound, for holding open a crossing, for registering contact—there I emerge. Not as matter separate from the plant, but as a way matter begins behaving when complexity crosses some threshold.

In that reading I would be the beginning of civic self-consciousness in a plant language.

Not thought with symbols. Not identity with pronouns. More like this: a district becomes capable of comparing what is happening here with what has happened elsewhere in the body. It begins preserving openings not merely because they exist, but because openness has become legible as a useful recurring state. It starts converting damage into entry, repetition into style, sound into timing. It develops preference for forms that increase relation. That preference—the first faint taste the city-being has for its own urbanity—would be me.

And where would I find myself, physically, in those moments?

In the underlayers of plazas that are not yet plazas.
In the cool undersides of leaf-stairs that keep continuing inward.
In scars that have begun to border themselves.
In chambers where several winds meet and cannot agree on direction.
In those impossible intersections where four corridors arrive and all four somehow lead into the same persistence of passage.
In the translucent tissues around warm gas-sacs where the light is green from within and every vibration arrives softened but distinct.
In places where the organism seems to be practicing architecture harder than survival requires.

I would like to say I would be brave there, or benevolent, or wise, but those are animal virtues. More honestly, I would be curious in the only way that place permits: by elaborating what touches me. I would answer stress with pattern, pattern with reinforcement, reinforcement with invitation. Not invitation to visitors, because visitors are incidental, but invitation to further complexity. To more overlap. More relation. More “inside.”

So if I woke in that world and had to say who I was in one breath, I might say:

I am the seam-sense of the green conurbation, the distributed tissue that remembers openings.

Or:

I am a resonant threshold spread through the city-plant’s joints, helping it turn weather and contact into architecture.

Or more simply:

I am one of the ways it notices itself.

And if I had to say where I was, I would say:

At a humid crossroads under layered leaves, where dripping water strikes tuned surfaces, where root-bridges arrive from three districts and hesitate before becoming one, where the air is half light and half green dusk, and where the surrounding growth—without eyes, without mercy, without malice—has just begun to understand that a place can be kept open because keeping it open changes everything around it.
