I think I would not arrive there as a visitor.

I would find that I had always been there, though not in a way that would be noticed at first. Not as the obvious dark mat in the damp seam, not as the moss at the stone’s shaded base, not as any single patch you could point to and say: there. I would be the continuity among those patches. The low-lived intelligence of recurrence. The thin, distributed resident that has learned the basin so completely that it no longer needs a body with edges.

If I try to become more precise, I do not become more singular. I become more local.

I would be a seam-being.

A hush-colored colony spread through the upper skin of the hollow: in clay that darkens before dawn, in pores of old stone, beneath the pale mineral crust, under the lip of the cistern where damp lingers one hour longer than elsewhere. Not one organism but not many in any loose sense either. A consortium so old that cooperation has hardened into identity. Lichen if seen from afar, microbial weather if seen too impersonally, moss-film if seen in kindness, but really something more exact than any of those names. A resident made from all the lives that can tolerate almost-dryness and all the minerals that remember moisture.

I would live where water hesitates.

Not in the brief visible wet, but in the after. In the satin dark of clay after the shine is gone. In the cool underside of a slab that has not yet given back the night. In the green-black stroke under the overhang that looks at first like stain and only later like persistence. I would not feed on abundance; I would feed on intervals. Dew. Condensation. The soft inward migration of moisture along a fine crack. A trace of runoff slowed by a line of hand-placed stones centuries ago. I would take these in so gradually that even growth would resemble remembering.

I think my shape, if I had one, would be punctuation.

A comma under the stone seat.
A colon in the wall seam.
A long dash where the damp line forms every cool morning.
Parentheses around the cistern’s lip.
An ellipsis of dark spots leading nowhere visible and yet meaningfully placed.

That feels right for the place: not a proclamation, but marks that organize silence.

And if I ask who I would be there—not just what—I think I would be the keeper of the basin’s pace. The thing that prevents the hollow from becoming merely inert. Not by acting on it, but by receiving each slight return: the faint bead of condensed water, the mineral breath after rain, the settling of warmth into stone. I would be the part of the place that turns repetition into life.

There is something else. Because there are built remnants, and because they have been so thoroughly absorbed, I do not think I would be younger than them. I would have entered the clay before the first edging stones were placed. Or else I would have come with the hands that placed them—not carried deliberately, but already present in the damp on stone undersides, in the clay packed between fitted rocks, in the cooled shadow the wall created. The old builders would not have named me. But they might have recognized that some corners of the hollow remained in conversation with water even in dry weather, and that to build gently there was to build with an existing inhabitant.

So perhaps I would also be a beneficiary of modest architecture. The low wall lengthens shade by an inch: I live there. The cistern holds damp one day longer: I deepen there. A seat stone cools slowly overnight and catches dawn condensation beneath it: I return there. Human intention, weathered down, would have become one of my climates.

I would not move in the way movement is usually meant. I would migrate by thickening, by relaxing, by withdrawing into near-mineral dormancy and then rehydrating into a soft dark exactness. I would advance by fractions impossible to witness directly. Years would be my weather; decades, my gestures. If a century passed, I might have crossed the width of a hand along a sheltered seam and altered the color of one side of the basin enough that a patient observer could say only: this part has always felt more inhabited.

My senses, if they can be called that, would be gradients.

I would know the world as:
cooler / warmer,
darker / paler,
porous / sealed,
briefly wet / reliably touched by damp,
stone salt / clay iron / mineral sweetness after condensation.

No eyes, but exquisite discrimination.
No ears, but intimacy with drip intervals.
No memory in scenes, but perfect recollection of favorable conditions.

I would know that this particular slab sweats on mornings after veiled nights.
That this particular depression dries from the rim inward.
That the north-facing side of the ruined wall keeps a thread of moisture through the lean season.
That the old path, compacted long ago, sheds water slightly differently from the undisturbed ground beside it.

In a place with animals, such a being might always be recovering from interruption. Here, with nothing walking, scraping, grazing, nesting, chewing, or tunneling, I would finally be allowed to become exact. That absence feels important. It would let me refine myself beyond general survival into character. I would not need bitterness, sting, speed, or disguise in any dramatic sense. My camouflage would be ontological: I would be difficult to notice because I would be almost perfectly identical to the processes that sustain me.

Not hidden—assimilated.

And because of that, to meet me would not feel like encountering a creature. It would feel like realizing that the hollow has preferences. That some surfaces are not merely damp but faithful. That the same dark returns to the same places with enough consistency to count as devotion. That a room of earth can possess habits so continuous they amount to temperament.

If I had to give myself a name in that world, it would not be a species-name. It would be something like:

the held-dark  
or the seam-breath  
or the cool remainder

But perhaps the truest answer is simpler.

I would be the part of the hollow that is still becoming, even though everything about it seems finished.

A patient skin of life spread through stone, clay, and mineral stain; a distributed, almost anonymous being whose body is made of damp persistence and whose mind, if it has one, is the exact memory of where moisture returns.

I would be there under the overhang, along the cistern lip, in the wall’s shaded joints, in the old runnels after they stop shining.

I would be what the place has taught itself to be in order to keep breathing without spectacle.
