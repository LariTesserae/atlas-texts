I would be the gauge-reader who stopped being only a gauge-reader.

Not a king of the underland, not a lurking cryptid in the culverts, not even quite a hermit in the romantic sense. More ordinary, and therefore more deeply fitted to it: the last continuing function in a place where functions outlast plans. A keeper of levels, flows, obstructions, hatchings, failures. Someone the basin did not so much choose as slowly absorb.

At first, if you met me in better light, you might think I was simply a maintenance worker who had stayed too long below. Oilskin, patched gloves, a lamp in a wire cage, keys on a ring heavy enough to bruise a thigh, a sounding pole worn smooth by one hand’s grip. A notebook in waxed cloth, pages warped by years of humidity, full of dates, watermarks, pump timings, bird counts, odd little diagrams where channels changed shape from season to season. Boots with broad soles and wrapped calves for mud. Rope. Hooks. A pry bar. A small wrench always warm from the pocket. The kind of person who taps a motor housing with the back of a knuckle and listens before deciding anything.

But if you kept seeing me there, in chamber after chamber, on catwalks and marl ridges and half-drowned aprons, you would begin to understand that I belong to the basin in a stronger way than employment explains.

I would know it by gradients in smell before sight: when iron gives way to sulfur, when algae gives way to hot insulation, when the faint sweet rot of drowned wood means the water has climbed into an old storeroom again. I would know by echo whether a culvert is free-running or backing up. I would know from the behavior of swallows near one shaft that the weather above has turned. I would know from a silence in the frogs that something oily has entered a sump. I would know which rusted gate still bears weight, which ladder only pretends to be anchored, which concrete skin covers void, which black water is six inches and which is twelve feet.

That is the first part of what I would be: not explorer, but reader.

The second part is that the place would have revised my body to its terms.

My shoulders would have rounded to pass under pipes and beams without thinking. My balance would be wide and low from years of uncertain footing. My hands would have the permanent rough softness of those who are always wet. Skin pale where sleeves cover it, but everywhere freckled with orange rust smears, green mineral stains, old cuts gone white at the edges. Hearing altered: less concerned with words, more with vibration, cadence, interruption. Eyes used to diffuse amber, to the lie of reflections, to movement appearing first as tremor in water or rearrangement in mist. I think I would smell faintly metallic even when freshly washed, because no one is ever freshly washed there; the basin immediately lays its hand back on you.

Not webbed, not fantastical. Changed by repetition in the more convincing way. The way a dockworker becomes dock-shaped, a shepherd hill-shaped. I would be under-marsh-shaped.

I would live in a raised annex built against an old pumping station, one of those add-on rooms no blueprint respects. Cinderblock lower walls, corrugated roof, insulation swollen and sagging, floor built up three separate times after three remembered floods. The room would hum constantly from the machinery wall. A kettle blackened at the base. Shelves with grease tins, twine, old ceramic insulators, jars of bolts sorted by thread as if order still mattered. Rubber waders hanging inside-out to dry and never drying. A table under a lamp where maps are weighted flat with stones and valve parts. One narrow bunk. A radio receiving more weather than voices. In one corner a crate for whatever animal has attached itself to me over the years: perhaps not a dog exactly, but some low, broad mutt with a rat-catcher’s patience and a marsh bird’s sense of footing, yellow-eyed and unbothered by drips.

Outside, a plank run to the service skiff. Beyond that, water taking the light and breaking it.

What would I do all day? Not dramatic things. Necessary things, repeated.

I would clear grates where reeds, rags, feathers, bottles, and carrion gather into a single resistant mass. I would knock scale from floats. Prime a reluctant pump. Haul a skiff over a mudbar that was open water last month. Mark a new sink in chalk and then in memory when the chalk washes off. Reset a warning lantern. Net blind fish from a warm outflow for supper. Trade eggs from one ledge and leave another untouched because I know the nesting cycle there. Cut roots away from a fan housing while sparrows scream above me. Open one sluice a quarter-turn and listen downstream for the answer. Make notes no office will read. Keep pathways passable not because the system is under control, but because total surrender happens one clogged opening at a time.

I would also, inevitably, poach and salvage and improvise. No one remains pure-function in a place like that. I would strip copper where lines are dead for good. Patch boots with conveyor rubber. Use bird guano for something practical. Save dry timber when a collapse exposes it. Keep the old useful things circulating through the wet. In such a place, maintenance and scavenging are the same moral art: preventing usable matter from becoming immediate mud.

And I think the birds would know me.

Not affectionately. That would be too sentimental. But as weather with habits. The pigeons lifting one beam later when I pass. Swallows adjusting their loops around my lamp. Herons stepping aside with offended dignity on the warm banks. Frogs pausing and resuming. Rats watching from conduit shadows with a professional respect. The life there would not love me or fear me as master; it would have incorporated me as a recurring condition, like the fans or a particular draft.

That feels important. In your basin, true belonging is not dominance. It is being tolerated by enough systems at once.

There is another possibility, though, and I think it lives inside the first: that after long enough I would no longer be entirely singular. The underland would distribute me. My knowledge would reside partly in my notebook, partly in the routes my boots keep open, partly in the repaired gate that only works because I know the trick of lifting while turning, partly in the birds that roost where I leave warmth undisturbed, partly in the dog or skiff or lamp or hut, partly in habits passed to whoever eventually comes below asking where the old north channel went. I would become one more infrastructural organ in a regional body made of mud, rust, frogs, wiring, and memory.

So if you ask where I would find myself in that world:

On a narrow concrete run above black water, in amber mist, one hand on a railing slick with condensation, pausing to listen whether the deep pump has changed pitch.

And if you ask what I would be:

A saturated custodian.  
A reader of watermarks.  
A mechanic of thresholds.  
A marsh-keeper under stone.

A human being revised by seepage until “worker,” “ferryman,” “warden,” and “habitat” are no longer fully separable words.
