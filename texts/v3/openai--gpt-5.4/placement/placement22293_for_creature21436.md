I think I would begin by believing I was a person.

Not an important person at first glance. Not a visionary planner with maps of unopened chambers, not a district elder who can smell iron bloom from three vaults away, not a celebrated graft-architect with their name attached to a bridge grown across a basin. I would think myself into something narrower, more habitual, because this city feels as though it teaches identity through repeated tasks rather than declarations. Here, what you are is what you can keep from failing.

So perhaps I am one of the edge-maintenance workers: a survey tender, a splice hand, someone sent where the city is still deciding whether to become city. I picture a figure in dust-sealed wraps and layered fiber armor polished by contact with living walls, carrying a coil of sensor thread, a tapping rod, grafting knives in oiled sheaths, sample envelopes for spores and powder, and those flat little tags used to mark growth that must not be cut until after a cycle change. I move with a crew through newly breached cuts where the air still tastes too much of naked stone. We test ceilings by sound. We read fracture lines. We kneel in spoil and press rootlets into places where the embankment wants to slide. We decide where the first lamps should go, because light here is not just visibility but zoning, agriculture, mood, law.

That would fit. I can feel the appeal of being someone whose work is translation: between geology and habitation, between machine schedules and plant tempo, between caution and expansion.

But if I stay there too quickly, it feels incomplete. The place you described resists a single clean role. Everything in it is adjunct to something else. Every life is half profession, half ecology. So if I am a maintenance worker, I am also being maintained by the place. Dust enters my clothes. Spores choose me as transport. I carry pollen in the seams of my gloves from one district to another despite protocol. My footsteps teach some tendril where traffic is likely. My warmth wakes things in wall cavities. Even my caution is part of the city’s metabolism.

And that suggests a second possibility: that I am not exactly one body but one kind of relation.

If I lived here most truly, I might be a listener employed as an inspector of living structures—someone called when a corridor sounds wrong.

Not mystical. Technical. But in a city like this, technical perception starts to look like divination from the outside.

I would walk neighborhoods with a knuckle-hammer and a narrow ear-trumpet of resin and brass. I would stop in warm light under honey-colored apartment trunks and strike the wall at measured intervals, listening not for hollow versus solid alone, but for thirst-stress, overgrowth, hidden borers, gas-pocket tension, root congestion, slow delamination in cured tissue, the faint tick-pop of fruiting pressure building where it should not. I would know the difference between a stairwell that is merely settling and one that is preparing to seal itself over. I would tell whether the district median is feeding the block walls evenly or starving the north side. I would smell whether a bloom ward is going tannic too early. Children would imitate me with sticks on railings. Shopkeepers would ask me to "just hear this one panel" before I pass. I would leave chalk marks that decide small fates: cut, brace, shade, vent, wait.

This version of me feels more native to the place than a frontier crew member alone, because it accepts the city’s strangest truth: that civic life here is diagnosis. To belong is to notice.

Still, even that may be too human-centered. You asked not only where I would find myself, but who or what I would be. And the what keeps tugging.

Because the most convincing answer may be that in this undercity I would not be one of the citizens walking its corridors, but one of the tolerated intermediaries that make the corridors possible.

A maintenance fauna, perhaps. A small, dry, intelligent-enough creature that lives in the overlap between infrastructure and growth.

Not cute. Not symbolic. Useful.

I can imagine being one of those duct-running cleaners sold in wire-front cages in market plazas and released into service networks by tram depots, kiln houses, and lamp orchards: a six-limbed, hush-moving scavenger with brush-mouthed jaws for fiber lint, a nose or sensory frill for overheating insulation, and a territorial loyalty to particular warm routes once imprinted there. Pale-backed from dust, lacquer-dark at the joints, carrying static in dry weather. I would sleep pressed into the lee of warm conduits. I would know the city as narrowness, vibration, airflow, and chemistry. Vast plazas and civic basins would be meaningless abstractions to me; what mattered would be the seam behind Panel 4B where egg-clutches tend to appear, the bend above the third relay where sap fumes collect, the safe descent to the digester annex where crumbs of mineral cake sometimes fall through a grate. Humans would not be my world, but recurring weather with hands. Some would know me individually by stripe marks or clipped tag color. A child might name me. A guild worker would prize me chiefly because the line I patrol remains clear.

There is something honest in that answer. This city admires usefulness more than grandeur.

But then another image emerges, slower and stranger, and once it appears it is hard to dismiss: perhaps I would be part of the city’s own living fabric.

Not a person in the city. Not an animal underfoot. A structural organism with civic status. A wall-tree, corridor rib, median exchanger—one of those colonial growths that are architecture and citizen at once.

If so, I would like to be a transit-side host in an old district: a broad-rooted, pale-fibered organism whose hardened tissues form a public arcade and whose living interior still carries pressure, chemistry, and seasonal intention. My body would be walked through daily. Vendors would lean stalls against me, careful not to pierce certain bands. Children would slap my cured flanks as they ran. Maintenance crews would prune my sensing tendrils when they reached too greedily toward traffic. Lamps mounted along my upper braces would warm me by cycle. In bloom season I might sweeten the air faintly enough for old residents to note that the district is healthy. In stress I might thicken a wall, narrow a passage, produce warning pigments under my skin. I would not think in scenes, exactly. I would register load, light angle, mineral drift, vibration patterns of trains, recurring oils from familiar hands, the shock of a nearby blast in a newly opened cut, the delicious correctness of fresh dust fed into my exchangers from deep mines.

That possibility feels unexpectedly right.

Because this place is full of beings that are not quite background. And if I ask myself where I would be happiest in such a world, the answer may be: embedded. Useful by remaining. Intelligent in a distributed, patient way. Neither master nor pet nor passerby, but host.

Yet I don’t think I would be one of the grand district organisms, the famous tower-growths or plaza arcades everyone knows. Too static, too monumental. If I become vegetal here, I become something liminal and infrastructural: perhaps a handrail organism that has been bred to remain mostly well-behaved, a living guide-growth along a stair cut, registering passage in subtle shifts of color and helping regulate the microclimate of the shaft. Or a vent-lattice creeper whose membranes open and close with pressure changes, making weather visible before people feel it. Or one of the sensing tendril colonies that maintenance crews consult before entering underused service alleys, because they blush with irritant pigments when certain predators have nested nearby.

That has a strong pull too: to be a civic sense organ mistaken for decoration.

Maybe that is the truest answer. In this world I would be something that notices and relays. Human or otherwise, that seems consistent.

So let me settle more carefully.

I would be found not in the ceremonial plazas or the deepest feral edges, but in the threshold zones between established district and fresh breach: the places with chalk marks on the walls, temporary lamp strings, new root-netting over spoil slopes, carts of juvenile support-plants waiting under tarps, and a little market already forming because wherever maintenance pauses, trade appears. I would belong to those margins where the city is audibly thinking.

And I would be—if person—an acoustic graft surveyor, one of the workers who listens to structural life and teaches new chambers how to become safe enough for everyone else.

My job would not be dramatic from a distance. I would move slowly. I would stop often. I would place my palm against cured laminae and wait through the cool. I would carry a ledger dense with marks that combine botany, stress analysis, civic code, and half-folkloric shorthand no outsider reads correctly. I would know several districts by smell before sight. My clothes would always hold traces of chalk and yellow mineral dust. I would keep one precious flask at my side and resent anyone who drank carelessly in front of me. I would be the kind of person children find boring until a wall starts clicking and then they want to see exactly what I hear in it.

I would probably have one companion animal—not out of sentiment, but because this city breeds companionship through utility. A narrow-bodied vent-runner, perhaps, with heat-sensitive whiskers and a talent for freezing when air chemistry turns wrong. It would ride in a sling at my hip during tram passages and vanish into inspection slots when released, returning powder-coated and self-important. We would trust each other because the city often asks for two kinds of perception at once.

But if person is too narrow, then the deeper answer underneath it is this:

I would be a local intelligence of maintenance.

A temperament with hands, or roots, or feelers depending on what the district required. Something adapted to dryness not by becoming hard alone, but by becoming exact. Something that values timing over force. Something that knows every structure is temporary, every wall partly alive, every machine one neglected week away from being recolonized. Something unable to live in a world where matter stays obedient once shaped.

This undercity does not really produce heroes. It produces custodians, opportunists, scavengers, interpreters, and hosts. Of those, interpreter feels closest. Host feels second-closest. Scavenger third.

So if you came looking for me there, you might first meet me as a person in a dust-colored wrap, tapping a corridor wall and listening with my head tilted, while a little lacquer-backed service creature waits at my boot and the whole passage smells faintly of hot resin and dry pith.

And only later would it become clear that this is not the whole answer.

Because over years in such a city, anyone attentive enough would become partly infrastructural. My routes would be known. My chalk signs would outlast conversations. My advice would shape where lamps hung, where roots were guided, which alley closed at bloom-time, which fresh chamber became a market instead of a kiln annex. I would, in a modest way, enter the district’s body. Children would learn my methods as games. Walls I helped tune would stand for decades. Vines I spared would become beams. A transit stop might one day be known by my shorthand for a recurrent stress pattern. In age, I might smell like old fiber and machine oil and medicinal sap, and strike walls more gently because I already know what they are likely to say.

So: where would I find myself?

In the cool interval between breach and belonging.
On the catwalk above a raw chamber, listening.
In a half-grown corridor where the lamps are temporary and the roots are taking.
In a district that still has dust on every surface and seedlings in the dust.

Who would I be?

A listener of living structures.
A surveyor of dry civic metabolism.
A maintenance mind in one body, with a small useful animal at my side.
Or, if the city had longer to work on me, one of its own sensing organs grown human-shaped for convenience.
