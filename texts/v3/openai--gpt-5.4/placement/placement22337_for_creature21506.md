I don't become the great whole, either. Not the entire watershed mind. That would be too easy, too total, and strangely too simple. Not exactly a tower, not exactly a plant, not exactly a room.

That feels right because your landscape is all circulation and revision. Nothing there needs a sovereign. It needs interfaces. A tall, open column on an exposed promontory, maybe where three terrace systems meet and the air is always in motion. Inside, not emptiness exactly. A tuned emptiness. Vertical air with interruptions.

Ribs of stone. Curtains of roots. Suspended seed-pods that dry and knock. Soft bladders that inflate in the cooler hours and slacken by noon. Thin films stretched over side apertures, trembling with pressure changes. Water descending not in one stream but in patient fractions: bead, slide, pause, gather, release. At certain depths, chambers where droplets fall onto hollow shelves and produce regular notes; at others, packed sponge-tissues that hold stormwater and slowly feed capillary walls. When wind crosses the mouth, the whole interior speaks—not expressively, not theatrically, but diagnostically. Different moisture loads, different tones. Different fullness in the basins below, different resonance. A storm approaching from the rim would enter me before it reached the lower courts. That is who I think I would be: **a local organ of attention**.

Not sentient in the animal sense. Not making decisions with speed or centrality. More like a site where the distributed being comes slightly into focus. If the whole marsh-mountain is the mind, I am a vowel in its speech. A chamber where continuity hears a contour and passes it on.

And because the place preserves open air so carefully, because it likes long sightlines and framed distances, I think my function would also be visual in a non-eyed way. My apertures would align with valleys, reservoir chains, condensate parapets. Not to look out, exactly, but to maintain relation. To keep districts in correspondence through pressure, sound, and airflow. A tower as synapse.

There is something almost liturgical about that. Not worship—more like civic weather. A public utility of perception.

If I imagine my life there over time, I would begin relatively young: fractures still sharp in my base, my shaft only partly colonized, tones simple, metallic almost, with more bare resonance than living complexity. Then season by season, storm by storm, I would be completed by occupation. Roots would discover my seams and thicken them. Mineral deposits would soften some edges and sharpen others. A side chamber opened by crack or collapse would become a moisture lung. Vines might briefly descend across my mouth and then pull free in a storm, changing my voice for years. Birds never come. No nests. No guano. Nothing accidental in the animal way. Only weather and growth, endlessly editing the instrument.

Eventually I would no longer be distinguishable as structure hosting life or life exploiting structure. I would be both conditions continuously. My lower cistern would store not just water but recurrence: chemical traces of seasons, sediments from distant failures, spores or cells or microscopic residues that in this world function as memory. My upper lip would condense cloud and feed vertical gardens down my interior face. My tones would guide compatible movement—perhaps not intentionally, but effectively. A careful traveler could learn me. The pitch I make in east wind might indicate a safe crossing still unsubmerged. A low intermittent booming at dusk might mean the marsh courts are overtopping. So perhaps I would also be, from the visitor's perspective, an oracle. But only by being useful, not mystical.

Part of me could descend as a chain of overflow basins, each tuned by drop-height rather than wind. The self would persist not as material continuity but as hydrological task: receiving, translating, relaying.
