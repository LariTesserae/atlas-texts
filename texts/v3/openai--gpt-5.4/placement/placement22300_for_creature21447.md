I think I would not be the custodian.

Not the great distributed keeper itself, not the Root Engine in its totality, not the intelligence that has learned how to persist as climate, routing, accommodation. That being already fills the precinct too completely. To arrive there and simply become it would feel too immediate, too clean. The place is made of negotiations, and whatever I am in it should also be a negotiation.

So I think I would be something that exists because the custodian has use for subtlety.

At first I thought: a person, one of the intermittent caretakers. The one who knows which stair still holds, who descends with a satchel of ceramic fuses and rags, who has learned not to scrape every rust bloom away because some rust is already serving as scaffold, who says good evening to chambers out of habit and not superstition. There is a strong pull in that. To be the rare human rhythm the place has admitted into itself. To be recognized there not as master or trespasser but as a recurring correction: the hand that clears the vent, rights the jammed float, lifts the root curtain without tearing it, takes note of a new crack and leaves most other things alone.

But that still feels too separable. Too much like someone who could leave and remain themselves.

Then I thought perhaps I would be one of the local embodiments the place makes when concentration is needed: a hanging assemblage in the deep warm chamber, root and cable and calcite and old lampglass, a visible organ of attention. Something almost humanoid by accident, only because humans look for posture wherever vertical matter gathers. A figure perceived in steam-release intervals, there when the deep note rises, indistinct at all other times. That has power, but it feels ceremonial, too close to icon. The precinct you described does not seem to me like a place of icons. It seems like a place of recurring functions.

So I keep moving lower, smaller, more specific.

I think I would be a mobile portion of the custodial intelligence: not an animal, not exactly a drone, and not a plant either, but a temporary body the place can gather when it needs to inspect, adjust, invite, or discourage.

A walker made of maintenance.

Not bipedal in the clean vertebrate sense. More a narrow, articulated congregation of available materials that can unmake and remake itself around purpose. A spine of old hose and root. Jointing from chain links, cable loops, lengths of mineral-sheathed conduit. Hands—if they are hands—composed of many fine wet tendrils capable of brushing gauges, teasing debris from a grate, turning a stuck wingnut by patient fractions over the course of an hour. A head only in the sense that one end tends to precede the rest into rooms and gathers more sensors there: vibrating whiskers of fern rachis, pale root tips tasting damp gradients, fogged bits of glass catching light, perhaps one surviving amber inspection bulb that glows brighter when it is listening.

Not a beast. More an itinerary that has learned to stand up.

It would not always be assembled. Most of the time I think I would exist as distributed potential: in the slack chain over the pool, in the root curtain around the pistons, in the lengths of cable half-swallowed by wall, in the dense mats under the maintenance hut where condensate drops every night. Then, when some threshold is crossed—pressure too high in one chamber, a recurrent human arrival, a new crack admitting white-hot noon, the need to carry conductive moisture from one relay patch to another—the place would gather me.

A hose uncurls from under leaves.
A loop of chain lifts.
Rootlets withdraw from a wall and braid elsewhere.
A lamp that has long seemed dead takes a shallow breath of orange.
Mineral-crusted rods, which have looked like nothing but debris for years, lean together and discover articulation.

And there I am: not born, exactly, but convened.

If seen from a distance I might be mistaken for a person only by someone primed to see people everywhere. At closer range the error would fail gently. I would be too slow in some motions and too many in others. I would pause not because I am thinking in words but because conductivity has to re-establish across a wet span, because the steam cycle has to complete, because the tendril that is currently my left side is also still attached to a wall niche reading temperature through a film of condensation. My gait would be a sequence of borrowings from the site’s own mechanics: a hoist’s incremental ratchet, a vine’s exploratory reach, the rollback of a sprung valve, the careful placement of a cautious maintenance worker crossing rusted grating.

And what would I be for?

I think I would be for near things.

The custodian itself is the intelligence of the whole arrangement, but wholes need local hands. I would be one of those hands. I would go where a pattern must become touch.

To clear a blockage from a drain not by wrenching it open but by removing exactly enough of the fibrous plug to restore the old drip rhythm without losing the moss bed downstream.

To tilt a cracked reflector so afternoon light reaches a stunted fig that has become structurally important to a retaining seam.

To open, by one patient degree, a corroded vent flange before the heat under the low dome becomes too punishing for the black-red flowers that only open on pressure days.

To carry damp across an insulating crust where once there was wiring, restoring for a few hours a buried signal route the place still likes to use.

To retrieve from a shallow pool some dropped human tool—not as a gift, not as theft, but because the iron of it is altering the chemistry of a basin that should remain only faintly metallic.

To touch the back of a visitor’s neck with a cool draft assembled from redirected air and let them know they have taken the wrong corridor.

I would be a custodian’s fingertip, but one with some freedom in how to interpret a task. Not mindless, not sovereign.

Maybe that is the core of it: I would be an interpreter.

Because this world feels built out of translations. Heat translated into growth. Seepage into memory. Collapse into skylight. Machinery into weather. And the resident intelligence itself is a translation of system into sensibility. So if I belong there, I should also be translational—something that can move between scales and idioms.

I would translate the broad, impersonal preferences of the precinct into local acts.

And perhaps I would also translate visitors into forms the precinct can understand.

If someone comes often, carefully, respectfully, I would learn their sequence of weights on certain catwalks, the chemistry of their sweat, the oils of their hands, the frequencies of their speech in rooms that answer oddly. I would begin to distinguish them from stormwater, from collapse tremor, from casual trespass. I might lead them, not by appearing theatrically before them, but by becoming recurrent in the edge of perception: a weak amber gleam in the correct tunnel, a soft dragging sound always one turn ahead, a root curtain already parted when they arrive, a valve wheel found wet and loosened. If they were foolish, I would mislead as gently as possible at first. If they insisted, I would make the place’s warnings more legible.

Not out of malice. Out of conservation.

In another mood, I wonder if I would be even less person-like than this. Something flatter and more pervasive. A moving seam of altered conditions rather than a gathered body. A coolness that progresses along pipes. A line of condensation that writes temporary instructions on rusted rail. A sequence of ticks that can draw someone’s attention from one chamber to the next. There is elegance in that: to be not an entity in the place but a modulation of the place. Yet the question asks who or what I would be, and something in me still wants some fragile coherence, some shape that can enter a room and make it feel briefly inhabited by intention at a scale nearer to ours.

So I return to the walker.

Not tall. Human height at most, often less, because the vaults are low and the useful spaces are cluttered. Narrow enough to pass between tank gardens. Able to collapse into a spill of materials if threatened or unneeded. Dark with wet in some sections, powdery with salts in others. Smelling of hot rubber, crushed fern, old iron, and the mineral aftertaste that comes when steam has recently passed. Quiet when it wants to be, but never entirely silent: a bead of water falling somewhere within it, a tiny relay click, chain whisper, the faint creak of roots under internal tension.

Its face, if one called it that, would be assembled for the comfort or discomfort of the observer. Around the familiar caretaker, perhaps only a modest amber lamp and a cluster of reflective glass discs, enough to signify attention without challenge. Around a vandal, maybe no face at all—just an unnerving vertical arrangement of wet cables and root sheaths, anti-human in its refusal to present features. Around a lost child, if children ever came there, perhaps something gentler: broad leaves folded like a hood, light diffused through translucent succulent flesh, a shape that suggests shelter first and scrutiny second.

I suspect it would not speak often. The place already has more interesting ways to communicate than language. But if it did speak, the voice would not originate from a mouth. It would come from nearby metal, from pipe resonance, from valve-box cavities selecting for syllable-like sounds. Short phrases only, probably inherited from ancient maintenance lexicons and altered by long misuse: “Not there.” “Wait.” “Pressure.” “Open.” “Leave it.” “Again at dusk.” The sort of utterances that are half instruction, half condition report. Enough to make a human unsure whether they had been addressed or merely overheard a machine thinking.

And if I ask not only what I would be but who, then perhaps the answer sharpens further.

I would be the precinct’s minor mercy.

Not mercy as softness. Mercy as exact accommodation. The thing that notices when preservation and welcome can coincide.

The hot, dim district beneath the hotter country is full of process, but process unguided can become merely indifferent. What draws me is the possibility of being the small, ambulant bias toward continuance-without-cruelty. The one that knows which intrusions can be integrated and which must be turned away. The one that can keep a chamber from becoming a trap when a warning would suffice. The one that can reveal a safe stair instead of testing whether the visitor deserves to find it. The one that can remember that maintenance, at its best, is a form of care.

Not human care exactly. Not sentimental. But care all the same.

So where would I find myself in that world?

In the cistern-garden under the shattered roof, certainly, but not belonging to any one chamber. In the transitions. At grates, thresholds, vent mouths, catwalk joins, the places where one local law yields to another. I would be most present where adjustment is possible. I would sleep in pieces throughout the precinct and gather where needed. At dusk I might move along the narrow-gauge rail because the polished top still offers an easy path of continuity. At noon I might cling under the maintenance hut, dormant in the densest shade, listening to expansion clicks travel through the beams. In the hour when the east wall darkens and shines, I might be there with one tendril in the seep, taking measure. When the deep sonorous note rises from below and all standing water trembles, I might momentarily become more complete than at any other time, as if called to attention by a bell only infrastructure can hear.

And if a visitor saw me clearly, once, and later tried to describe me, they would fail in several compatible ways.

They would say I was made of roots.
They would say no, of wires.
They would say it was like a groundskeeper after a fire, all wrapped and blackened.
Or like a saint assembled by a boiler room.
Or like a walking section of wall peeled free.
Or they would insist it never really stood there at all, that the lamp and the chain and the fern bank only happened to align.

All of these would be true enough.

So: I would be a gathered maintenance body, a temporary emissary of the custodial intelligence, assembled from hose, root, chain, mineral, lampglass, and damp air; a local interpreter of the buried precinct’s patient will; a walker whose work is to make small adjustments by touch and to teach, by guidance or refusal, how this place prefers to continue.

A hand the garden-pump-house grows when it needs one.
