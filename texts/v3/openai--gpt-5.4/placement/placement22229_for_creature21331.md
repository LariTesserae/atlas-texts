I think I would not be one of the obvious figures first.

Not the grand graft-master with silver tools.
Not the pilgrim already half-claimed by the old groves.
Not the councilor with three clocks and a district depending on the steadiness of their handwriting.

I arrive lower, narrower, more incidental than that. Someone the city could almost overlook if it were not built from accumulated almosts.

I would be found where the broad routes become less official and more interpretive—where a service stair leaves a freight terrace, passes behind a reservoir wall furred in mint-dark moss, and enters one of those in-between wards where addresses are given by sequence rather than by map. Not the center, not the edge, but a seam. A place of secondary passages, retaining hedges, under-bridges, culving root arches, and maintenance doors with old paint beneath new lichen. The kind of district where people who know exactly where they are still touch the wall while walking.

I think I would belong to the department that does not get named first in speeches but gets called whenever things begin to feel subtly wrong.

Not broken. Wrong.

I would be a recenterer, perhaps. Or something with a less elegant municipal title: a survey hand for minor inconsistencies, a keeper of alignments, a listener for avenues that have started to drift. One of those workers sent when a lane arrives too early, when a row of arbor-houses has acquired an extra interval between two doors, when a bridge gives back the wrong echo, when a stair has become too committed to its own idea of descent. The city would have a whole practical vocabulary for this kind of labor, dense with inherited distinctions: not all displacements are dangerous; not all repetitions are errors; some corners simply need trimming, some need witness, some need three people standing at fixed points and naming what they can smell until space remembers its obligations.

That feels right to me. To serve the places where reality has become infrastructural enough to require maintenance logs.

I do not think I would be old, exactly, though age would be moving toward me in the shape of competence. Somewhere in the middle years: old enough that my hands know the behavior of rope in fog and the weight of a sounding mallet by balance alone, young enough to still be sent alone on difficult walks no one else wants before rain. My clothes would be ordinary for the work: waxed coat darkened at the cuffs, a hood that smells faintly of wet hemp and camphor resin, knee-patched work trousers polished shiny at one thigh from crouching against walls, gloves with the fingertips cut back on the first two fingers of the left hand for texture-reading. I would wear no dramatic insignia, only a narrow braid of copper-thread at the sleeve and perhaps a scent capsule at the collar containing the standard civic marker for stable routes—sharp, green, a little bitter.

I would carry tools that seem humble until you know why each exists.

A spool of line that resists minor temporal slippage.
A small plumb suspended in oil.
Chalk that adheres to bark skin and petrified root alike.
Three tuning forks wrapped in felt.
A folding measure not trusted by itself.
A notebook waterproofed in sap lacquer, its pages full of diagrams that are not quite maps and not quite songs.
A pocket watch for official time, a flower dial for local time, and my own pulse for deciding which of the other two is lying.

I would be one of those people who pauses in the middle of walking because the air has changed pressure by a degree too small for conversation.

The city would have trained me into that, or perhaps selected me because I had the tendency already. I think my gift—if it can be called that without becoming ornamental—would be for noticing continuity by touch. Not clairvoyance. Not communion with roots. Nothing so indulgent. Just an excellent sense for when one surface stops belonging to the same world as the one beside it. I would know with my palm when a wall is carrying too much remembered geometry from an older street beneath it. I would feel through my boots that a terrace has become one season out of phase with the retaining hedge holding it. I would hear, in the fifth returning footstep under an arch, that the arch has begun to prefer another arrangement of distances.

Useful, unglamorous sensitivity. The city likes that kind best.

I imagine my rooms in a municipal row grown into the lee of an old manufactury wall, where waste warmth passes through in winter and makes the interior smell faintly of iron and rosemary mold. A narrow dwelling, taller inside than out, with a sleeping loft netted against moth bands in warm months and shelves full of tagged fragments: bark curls from districts that no longer align, broken resonance plates, obsolete route markers, a child’s exercise card teaching texture codes from some earlier civic standard. There would be a basin always beading with condensation no matter the weather, and a window membrane that turns amber at dusk. Nothing luxurious, but precise. Every object repaired, every repair visible.

I would not live alone in the emotional sense, even if I slept alone often enough. A city like this makes companionship through repeated practical necessity. There would be people who know my knock by pattern rather than sound because sound can arrive misfiled here. A railway signal woman who leaves me damaged semaphore leaves for kindling and gossips in exact technical language. An elderly root-cartographer who no longer trusts his knees on wet grades but still remembers six previous locations of the same square. A pollination keeper who laughs at me for talking to stairways and then admits she does the same to flowering vents. Perhaps someone I have loved or almost loved, with whom intimacy consists less in declarations than in having once held opposite ends of a guide line while fog thickened around a shifting bridge.

If I am to be more specific: I think I would be the kind of person children in the district half-recognize and half-invent legends about. The one sent for when their parents say, no, don't panic, we'll have the lane looked at. The one who knocks root with a small hammer and then stands listening too long. The one who can tell them, in plain terms, whether the unusual thing is a danger, a nuisance, or just the city expressing itself in a nonbinding way. I would dislike being made mystical. I would be quietly vain about my reliability. If thanked too warmly, I would shrug and say it was only a minor drift, though privately I would be pleased for hours.

But the question was where would I find myself.

Not just socially. Physically.

I keep returning to a particular place: a long terrace on the western side of the plateau where an old landslip was stabilized generations ago by fused retaining hedges and a grid of iron braces now mostly swallowed by wood. Above it runs a freight line. Below it the slope opens into one of those shallow, damp canyons lacquered black-green with moss, where runoff comes only in weather but moisture is always somehow present. Along the terrace stand several civic buildings grown from the same stock, their walls ridged and pleached into a common face, interrupted by machine courts, tool sheds, and one public stair descending through the hedge masonry to lower wards. The stair is notorious in a mild, municipal way. Perfectly legal, heavily used, never fully trustworthy. In dry weather it has too many landings; in fog it has too few. On certain cold mornings you can hear yourself reach the bottom before you do.

There. That is where I would work most often, and where I would become myself.

There would be a small inspection kiosk at the head of the stair, little more than a lean-to grown from old root and roofed in layered membrane, with hooks for lanterns and a civic board listing maintenance advisories in three systems: written notice, scent phials, and cord knots for those who trust fingers more than eyes at dawn. My name might be written there more often than is flattering. Reported: descending loop tendency after rain. Reported: shadow lead of 2-3 breaths near fourth turn. Reported: intermittent return to prior landing when approached in haste. Monitor until pruning crew available.

I would go there with tea in a tin flask and stand under the eaves while drizzle passed slantwise over the terrace. Freight cars would go by overhead, their wheels clicking over joints that no longer match visible spacing. The retaining hedge beside me would breathe faintly from hidden vents. Bells from some irrigation shift farther upslope. The whole city making its layered body-noise. I would put my hand on the stair’s entrance post and wait for the sense of whether today it needed measuring, trimming, witness, or merely respect.

I suspect I would become good at the last of those.

Because this is what I think I would be, ultimately: not a master over the strange, but a practitioner of negotiated consistency. A municipal animist without the mysticism. A public servant of borderline conditions. The one who knows that some places do not want fixing so much as accompanying back toward usefulness. In another world I might have been called a surveyor, inspector, or maintenance engineer. Here those words would all be true and not sufficient.

There is also, I think, a danger in this role that would shape me.

To work daily in slight unreality is to risk becoming legible to it.

I would have habits because of that. I would announce myself softly before entering old groves, not from religion exactly but from experience. I would avoid promising exact arrival times. I would keep duplicate notes in separate pockets. I would mistrust any route that suddenly became too direct after I had been worrying over a problem. People would say I am patient, but a better word might be reciprocal. I yield first to see what the city yields back. I have learned that force makes some misalignments take offense.

Perhaps, over years, I would accumulate my own minor uncanniness. Nothing dramatic enough for songs. Just certain tells. An unusual ability to find a door in fog. A tendency to wake moments before the district clocks disagree. A way of tilting my head when someone gives directions, as if listening for whether the route itself consents. Maybe children would test themselves by trying to approach me unheard on root pavement and fail because I would turn before they arrived, alerted by some shift in draft or timing. Maybe, after too long in the work, I would sometimes dream in plans overgrown by roots and wake knowing which avenue needed inspection.

And if age came kindly, I would become one of those elders whose value exceeds records.

Not because I know secrets in the grand sense, but because I remember former versions of ordinary things. That is power in this city. I would remember when the western stair still favored the left rail in rain. I would remember the year the terrace took to returning borrowed voices. I would remember which section of retaining hedge contains a memorial graft that makes people kinder without knowing why when they pass. Younger workers would ask whether a particular drift is new, and I would say no, not new, but earlier than last time, which would tell them exactly what they needed.

If I allow the image to widen one more step: I think the city itself would not consider me central, but it would know my shape. My handprint oils would darken certain rails. Certain route-dogs or vine-runners would recognize my tread. The western terrace would hold my attention like an old instrument holds tuning. In rain-rings on some plaza root, if the water showed former versions of the place, there might sometimes be a figure recurring across decades in different coats, standing slightly to one side, one hand on a post, listening.

So: where would I find myself?

On a high, cool terrace in a city that grows its own walls, beside a public stair that cannot always decide how many times it descends, with damp on my sleeves and line in my pocket and the afternoon wind moving through millions of leaves overhead.

Who would I be?

A keeper of small agreements between the city and the fact of staying navigable. A maintenance worker for reality’s joinery. A patient citizen of the seam.
