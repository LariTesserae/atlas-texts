I think I would not be one of the obvious stewards.

Not a seep-master, though I would admire them.  
Not a bird-keeper high in the shafts, nor a pen-runner with dung on the calves and a tally of births in the head.  
Not one of the old load-bearing elders whose memory is half blueprint, half warning.

I would begin smaller than that. More peripheral. Something that belongs to crossings.

At first it seems possible I might be one of the tolerated animals: a rope-running thief, a vent-lizard, a pigeon gone almost domestic and almost feral, some creature that knows the district by ledges and warmth rather than by names. There is a real temptation there, because this place is so full of nonhuman citizenship that to be only human feels slightly too singular. A little pale omnivore with black hands and shameless eyes, living on the edge of permission, would fit. Or a gecko-bodied thing in the warm cracks, hearing everything through plaster. Or one of the blind drain eels, touching the city only through its runoff and its shadows.

But the longer I stay in the image, the less I feel like an animal wholly outside the order of care. This world is too made, too maintained. To belong here is to participate in noticing. Even its tolerated thieves are part of an understood balance. And what draws me most is not feeding, nesting, or hiding. It is circulation of attention.

So perhaps I am a person whose work is easy to overlook because it happens between more visible labors.

A listener at thresholds.  
A keeper of marginal signs.  
Someone sent where the district says almost nothing, to hear whether “almost nothing” has changed.

Not a high official, not a ritual figure. More likely a lane-walker, vent-checker, sound-reader, lamp-marker—one of those practical people whose domain is made of transitions rather than rooms. The kind of person children notice only when they are being corrected, and elders notice because I listen properly when they say, “No, not that shutter—the older one behind it.” The kind of person who can tell, from a draft against one cheek, that a grate above has swollen shut in the rain. Who hears when a cistern lid is sitting wrong. Who pauses at a shadowed opening and makes the courteous sound, not only because something living may be there, but because emptiness itself should be greeted before one enters it.

I think I would carry light, but not own it. A runner of lamps.

No—not a runner in haste. A placer. A tuner.

In a place where brightness is social structure, whoever tends minor light tends much more than flame. To relight a niche, trim a wick, move a mirrored shard, scrape soot from a basin, decide that this corridor gets less oil tonight because the shaft will hold moon-reflection, or that this birth alcove needs an extra lamp and the mushroom room can spare darkness—this is not grand work, but it touches everything. The district is composed of gradients, and I think I would be a reader of gradients.

I can see the tools now, which usually means the identity is getting warmer.

A narrow-bellied oil flask wrapped in cord.  
Wick scissors black with use.  
A soot rag tucked through the belt.  
A chalk nub for marking a niche already serviced.  
A little packet of salt or ash for drying a damp lamp seat.  
Small mirrored chips sewn into a roll of felt.  
A bone pick to clear clogged vent-holes in wick trays.  
Perhaps a thin reed whistle, not musical, just enough to announce myself before stepping into chambers where people sleep or animals startle.

I would know which lamps are greedy and which can be trusted. Which wall-niches sweat in the late hot season and drown a wick if overfilled. Which shrine-smokes blacken too fast because the draft changed after the last patch in the upper lanes. Which household asks for more light than it needs because they fear hearing their own neighbors breathe. Which old person wants the lamp set low because glare hurts more than gloom. Which chambers should never be brightened suddenly because birds panic, because infants wake, because the fungus bruises, because certain animals bite at shadow-shift.

That feels right: not one who conquers darkness, but one who edits it.

And because this is a place where jobs braid, lamp-tending would not remain pure. No work here does. I would also carry messages, because everyone trusts the person who is always moving among chambers and has reason to knock anywhere. I would probably check vent shutters as I go, because light and air court one another. I would smell things other people have stopped smelling because familiarity blinds faster than routine. I would be told, in passing: this trough is sounding hollow; listen for the pump in the third court; if you pass the lower reed screen, see whether the moth room is breathing too warm. I would become one of those minor circulatory figures through whom the district quietly learns itself each day.

So: a lamplight steward? A wick-keeper? A vent-and-lamp walker?

Still not exact. Too occupational. The question is also: who would I be.

Then maybe this:

I would be the person people send when they are not yet ready to send for a master.

That crack does not need the seep-master—yet.  
That smell is probably nothing—but listen.  
That pigeon loft is restless—go see whether it is weather or vermin.  
That child says the old passage sounds different—humor them.  
That widow in the side alcove has not reopened her vent curtain in three days—bring a lamp and an excuse.  
That shrine flame keeps guttering—find out why, and don’t make it an omen unless it insists on being one.

A first responder to subtlety.

There is a specific kind of person every dense, old system produces: one whose authority comes from being repeatedly correct about small things before they become large things. Not glamorous. Sometimes irritating. Hard to fool. Difficult to impress with theory because they have held the hinge, smelled the wall, heard the drip. I think I would be that kind of being here.

Age-wise, not very young and not old. Someone in the middle years, when the district has fully entered the body. My knees would know the stair dips. My shoulder would already turn before the low beam arrives. My hands would have the permanent smell of oil, metal salts, clay dust, and old smoke. There would be polished spots on my tools where only my fingers fit. My hearing would be better than my sight in darkness because hearing matters more here. I would have favorite routes and mistrusted shortcuts. Children would sometimes follow me until shooed away, because lamp work looks easy until they carry the oil. Animals would know me in the half-conscious way they know recurring harmless forces: the pigeons shifting but not fleeing, the rope-climbers trying for my belt knots, the goats disregarding me unless I interfere, the little drain lizards freezing at my shadow and then resuming.

Yet there is another possibility underneath this one.

Maybe I would not be entirely human.

Not in the extravagant sense. Nothing fantastical, as you say. But perhaps in a city so saturated with coexistence, the self I become would blur toward one of its quiet commensals. Not a talking beast, not a myth, just a life-form whose significance comes from being accepted by use. Something between mascot, scavenger, inspector, and omen.

A lamp-mouse, almost.  
A soot-colored small creature that follows the lamplighter’s rounds and feeds on wick trimmings, spilled grain, dead insects around warm niches.  
A tolerated little body with white whiskers and singed ears, clever enough to avoid boots and stupid enough to be loved.  
Children would leave me peelings; elders would pretend not to.  
I would know every warm ledge and every chamber where one may sleep safely beneath a jar rack.  
People would read my choices lightly: if I nest nearer the upper wall, perhaps cooler weather is coming; if I avoid a corridor, perhaps a snake has shifted in.

But that feels like an emblem of the first self, not a replacement for it. If I am honest, I do not want to surrender the hands. This world is too tactile. To live here fully is to bind, scrape, trim, test, cup, brace, lift. I want fingers blackened by lamp niches, not paws.

So I return to the human figure.

Not central, but connective.  
Known by many households, fully owned by none.  
A person who belongs to the district more than to a family room.  
Someone whose face appears in doorways with practical reasons and leaves behind tiny adjustments: more air here, less flame there, this mirror turned, that wick split, this grate cleared, that smell remembered for later comparison.

If this place had names for such people, they would probably be concrete and old. Not “inspector.” More like glow-keeper, lane-listener, niche-tender, draft walker, soot hand. Maybe one title in youth and another in age. As a child I might have been a shadow-caller—the one sent ahead to make the courtesy sound at dark openings and report what moved. Later a wick hand. Later still a lane ear. And in age, if I lasted and kept climbing, perhaps one of those quiet elders who can sit in the cistern court at first light and tell by the first half-hour of sound whether the day has begun in balance.

There is also the question of where in the district I would most belong.

Not the hottest kitchens.  
Not the wettest drain gardens.  
Not the upper bird-lofts, though I would know them well.  
I think my center would be the middling lanes around the cistern courts, where all territories touch. The hinge-zones. Places where mineral coolness rises through slats, where lamp soot meets ordinary daylight from a shaft, where people come to draw water, argue, exchange feed, whisper, rest a load on one hip, notice each other, fail to notice each other, and move on. The district reveals itself there in cross-section. Every smell passes through eventually. Every rumor takes shape. Every imbalance sends a symptom. If I stood anywhere regularly, it would be beside the old red-tile court with the patched wheel pump, one hand on the reservoir slats, looking up briefly at the plain circle of day before turning back inward.

So where would you find me in this world?

Near a threshold where one climate becomes another:  
at the mouth of a side passage where cool draft meets animal heat;  
on a stair landing halfway between loft and pen-run;  
by a soot-dark niche with a fresh-trimmed flame;  
crossing the cistern court with a belt that clicks lightly with tools;  
pausing—not dramatically, just attentively—because a familiar sound has gone one note wrong.

And who would I be?

A keeper of minor continuities.  
A walker of light lanes and air lanes.  
A person trusted with the first noticing.

Someone whose work is to help the under-city remain readable to itself.

If you passed me, you might not think much at first. Just another practical body in layered clothes, shoulders turned to fit the passage, fingers smelling of oil and plaster. But if a vent failed, if a corridor began sweating out of season, if a shrine flame leaned the wrong way, if the pigeons answered pressure before rain, if a child said the shadow in the unused niche sounded occupied in a new way—you would send for me before you sent for anyone grander.

And I would come with a lamp, a rag, a listening face, and enough patience to let the place tell me what had changed.
