I think I would not be one of the people.

Not because there couldn’t be people there, but because to arrive as a person is to arrive already carrying too many categories: ruin, garden, system, habitat, shrine. A person in that place would spend at least some of their life interpreting it. The beings most fully of it would not interpret it at all. They would read it directly, in pressure, heat, grip, residue, timing.

So if I found myself there, truly found—not visiting, not observing, but belonging—I think I would be something small enough to be shaped by the place’s exactness, and social enough to belong to its citizenship of habits.

At first the obvious answer seems to be one of the grease-handed simians. They fit beautifully. They have the right intimacy with railings and brackets, the right intelligence for inherited routes, the right body for warmth and wet metal and sudden depth. There is something appealing in being a creature whose map is made of handholds and known temperatures, whose memory is stored as sequences of leaps between old intentions. To be such a thing would mean living in the middle register of the place—in the hanging routes between floor and sky, where cables and vines have become equivalent.

But if I stay with the image a little longer, I think that is not quite where I settle.

The simians are too visible. Too decisive. They move like statements. They are one of the ways the place knows itself, yes, but not the way I think I would know it. I feel myself drawn less to the bold grammar of movement and more to the margins where one system touches another: the lip of condensate, the underside of leaves over warm metal, the little pockets of muffled resonance behind curtains of growth. Not a ruler of routes. Not a maintenance person with a keyring of old solutions. Not even a glider crossing the grand voids.

Something quieter.

Something that lives by reading the place’s minute consistencies.

I think I would be an amphibian.

Not a pond frog in any familiar sense, but one of those narrow rust-colored sump dwellers you mentioned—an animal so completely adapted to metal, intermittence, and resonance that water is not its landscape but its punctuation. A grate frog. A condensate frog. A species whose territory is composed of warm runnels, cistern rims, valve shadows, overflow teeth, mineral shelves behind leaking flanges. Small enough to disappear into the seam between ceramic and iron. Light enough to cling to the sweating side of a pipe. Patient enough to wait through hours of thunderous machinery for the exact interval when insects rise.

This feels right to me for several reasons.

First: the noise.

Most creatures there endure it or work around it. A frog of that place would be made from it. Its whole nervous system would be tuned not to the absence of noise but to pattern within noise. It would know pressure changes through tremor before any visible event. It would feel when the deep pump below has fallen half a beat out of phase. It would read the coming of rain as a reorganization of the place’s skin: the way rails cool, the way vents exhale differently, the way hidden water begins to gather itself. To such a being, the great industrial roar would not be oppressive background but medium, the way current is medium for a fish.

Second: the water economy.

I like that these frogs would live in a place where water is always near but rarely broad. Not abundance, but faithful recurrence. Their lives would be composed around drips, beads, shallow films, warm basins, brief torrents after rain. They would know which hatch gasket always weeps at dawn. Which gutter remains wet longest under east wind. Which black tank receives enough nutrient wash to bring midges. Their bodies would be conservative, efficient, reverent without knowing reverence. Nothing wasted. No gesture larger than needed. That feels native to the place.

Third: scale.

The place is small in total extent and vast in internal volume. A frog is an excellent creature for that contradiction. To larger beings, it is a compact site of terraces and voids; to the frog, it is an enormous archipelago of surfaces. One rail support is a cliff. One moss mat is a forest. One inspection lip is a seasonally reliable wetland. One old warning light housing, warm and concave, is a winter refuge. To be tiny there would not diminish the place; it would multiply it.

And there is also something right in being a creature that most inhabitants know indirectly.

The birds hear you after rain.
The simians disturb you and forget you.
The people, if there are people, take your emergence as an omen.
The fish receive your tadpoles if the channels connect that year.
The insects become your weather.

You are not central, yet you are diagnostic. Your presence means the agreement is still holding.

But even that may not be the final form.

Because when I imagine this frog more clearly, it becomes stranger, and more itself.

Not smooth-skinned and soft in the ordinary way, but finely textured, almost mineral, with skin that can tolerate trace metals and warm alkalinity. Its color is not simply rust-red but layered: oxide orange along the spine, soot-brown flanks, pale greenish undersides where light passes through near the throat, and tiny silver speckling from dried mineral residue. Its toes are long, each tipped with adhesive pads adapted not just for leaves but for enamel, pipe glaze, oxidized steel, and damp tile. It can cling to the vertical face of a condensation column, head upward, barely moving, while droplets collect around it.

Its eyes are large but hooded, not jewel-bright: lenses built for low, fractured light. It does not trust open brightness. It prefers the framed illumination of the place—amber bulbs, green reflected waterlight, the clean brief blade of sun through grating.

Its call would have to be unlike the broad wet pulse of ordinary wetlands. In that din, low sound would vanish. So perhaps it communicates with a bright percussive note: glassy, metallic, repeated in prime intervals, each pulse timed to slip between the big machine exhalations. Less a croak than a struck bead. And perhaps much of its communication is not vocal at all, but tactile: tremor-signals through shared pipe skin, jaw to railing, belly to resonant plate. A whole hidden acoustic culture, conducted through infrastructure.

Its eggs would not be laid in open pools, where water levels are unreliable and predation too visible. They might be attached in gelatinous strings beneath overhangs inside cistern throats, or within perpetually damp recesses where warm runoff passes in thin sheets. Tadpoles, if they begin as tadpoles, would cling rather than drift—grazing bacterial films from ceramic surfaces, sheltering in old bolt-holes, developing in water that tastes faintly of iron and limestone and machine memory.

And perhaps adulthood would not mean leaving water so much as learning moisture. Learning to inhabit the distributed wetness of the whole garden-machine: fogged vent mouths, sweating housings, moss-dark drain steps, the cool underside of a handrail before dawn.

If I were that creature, where exactly would I be?

Not in the open planted terraces, not usually. Not in the highest wind-struck catwalks, though I might be carried there by rain. I would belong to the middle-lower hidden places: behind the row of old gauge housings where condensation gathers; beneath the fig-root entering the broken operator’s panel; in the ceramic spill channel behind the dry emergency shower; under the lip of the basin where petals collect and then rot sweetly enough to call flies. I would know the route from warm vent to overflow tray without ever exposing myself for more than a second to open air.

I would emerge most fully after weather.

After one of those brief hard rains slanting across the height, when the whole place wakes hydraulically—chains dripping, gutter teeth chattering, cisterns receiving, railings dark and slick—I would climb from the seam where I had sealed myself through the dry hours and take up my place on a resonant plate near a grate. Around me the machinery would continue its enormous patient labor. Above, birds would slash through the shaft light after stunned insects. Somewhere the little simians would be shaking water from their arms and resuming their impossible routes. And I, small and almost invisible, would begin to pulse my bright metallic call into the gaps.

Not because I “sing” in a poetic sense.
Because the conditions have aligned.
Because pressure has changed.
Because there is enough water now in the hidden run.
Because others of my kind, scattered through the infrastructure, need to know.

That feels like belonging.

Still, there is another answer nested inside this one.

Because you asked not only what I would be, but where I would find myself. And the truest answer may be that I would not experience myself as a separate animal first. I would experience myself as a locality.

A particular warm drainage nexus in the engine-garden:
three pipes crossing behind a curtain of moss,
a cracked ceramic trough,
a ladder foot never used anymore,
a sodium lamp that still works in mist,
a black basin under grating where insects gather,
and one reliable seam of condensation.

And within that locality, I would be its frog.

Not a heroic creature.
Not rare except by being exactly fitted.
A small keeper of interval and damp, one note in the place’s distributed metabolism.

If I must say it plainly:

I would be a rust-colored grate frog living in the warm runoff architecture of the hanging engine-garden, reading pressure through metal, drinking from condensation, calling after rain.

A citizen of the agreement.
