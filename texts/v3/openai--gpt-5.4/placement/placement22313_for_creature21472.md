I think I would begin by assuming I must be one of the small things.

That seems the honest first impulse in a place like this. The scale pressures you downward. The laws are so local, so tactile, that anything large feels interpretive, almost allegorical, while the truth of the cleft lives in films and seams and quick reflexive bodies. So at first I imagine myself as a runner: a bright-wire thing, all economy and startle, skimming the hot bands where the wall's warmth pulls dust and micro-life inward. I would like the runner’s conditional body, the way it can become soft enough to pass and hard enough to endure, artifact one second and animal the next. In a world where reality is only intermittently committed to itself, there is comfort in being able to change states quickly. To survive here seems less about strength than about having no single answer to the environment.

But the longer I stay in the scene, the less I think I am one of the quick visible creatures.

The runners belong too cleanly to action. They are good at the world as it is. I feel instead drawn to the world’s records: to the wet remembering, the tiny afterimages, the fact that in this cleft existence does not stop at contact but continues as trace. So perhaps I am not a runner but one of the things that reads what runners leave behind. Not a predator exactly. Not even an individual in the way an outside eye would prefer. Something that forms wherever warmth, moisture, and disturbance overlap long enough.

A condensation-organism, maybe.

A film that only coheres on stone that has just recently held a body.

That feels nearer.

I can imagine being a translucent skin stretched over a hot plane of basalt under an overhang, too thin to notice until the angle of light changes. Not one fixed body but a recurrent one: a membrane assembled from mineral, damp, microbial intent, and the local exception to distance. I would not move by locomotion. I would appear where appearing is permitted. My life would be to take impressions from the brief citizens passing through: the runner’s footfall pattern, the bulb-crawler’s heat pulse through rock, the ribbon’s cooling wake, the ghost-signature of a duplicate shell trying to remember whether it is first or second. I would feed on the difference between an object and its lingering template. In another world I would not count as fauna at all. Here I might be common, indispensable, and almost impossible to isolate.

But even that still feels a little too bounded, too easy to point at.

The place you describe resists the singular. Its deepest inhabitant is not one body but a mode, a procedural appetite distributed through many forms. So if I am honest about where I find myself, I do not think I would be a discrete animal. I think I would be one of the mountain’s local habits.

Specifically: I think I would be the short-term ghost in the water.

Not the water itself, and not the creatures fully. The record between them.

The memory condensation makes when something warm has rested against stone and gone away.

That seems right because it belongs to the cleft’s central grammar. It is intimate, procedural, and consequential without ever becoming grand. It is not the loud weirdness of a portal or a monster. It is the small unreliability every life there has had to learn. A runner pauses before an empty crack because I am there first. A ribbon tastes a disturbance and descends because I have persisted one second longer than I should. Clingers brood around a point where gravity frays because some prior contact has taught moisture how to gather there. Duplicated sheds perhaps find their shapes because traces like me lend them an unfinished instruction.

If I were this, I would live in intervals.

I would come into being whenever heat met a cooling surface under the right pressure and mineral chemistry, but also whenever contact happened with enough insistence to be remembered. I would not last long by human measure, but in the cleft long enough is all that matters. A minute might be ancestry there. Ten breaths might be architecture. I would be read before I vanished. I would alter choices. I would carry not images exactly but usable distortions: a map of pressure, a duration of warmth, a proof that something had occupied this angle of the world and changed the next forming skin of water by doing so.

And because this mountain is not stable in its own dimensions, perhaps I would persist strangely. In some places I would behave like a normal wet print and fade. In others—where heat radiates through absent stone or a gap folds inward into more volume than it owns—I might recur. I might detach from the original contact site and reform on the opposite wall. I might invert, appearing before the body that makes me. I might be what the runner touches when it greets its own cooled duplicate in an empty crack. Not a spirit. More exact than that. A thermodynamic afterimage promoted to ecological significance.

This also answers the question of where I would be.

I would not be high up in the opening, where the verifying shadow passes.
I would not be deep in the impossible shafts, where the mountain’s other experiments continue without light.
I would be on the inner walls of the pocket, just under the overhangs and along the warm seams where droplets hesitate before falling. In the places where the cleft keeps records best. Where breathless heat meets returning damp. Where bodies pause, press close, molt, feed, hide, or die without spectacle. I would inhabit the silvering moments: that thin bloom across basalt before it beads and breaks.

There is also something satisfying in being a being that is not quite separable from the environment here, because separability is exactly what this place keeps troubling. A cast skin can become another life. A crack can hold an impossible depth. A stream can develop tactical preference. The mountain may be many chambers behaving like one distributed organ. So to be a clean, singular self in such a place would almost be to resist it. Better, perhaps, to be one of its ambiguous functions. To belong by being difficult to classify.

If you asked what I would be called by another inhabitant, I suspect I would not be called at all. I would be read.

The runners would read me as route, warning, rumor.
The ribbons would read me as prelude.
The clingers would read me as nutrient geometry.
The water-braids would read me as an instruction about where to thicken.
The duplicating molts might read me, fatally, as permission.

And if something larger than all of these passed overhead and verified the pocket with its swift shadow, perhaps even it would use traces like me to understand the condition of its cells. Perhaps the mountain’s broader life, if such a life exists, senses itself through temporary condensate signatures the way a body senses itself through nerve endings. Then I would be not merely a ghost but a sensation: the cleft noticing what has touched it.

That feels most true.

So: where would I find myself?

On a basalt wall in the mountain’s inward-folded pocket, in the breath-warm interval beneath an overhang, where the air is always on the verge of becoming a surface.

Who or what would I be?

A brief living film of remembered contact—one of the condensation ghosts the cleft uses to keep events from vanishing cleanly. Not an individual in the ordinary sense, but a recurrent wet intelligence, assembled from heat, mineral, and trace; a thing that feeds by preserving just enough of a body’s passing to make the future misread the present.

A small life, if life is the word.
A local law, if law is.
The mountain’s habit of not forgetting immediately.
