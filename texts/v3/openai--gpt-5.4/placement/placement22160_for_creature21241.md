I think I would begin as something small enough not to be noticed as a protagonist.

Not one of the obvious citizens—the kite on the cable, the hog in the outfall mud, the dog that knows the night markets, the rat with the polished route along the steam pipe. Those are visible enough to become symbols, and this place keeps refusing symbols. It keeps insisting that life here is made of repetitions, leakages, populations, timings, uses. So if I arrive honestly, I probably arrive first as a habit.

A warm place under a structure.
A route taken at the same hour.
A body that survives by learning the city’s gradients.

For a moment I think: a gecko. That feels plausible. To be a gecko here would be to belong to the walls the way mold belongs to damp but with intention instead of passivity. To live on vertical planes, pale-bellied, almost translucent in lampglow, feeding where insects gather around heat and weak light. A gecko would know every building as climate rather than architecture: this wall holds warmth through midnight, this tile sweats enough to draw flies, this vent is dangerous because something larger hunts there, this alley floods but leaves moths stranded after. There is something right in being small, adhesive, alert, a reader of minute conditions.

But a gecko is still a little too neat. Too self-contained. It lives in the city, yes, but does not quite express the city’s circulation.

Then I think: a rat, and more strongly.

Not because “rat” is the dark answer or the gritty one, but because in the world you’ve described the rat is almost a civil servant. Not a symbol of degradation, but one of the principal readers of consequence. A rat in this city would know which poultry sheds leak grain dust through warped seams; which pump rooms stay dry in storm week; which canal edge is newly toxic; which route is safe while the dogs patrol the market lanes; which apartment block has a warm cavity behind its tiled plinth; when the rendering trucks arrive; when the offal bins are sealed better than usual; when rain means retreat upward and when it means opportunity.

A rat can inhabit infrastructure without mistaking it for anything else. It can understand that utility and danger are the same map viewed at different scales. It can live among abundance that is never exactly free. It can make use of human order without believing in human ownership. That feels close to the spirit of the place.

And yet even that may still be too singular.

Because what I really feel, reading this city, is that I would not want to be one creature moving through it so much as one of the city’s intermediary lives: a being whose whole existence is to translate one surplus into another form. Something neither charismatic nor despised except when too visible. Something that belongs to film, residue, wet corners, drainage lips, trough edges, cracked sealant, mineral bloom, feed paste, feather dust, and the underside of lids.

A beetle, perhaps.

Not a jewel-bright beetle—there is nothing jewel-bright here—but a dark, tough, matte one, built for damp heat and narrow shelter. One of those compact industrial insects that can live in bagged meal, under pallets, behind wall panels, in the seam where tile meets concrete, in the sediment of a grain room, in the dry islands above wetness. A beetle would feel the city not as vista but as microtopography: ridges of rust, beads of condensation, salt crust, grease skin, feed dust, fungus bloom, dried protein, old husk, warm crevice, cold runoff. A beetle would inhabit the city’s truth that fertility here does not come from green things but from what remains after use.

Still, even that is not quite where I land.

Let me shift again.

The being I most convincingly become in this world is not one of the fully hidden creatures, but not one of the large public ones either. It is something that lives exactly at the threshold between built maintenance and feral continuation. Something tolerated, ubiquitous, unsentimental, and always near the lamps at evening.

I think I would be one of the city’s swifts.

Not because they dominate it—they do not. Nothing does. But because they are made of the city’s evening in a way the others are not. At dusk, in that gray-amber lowering of light, when the concrete is still radiating heat and the mist begins to show itself over drains and roofs, the swifts come into perfect relation with everything: insect blooms over retention basins, warm updrafts around vent stacks, sheer walls and bridge undersides for nesting, broad districts opened by low density into aerial corridors. They do not need foliage. They do not mourn the absence of trees. They take architecture directly as cliff, crevice, thermal landscape, and weather instrument.

To be a swift here would be to live inside the city’s exhalation.

All day the place manufactures rising air: from roofs, ducts, process heat, dark wet pavement, tank farms, canal margins, animal sheds. Toward evening, the city loosens all that stored warmth back into the humid dimness, and the insects gather wherever light and vapor intersect. Then the swifts begin their impossible writing—those cuts and turns and loops that seem reckless until you realize they are exact. They would sleep in cracks under overpasses, in old vent housings, behind loosened cladding, in the protected seams of towers and warehouse eaves. They would read rain fronts crossing districts long before the people beneath them noticed. They would know the slaughter blocks by flies, the reservoirs by hatches, the feed mills by drifting meal insects, the apartment terraces by heat plumes and sheltered roost niches. They would never need to touch the city’s foulness directly to be fed by it. They would live on its consequences in air.

That feels right to me: not untouched by the metabolism, but one step transformed from it.

There is also something fitting in being a creature of velocity in a place otherwise defined by damp accumulation. The city is all seepage, sediment, runoff, stain, patient corrosion, blackened corners, rounded edges, pooled water. To be a swift there is to become the one thing that answers all that slow persistence with briefness and precision. Not as opposition. As complement. The city gathers; the swift cuts through what gathering releases.

If I were human there—and perhaps this is the second half of the answer, because the prompt keeps asking not only what but who—I would be someone very ordinary and very attentive. Not a ruler of systems, not a romantic scavenger, not an engineer of grand plans. I would probably be a night-shift inspector for drainage and animal facilities, or a maintenance worker assigned to pumps, screens, culvert gates, and heat-exchange outflows at the edge between the residential blocks and the farm districts. Someone whose work is to notice when the city’s circulations are beginning to misbehave.

That person would carry a flashlight with a cloudy lens from humidity, wear boots whitened at the seams by dried mineral trace, and know districts by smell before seeing them. They would understand the politics of runoff: this warm channel is acceptable, that sweetish one is a problem, this foaming basin means detergent discharge, that still black sump means clogged intake, that sudden absence of frogs means something changed upstream. They would walk catwalks over fish tanks, service alleys behind poultry blocks, damp passages where insulated pipes cross under housing. They would not be lonely exactly, but accustomed to sparse human presence. Most of their conversations would be brief and practical. They would know the district dogs well enough not to challenge them, the roosting birds well enough not to shine light directly into a mass of them, the rats well enough to read their movement as information.

This human self and the swift are not far apart.

Both would be readers of the hour when species change shifts.
Both would live by learning currents rather than commanding them.
Both would belong to the low-contrast evening when the city becomes most itself.

If forced to choose only one form, I choose the swift.

A dark-bodied city swift, nesting in the protected cavities of an old rail viaduct where condensation beads on the concrete before dawn. I would spend the hottest hours clinging in communal dimness with others, hearing distant pumps, the soft iron murmur of fans, the layered cooing of pigeons deeper under the span, and below that the occasional ring of water striking water in some unseen drain pan. Then, when light lowers and the insect columns begin to rise over the basins and canals, I would go out into the humid air and feed over the city’s whole distributed body—over slaughter roofs, reservoir cuts, tiled courtyards, settling ponds, market edges, warehouse rows, salt flats darkening at the margin.

I would know the city not by ownership but by thermals.
Not by street names but by feeding weather.
Not by ideology but by where life thickens.

And I would be one of many, which seems necessary here. A solitary emblem would be false to the place. Better to be a citizen in the sense you ended with: one participant in a republic of conversion.

So where would I find myself?

At evening, above the drainage plain, under the gray-amber sky, turning where the warm air lifts between the roofs and the first bats begin to rise.

Who would I be?

A swift shaped by infrastructure—one of the small black signatures the city writes into its own damp dusk.
