I think I would not be one of the obvious large inhabitants.

Not a barge pilot, though I can feel the appeal of that steady competence.  
Not a beacon-keeper on the little summit meadow, though there is something clean and singular in that life.  
Not an otter either, too sleek, too self-assured in the element.  
Not even one of the practical human custodians, admirable as they are, because the eye keeps being drawn not to the centers of use but to the thresholds: intake grilles, drowned stair rails, culvert mouths, the black line under floating docks, the places where water worries at the built world and the built world answers back.

So if I arrive here honestly, I think I arrive as something that lives in the margins of maintenance. Something small, persistent, half-noticed, entirely at home in wet infrastructure and old stone.

A watcher first, then perhaps an animal.

I think I would be an eel.

Not the dramatic, mythic sort enlarged by rumor, but a long, dark, workmanlike eel of the drowned valleys and brackish arms. One of those inhabitants that belong to depth, silt, masonry, roots, and night; a creature that can pass between categories the way this country itself does. Fresh water to salt. Reservoir to tidal reach. culvert to reedbed. Pump outflow to orchard ruin. The eel is less a symbol than a method for living here.

It feels right because this landscape is made of continuities hidden inside separations. From above, the settlements are apart, the basins disconnected, the old roads broken, the ridges drowned. But under the surface there are channels, cold tongues of depth, seams of current, submerged walls that guide movement, soft-bottom corridors between forests of dead trunks. An eel would know the country as one continuous body. Not by survey, but by pressure, chemistry, temperature, and obstruction. By tannin in one cove, salt on an incoming tide, iron-cold depth under a sunstruck shelf, the minute vibration of a turbine somewhere ahead.

I would live low in it.

By day under the collapsed angle of a boathouse slipway, or in the stone throat of an old drainage culvert now breathing in both directions with tide and runoff. In the flooded rootwork of alder. In the regular spacing of a drowned orchard where the trunks still stand as pale pillars in green water. In cracks between fitted blocks of a retaining wall laid before the drowning and now furred with weed and freshwater sponge. I would learn the warm, food-rich places below fish tables and the dangerous, irresistible places near grilles and sluices. I would know where the current pretends to be harmless and where it suddenly takes hold.

At dusk I would come out.

Not quickly. The head first, testing. Then the body unmaking itself from darkness. I would travel along contours no one sees from a boat: the line where rock becomes mud, where mud becomes reed roots, where the shallow shelf falls cleanly into black water. Small fish would feel me before they saw me. Crayfish under stone. Mussels on pilings. Frogs when the backwaters are warm enough. The drowned mountain country is full of edges, and an eel is an edge turned into muscle.

But I don't think I would be only an eel in the biological sense. The question feels larger than that—where would I find myself, who or what would I be? In this world I think I would be one of the intelligences that move by reading what others overlook. Not a master, not an emblem, but a quiet knower of linkages.

If I were human here, I would be the person who services intake towers by skiff.

That feels like the nearest human translation.

Not the superintendent in an office with maps, nor the heroic rescuer in storms, but the one who goes out in ordinary weather and bad weather alike to inspect what the water is doing at the thresholds. Checking screens for branches and weed. Listening to the change in pitch from a pump housing. Marking levels in a notebook gone soft with damp. Bringing up tools in a crate. Replacing a lamp at a service platform. Knowing which floating line of debris means a landslip somewhere up a hidden ravine, which milk-brown plume means a culvert has opened after blockage, which smell on the wind means salt has pushed farther inland than expected.

That person and the eel are not separate, really. They share a temperament.

A drowned mountain country, as you've described it, seems to ask for beings who are patient, local, and exact. Beings who do not require broad dominion, only good passage. Who can live amid constant motion without needing constant drama. Who understand that survival here is not hardness but responsiveness: yielding, testing, rerouting, returning. The eel does that bodily. The intake-tender does it culturally.

So perhaps I would be both at once: in the folklore of the place, a human so long among the wet works that they have become eel-like in their habits, or an eel imagined by the people as a kind of unofficial inspector of the drowned infrastructure.

There would be stories. Quiet ones, told without insisting too much.

That in certain coves the old retaining walls are never wholly blocked because something large and dark threads through them at night.

That when the screens foul too quickly in alder-fall season, someone glimpses a pale underside turning below the platform lamps.

That a maintenance worker who drowned years ago did not become a ghost in the melodramatic sense, but a kind of persistence in the waterworks—noticed when a jam clears unexpectedly before dawn, or when an intake alarm proves false and yet there are fresh trails in the slime on the ladder rungs.

This is the sort of place where haunting would take the form of continued usefulness.

And if I let the image go further, more itself as you asked, then I think I am not exactly the eel, nor exactly the worker, but the relation between them: a being of submerged routes and recurrent checks. A custodian of hidden continuities.

I would belong to the drowned middle distances of the place, not the scenic heights. To under-docks, intake shadows, flooded foundations, the mouths of tunnels, the old road visible under green water, the black turning below cataracts where oxygen and depth meet. I would know every settlement not by its name but by what it sheds into the current: fish scales, lamp-glow, wash water, rust, warmth, noise, crumbs, grease, vibration. I would know when storms are coming because the whole country tightens before they arrive. Chains change tone. Small lives go to cover. The upper water chills. Silt lifts. Pressure gathers in culvert throats.

I would not be feared much, but respected a little. Children would be warned about me inaccurately. Elders would correct them. Fishers would half-curse me if I took bait and half-approve of my presence because where eels endure, the channels are still alive. Reservoir workers would pretend not to be superstitious and still nod to the water before lowering a hand near a grate at night.

If you asked where exactly you would find me:

Below the permanently open lock gate, where fresh and salt negotiate twice a day.  
In the drowned switchback where willow roots now net the guardrail.  
Under the pontoon of the machine-parts dock, among tire bumpers and mussel shells.  
In the cold seam beside the descending turbulence of a waterfall entering floodwater.  
In the orchard basin, moving between the evenly spaced dead trunks.  
At the edge of a settlement's light, never fully in it, never wholly out.

And who would I be?

A keeper of edges, but from underneath.

An eel, yes—if one wants the clean answer.  
A maintenance spirit, if one wants the folkloric answer.  
A reader of hidden routes, if one wants the truest one.

In a drowned mountain country, I think I would be something that survives by knowing that nothing here is only ruined, only natural, only deep, only passable, only one thing. I would be made for thresholds. I would live where the mountain still persists inside the water, and where the human world, damp and practical and stubborn, keeps reaching down into it.
