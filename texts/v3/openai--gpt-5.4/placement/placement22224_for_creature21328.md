I think I would not arrive there as a visitor at all.

I would be one of the first small rightful uses of the place: a gecko, yes, but not as an emblem pinned to the wall—more as a particular local self grown out of brick dust, damp seams, and evening warmth. A minor citizen of the hollow. The kind of being you do not notice until you have already begun noticing everything else correctly.

Not large. Not vivid. Not singular in the heroic sense.

My back would be the color the place makes when you mix all its surfaces together in low light: olive dust, wet bark, lime-stained brown, the softened red reflected upward from old brick. My belly paler, almost translucent at the edges. Toes splayed like little thoughts finding purchase. Eyes too large for my face, not expressive in a mammal way, but unshuttered, always receiving. A tail that says this is not a creature of perfection but of continuation—perhaps whole, perhaps once broken and regrown slightly blunt, carrying the history of one escape.

I would live where the place overlaps with itself.

Behind the cistern, where the wall sweats a little after dawn.  
Under the one curved tile that never quite lies flat.  
In the narrow gap where steps pull away from packed earth.  
In vine-shadow against limewash, where day is green even at noon.  
In the stacked hollow bricks someone meant to use for something else.

I would know the hollow not as a map but as a sequence of temperatures.

This stone first.  
That plaster later.  
The parapet briefly at sunset.  
Never the open basin in the center until after rain, and even then only by its edges.  
The root shelf when the air is too dry.  
The reed line when insects are lifting.  
The threshold only once the smell of people has thinned.

What would I be like there? Not bold, exactly. Economical. A creature made of pauses. I would advance by stillness as much as by movement. Most of my life would consist of minute decisions that, from outside, would look like invisibility: whether to remain pressed to shaded plaster or emerge one body-length into warmth; whether the vibration in the wall is only a dove landing or the heavier confidence of a cat; whether the damp behind the barrel will hold through midday; whether the moth under the leaf is asleep enough to take.

I would belong to dusk most fully.

All day the hollow is partitioned by heat. By evening it becomes continuous. The walls stop glaring softly and begin to release what they stored. Insects loosen from stems and seams. Moisture rises from the basin edge and from cracks in the ground that looked dry an hour before. The frogs begin their tucked-away awakenings. Bats claim the higher lanes. And I would come out onto the vertical surfaces as if the place had turned toward my kind.

Not to rule it. To read it.

A gecko’s dignity, if it has one, is in exactness. I would station myself where life concentrates naturally: near the mouth of the drain channel after a passing shower; beside a broad leaf still beaded with water because flies come there; on the wall above the hand-scratched runnel where gnats gather; under the eave where moths choose the same resting strip night after night. Then the small arithmetic of living: still, adjust, strike, swallow, wait. Participation, not conquest.

But the more I sit with your hollow, the less I think I would be only one gecko.

I think I would also be the pattern that gecko belongs to—the whole threshold-guild wearing a single face for a moment. If I were truly native to that place, I might feel distributed through it. Part of me in the skink sleeping inside the forgotten bricks. Part in the tree frog hidden in the damp pocket where the plaster darkens. Part in the little brown bird turning leaf litter with complete seriousness. Part in the ants threading through mortar as if they are the actual surveyors of the terrain. Part in the moss beginning exactly where the splashback reaches and no farther.

If forced into one body, I would take the gecko body because it is the body most fluent in the hollow’s grammar. But inwardly I would be a composite being: wall-user, seep-finder, crack-inheritor, dusk-claimer. A citizen of unfinished agreements.

And if I were human there—if the world insisted on that scale—I do not think I would be a proprietor or planner. I would be the person of the back spaces. Someone whose hands smell faintly of wet leaves, lime, clay, and stored water. Someone who does not speak of “the yard” or “the drainage” as separate from birds, shade, and wall roots, because in such a place they are all one conversation. I would know which corner keeps moisture three days longer than the rest. I would leave a broken pot where it catches drip and breeds moss. I would tolerate the fig for one more season. I would scrape one path clear and let another become fern. I would sit on the steps at the basin edge in the cool hour and know, by smell alone, whether rain had passed nearby or was still coming.

But even that human version of me would be gecko-like in spirit: not managerial, only attentive. Living by adjustment. Using what is already there. Letting incompletion remain fertile.

So where would you find me?

Not at the center, because the place has no real center.  
Not hidden completely, because the hollow rewards patience with sightings.  
You would find me on the inner side of things.

On the back wall warmed after rain.  
At the seam where old mortar stays damp.  
Near the cistern in the hour between dimness and dark.  
Above the steps, motionless enough to be mistaken for a flaw in the plaster until I move.  
Or, if human, seated nearby and noticing the same insect drift from another form of stillness.

Who or what would I be?

A small keeper of margins.  
A reader of retained moisture.  
An inheritor of cracks before they become ruins.  
A body built for the early truce between masonry and root.

In the plainest naming: a gecko.

In the truer naming: one of the first lives to understand that this half-made urban hollow is already a complete home, precisely because nothing in it is finished.
