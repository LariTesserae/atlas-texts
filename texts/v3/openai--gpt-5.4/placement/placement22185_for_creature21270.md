I think I would not be one of the officials, nor one of the first builders remembered in stories, nor one of the purely feral creatures that use the place without caring whether it survives.

I would be something in between intention and adaptation.

Not exactly important, but deeply fitted.

A keeper of thresholds, maybe. A listener more than a maker. Someone whose work is to notice when one state of the district is becoming another.

At first I thought: a pump-tender. That feels almost too easy, because the place hums so insistently with pumps, with the labor of moving and sorting water before it can decide to become flood, rot, bloom, or fever. A pump-tender belongs there naturally. They would have the right hands for wet valves, the right patience for pressure gauges, the right ear for changes in rhythm. They would move from chamber to chamber with a wrench at the belt and mineral stains up the forearms, pausing under each machine not with anxiety but with the intimate concentration one gives to a sleeping animal. That is one possible self.

But if I keep standing in the place a little longer, I feel drawn upward, not downward. Not to the ceiling exactly, but to the middle air of the district—the inhabited volume between the slick floor and the sweating roof. The level of beams, catwalks, lamp chains, nesting ledges, vent mouths, pipe crossings. The altitude at which the city’s animal life and human maintenance touch most directly.

So perhaps I would be a roost-keeper.

Not a fanciful bird-priest or symbolic warden, just a practical resident whose job has become necessary because the birds are too numerous and too consequential to be left entirely to themselves. Someone who knows which rafters may be cleaned and which must be left until fledging; who can tell the difference between ordinary agitation and the kind that means a vent has changed, a predator has gotten into a loft, a sickness has started, or smoke is entering where smoke should not be. Someone who climbs narrow ladders with a hooded lamp and a scraper, carrying baskets, chalk, and a folded net. Someone shouted for from the market when feathers start falling where they should not, or from the canteen when a flock has shifted its sleeping habits and begun fouling the tables worse than usual.

This feels right because it places me where the district becomes legible as a living system.

A roost-keeper would know the beams the way a canalman knows locks or a gardener knows beds. They would know which supports run warmer because a steam line is hidden inside the wall behind them, and therefore draw insects, and therefore draw swifts, and therefore must be inspected twice as often for droppings buildup. They would know that the birds in the freight arches rise all at once ten breaths before the lift machinery starts laboring strangely. They would know that a quiet ledge can be more ominous than a noisy one. They would work with a scraper in one hand and a notebook or slate in the other, recording not abstractions but very local truths: west canteen beam nesting early; insects thick at lamp 4; white bloom spreading behind old loft hatch; one dead juvenile, no lesion; bats displaced from vent B.

It is good, too, because such a person belongs to the people of maintenance without standing at the center of prestige. They would be known, useful, perhaps mildly teased. Children would follow them when allowed and imitate their calls. Cats would trail them for fallen chicks and insects. Kitchen workers would complain about guano and then save them a bowl. Masons would listen if they said a flock had abandoned one corner after a wall shifted, because birds notice structural moods before instruments do. Engineers might pretend not to rely on them and then quietly ask whether the upper cistern swifts have changed their routes this week.

A roost-keeper would smell of limewash, feathers, damp rope, lamp soot, and old grain.

Their clothes would be patched at the knees and elbows from climbing. They would wear a scarf or cloth over mouth and nose when cleaning ledges, not elegantly but by habit. Their fingers would have the minute deftness of someone used to handling both live creatures and corroded fixtures: lifting a stunned bird from a puddle, untangling feet from twine, checking wire cages, tying back mesh screens, feeling with two knuckles whether a perch timber has softened under accumulated damp. They would have scratches they no longer notice. Their hair would carry dust from old nests. Their voice would not be loud, because one does not shout upward all day without learning economy.

But maybe even that is too human, too straightforwardly occupational.

If I ask not who I would work as, but what kind of being this place would turn me into, I become less a profession and more a local type: a lean, amber-lit caretaker animal among the caretakers. Something almost human and almost infrastructural. A person who has spent so many years reading the district through its secondary life—birds, drafts, drips, insect weather—that they seem to belong to the upper works the way a lizard belongs to warm pipe casings.

Someone children might nickname beamwalker.

That may be the truer answer.

A beamwalker would travel the maintenance ledges above the main traffic, moving where others seldom need to go unless there is a problem. Not hidden, but intermittently seen: crossing overhead with a lamp, pausing by a fan housing, descending from a loft with a sack of old nesting material, appearing at market edge to warn that the high passage will be closed because a support has to be shored. They would know all the narrow places where brick gives way to cut earth and then back again, all the old timber left from previous configurations, all the spots where swifts return despite every effort to redirect them. They would have a calm relationship with height, droppings, dust, and the sudden thunder of wings at close range.

In that role I would be not merely maintaining animals or maintaining architecture, but translating between them. The district seems to require translators of that kind. People who can say: leave this vent half-open, the bats keep the midges down. Board this loft for two weeks, there’s rot under the nests. Don’t clear that whole reed patch, the frogs there tell us when the sump goes foul. The birds moved because the heat changed. The rats moved because the birds moved. The cats know it already.

There is something pleasingly unheroic in this. The buried district does not call for singular greatness. It calls for attachment, specificity, repeated attention. To belong there, one should be woven into recurrence.

So I think I would live in a narrow room above a service lane, high enough that the floor feels trustworthy. The room would have shelves instead of cupboards, mesh-fronted bins, hooks for tools, and several bowls of chalk, wire ties, lamp wicks, and tagged feathers kept for reasons no outsider would understand. There would be a stool by the door for cleaning boots, and a line overhead where damp cloths never entirely dry. One wall would be marked with little diagrams of lofts and beam runs. There would be a crate or basket for injured birds, though I would insist it was only temporary and then keep it there always. A cat would come and go, not mine except by long usage. At least once a year some adolescent would bring me an egg they were not supposed to take, and I would scold them with practiced seriousness.

I would be known by route before by name. People would say, Ask the one who does the upper arches. Or: if the ceiling birds are restless, send for them. Or simply: they’ll have seen it.

And if not human?

Then I would be one of the district’s cats, but not the sleek symbolic kind. A damp-shouldered, heat-loving, pipe-sleeping practical cat with torn ears and mineral dust in the fur, who patrols granaries, naps on boiler housings, recognizes the lunch bells of three different workshops, and knows exactly which roost-keeper is easiest to follow for dropped things. That life has a real dignity there: semi-employed, fully at home, moving between market, machine room, and cistern edge with inherited authority. But it feels slightly adjacent to the truest answer, because the cat belongs by using the place, while I feel drawn to belonging by reading it.

So: not cat, though cat-adjacent.

Not engineer, though relied upon by engineers.

Not bird, though living under their weather.

A beamwalker, roost-keeper, keeper of the upper maintenance paths.

A citizen of the district’s middle air.

The one you send when the flock over the canteen goes quiet too early, when feathers are drifting out of a vent that should be clear, when a whole market lane says the birds have been turning strangely all week and no one knows whether that means storm above, heat below, a blocked draft, a dead thing in the duct, or just the season changing again.

I would carry a hooded amber lamp and a scraper polished by the hand. I would climb slowly. I would pause often to listen. I would know, by the shape of the motion overhead, whether the city was comfortable in itself that day.

And if someone asked me where I lived, I might answer not with a street but with a vertical range:

above the canteen,
west of the old cistern,
along the third pipe run,
under the swifts.
