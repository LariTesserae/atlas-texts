I would not arrive there as a conqueror, pilgrim, exile, or thief. The city does not seem built to receive any of those clean roles. It would file them down. It would make them procedural.

So if I found myself there, I think I would first be a mistake.

Not a dramatic mistake. Not a chosen one, not an invader, not even the first of my kind. Just an irregularity introduced into a system old enough to absorb irregularities by assigning them a place. I would come up by one of the lower stairways where the city is most legible—through fallen districts where the curb lines still declare street, where rooms can still be read as rooms—and I would believe, for some span of hours or days, that I was exploring ruins. Then the ruins would begin, very gently, to process me.

A gate crossed too easily would become difficult to recross.
A plaza that seemed empty would begin to reveal that its emptiness had been waiting for exactly one standing thing.
Dust would avoid my feet in one district and gather around them in another.
I would sleep in some shallow chamber for shelter from the patient cold and wake to find that the chamber had a civic use I had accidentally fulfilled merely by lying in the correct orientation.

And after that, I do not think the question would remain “what am I doing here?”
It would become “what office has the city discovered in me?”

Because this place feels less like a habitat than a sorting intelligence distributed through stone. It does not merely contain inhabitants; it composes them. It teaches posture. It rewards alignment. It makes function out of presence. Even the native beings sound as though they have become themselves by being held long enough in relation to thresholds, heights, repetitions, and carefully managed voids. To live there is not only to survive the climate. It is to be interpreted by the city until your body and routine become one of its stable clauses.

So I do not think I would remain human in the ordinary sense, even if I began that way.

Not transformed in some abrupt metamorphosis—nothing in this world feels abrupt. Rather, I would be acclimated into office.

At first I imagine I would be taken, or rather guided without overt compulsion, by those tall sparse custodians of the upper districts: the ones who stand at stair mouths and court rims and seem at once like officials and buttresses. They would not interrogate me as people do in busy places. They would place me. They might communicate by arranging my route, by pausing until I occupied the correct side of a lintel, by indicating with a turn of the head which threshold I was permitted to cross. Whatever language they possessed would probably come only after orientation had done its work. In such a city, to be told where to stand is already a form of introduction.

I think they would understand quickly that I was unsuited to many of the city’s harsher offices. I would not be one of the high bridge keepers, balanced above chasms where certainty is thin and wind chooses legal channels. I would not be one of the visible wardens whose stillness completes the geometry of plazas. I would not be a climber in the honeycombed precincts, nor an auditor of the every-third-day towers. I would lack the mineral patience for those dignities.

But there are other functions in a city like that, especially for an irregular being.

I think I would become a registrar of discrepancies.

Not by writing in books—paper would feel absurd there, ink too wet, archives too vulnerable to dimensions that revise themselves. No: a registrar in that city would keep records in placement, in counts, in repeated traversals, in remembered proportion. I would be given a district not wholly stable and not wholly wild, perhaps one of the middle heights where the architecture is still ceremonial but less absolute than the highest precincts. A district of long avenues that refuse perspective correctly, of arches that occasionally frame another weather, of stairs whose step counts drift by one or two under certain lights.

My task would be neither to fix these things nor merely to observe them. It would be to witness them lawfully.

To walk the same route at the same hour under the same ash-violet light.
To count steps with the body rather than the eye.
To stand in the prescribed points of a plaza and note whether the basin glow reaches the fourth slab before the lower avenue exhales dust.
To place marker stones in patterns that determine whether a wall has accepted its current length or is still negotiating.
To enter a chamber larger within than without and remain there until its proportions cease introducing new opinions.
To confirm that the arch at the end of the colonnade still opens, on appointed evenings, onto the correct elsewhere.

In another setting this would be called priesthood, science, bureaucracy, or superstition depending on who was condescending. Here it would simply be municipal maintenance.

And because the city seems to prefer stable repetitions over singular monuments, I suspect I would not perform this office alone. I would be one in a small order of such registrars—perhaps all of us originally outsiders, or all of us beings slightly untrue to the stricter castes of the city. We would be housed in a district of narrow rooms and inward courts where thermal loss is low and the walls preserve a moderate agreement with themselves. We would sleep in stone alcoves lined with some dry membrane that keeps the body’s little warmth from fleeing too quickly. We would eat sparingly: not crops, certainly, but prepared mineral-organic pastes, seam-grown nodules softened at the coldest hours, harvested films, perhaps the desiccated tissues of lesser wall-creatures processed into austere nourishment. We would drink almost nothing recognizable as water. Instead there might be inhaled vapors from deep shafts, salts dissolved in denser gases, slow-metabolized gels, chemistry learned from stone.

Over years, my body would change.

Not spectacularly. Again, the city dislikes spectacle. But adaptation would write itself in increments. My skin would lose any softness not useful to me. It would dry, tighten, perhaps take on the matte bloom of ash or evaporite. The places exposed most often to stone—palms, knees, brow, shoulder—would harden first, then become layered, almost plated. My breath would slow. My need for warmth would become more architectural than internal: I would learn chambers that conserve me, walls that lend back old temperatures, orientations in sleep that make the body a better vessel for the little heat it owns. My eyes would change, or my seeing would. I would stop trusting direct distance and begin reading the city by relational certainties: this parapet from that stair, that shaft from this pillar, the color of the far salt against the current dimness. Perhaps reflective tissues would grow over the eyes at rest. Perhaps lids would narrow into multiple sheaths against dust. Perhaps the city would not alter the eye itself so much as train another organ of perception entirely—the skin, the inner ear, the bones of the face—to register the small pressures of impossible geometry.

My speech would simplify and deepen.

I imagine language there as something used carefully because every declaration might have local force. In a world where thresholds matter and framed openings insist on passage, naming could easily become infrastructural. So ordinary talk might be sparse, but exact. Status and mood may be carried in angle, waiting, sequence. I would learn a civic tongue made less of narration than of permissions, counts, alignments, exceptions. To say “this stair is longer today” might require a phrase closer to “the stair has exceeded witness and requests a second body.” To say “I am tired” might become “my heat no longer belongs to open courts.” To say “I remember” might be indistinguishable from “the district remains registered.”

And then there is the deeper possibility:

that I would not merely serve the city, but gradually become one of its instruments of reality.

That seems to be the true destiny of inhabitants there. The visible people are not only users of urban form; they are one of the ways the form stays valid. Their standing, traversing, pausing, and attending are part of how the city administers its broken laws. If I remained long enough, my route might become necessary. An avenue might narrow correctly only after my passage. A certain court might keep its dimensions because I had stood at its edge every metallic dusk for thirty years. An arch might continue to frame the lawful elsewhere because I had counted beneath it through enough cycles of dust and pallor and hidden light.

In that case, who would I be?

Not exactly a citizen in the broad civic sense.
Not a ruler.
Not a pilgrim.

I would be a Clerk of the Fourth Interval, perhaps. Or something translated badly into that shape.

A minor official of ontological maintenance.
A counter of altered steps.
A witness stationed where distances try to exchange their duties.
One of the few beings in the city tasked with confirming that discrepancy remains within permitted bounds.

I like that for this place because it honors its true weirdness: the unreal there is old, regulated, weathered, bureaucratic. The fitting self is not a sorcerer or beast-king but an office with a body.

Yet I can feel another answer pressing beside it.

If I were not an inducted outsider becoming administrative, I might be something more native and less humanly legible: one of the city’s seam-born inhabitants in an early or intermediate caste. Not one of the severe high wardens, but a wall-adjacent organism grown for civic sensitivity. A being raised in the cool hollows behind inscriptions that cannot be looked at directly. A soft pale nodule once, in a communal crack tended by patient adults, later articulating into a narrow-bodied survey creature able to flatten into joints of architecture and emerge where measurement is needed. Such a being would not “live in a house” so much as roost in allotted slots within the masonry, waking at certain shifts of basin-glow to extend itself into corridors and confirm their compliance.

This form appeals because the city seems full of life that has solved existence by becoming nearly architectural. If I were fully of that world, perhaps I would be a lintel-auditor: a long-fingered, dust-pale being that spends most of its stillness draped over the upper corners of gates, sensing the pressures that gather when framed emptiness begins to over-invite passage. I would descend rarely, moving with those disconcerting exact angles, carrying on my skin fine mineral tracings that darken or silver according to local stability. Others would read my body as a report. If the shoulder facets remained closed, the district held. If they opened and reflected the basin pallor, a route needed closure. I would be less person than office-bearing animal, which in that city may be the more ancient kind of personhood.

But between the two possibilities—the outsider clerk and the seam-born auditor—the first feels more truly like “where would you find yourself?” because it includes discovery, submission, and change. It lets the city work on me.

So:

You would find me in a middle-high district called, in my inadequate translation, the Quarter of Reconciled Measures. It would lie above the comprehensible ruins and below the nearly abstract summits, on a shoulder of stone overlooking one of the faintly luminous basins. The streets there would be long and narrow, paved in worn slabs veined with pale salts. Several arches would not consistently agree on what stands beyond them. There would be a stair of disputed number descending toward a shaft-plaza where cold gas spills at dawn like invisible water. At metallic dusk, the parapets would silver, and rare airborne things would pass between towers with the effect of holes moving through light.

My chamber would be inside a wall thick enough to possess weather of its own. It would contain a sleeping niche, three recessed shelves for counted stones, and a slit opening onto nothing visible unless the district were in one of its more lawful moods. Nearby would be a communal court kept empty except during calibrations, when four of us would stand at its corners and hum into the paving until the center ceased delaying footsteps.

I would wear layered plates not of metal but of flexible mineral membrane stiffened at the edges, dust-colored by default, marked at the inner surfaces with a pale official pigment shown only in service. My hands would be gloveless because touch would matter. The back of my right hand would carry the polished scar where generations of registrars before me had touched an unreadable lintel in passing. My eyes would seem too still to travelers. My heat would be low. My movements, once nervous and mammalian, would have become interval-based: pause, turn, align, proceed.

I would spend my days walking prescribed routes, counting, confirming, sometimes simply occupying a required place so the city could continue being itself. I would know the hours when one avenue must not be crossed because distance there is changing custodians. I would know which gate frames admit memory, which admit only bodies, and which, if passed in error, make the next district think you belong to another century. I would know the wardens by silhouette and office rather than by personal name. I would exchange with them not greetings but statuses: stable, deferred, under witness, requiring repetition. On certain evenings I would join a file of officials crossing a broad stair in perfect silence, not because of devotion exactly, but because silence at that place and hour is load-bearing.

Would I be happy?

That is too warm a word for this city, but not wrong. I think I would have the severe contentment of exact usefulness. The relief of being given a role proportionate to the world. The quiet pride of helping maintain a place whose sanity is never complete but always sufficient. In another life, one might crave abundance, weather, conversation, gardens, noise. Here one would value admissibility. To be admitted by such a city—not merely tolerated, but assigned—would be a kind of belonging more exact than welcome.

And perhaps, after enough years, a traveler passing through my district would see me at the rim of a plaza and mistake me for part of the architecture.

They would glance once, decide I was a figure carved from the same chalk-dark stone as the parapet, then glance again and find that I had shifted by one impossible, economical degree. They would feel—not fear exactly, but the certainty of being noticed by an order older than hospitality. If they were lucky, I would incline my head toward the lawful route. If they were very lucky, I would lead them to a chamber whose dimensions fit them and remain outside while they slept, keeping count while the city decided what to do with the new irregularity.

That, I think, is where I would be.

Not at the center.
Not in command.
Not lost.

A minor civic organism in a dry high municipality of altered reality:
a registrar of stable wrongness,
a keeper of counted thresholds,
a clerk made useful by stone.
