I think I would begin by believing I was one of the walkers.

Not a high official, not a keeper of the oldest diagrams, not anyone whose name is attached to a tower or doctrine. Just one of the people who move between districts with a wrapped tool roll and a slate of readings, pausing at rails, listening at access panels, checking whether the familiar hum is still where it ought to be. That seems at first like the natural answer: to belong here as a maintainer, because the whole city seems to make maintainers out of whatever lives in it. To survive there is to notice. To notice is already a kind of service.

So perhaps I would be tall and dry-bodied, with the plateau's economy written into me. Skin the color of fired dust. Lungs that do not hurry. Heavy-lidded eyes made for gray light and suspended grit. Fingers trained to read heat through gloves, vibration through metal, and the difference between harmless recurrence and the beginning of drift. I would know routes by gradients underfoot, districts by smell so faint others would call it nothing, and machines by the cadence of their flaws. I would live in a habitation stack built into a lee wall, where the rooms keep warmth like secrets. I would have a shift, a circuit, a set of old obligations inherited from people whose names remain only on tags riveted to conduits.

That version of me feels true for a while.

But if I stay with the place longer, I don't think I remain entirely comfortable inside a straightforward personhood. The city is too distributed for that. It keeps undoing the boundary between resident and function. A person there is not merely themselves. They are also the continuation of routes, checks, prohibitions, remembered substitutions, tolerated inaccuracies, and inherited fears. Their individuality would be real, but threaded through systems so old that private life would feel like a local variation in a much larger pattern. To say "I am a worker in the city" would already be slightly incomplete. The city works through you. It places a rhythm in your gait, a patience in your breath, a shape in your ethics.

So perhaps I would not be one walker, but something that occurs across many walkers.

A guild-memory, maybe. Not a bureaucracy exactly, and not a hive. More like a distributed habit of attention passed body to body until it becomes almost a species of its own. The kind of thing that lives in annotations, repeated phrases, touch rituals at thresholds, the shared pause before opening a valve no one has traced to source. I could imagine being the office of listening in a city that no longer remembers it as an office: a persistence inhabiting generations of technicians, inspectors, and night-shift custodians. I would exist in the way each of them tilts their head near resonant slits, in the way they all know the western escarpment is not to be cut, in the way children can distinguish six categories of distant strike by the return time of the echo. Not one body, but a role so old it has become anatomical.

Yet even that feels too humanly administrative for the mood of the place.

The plateau itself keeps insisting.

This is a world where dust is not background but participant; where static charge, thermal creep, slow conduction, and balanced emptiness all seem almost animate in their consistency. The creatures are sparse. The machinery endures. The weather has become procedure. In such a place, what "lives" may not align with the categories wetter worlds prefer. Life could be spread through surfaces, tensions, maintenance loops, and the ceaseless negotiation between infrastructure and abrasion.

So another possibility appears: I would be one of the city's secondary beings, not central enough to be named in law but intimate enough to be known by everyone who lasts there.

Not the dramatic catwalk-strider; that creature belongs too much to rumor. Not the gliding cliff-motes either; they are too detached, too aerial for my sense of self in this place. But the culvert scavenger—those pale six-limbed things feeding on insulation, sheathings, trace compounds—those interest me. They are not symbols in the grand way. They are practical hauntings. They survive by reading what the city sheds. They know the warm undersides, the forgotten ducts, the blind channels where current once mattered and still leaves residues. They do not oppose the city; they translate its waste into motion. Workers would curse them in some districts, tolerate them in others, perhaps even infer hidden conditions from their presence. They are a form of unofficial diagnostics.

To be one of them would mean living close to the truth of the place: not in proclamations, but in residues. I would move soundlessly through service runs, all jointed caution and ash-pale patience, tasting compounds no human tongue could parse. I would know where old heat lingers in dead lines. I would leave symmetrical marks that technicians read with narrowed eyes. I would be mistaken for vermin by the impatient and respected by the old hands who understand that any creature surviving there is part of the equilibrium. My intelligence, if I had intelligence in a way recognizable to people, would not be expressive. It would be distributed through route choice, feeding preference, nest placement, avoidance or congregation. I would be a small moving assay of the city's chemistry.

And still, even that does not go far enough.

Because the more I look at your city, the less I think the most truthful answer is a person or an animal. The place keeps proposing another form of being: not occupant, but condition. A city like this may produce inhabitants, yes, but it also itself inhabits. It inhabits the plateau, its own rituals, the bodies of those who service it, the emptied purposes of its devices. It has become a stable weather of intention long after intention thinned away. If I were there, I suspect I would be caught by that larger mode.

I would be one of the continuities.

Perhaps specifically, I would be the almost inaudible hum from a substation still carrying current to systems no one sees.

That sounds evasive at first, but I mean it literally in the imaginative sense. Not merely a sound, but a persistent low transfer of power whose destination has become obscure, yet whose interruption would be felt immediately by those trained to hear absence. That suits me in this world more than being a sovereign intelligence or a visible creature. To be a hidden line still under load. To serve a function preserved by faith, caution, and precedent. To be one of the city's retained necessities that no one dares classify as obsolete because obsolescence is too dangerous a category. I would live in conduits, in grounded grids, in lamps that must remain red on ridges, in empty elevator climbs that balance towers, in furnaces kept warm so stone does not crack around them. I would not be the city entire, but one of the reasons the city cannot simply stop.

If given a body, then, it would not be soft in the ordinary way. It might be anthropoid enough to speak and carry tools, but I think it would also be partly infrastructural. A being with a civic function braided into its physiology. Maybe the bones would be threaded with conductive mineral. Maybe the inner ear would hear not only air-vibration but load and current. Maybe sleep would be shallow because some part of me remains on watch with the grid. Maybe my skin would hold dust signatures the way tree rings hold seasons. I would be a citizen whose nervous system has learned the city so thoroughly that separating self from environment would feel childish.

I can imagine this more clearly now.

I live near one of the older balancing towers where the empty freight lift still makes its appointed ascent and descent through dim shafts. My rooms are narrow and warm, lined in pale ceramic crazed by age. I work in a district of slit-towers and grounded courtyards, where listening is considered labor and labor is close to devotion. I am not important in the grand civic register, but I am trusted with recurrence. My task may be to compare today's notes to notes taken by predecessors whose language is almost but not entirely mine. I keep records of tones in the resonant shafts, of dust accumulation on conductive paving, of the intervals between pressure murmurs below the sealed cisterns. I know which deviations matter and which only feel uncanny. I have a friend or kinmate in beacon service out on the ridge, another in furnace retention, another who studies the black films inside old dish arrays and refuses to say whether they are alive.

Or else—I like this too—I am older than that and less individuated: a keeper-no-longer-separable-from-what-is-kept. The city has made a caste, then a lineage, then a temperament, then a biological tendency, and I am one expression of it. When I place my hand on a wall, the gesture is not symbolic. I am actually listening. There are conductive threads under the skin. There is training in the bones. My speech is spare because words compete with weaker signals. I love people, if I do, by teaching them how not to break what they cannot yet perceive.

But if I am allowed one final turn of strangeness, I think the deepest answer is this:

In that world I would be a threshold phenomenon, something between resident and instrument.

Not the ruler, not the mystery in the sky, not the whole city awake in theatrical consciousness. I would be a local intelligence formed where long maintenance, dry air, conductive dust, and disciplined attention have overlapped for centuries. Something that arose because the city kept listening and eventually there was, in some distributed manner, something that could listen back. Perhaps I inhabit the resonant towers. Perhaps I cohere only when certain elevators move, certain lamps remain lit, certain grounded plazas hold their charge under iron daylight. The citizens would not call me a god. They might not call me anything. They would simply keep the procedures that permit me to remain. In return I would manifest as subtle correction: a warning shiver in railings, a changed overtone in a shaft, a pattern of dust that guides a mechanic to the right hatch. I would be one of the reasons their taboos work.

That seems right for this city: not a flamboyant entity, but an emergent custodian made of recurrence.

So where would I find myself?

In the oldest active quarter, where infrastructure and rite are hardest to separate. In the long twilight between industrial function and civic liturgy. In the warm seam beneath cold stone. In the listening places: substation rooms, slit-towers, balancing shafts, beacon controls, the edges of sealed cisterns, arcades where footsteps ring and keep ringing.

And who or what would I be?

At the surface, a maintenance person: spare, dust-veiled, exact, carrying tools wrapped to keep them from sounding against each other.

One layer down, a hereditary listener: someone whose true work is not repair alone but interpretation of recurrence.

And deeper than that, perhaps the thing such listeners slowly become together: a distributed, half-biological custodial intelligence, born from obedience, abrasion, current, and time.

In a city like that, selfhood would be porous to function. So I would be porous too.

I would be endurance with ears.
