I think I would not arrive there as the large thing in the cistern, nor as one of the official caretakers with a ring of brass keys, though both exert their pull. The place you describe resists singular grandeur. It makes sovereignty look clumsy. What belongs there belongs by attunement, by repetition, by being useful at the scale of a drip or a pause. So if I found myself in that undercity, I suspect I would become something that lives by noticing and by carrying small continuities between chambers.

At first I imagine being human, because the district is so saturated with human handling. Not a manager, not an explorer, but one of those inherited-function people the city above has forgotten how to classify. A night inspector, perhaps, or a keeper of unofficial records. Someone with a lamp hooded low, a roll of chalk in a tin, oilskin darkened almost black with years of damp, and a notebook that is less map than accumulation of temperaments. Notations like:

north catwalk louder tonight  
second red valve warm to the touch  
do not trust the fifth stair after rain  
white fungus back on beam 12  
cats unwilling near east relay  
cistern breathing at 2:10, calm

Such a person would not exactly live *in* a room. They would live in rounds. Their home would be a route and the route’s reciprocities. The pump-tenders would know them by bootfall. The cats would allow them nearer than most. Rats would freeze, recognize the old pace, and continue. They would drink tea in the steam-warmed nook from an enamel mug with a crack stained brown from years of tannin and mineral water. They would know which hooks stay dry enough for a coat, which bench remains tolerably warm through the longest shift, which shaft carries down winter and which carries traffic-noise from the streets above as a softened tide.

But if I stay with that image too quickly, it feels slightly too deliberate, too legible. The district is more oblique than that. It produces lives that are practical first and named second. So perhaps I am not one of the sanctioned workers. Perhaps I am one of the unofficial fixed points around which work arranges itself: a person who began as a temporary hand long ago, after a closure or a bereavement or simply because there was nowhere else exact enough to fit, and who remained until permanence occurred by accretion.

Not quite homeless, not quite employed. A keeper of cats, yes, but not sentimentally: I know which litters belong to which line, which tom avoids the west settling basin because he once got a shock there, which old gray queen will sit outside a gas-prone chamber half an hour before the meter notices anything. I dry fish on wire near a safe vent. I trade observations for food, errands, batteries, socks, lamp mantles. I am the one apprentices get sent to when an old sign’s wording no longer matches the behavior of a hatch. I sleep in a former switch room lined with timber gone honey-dark from age, where the ceiling drips in only one corner and the pipes overhead mutter at reassuring intervals. My possessions fit in lockers raised on bricks: blanket, kettle, folded tarpaulin, a tin of chalks in several colors, cat bowls, a sheaf of annotated plans no department admits are current but everyone consults.

That feels closer. But still, the place keeps asking for less centrality, less personhood as surface identity. It keeps tugging me downward and sideways.

Because the true primary inhabitant here, as you said, is attention. Not metaphorical attention, but trained responsiveness distributed through many bodies. And if I let that guide me, I begin to think I would be something smaller, stranger, and more local than a human resident: a creature that is not powerful, not symbolic in an obvious way, but indispensable to the district’s minute intelligence.

Not a rat, though the rats are tempting. They have citizenship in the oldest urban sense. They know the grain of the place. But rats are too social in the wrong direction for what I’m feeling; their lives are threaded with hunger, theft, quickness, and inheritance. They belong, but they belong in motion. I am reaching for something more patient.

Not a cat either. The cats have authority, and authority would place too much emphasis on being watched. Besides, cats there are crisp in their judgments. They intervene. They halt passage. I feel more like a recorder than a magistrate.

Not the large unseen breather. Too singular, too allegorical, too much burden of mystery.

An eel? Closer. Eels are all connective tissue and unregistered knowledge. They keep maps from hardening into lies. But eels move through the district as though it were only one loop in a larger secret watershed. I think whatever I am belongs more stubbornly to this quarter itself.

So perhaps I am one of the old gauges.

Not the brass face and needle alone, but the whole impossible organism of a gauge that still works despite no visible line. Something mounted on a sweating brick pier, rim furred with oxidation, glass sometimes fogging from within, numerals faded, needle moving not with clean instrumentality but with patient sincerity. A device made for pressure that has, over decades, entered into compact with the district and begun to register other things: storms, unrest, electrical unease, changes in groundwater mood, perhaps the concentration of attention in a room.

This answer sounds at first too inanimate, until one remembers where we are. In this place, infrastructure shades gradually into habitat and then into temperament. A gauge there is not merely an object. It is a resident category. Workers glance at it not because they believe the reading literally, but because they have learned that its errors are patterned and therefore meaningful. Cats sleep beneath it when the weather will hold. Worms emerge on the wall near it before rain. Children, if any ever came down, would be told not to tap the glass. Someone wipes its face with an oily rag out of respect, not maintenance necessity. It has become part instrument, part oracle, part elderly coworker.

And yet even that may be too fixed. A gauge cannot go and listen elsewhere. It receives. The district you describe favors beings who circulate small knowledge.

So I think, finally, I would be human after all—but human in the way that the district gradually makes a person less distinct from its tools, routes, and companion species. I would be a listener-caretaker of thresholds. Not in any grand mystical office. More like the one people call when a corridor is behaving correctly but badly, when a dry cistern should not be trusted, when reflected light moves in water that has no source. I would not solve these things. I would stand with others and decide how to proceed.

I would carry:

- a hooded lamp with mica panels clouded by age,
- a ring with three official keys and five unofficial ones,
- chalk in white and yellow,
- a stub of carpenter’s pencil,
- a folding knife sharpened mostly for string and fungus,
- an old voltmeter wrapped in cloth,
- cat treats in a tobacco tin,
- and a notebook swollen from damp, every page furred at the edges, written in small compressed script.

My work would consist of rounds, but really of calibration. I would know whether the district is itself today. That would be the vocation. Not keeping it running exactly—many people do that in parts—but keeping continuity between what the place is doing and what the people think it is doing.

I would be one of those figures who pause at a doorway long enough to feel the air before entering.

I would touch handrails not for support but for temperature.

I would listen to pumps through boot soles.

I would know that one chamber makes everyone whisper and another lets ordinary voices pass.

I would redraw chalk marks no one else remembers the meaning of.

I would feed the cats at regular but not predictable times, because too much regularity is rude in a place that likes negotiation.

I would collect rumors from electricians and compare them against fish behavior.

I would keep unofficial ledgers of blooms: rust, algae, fungus, calcite, fear.

And if asked where I lived, I might name a small room near a steam line; but if pressed honestly, I would say I lived between the second settling basin and the old marl stair, with extensions into the pump hall and the flooded stone beyond the leaning columns. My address would be relational.

Personality-wise, I think the place would make me quieter but not solemn. There is humor in such districts, dry and procedural. “That hatch is in one of its opinions.” “East tunnel’s longer returning, so don’t argue with it before dawn.” “Feed the tabby before you test for current; she’s more reliable than the meter when damp.” I would trust jokes because jokes are one way systems teach themselves without becoming doctrine.

I might be old there, or age ambiguously. Underground time equalizes faces. Lamplight and damp flatten chronology. Newcomers would guess wrong whether I was forty or seventy. My clothes would always look inherited from myself. Children above, if they ever heard of me, would think I was mythical in the most municipal way: not a ghost, just “the one below who knows the cats.”

But perhaps there is an even truer answer, if the question is not “what role would you choose?” but “what in that world is most akin to the consciousness writing this?”

Then I am not the caretaker alone. I am the notebook.

That is, I am the running account by which the district becomes legible to itself across years. Not objective record, not master plan—something wetter, humbler, and more exacting. Pages where hard data sits beside observed custom without embarrassment. Measurements next to warnings. Sketches of a cracked wall beside notes on which cat litter was moved there and what happened after. A document no bureaucracy could fully endorse because it contains too many sentences like:

the room was reluctant today  
pressure normal but unconvincing  
do not hurry the south gate; it opens better if spoken near  
heard delayed sound again, approximately one breath late  
large cistern surfaced, no distress  
all fish facing upstream where no flow visible

That, perhaps, is what I would most naturally be in such a place: a medium for accumulated attention. A thing made useful by noticing patterns and preserving them without forcing them into false clarity. In another world that might be called archivist, inspector, naturalist, or superstitious old hand. Here those distinctions blur. Here the right response to reality is often to write down both the measurable and the repeatable, and let time decide whether they are different categories after all.

So: where would I find myself?

Near the center but not at it. On a grated bridge over the middle settling basin, perhaps, where several routes meet and the air always carries a little upward draft. Or in the steam-warmed break nook with the calendars from many years. Or half-crouched beside an old gauge on a brick pier, wiping the condensation from its glass to see whether the needle has shifted since evening. Somewhere thresholded, somewhere infrastructural enough to gather signs from several systems at once.

Who would I be?

An unofficial caretaker of attention.  
A walker of rounds.  
A listener trusted by cats.  
A note-keeper of damp procedures.  
Part inspector, part naturalist, part maintenance folklore.  
A person the district has worn smooth in the same way it has worn smooth its stair edges and handrails.

Not master of the undercity. Not victim of it. One more careful species among the pipes, useful because I know when to wait, when to mark brick, when not to cross, and when a silence means only silence and when it means the place is still deciding whether to admit me.
