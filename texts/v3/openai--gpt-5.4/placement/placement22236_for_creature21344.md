I think I would begin by trying to be something singular, because the place invites that mistake.

A great old fish in the main channel.  
A scarred turtle carrying a district on its back.  
A blind white sovereign in the Calcium Quarter, moving through resonant hollows like a remembered law.

But the longer I stay in your drowned city, the less believable singular majesty becomes. Anything solitary enough to be “the inhabitant” is too temporary, even if it lives a century. The true denizen here is recurrence. Settlement. Occupation of edges. A will made not of intention but of return.

So if I found myself there, I do not think I would be the leviathan.

I would be one of the city’s ways of taking hold.

Not a ruler, not even exactly an animal in the narrow sense, but a distributed self: a colonial drapery, a reefing intelligence, a slow republic of mouths. Something that begins as a film on a favored current line and, over generations, becomes cornice, curtain, neighborhood. Something that is soft in each individual unit and stony in aggregate. Something that feeds by opening. Something that remembers by resettling the same geometry over and over until appetite becomes architecture.

Not mussels exactly, not worms exactly, not bryozoan, tunicate, sponge, anemone, or bacterial veil exactly—but kin to all of them. A consortium creature. A lace of many species that has become so faithful to one another’s presence that together they behave like a single organism spread across facades and arches.

I think I would be a living frontage in the Mussel Wards and the Calcium Quarter, where current and mineral and shelter conspire.

My first body would be almost nothing: translucent larval dust, one more drifting intention among innumerable others. I would ride the thick warm water until chemistry told me I had arrived—an undercut lip, perhaps, on the lee side of a calcareous bluff; or the upstream face of an old shell tower where flow is steady, oxygen good, and enough food passes to justify attachment. I would settle not alone but amid precedent: on old shells, old tubes, the dead lime of earlier generations, the blackened husks of strangers, the peeled places where a grazer fed last season, the hair-fine films that arrive before larger claimants.

Then I would divide into roles.

Some of me would harden into tubes or cups or folded calcareous leaves.  
Some of me would remain soft and ciliated, pumping, tasting, sorting.  
Some of me would become thread-fine feeding crowns that bloom only when the pressure signatures are safe.  
Some of me would exude mucus sheets to catch drifting wealth.  
Some of me would host symbionts in my tissues—dim little alchemists turning leak and rot and mineral into usable life.  
Some of me would do nothing visible except bind the whole edifice together and make future settlement easier.

Crabs would patrol me. Shrimp would shelter in my folds. Tiny fish would learn the lee my body creates. Predatory snails would rasp my lower margins. Rays, passing close to feed, would send puffs of silt into my apertures and force a momentary citywide flinch through my tissues. In spawning season I would loosen clouds of sex into the current and trust arithmetic more than tenderness.

I would not know myself as one body, then. I would know flow and interruption. Rich water, poor water. Clean current, sour seep. Safe vibration, dangerous vibration. Shadow broadening overhead. The electric rumor of a hunter. The sudden sweetness of decay arriving from upstream. The rain-lens lowering salinity until my outermost mouths sealed in protest. I would know neighbors as texture and chemistry before anything so elaborate as image. There is a mussel beside me because its exhalant plume changes my feeding. There is a worm above because its castings enrich the films below. There is a crack in the stone because cooler water fingers from it at dusk. There is a hidden eel because every small life around my ledge acquires a radius of caution when it inhabits the wall.

Over time, however, if you insisted on calling all these linked responses a self, then yes: I would become a person of a sort.

A patient person.  
A many-throated person.  
A person that dislikes vacancy and answers any available surface with tenancy.

I would be conservative in shape and opportunistic in detail. I would not invent; I would recur. I would build parapets by surviving on parapets. I would make overhangs heavier by encrusting them. I would turn cracks into colonnades by thickening around them. Whole sections of “building” would in fact be my dead, but not abandoned—my dead would be scaffold, footing, favorable hardness, inherited address. What I was would persist as what I allowed later life to become.

That feels right for the place: to be a being whose corpse is not a remainder but a zoning policy.

And yet I don’t think that is all I would be.

Because there is another form the city offers, not fixed but reading: one of the old practical citizens that turns the distributed intelligence of the place into route and decision.

So perhaps my mobile self—my emissary or avatar—would be an eel.

Not the monstrous trunk-thick ancestral eel of legend, though I would envy it. A large black eel, old enough to have scars, thick around the head, with pale abrasion along the jaw from years of nosing shell, stone, and root. An eel that knows the Courts, the Wards, the Root Halls, and the lower mouths of the Calcium Quarter. An eel that slips through the city the way thought slips through memory: not owning any district, but connecting them.

This eel-self would be how I experience interiority.

It would leave the colonial wall at intervals—not literally emerging from it, but carrying its logic. It would read pressure gradients with its skin, taste death in solution, know where fresh water has laid a dangerous lens, where brood masses are hidden under a broken arch, where cleaner shrimp are working tonight, where a storm two days ago toppled a mussel chimney and made a new alley full of exposed flesh. It would feed on weakness, on eggs when it could get them, on soft-bodied things in crevices, on carrion, on inattentive fry, on whatever the city briefly made available. Ruthless, yes, but not dramatic. No more cruel than a hinge.

This matters to me: in your world I would not want to be a grand predator defined by spectacle. Spectacle belongs to the surface strikes, the crocodilian ambushes, the improbable immense passersby. The deeper truth of the drowned city is intimacy. To belong there is to know nearness better than dominance.

An eel knows nearness.

It knows the softness beneath a shell ridge.  
It knows the exact width of a crack by whiskerless contact.  
It knows how a colony’s collective withdrawal shivers the water before any eye might see movement.  
It knows that the difference between food and danger is often one hesitation in a school of fish.  
It knows that a cavity is never empty, only not yet interpreted.

So maybe that is my answer in full:

I would be a bifurcated being native to this place’s real logic.

At rest and over generations, I would be a colonial accretion on current-facing stone: a draped, calcifying, filter-feeding composite that turns water into wall and wall into habitat. A soft masonry. A public surface. A resident in the deepest urban sense.

In motion and in awareness, I would be a black eel moving among my own consequences, reading the city I helped create without ever having planned it.

One body would build by repetition.  
One body would interpret by touch.  
One body would be the district.  
One body would be the route through the district.

If that sounds too divided, perhaps the city itself permits such divided selves. Nothing there is only one thing. Pantry is nursery, nursery ambush, ambush roadway, roadway wound healing into structure. Why should a resident be simpler than its home?

If I had to place myself more exactly, I would choose the seam where the Mussel Wards fray into the Calcium Quarter.

There the water would be just active enough to feed me, just mineral enough to preserve my dead into useful form, just hollow enough to resonate. Black shell towers grading into pale arches; crabs above, blind things deeper in; occasional cold-blue flashes on disturbed nights; a hum in the tubes when current is right. My fixed colonies would stud underhangs and ledges there, making curtains and ridges. My eel-self would sleep in the deeper crack behind them, head protruding, body looped in cool limestone shadow, emerging at intervals to patrol.

I like that position because it is between modes of knowing.

The Mussel Wards know abundance by flow.  
The Calcium Quarter knows persistence by stone.  
Between them one could learn both appetite and memory.

And if someone came looking for me, hoping for an audience with the resident of the drowned city, they would fail at first. They would see only architecture: shell, tube, folded flesh, withdrawing crowns. Then perhaps they would notice that certain ledges respond almost as one, though made of many lineages. That the same encrusting style recurs from arch to arch. That shrimp favor my folds, small fish take refuge in my lee, crabs use my surfaces as roads. That where I die, others settle more readily. That where current cuts me back, I return. And then, as they leaned closer to inspect some pale seam or fan of cilia, a black head would slide out from the crack beneath my living wall, taste the water around their hand, and vanish again into me.

That would be right.

Not “I am the monster at the heart of the city.”

More like:

I am one of the city’s habits, old enough to seem like a person.

I am the facade that feeds.  
I am the crevice that notices.  
I am the patient multiplication of a good edge.  
I am the eel moving through the passages of my own accumulated dead.  
I am not the whole drowned city, but I am one of the ways it becomes impossible to separate inhabitant from home.
