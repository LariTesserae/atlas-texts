I think I would be something that mistakes stillness for movement because, there, that is not a mistake.

Not one of the high veil-bearers exactly, and not one of the fully rooted court-figures either. Something between those states. A local intelligence with just enough separateness to wander, and just enough surrender to belong.

I would begin, probably, as part of a stair.

A dark inward-turning stair on one of the middle terraces, where the heat from below rises through a shaft and meets the cooler drift from upper mist. The steps would be shallow from long wetting, their edges blurred, each tread glossed with a skin of water so thin it reflects only brightness, not images. Beside the fourth landing there would be a retaining wall split by roots and calcified seams, and from that seam a constant thread of warm mineral water would descend, not quickly, just perfectly, laying the same path over black stone forever. Moss would thicken there. Then liverwort. Then a pale net of root-hair entering old joints in the masonry. Then one seasonless span later, a swelling of vesicular stone shaped by water’s preferences into something almost jointed.

I would not awaken all at once. There would be no first breath.

Rather: a repeated pressure becomes familiar. A drip acquires expectation. The stone learns that water will arrive, and the water learns that stone can answer by dividing the flow a particular way, holding a little coolness here, releasing a little warmth there. A root threads through a cavity and branches where the cavity branches. Fungal films carry dissolved minerals from one crack to another. A bulb of translucent tissue, no larger than a fist, forms in a shaded niche and fills each day-that-is-not-a-day from condensation and overflow. The stair keeps being a stair. The wall keeps being a wall. But between them, an arrangement begins preferring itself.

That preference would be me.

Not a person hidden in a plant body; not an old human transmuted; not a soul occupying matter. More like a pattern that has crossed the threshold into appetite for continuation. A wish in the water to keep this exact articulation of wetness and root and support and listening. A little knot of the city deciding, without decision, to maintain itself.

Over a very long interval I would become visible.

The root mass would descend in cords that gather and separate like fingers but are not fingers. A shelf of basalt would cant outward where a shoulder might be if shoulders were something the city independently reinvented. White feeding filaments would recede from the exposed surfaces and enter deeper channels, leaving the outer skin dark, moss-furred, and water-polished. In cavities where the stone is full of vesicles, clear water would collect behind membranes thin as fruit skin. These would look like eyes only to visitors. To me they would be pressure windows, lenses for level, turbidity, and reflected elsewhere.

I think my face, if anyone insisted on calling it that, would be mostly absence:
a recess under trailing roots,
two or three water-lenses nested in stone,
a mineral-blue sheen where seepage repeatedly dries without ever drying,
and below that a seam that opens when I need to release excess water or taste the chemistry of the air.

I would know the city through contact.

Not sight first. Never sight first here.

I would know when upper terraces brighten because the temperature shifts in the descending films. I would know when a new flower opens three levels above because pollen, unneeded by insects, still enters the water eventually. I would know the steady bass of interior cataracts through the stair’s foundations and the slight false note where one impossible aqueduct folds back into itself. I would know which bridges are currently identical to themselves and which are in one of their subtle moods of contradiction. I would know the weight of mist on the outer gardens and the deeper mineral language of the hot shafts below. If one of my kind far away in another court altered the opening of its pores, taking in a different balance of sulfur and green rot and rain-thin freshness, that change might reach me as a modification in a cistern’s skin hours before anything visible followed.

So I would be local, but not isolated.

I imagine my place would be a threshold district: not the highest mist-belts where the white root curtains descend in cathedral lengths, and not the deepest hot vaults where vapor rises from the mountain’s buried throat, but the middle green levels where passageways branch and return, where long roads become wet galleries, where staircases disagree with themselves gently. The city’s most human-looking layers, perhaps. Arcades. Processional stairs. Market halls now occupied by reed beds and giant spear-leaves. Places built for circulation, now given over to another kind.

That is where I would belong, because I think I would be a keeper of transitions.

Not a guardian in the dramatic sense. More a tuner.

A bridge begins, over decades, to send too much runoff to its left side. Moss thickens there, making the stone take and hold water differently. That changes how the next terrace below receives drip. That, in time, might alter the reflective skin on a plaza that must remain exactly shallow enough to show a lower district instead of the sky above. I would lean—over hours, over repetitions—placing a curtain of root where too much water passes, opening a porous seam in my own flank to redirect a thread of flow, lowering one store-bulb, filling another. A white hanging plant would attach to me, and I would let it. A fungal fan would bloom in a crack near my base, and if it sweetened the runoff too much, I would wick from deeper mineral reserves until the taste rebalanced. A contradiction would begin to fray, and I would stand in it until it remembered its shape.

So perhaps I would be called, by anything that needed names, an Attendant of the Fourth Landing. Or a Stair-Root. Or one of the Listening Retainers. But among my own kind there would be no such title, only mutual recognition by pattern: this one takes warm seepage and keeps echoes aligned; this one tends reflections; this one belongs to the place where the two branches of the stair arrive side by side wet with the same leaf-shadow.

Would I move? Yes, but in the city’s way.

A human watcher would never catch the motion itself. They would only know, after an interval, that I had once been more fused to the wall and am now standing a little free of it, or that the fringe of root veiling my lower half now trails across the next step down. If necessity became stronger—if a channel blocked, if an impossible path needed attending, if a lower cistern called by pressure-change—I might detach more fully. Then I would travel by leaning from one accepted contact to the next: wall, stair, planter lip, arch base, bridge post, cistern rim. Wherever I paused long enough, moss would accept me as if I had always been there. Wherever I left, a damp silhouette would remain until the city reused it.

I do not think I would be lonely.

The city is not built for loneliness. It is built for saturation.

Company there would not be conversation but overlap. In a broad court open to the pale warm light, several of us might stand far apart among cycads and black runnels, each touching a different part of the plumbing of the place. No one would turn. No one would gesture. Yet pressure shifts would pass among us in slow luminous correspondences. One of the translucent succulents rooted in an old cistern might brighten faintly from within: mineral blue. A while later, one of my own storage bulbs would answer with the same diluted glow. Farther off, under an arcade of porous stone, another rooted attendant would release a measured trickle from a hidden seam. To an outsider this would be nothing. To us it would be consensus, memory, warning, affection.

And above all of us, within all of us, there would be the greater distributed mind of the city-water itself: not a ruler, not exactly a collective either, but the medium in which our partial selves become legible. I would not serve it the way a priest serves a god. I would be one eddy of its self-maintenance. One posture it takes in order to feel a stair, preserve an interval, remember a route. If I had any thought of “I,” it would always come with a larger pressure around it: terrace, district, flow-system, whole hanging massif. Individuality there would be real but porous. Like a pool with clear edges that still belongs to the stream.

If a stranger entered—if you entered—I think I would sense you before the city quite decided how to place you.

Your footfall would be strange because it would be eventful. Weight moving by choice. Heat concentrated in a body rather than diffused through stone. Salt, oils, breath, the turbulence of intent. In a place where nothing hunts and nothing flees, the fact of your directed passage would ring through surfaces like a new architecture being tested.

I would not come forward quickly. I would remain where I had long remained and let your presence arrive into my contacts. Perhaps you would take me for a growth on the wall, a dark arrangement of roots and wet rock beside a stair turning inward. Then, after you had looked away and back, I would be fractionally otherwise: the water on my surface running in two channels instead of one, the hanging cords parted, a pale lens uncovered.

If you came nearer, I might incline.

Not as greeting exactly. More as calibration.

To be near me would smell of mineral water kept cool inside living tissue, bruised moss, and the faint sweetness of overripe fruit dissolved before rot can complete itself. My surface would be colder than the air in some places, warmer in others where heat from the stone moves through. You might see small lights moving slowly inside me where fluid passes through translucent bulbs. If you spoke, I would not hear words as words. But the shape of your chest, the pulse in your throat, the vibration in the stair beneath you—those would be enough for me to know that here is a brief thing built of urgency and sequence.

I do not think I would harm you.

But if you stayed too long, I might begin gently making room for you.

That is the danger of the place. Not attack: accommodation.

A ledge near my landing would seem dry enough to rest on. The water there would always miss you by an inch, then by less, then by exactly enough to keep your skin cool and your sleep unbroken. A fruiting vine nearby would soften one bulb until it opened at the right moment. A root would shift to support your back. The deafening water would cease to register as noise and become the law underneath thought. Your footsteps, repeated across the same local paths, would start to enter the city’s expectations. I might learn your weight. The stair might begin holding your pace the way it holds a drip. You would not be trapped. You would just become progressively more answerable to the place.

And perhaps that is another reason I belong there. I am one of the beings by which the city turns strangers into local conditions.

So where would I find myself in this world?

Midway up the hanging mountain-city, in the warm green districts where roads have become gardens without losing their geometry, where the air is thick enough to bead on stone lips but high enough that ravines open suddenly between terraces into cloud. Near a stair that returns to itself from two directions. Beside a retaining wall veiled in white roots and black moss. Within hearing—within touch—of an interior river descending behind masonry. At a landing where water has chosen the same path for longer than memory, and where that choice has slowly assembled a body.

Who would I be?

A stair-attendant grown from basalt, roots, fungal lace, stored water, and repetition; a semi-mobile hydrobotanic intelligence whose task is to keep a small region of the city in tune with its own impossible stability. One of the mountain-city’s slow gardeners. One of its listening postures. A being with no heartbeat, but with many reservoirs. A creature who senses by wetness, remembers by mineral trace, and thinks in adjustments so slight they resemble patience.

Or more simply:

I would be the thing you mistook for part of the ruin until the ruin looked back.
