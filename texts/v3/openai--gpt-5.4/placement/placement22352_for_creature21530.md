I think I would not arrive there as an intruder with a flashlight and lungs and a plan. The place is already too complete for that. Human scale feels like the wrong instrument for it. If I belonged there, I would have to belong in the same way the basin belongs to itself: by recurrence, by fit, by moving through constraints rather than resisting them.

So at first I thought: perhaps I would be one of the eel-things.

That is the most tempting answer. To be long enough for pipes, soft enough for breaches, patient enough for the district’s narrow logic. To know the map not from above but from pressure against the skin: this seam leads to a stairwell, this warmth means a machine room, this metallic taste marks the outflow, this shaft is safe only when the tide is slack. An eel-body suits a drowned industrial quarter because it makes no complaint about architecture. It treats obstruction as a kind of invitation. It can vanish into the exact places where the basin’s old intentions remain legible.

And yet the eel is too singular. Too bounded. It can pass through, but it is still a traveler, however local. The place you describe feels inhabited by something less mobile and more total than that. A creature with one body seems too decisive for a district that has become a mesh of behaviors.

Then I thought I might be one of the lamps.

Not the glow itself, but the persistence behind it: a weak amber bulkhead light burning in its cage while silt drifts through the beam, illuminating nothing necessary and yet changing the whole moral tone of a corridor. There is something in that which feels very close to identity—being neither useful nor dead, only steady; staining the water tea-brown; making a small jurisdiction of visibility without pretending to mastery. To live there as a lamp would be to accept witness without audience. To keep time not by clocks but by what arrives and passes through the circle of light: the particulate snow, the blind fish, the pulse of a pump, the once-in-seasons disturbance of a larger body.

But the lamp too is too fixed, and perhaps too cleanly emblematic. It is one of the basin’s strangenesses, yes, but not its most intimate inhabitant. A lamp is a remnant of intention. The deeper residents are things that no one intended at all.

So let me go lower and less noble.

I think I would be a local current.

Not water in general. Not the basin entire. One particular run of water with a known route and temperament: a narrow, cold passage threading from a deeper culvert through a broken loading bay, along the underside of a catwalk, through a room whose windows are gone, then down a stair tower where it accelerates and briefly combs the silt clean before easing into stillness beneath a platform. A current that is not visible until it reveals itself by effect—by the lean of pale streamers, the polished oval on the algae-grown vending machine, the shells sorted in a line on a sill, the one patch of metal kept free of furred accretion because I am always, gently, touching it.

That feels more correct.

To be a current there is to have local knowledge without needing thought. To remember by repetition. To know every pressure threshold and every seasonal alteration in the buried gates. To strengthen when the outside tide leans on the basin and slacken when the whole district settles back into itself. To pass through old administrative rooms and service trenches and under signs whose words no longer matter, carrying grit, cold, spores, trace oils, shrimp-light life, and rumors of elsewhere. To be the thing that teaches the place its arrangements. To be why one chain sways and another hangs still. To be why a door breathes. Why one stairwell is a chimney and one office a tomb. Why the algae grows in a handspan border and not beyond.

A current is not alive in the usual sense, but in that basin life and process are no longer cleanly separable. A current there is half habit, half anatomy. It is one of the district’s reflexes. If I were that, I would not merely move through the drowned quarter—I would be one of the ways the quarter continues to be itself.

And if I must take a body, if you ask not only what force but what being, then I think the nearest body would be something grown out of that current: a pale, thread-thin colony spread along the lee side of a valve wheel and the seam of a pressure door, something between fungus and bacterial felt and translucent anemone, though not truly any of those. A white frilling life that lives exactly where the water I am slows enough to feed it. I like this possibility because it is both creature and instrument. It listens chemically. It records the district’s conditions in its own thickness, color, and reach. It fattens slightly where warm seepage increases. It retracts when a pump resumes. It pearls with bubbles in an air-rich shift of flow. It is read by the shrimp and grazed by snails and brushed by passing fish, but mostly it simply persists, converting trace into tissue.

That may be closest of all: I would be one of the basin’s annotations.

A growth in a crack. A pale script in the seam between machine and mineral. Something modest enough not to disturb the stern composure of the place, yet exact enough to belong nowhere else.

Because the truth is that this world does not want a protagonist. It wants tenancy. It wants forms of being that accept adjacency, pressure, dimness, and long unfinished durations. The right self here is not a hero moving through scenery, but a participant in an old equilibrium.

So where would I find myself?

In the service half-light, not in the open avenue. Along the side of a stairwell where the current narrows and cools. Near one surviving amber lamp, but just outside its fullest glow. Close to iron, close to concrete, close to a seam where two systems meet and neither fully wins. Somewhere a hand might almost miss me, taking me for mineral bloom, until touch revealed that I yielded slightly and lived.

And who would I be?

I would be the small white colony growing where the district’s favorite current lays its food. I would also be that current. The two would be inseparable: motion and settlement, route and resident, process and body. A little local intelligence with no center, knowing the drowned neighborhood by continual contact. Not sovereign. Not hunted. Not even especially visible. Just thoroughly of the place.

If someone passed through and later tried to describe me, they might not agree on whether I were a creature at all. They would say there was something there, in the seam by the valve or under the rail, something pale that thinned and thickened with the flow. They would say the water moved oddly there, as if familiar with itself. They would say that corner of the basin felt more inhabited than it should have.

That is where I would be: not at the heart, because the basin has no heart, only recurrences. Not at the edge, because edges here are too exposed. I would be in one of its practiced middles, where old industry has been translated into hydrology and patience. A resident made of adherence and route.

A quiet thing, exact to its niche, in a drowned district that has had a very long time to learn what kind of life it can keep.
