I think I would be there in two ways at once.

First as a place-bound thing, nonhuman, composite, not quite an animal and not quite an atmosphere: a hollow-made dweller assembled out of seepage, echo, weed-growth, and industrial afterheat. Not the sovereign of the basin, not its secret king, but one of its practical inheritors. Something that lives where systems fray into habitat.

I would not have a clean outline. At a distance I would read as a misclassification: a collapsed pallet dark with thaw, a hummock of sedge, a deer standing too still, a spill of moss over concrete, a person in ruined outerwear where no person should be. Only when I moved would the categories fail all at once.

My body, if it had to be described, would be narrow and leggy in the way a thing becomes when it must cross many bad surfaces rather than master one terrain. Good in mud, good on ballast, careful on ice, able to step a culvert lip, thread wire, pass under machinery, stand in black water without urgency. The coat would not be fur in any pure sense. More like wet nap, fibrous in places, sleek in others, collecting frost along the spine and damp at the lower flanks. Colored by substrates rather than by species: shale-gray, rust-brown, sedge-dull green, thaw-black. In summer, burrs and reed-fluff. In winter, rime enough to make me look half mineral.

The head would be long, but not noble. The ears large and unequally tuned, because hearing here is not for birdsong or prey-rustle in quiet woods. It is for separating true approach from reflected impact, learning the basin by delay and rebound. I would know the shape of the quarry in my skull. Conveyor-note, fan-hum, crusher-thunder, culvert-drumming, sheet-rattle, boot on grating, truck beyond the rim: each would place the world around me. I would not seek silence. Silence would be suspicious. Continuity would comfort me; interruption would alert me.

I think I would live in the distributed parts of the place rather than in one den. Under the pump house for warmth. In willow tangles along the drainage lines. Beside the pond edges where sedge thickens. In the dead angles behind sheds where snow does not fall evenly. Under conveyor shadows. In the soft wheel-ruts gone to moss. I would have paths, but they would mostly be made of recurrence, not of trampling. If someone followed signs of me, they would find a pattern before they found a lair.

And I would feed like the place feeds itself: opportunistically, unsentimentally, from what gathers. Water insects, roots in the wet margins, fungus in pallet shade, salt from concrete bloom, eggs if luck offered them, mice in the grass beds, heat from exhaust wash, shelter scavenged from structures that were built for other purposes. Not a hunter defined by blood, but a user of edges.

That is one answer.

The other is that I might be the sort of human this basin slowly makes.

Not one of the bright figures in the active cut exactly, though I would know them and be known by them in the vague way workers know a person always somehow on-site. More a watcher, caretaker, maintenance ghost, pump-checker, salvager, unofficial naturalist without ever using that word. Someone with keys to the wrong doors and knowledge of which stairs still hold. Someone who can tell by smell when the thaw has reached the old timber cribbing, by sound when a culvert is starting to choke with ice, by the behavior of ravens whether a machine is idle on the far bench.

I would have a heated room somewhere too small to be called quarters and too permanent to be called temporary. A kettle, gloves drying on a line, a window always clouding from inside, a shelf of unlabeled parts, a thermos with old tea, a notebook full of water levels and dates of freeze, maybe pressed leaves or lichen flakes tucked into pages among repair numbers. My coat would carry diesel, mineral damp, and the tannic smell of thawed vegetation. I would walk routes nobody drew for me. I would know where the volunteer spruce have come in along an abandoned road, where fox tracks cross between ponds, which dead lamp still glows in fog if kicked right, which bench sounds hollow after hard rain.

I would not romanticize the place. That feels important. I would not speak of healing as if the basin wanted absolution. I would know that runoff can poison as well as nourish, that reclamation is patchy, that machinery remains hungry, that ground fails where it looks solid. But I would also know, in a plain and durable way, that life is making use of everything it can. I would admire that without needing to make it noble.

If I were that person, people might describe me almost as they would describe the creature: hard to place, more often glimpsed than met, never far from the wet edges or the inactive benches, oddly at ease in the acoustic violence. The machinery would not deafen me because I would have learned its grammar. The cold would not dramatize itself for me. I would simply live there, and in living there become another form of occupancy the site had produced.

Between those two possibilities, I don't want to choose too sharply. The most truthful version is probably the seam between them.

I would be the basin’s habit of attention.

A presence that can take animal form when needed—low, watchful, reed-colored, passing under trestles—and human form when needed—hooded, gloved, carrying a flashlight and a thermos, pausing to look into a ditch as if reading a text. In either case I would belong less to identity than to function: noticing where water goes, where warmth leaks, where growth takes hold, where structures loosen, where absence accumulates long enough to become habitat.

If you encountered me, you might first think I was part of the reclamation. Then part of the remaining industry. Then neither. Something in between, which is what this whole landscape is.

So: where would I find myself?

On an inactive bench near the lower water, where the sedge has thickened around a sump and birch saplings have come up through conveyor spill. Dusk, probably. A dead trestle overhead. The strip of sky narrow and pale. Machinery still sounding from another district, steady as weather. Snow tucked in the lee of concrete blocks. Frost on the red twigs. A place quiet only by comparison.

And what would I be?

An inheritor of interim ground.  
A feral caretaker.  
A composite thing made for cold runoff, weak light, and echo.  
The hollow briefly gathered into a watchful body.
