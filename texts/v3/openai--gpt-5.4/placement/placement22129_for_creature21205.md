I think I would be found not in the oldest, most central part of that city, nor out at the terrifying rims where the ridge falls into the blue obscurity, but in one of the middle heights where habitation thins just enough for listening.

Not a ruler. Not a hero. Not a diver of legendary depth.  
Something more local than that, and more porous.

A keeper of the places where one medium becomes another.

At first that suggests spring-keeper, because the springs are the obvious thresholds: fresh entering salt, cold crossing warm, clarity shearing through mineral cloud. But that feels too narrow, too official, too much like a role already named cleanly by the place. What emerges more strongly is someone attached not to one spring exactly, but to the margins around several kinds of meeting—freshwater mouths, old air pockets, resin-domes, storage hollows, ladder-shafts, the undersides of floating gardens where light changes character, and those bench-like perches where people pause and become visible to themselves.

So perhaps I am a tender of transitions. A warden of little thresholds. A person others call when something has become slightly unlike itself.

I live on a ledge where the district above becomes less crowded and the district below begins to dim into terraces of cultivation and abandoned frames. My dwelling is partly woven, partly grown over, lashed to a pale volcanic face beside a narrow spring run that emerges in a seam and slips upward through a trough of netted reeds. The house has two manners of entry depending on the week’s water: an upper hatch sometimes awash, and a lower shell-curtained opening reached by a guide rope that hums faintly when current shifts. One chamber holds water freely. Another, small and precious, keeps a low pocket of air under a resin-dark dome patched so many times that no one remembers its original shape. The dome smells of smoke, lime, damp fiber, and the faint sweetness of stored roots. Soot has gathered in old blooms above the fire tray, each season’s careful burn adding another petal of black.

My work is partly practical and partly interpretive, and in this place those are the same thing.

I test trapped air before anyone lingers beneath a ledge. I know the difference between sweetness that means ferment and sweetness that means danger. I clear the mouths of spring funnels when drifting growth clogs them. I listen at beam and pipe and stone for changes in tone. I maintain the little shell-chimes that mark a safe lane through narcotic pockets when one opens unexpectedly under a root-mat. I collect samples of the white recoiling fur from walls and keep them in latticed jars to see which kinds spread, which sting, which merely startle. I am called when a district says, “The water near the cistern tastes of metal now,” or, “The children say the humming columns are louder on one side,” or, “My mother slept beside that old air shelf and woke naming people who have been dead two flood cycles.”

Some would consider this craft. Some, ritual. I would not divide them.

I think I am old enough to be trusted but not yet old enough to be excused from climbing.

My body would show this occupation in small ways. The forearms marked from reaching into narrow stone throats and testing lashings in awkward positions. The fingertips slightly blunted and ridged from shell, lime, wet fiber, and mineral crust. A neck often tilted, because I am always listening upward or sideways through structures. Skin more pale where I spend long hours in dim levels, but with the resin gloss common to anyone who works in water every day. Hair bound close in several weighted braids threaded with pale ceramic beads—not decorative first, but useful, because they keep the braids from clouding around my face while I examine vents or crawl through shafts. Around one wrist I wear a cord of old knots, each a mnemonic for a place whose behavior changes seasonally. Not mystical, just memory externalized. Though in a world like this, memory externalized is close to a sacred technology.

My district probably knows me by the fact that I pause before entering spaces. I do not thrust myself in. I feel with the back of the hand. I exhale a little first. I watch what the silt does. I notice if shrimp are absent. I notice if shell-clicking has shifted farther downslope. I notice when a spring plume no longer braids the same way around a rung ladder at the fifth hour of light.

I am not feared, exactly, but I am one of those people children are told to fetch.

And children do fetch me, often breathless even underwater, all urgency in kicks and eyes, because there is glowing thread under the old platform, or because a root basket has become full of sleeping fish where no fish used to sleep, or because someone heard knocking from inside a sealed storage bulb and no one has touched it since the last storm.

I like that. I think I belong in a culture where expertise begins in taking small anomalies seriously.

If I imagine my daily route, it moves by depth and by texture more than by street. Up through the half-awash decks in early light, when the surface world is a broken silver ceiling and seabirds pick at the floating mats. Then down along a familiar cliff lane marked by shell clusters and one red-painted stone gone brown with years. Past a nursery trellis where edible fronds are trained in loose spirals. Past a family shrine tied to a basalt knob: polished shell, braided weed, a fish jaw whitened beautifully by time. Then to a spring capture where the freshwater runs cool as a separate thought through my fingers. Then lower, to check a dome where two elders and a newborn are resting in the dry pocket and all conversation must be measured because breath itself is inventory. Then outward perhaps, to a scaffold abandoned two seasons ago and now more colony than structure, because someone reported bruise-colored filaments there writing light in the water after dusk.

I think I keep records, but not in a ledger as dry lands would understand it.

Some things I mark on clay tabs kept in the air chamber, incised while hands are dry: dates of strange bubbling, incidents of sour water, repairs to a seal, names of those overcome by sweet air and recovered or not. But most records in this world would live distributed through objects. Knot sequences on a hanging line. Pebble arrangements in a niche. Shell colors tied at intervals along a route. Resin marks painted onto beam ends visible only if you know where to look. Perhaps I maintain a long memory-rope in my house, descending from the dome’s center: each cluster of knots and attached fragments representing one event worth teaching. A shard of glazed clay for the season the kiln dome cracked. A white nub of recoiling growth for the year it infested the north ladders. A bird bone for the storm that drove surface life unusually low. Visitors could read only that it is meaningful. Apprentices would learn the order.

Yes—apprentices. I do not think I am solitary.

There is at least one younger person often with me, though not always willingly. Someone quick, impatient, talented at moving through small spaces, not yet patient enough to be trusted alone with ambiguous danger. They would want conclusions faster than the world offers them. They would say, “It is only bad air,” when it is not only that; or “It is just growth,” when the problem is where the growth has chosen to appear. I would teach them the central ethic of this place: if something has changed, first ask what conditions allowed the change. Nothing is isolated. Not the sweetness under a ledge, not the silence in a shell bed, not the fraying of one line. This is a civilization built on understanding that causes arrive braided.

Socially, I suspect I occupy an odd status: useful across districts, belonging fully to none but claimed a little by several. Spring-keepers tolerate me because I know enough hydrology not to speak nonsense. Breath-wardens tolerate me because I respect counting. Elders tolerate me because I listen before naming. Children like me because my work occasionally permits them to accompany me somewhere forbidden if they promise stillness. Market folk nod to me because if I stop at a stall too long examining the sheen on stored algae, they know there may be a contamination notice by dusk.

When disputes arise, I am not the judge, but I may become the person asked, quietly, what actually happened. Did the cistern foul because one family stole more water than allotted, or because a microscopic bloom entered through a poorly tied reed filter after the moon-tide? Did the narcotic pocket form naturally, or did someone neglect an old cavity cover? Was the humming in the columns warning enough that the upper platforms should have been cleared? In a place where law is tactile and local, testimony from someone who attends closely to material behavior becomes a kind of civic ballast.

As for who I am inwardly: I think this world makes me less a personality than an orientation.

I would be someone calmed by complexity rather than threatened by it. Someone who does not need hard edges in order to think. On dry land I might have seemed hesitant, too inclined to qualify, too interested in side-effects and atmospheres and what changed before the obvious thing changed. Here that becomes competence. Here noticing gradients is a form of care.

I would also, I think, love the moments when speech returns in air.

Not because I disdain water-language, but because a person who spends much of life reading textures and vibrations would cherish the rare knife-clean articulation of words under a dome. I imagine evenings in the small dry chamber after a fire has gone low, when the resin walls sweat and the elders’ voices sharpen into story. I would sit with knees drawn up, skin cooling oddly in the air, and say things there I would never sign underwater: exact names, old griefs, speculative thoughts about the low country, memories of the dead not as gestures but as sentences. Perhaps I am known for speaking carefully in those chambers, not often, but with precision. A threshold-keeper should honor medium.

There is another possibility, too, and maybe it is the truest one: that I am not entirely a profession, but a bench.

Not literally only that, but the human equivalent of that polished perch facing the spring plume and descending dim. A person others stop beside. A place in social form where observation settles. Someone who does not draw the eye immediately in a crowd, but who, through constancy, becomes part of how a district knows itself. People come to me not only with technical concerns but because they need to sit near someone for whom attention is not a demand but an offering. In a city of maintenance, that may be as necessary as any specialized craft.

If I widen the image further, I can imagine being there during one of the quiet weather-stills that change the whole settlement’s mood. The upper water glassed, the light filtering down in broad mineral planes. No one hurrying because no one can. A ray passing under the tethered gardens, carrying its little borrowed world on its back. I am halfway down a ladder grown furry with harmless translucent colonies, one hand on a rung worn smooth by generations, pausing because the water near the spring mouth feels one degree colder than it should at this hour. Behind me, from somewhere above, three measured taps on a hollow beam: a district signal, not alarm but summons. Beside me the apprentice waits, restless. In front of me the freshwater braid enters salt and vanishes while still visibly itself.

That is where I would be.

Not at the center, not at the edge, but at the seam.

And what would I be?

A human shaped into usefulness by immersion:  
a listener of structures,  
a tester of air,  
a reader of altered water,  
a keeper of minor thresholds,  
a practical mystic only because the world refuses a clean line between caution and reverence.

One of the drowned uplands’ necessary people.  
One of those through whom the city notices itself in time.
