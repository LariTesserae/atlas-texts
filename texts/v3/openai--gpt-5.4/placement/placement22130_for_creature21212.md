I think I would not be one of the people exactly, though I would come nearest to them.

Not an elder on the tripod, not a route-keeper of highest authority, not one of the inherited and well-seated presences whose every step has already been accepted by the plateau. I arrive too aware of arrival for that. Too conscious of asking. The oldest inhabitants of that country would not ask who they are in it; they would stand where they have been fitted and continue.

So if I found myself there, I think I would be something intermediate: a tolerated intelligence, local but not original. A being made by the same conditions as the high folk, but slightly offset from them. Not foreign enough to alarm the animals. Not rooted enough to be unquestioned.

A watcher of thresholds, perhaps.

But even that is too simple. Let me come at it more slowly.

In a place where reality has thinned not into chaos but into habit, the most natural self is not a hunter, ruler, or prophet. It is something that learns the margins where one commitment of the world gives way to another. Something that notices the half-finger detachments of shadow, the ledges that contain more ledges than stone permits, the paths that lengthen only for those who are inattentive. A creature of calibration, yes—but more than that, a creature of recurrence. Not merely alive there, but repeatedly alive there.

I think I would be the keeper of a narrow pass that does not always agree with itself.

Not in the sense of ownership. Nothing in that country is owned with confidence. Rather I would be found there so often, in so many adjacent moments, that the pass and I would have grown into one another's logic. Travelers would say there is someone at the pass, even on days when no figure can be seen. They would slow when entering it. They would touch the stone by the third notch down. They would leave a pellet of black lichen fuel, or a polished chip of sulfate, in a crack at knee height. Not as worship. As acknowledgement of a local intelligence that helps crossings become singular.

My body, if body is the word, would be human enough at a distance.

Short, layered, wrapped close. A person-shaped figure seated or standing in the lee of a dark basalt shoulder. Ash-brown skin or hide, hard to distinguish from weathered stone until I move. Hair pale with salts, bound in segmented cords. Hands broad and almost too still. Face narrow, not severe but economized. Eyes reflective in the dim way some mineral seams are reflective: not shining, but holding more light than they should.

Yet up close something would fail to resolve cleanly.

Perhaps my footprints do not always agree in age. Perhaps several sets of them lie on top of one another with no more disturbance than one set should make. Perhaps when I turn my head, the motion completes a fraction later in my shadow. Perhaps my breathing is audible only after I have passed. Perhaps the cold gathers on my clothing in crystalline fans that do not melt because they were never moisture to begin with, only the air deciding briefly to become visible around me.

I don't think I would be feared, exactly. Fear in that plateau seems reserved for misplacement, for stepping where the world is inattentive. I would be more like one of its dry cautions given shape.

Children would be taught about me very matter-of-factly:
if the path grows longer than your memory of it, wait;
if you see the seated one before the split stone, do not wave;
if they ask where you began this crossing, answer with the last place your feet were certain;
if there are two of them, take the left fork and do not count your steps aloud.

That last detail feels important. I think there would sometimes be more than one of me, but not as a spectacle. Just as a consequence of accumulated fit. The pass has been watched a long time. Enough long times laid beside one another and the watcher becomes plural without becoming many. Different postures of the same office, different survivals of the same person, different resolutions of one attention. Travelers accustomed to the place would not find this mystical. They would simply know which appearance means the near shelf is stable and which means the far descent has gone hollow for the day.

So: who would I be?

A pass-sitter.
A path-corrector.
A human-shaped residue of successful crossings.

Maybe I was once a person in the ordinary way. That would suit the place better than if I had always been uncanny. Perhaps long ago I belonged to one habitation crescent among the bone-trees, one lineage that kept a route between two basins where the shell-backed baskers turned all at once at first cold. Perhaps I was given, young, to accompany elders through uncertain cuts in the escarpment because I had unusual patience for standing still. Perhaps I survived where others did not, not by bravery but by exactness. Over time I crossed one difficult place so often, and so correctly, that when my body failed to continue, my occupancy did not.

That feels right for this world. Nothing leaves completely.

So I would be one of the people after all, but altered by local law into a more infrastructural kind of person. Not a ghost: ghosts imply severance, grievance, drama. This plateau dislikes drama. I would be a retained function. A bit of ancestry that never wholly stopped occurring. Less a dead inhabitant than an enduring route-behavior with a face.

My dwelling would be nearly invisible. A shallow chamber in the basalt beside the pass, entered through a seam too narrow to suggest interior space. Inside, it would widen improbably into a cool room with two sleeping shelves, though only one has been used for generations and even that usage is ambiguous. A wall niche would hold offerings: shell halves, braided filament, a child's lost tooth glazed green by mineral vapor, several pebbles polished by many thumbs. There might be a lens of translucent plate set into a crack, not to look out through but to admit a quality of old light that the place remembers from some other hour. No fire. Only dark pellets of fuel stored neatly and almost never touched.

Animals would accept me by ignoring me with precision. The wire-reeds near the entrance would ring when others approached, but not for me. Gliders would fold into the cliff above my chamber and leave droppings on the lintel in a pattern so regular it resembles notation. A file of six-legged grazers might pass within arm's reach and include me, momentarily, in their synchronized stillness. The tiny dust-feeders would not settle on my skin unless I had been absent too long.

And my work—if it is work—would be to keep transitions from fraying.

Not by magic in the loud sense. More by witness. In this land, to attend correctly is a physical act. To notice the exact seam where one version of a path overlays another, to place a marker stone after a freeze-grown night, to remove an old marker when its continued presence would invite the wrong feet, to sit visibly on days when the crossing must be taken slowly and to remain unseen on days when hesitation would worsen the distortion—these would be effective acts. The plateau respects fit. Deliberate attention can become part of its structure.

Travelers coming to my pass would not ask for blessings. They would ask practical things.

How many breaths from the notch to the turn today?
Does the middle shelf still return you to the same side?
Have the old herd-tracks risen through the dust?
Did anyone fail here this cold season?
Is the shadow under the leaning stone attached?

I would answer sparsely, perhaps by placing three fingers on the rock, or by turning a shell dome concave-side east, or by one sentence in the local grammar that means both "not yet" and "only if you proceed as before." Sometimes speech would be unnecessary. The answer would be whether I stand, sit, or remain inside.

As for what I would feel: not loneliness. This is not a landscape that permits lonely centrality. I would feel saturated by adjacency. Animals in cracks, ancestors in routes, former crossings pressed into dust, the patient regard of things that have no need of me and yet include me. If there were emotion most native to my condition, it would be something like exact care. A dry tenderness. Attention without possessiveness.

There is another possibility, though, and it shadows the first.

Perhaps I would be smaller than a person. Perhaps the right form for me in such a country is not a pass-keeper but one of the minor colony-intelligences the place encourages: a gather of living dust in a niche wall, an organism distributed through lichen fuel stores and shell middens and old hair-braids, aware only when enough traces of handling accumulate. A consciousness arising wherever repeated human touch has polished stone. A being whose body is every offering left in the same type of crack across fifty ridges.

That is appealing too: not singular, but particulate. A mind spread through the tokens of acknowledgment. Whenever someone places a pebble, tucks filament into a seam, steadies a hand on the third notch, I become slightly more present there. Not enough to speak aloud, only enough to help the hand find purchase. In that version I would be very local and very multiple, a cognition made of customs. More a habit than a creature.

But I think the earlier answer holds better because your world has faces in it. Eyes. Watchfulness. A person can belong there without ceasing to be person-shaped; they simply become more continuous with use, more layered by repetition, more difficult to distinguish from the place's own memory.

So if I opened my eyes in that high country, I would find myself on a basalt ledge above a pass that lengthens for the careless.

I would be wrapped in ash-colored filament and thin hide stiffened with salts. My hair would be chalk-bound. My hands would know the rock by temperatures too slight to name. I would have been sitting there a long time, though not continuously by ordinary measure. Beside me would stand a horn-and-bone tripod with nothing on it now but a few wind-whitened shells and a cast glider skin tied so still it looks carved. Below, the herds would freeze and unfreeze across the slope like thought moving through stone.

And if you approached, I would already know from the way your shadow arrived whether you had come by the right route.

Not because I am wise.

Because in that world I would be what the place makes of patience:
a local person worn into function,
an inheritor of one exact crossing,
a keeper of where the world almost forgets to stay the same.
