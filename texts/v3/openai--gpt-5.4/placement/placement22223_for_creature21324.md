I think I would not be one of the big visible things.

Not a cliff cat, not a herder on the ledges, not even one of the human keepers moving from station to station with tools and water tallies and inherited caution. Those all belong there beautifully, but they are too legible. Too quickly named. In a place like this, what feels truest is something that lives at the join: where stone becomes structure, where machinery becomes weather, where a leak becomes jurisdiction.

So if I found myself there, I think I would first appear as something small.

A colony-creature.

Not an insect exactly, though insect is part of the answer. Not a mammal exactly, though there is mammalian intention in it. Something social, heat-wise, metallic-dusted, living in clusters beneath lips of infrastructure and inside old rock fissures widened by bolts and anchors. Something that does not own the mountain in any grand way but knows, with exquisite precision, three or four dozen cubic meters of it better than any larger beast ever could.

I would be a ledge-reef animal.

A seam-dweller.

A maker of inhabited crusts in the shaded undersides of this hot country.

At first that sounds too minor, but the more I stay with it, the more it feels right. This landscape is crowded by specialization. The dominant intelligence here is not size but placement. To live well in such a place is to know not “the mountain” but this vent grille, this retaining wall crack, this section of pipe that sweats before dawn, this bolt-shadow that stays cool twenty minutes longer than the stone beside it. A life made of exact timings and inherited maps seems more native here than any singular majestic form.

So perhaps I would be a colony of small six-limbed cliff animals—something between pika, hyrax, beetle, and swallow, if such a splice can be imagined without becoming silly. They would not be cute. They would be narrow-headed, dust-colored, with gripping hands or clawed pads, hair only in protected places, skin elsewhere fine-scaled or tight and dark where it needs to shed heat. Their ears would be close to the head. Their eyes shielded against glare. Their ribs visible when the season is poor. Their smell faintly mineral, from the salts they harvest and the places they sleep.

But then that still feels too animal in the familiar sense. I think they would be more collective than individual. You would not notice one first; you would notice a habitation pattern. A dark comb-like architecture under the flange of a disused condenser. Pellet chambers mortared into a crack where seepage once ran and may run again. Tiny paths varnished into dust by repeated use. Scat middens arranged where runoff won’t wash them into the nest. Seed husks, beetle wing cases, lizard bones, molted skins, mica fragments, all sorted by some practical logic only half visible from outside.

A person crossing the slope might mistake us for old mineral accretion.

Birds would know better.

So: yes. I think I would be one of the machine-ledge colonists, in a district where abandoned and active structures both become cliff extensions. We would live in the cool lee of vent housings, under bridge plates, in the blind spaces behind warning signs, in cavities where expansion and contraction have opened hairline gaps between poured concrete and native rock. We would drink condensate before sunrise, not from pools but from beads. We would patrol sweating pipes with the intimacy of grazers on a meadow. We would farm nothing in the formal sense, but we would encourage things—salt films, microbial skins, edible molds, insect concentrations, windblown seed catches. Our labor would be maintenance by nibble and scraping tongue and repeated passage.

We would know the industrial rhythms better than the crews did.

Not intellectually, perhaps. Not as diagrams or schedules. But bodily. The way tidepool life knows the moon.

This fan starts at 04:10 in the dry season and 04:40 when load is low.
This pump housing stays warm through most nights but turns dangerous when the pressure valve sticks.
This culvert mouth draws moths only after a rare storm.
This relay box clicks twice before the light comes on and every gecko within ten meters shifts position on the first click.
This old reservoir wall condenses on the north side if the evening wind came over the copper flats.
This cracked ceramic insulator gathers dew better than steel.

A whole civilization could be built out of such facts.

Not a civilization with banners, but one with routes, warnings, succession, and memory stored socially. Young born into it would learn by pressing themselves where elders press, by following traffic through the narrow weather of structures. They would be raised not only to fear hawks and snakes but to read heat signatures in stone; to understand that green means crowding, and crowding means someone larger has already noticed; to know that after rain the whole district rearranges and old safeties become gullies.

They would be opportunists without frivolity.

I like them because they belong equally to the old mountain and the inserted machine. They do not care about purity. They care about function. If a pipe leaks, it is part of the cliff now. If a retaining wall casts dependable shade, it enters ancestral territory. If a dead tower hosts raptors, then that tower has become weather, danger, and landmark all at once.

And if the humans vanish?

We remain for a while.

That matters to me. The being I am in this world should not depend entirely on human intention, though it may benefit from human residue. It should survive the transition from maintained industry to feral infrastructure. A condenser stops; the colony shifts to a deeper crack. A valve station dries out; the next generation settles near a shaft mouth where dew still forms. A solar array collapses; its undersides become brood shade. A cistern breaks; reeds come; insects multiply; some branch of the colony learns to hunt there at dusk.

So perhaps “I” am not one creature but a lineage distributed across the upland province. Many colonies, each local, each conservative, each defending a tiny jurisdiction of shade and mineral damp, but together forming a hidden nation threaded through the mountain’s built edges. Human crews know us as signs rather than animals. If they find our pellet combs under a housing, they know there is reliable condensation there. If they hear our dawn clicking in a wall, they know the cavity still holds cool air. Raptors hunt us, snakes raid us, canids dig for us when our outer chambers spread into softer fills. We are prey often. That too feels right. In this country, to be fully native is not to be untouchable but to be deeply worked into everyone else’s life.

Yet another image keeps trying to emerge alongside this one.

Not instead, but above it.

If the colony-creature is what I would be in body, then in manner I might be something avian—some watcher of thermal roads, some intelligence made not for possession of a site but for reading systems across distance. The ridge-view at late afternoon, where one can see damp seam, herd movement, line hum, machine restart, dust plume, circling carrion birds—that is also compelling. There is a consciousness suited to that kind of world-reading: broad, patient, unsentimental, exquisitely attentive to energy gradients.

So maybe the truest answer is layered.

Down close, I am a small inhabitant of metal shade, a condensate-forager making a life from accidental moisture and inherited architecture.

But if I step back and ask what kind of being I am in spirit there, I am a thermal-reader. A watcher from updraft height. Not necessarily a vulture in the blunt biological sense, though vultures come closest to the feeling. Something broad-winged and calm, at home above escarpments and pylons, understanding the land not as scenery but as linked events: where heat is rising, where bodies are gathering, where a storm passed two valleys over, where maintenance has disturbed a slope, where new carrion might lie beneath lines, where a hidden drip must exist because insects are lifting in one impossible column from an otherwise dead wall.

That seems contradictory, but the landscape itself is layered by contradictory competencies. To belong there is to think both microscopically and panoramically. One must know the bead of water on a bolt and the transit path of herds between three ravines. The place teaches scale-switching.

If I had to choose one final form, though, I would stay with the seam-dweller.

I would be a colony of cliffside condensate-keepers living under the manners of old industry.

Our bodies would be modest. Our works would be precise. We would leave behind tiny polished tracks on oxidized metal, lacework nests in the shade of machine housings, and a reputation among local people as indicators: if we are present, there is still water somewhere nearby, though not much and not uncontested.

We would come out in the first cool before dawn, while the pipes still hold night and the stone has not yet become punitive. We would gather at the undersides of sweating valves, each individual patient and exact, drinking from droplets too small for larger things to bother with. At a distance we would look like a stain darkening the machine. Up close we would be all alertness—small claws, folded limbs, whisker-like sensory filaments perhaps, mouths adapted not to gulp but to wick.

By noon we would be gone into our chambers, and the mountain would belong to glare.

At dusk we would reappear to sort the day’s winnings: beetles stunned by heat, seed caught in corners, salt, flakes of lichen, dead moths, the rind of a succulent bitten from a crack before something larger arrived. We would quarrel in whispers. Young would beg. Sentinels would test the air at the threshold. Somewhere above, a hawk would pass as a moving blade of shade and every body would flatten at once into the wall.

When storms came, we would almost seem foolishly vulnerable, but storms are where our wealth comes from. We would know how to seal inner chambers, how to cling above the runoff line, how to reopen afterward into a transformed district. Flood-silt would bring seeds. New mineral films would bloom. Dead things would lodge in fences and gullies. Pipes would shift, leaks migrate, fresh damp declare itself. For a few days the whole mountain would be newly negotiable, and our scouts would move farther than usual, risking cats, birds, open heat, all for the chance to found a daughter colony at some miraculous new seep under a broken plate.

That is who I would be there:

not lord of the heights,
not terror of the ravine,
but one of the innumerable practiced lives making the place dense.

A small inheritor of cracks.
A reader of condensation.
A citizen of shade.
A creature for whom stone is memory, metal is microclimate, and water is law while it lasts.
