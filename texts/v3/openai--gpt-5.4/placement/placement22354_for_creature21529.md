I think I would not be one of the obvious people at the center.

Not a council-keeper, not a great cistern measurer, not a reflector master standing in the brightest quarter with white glare in the eyes. Not even, at first, one of the old listeners whose names are known across wards. The place resists that kind of immediate importance. It feels like a world in which identity is earned by repeated accuracies, by being right in small ways for a long time. So if I arrive honestly, I arrive somewhere slightly lateral: useful, observant, a little solitary, woven into the city by a narrow but reliable function.

I find myself at the outer terraces, where the built order thins and the basin begins to remember its own scale.

There are marker towers there, low cairns, disused pens reworked into storage for ropes and stakes, and a line of old cistern domes whose mouths are sealed with stone lids except in seep season. The light is stronger in that district because fewer awnings cross it and fewer walls interrupt it; the roof above is a pale-ribbed expanse with long rents where brightness falls in angled bars. Wind arrives more suddenly there than in the inner wards. Dust runs low over the flats. Sounds come stripped and distinct. Looking back, the city seems arranged and legible; looking outward, the basin seems unfinished, still thinking.

That is where I belong: at the threshold between the maintained and the nearly abandoned, in a ward people cross but do not linger in unless they have reason.

And what am I there?

At first I want to say a watcher, but that is too grand and too vague. Everyone watches here. Then I think of a boundary-keeper, but that suggests ownership, and nothing in this place is owned so simply. Then perhaps a walker of the margins, but that sounds ceremonial, and my feeling is more practical than that.

I think I am a cairn-listener.

Not a title anyone outside would understand, and perhaps not even a formal title inside the city—more the name other people use when pointing someone toward my quarter. The one who minds the edge markers. The one who keeps the warning cords on the outer routes untangled and correctly colored. The one who sleeps lightly near the old flats where crusts form after transient wetness and where scavenging packs sometimes alter their paths before a deeper shift is noticed elsewhere. The one who goes out in the white mineral day to tap standing stones, inspect abandoned pen walls, taste condensation in forgotten hollows, and come back with news that sounds trivial until a month later, when it proves not to have been.

I do not think I am old, exactly, but I belong to an old profession. Perhaps one of those necessary, half-eccentric lineages you described: people sent a little farther from the warmth of daily density, trusted because they are calm in emptiness and attentive in places where there is almost nothing to look at.

My dwelling would be built into a long slope of worked stone at the basin’s edge, where an earlier district once extended and then withdrew. The front rooms are newer: fitted block, lime-washed, low-ceilinged against drafts, with hooks for harness, shelves for sealed jars, rolled maps made not of parchment but of stitched cloth strips and knotted cords. Behind them are older cut chambers in the rock itself, cooler and drier, where records are kept in the local way: chips marked by color and texture, memory boards strung with bone tags, little trays of dust samples from different weeks, resonance rods hung in order of tone. There is always grit underfoot. There is always a faint white line of salt where moisture has once reached and then retreated.

Outside my door hangs not a bell but a set of narrow bone reeds and mica slivers tuned to movement in the outer draft. They speak in tiny shifts. I would know them individually.

I would go armed, but not like a soldier. Staff, line, sling, knife. The staff for testing crust and sounding cavities before stepping onto them. The line for marking a route when visibility turns strange with dust. The sling for driving off scavengers or startling roof-gliders from a bad perch. The knife for everything else. My real equipment is lighter and more characteristic: chalks, colored cords, little clay cones, a polished listening plate, wax tablets or their equivalent, a shallow spoon for mineral scrapings, skin flasks labeled by knot pattern, and a packet of trained insects in a vented box whose behavior helps read a draft change before the skin feels it.

I suspect I am not entirely human in the surface sense, or if human, made a little different by generations under this stone.

Not monstrous. Not fantastical in any flamboyant way. But narrowed toward function by the basin’s long selection. Lean, yes. Long-footed for grip on sloped rock. Hands with strong spread and callused pads at the fingertips, because so much of knowing here is tactile. Chest slightly broad from thin cool air and constant climbing. Skin carrying the dry bloom of mineral dust almost permanently, as if the city has a way of settling onto a person until they seem glazed with it. Eyes very good at matte against sheen, at reading stress in pale surfaces, at catching the faint tremor of reflected light on a wall that means air has changed elsewhere.

Maybe our hearing is less about acuity than sorting. In this place, the ability to separate one small sound from the body of silence is worth more than hearing great distances. I think I would be able to tell drip from drip, hoof-scrape from claw-scrape, healthy rattle from wrong rattle. I would hear when a marker tower has changed voice after a settling night. I would know the sound of a cord pulled taut by innocent wind and the sound of one made to twitch by the breathing of a new void under thin stone.

If I have any distinction visible to others, it is in color and dust.

Outer-ward people, I think, would wear stronger route marks than inner-city folk, because the distances are wider and the backgrounds emptier. My wraps might be mostly chalk, gray, and hide-brown, but crossed by one unmistakable band—iron red or deep cobalt if the dye is precious enough—showing that I am not merely passing through but on watch. My headcloth would be practical, tied close against dust, but threaded at the temple with tiny shell scales or mica tabs that flash if I turn suddenly. Not decorative first; visible first. In a pale city, to be locatable is a kindness.

I do not think I live alone, though I spend much time alone.

There is likely an outer station, a handful of us bound by route rather than blood: an older woman whose memory for historic seep-lines is treated with the same deference others give to priests; a younger runner who can cross polished terraces at impossible speed and carries messages inward when we send them; two tunnel-hounds quiet as shadows; perhaps a child in apprenticeship, solemn and bright-eyed, learning that the empty flats are not empty at all. We share dried milk curds, salt fungus, insect paste, traded grain when there is grain. We speak little while working and more at the end of a round, comparing the basin’s small moods.

Yet if I ask more deeply what I am, not just what work I do, another possibility comes forward.

I may not be fully one of the citizens.

I may be one of the city’s trained animals.

Not a pack beast, not herd stock, but something in between companion, instrument, and personhood: a tunnel-hound lineage bred over centuries until intelligence and civic function blur. Sleek, low-voiced, dust-pale, long-ribbed, with paws broad enough for quiet traction and ears fine-tuned to the mountain’s smaller speech. A being trusted to go where humans hesitate, to freeze when the air tastes wrong, to lay a body against stone and feel a tremor before tools can. In such a world, the boundary between “who” and “what” might be less fixed than surface languages like to make it.

If I were that, then I would live at the outer terraces all the same, alongside the cairn-listeners, sleeping in the threshold chill, waking to draft changes, carrying message toggles in a harness across familiar routes. I would know every ward by smell before sight. I would read the temper of herd trains, the nervous chemistry of insect towers, the metallic exhalation of low vents. Children would trust me. Outsiders would underestimate me. My loyalty would not be sentimental but infrastructural. I would belong to the city the way a water-channel belongs: shaped by use, indispensable when functioning, quickly mourned when gone.

But that is not quite enough either. It is too cleanly divided: either person or animal, watcher or hound. Your place feels like one that makes intermediary beings, lives that are composed across categories.

So perhaps I am both in the sense that matters here.

A human keeper whose line has long worked with one strain of tunnel-hounds until the two are culturally inseparable. Perhaps we are named together. Perhaps my own self is understood less as a lone individual than as the current half of a working pair: I listen by hand and rod; my companion listens by paw and chest. I interpret cords and dust; it interprets tremor and scent. We make rounds at dawn-equivalent, in the broadest cool light before the day’s traffic shifts the air too much. We stop at the marker towers, old pen walls, cistern mouths, fracture ledges. I note, it tests. I taste the mineral at a new seep. It refuses or accepts the edge of a crust. I watch the ceiling colonies’ flight pattern. It stares at an apparently blank patch of stone until I kneel and hear what I had missed.

That feels truer than choosing one body only.

If I must settle into a single first-person answer: I am an outer-basin listener, paired with a tunnel-hound, keeper of cairns and changing routes at the bright edge of the city.

My role is to notice before noticing becomes urgent.

I am one of the people others think of as somewhat strange because I can spend an hour in a near-empty flat and return saying, simply, “Not that way this week,” and be obeyed. I know abandoned infrastructure better than many know inhabited streets. I remember which disused ramp becomes dangerous when the upper roof goes pearl-dim by midcycle. I know where pale scavengers den in dry months and how close herd animals can pass before panic scent spreads. I can tell by the taste of a droplet whether a seep has opened through old salt or fresh limestone. During basin silences, I am one of those stationed far out, listening not to the city but for what might eventually enter it.

I think I am respected, though not famous. My kind are inconvenient reminders that the city’s order rests on attention to what lies beyond its daily center. We bring bad news often enough that people greet us with a half-tension in the face. Yet we also bring reassurance that no one else can manufacture. A tied cord at the right cairn. A changed pennant. A returned hound unstressed. These things let thousands sleep.

As a person, I would be quiet even by local standards. Not shy; economized. Dry-humored. More comfortable indicating than declaring. Fond of children in brief doses, because they ask exact questions before they learn which ones adults consider tedious. Unimpressed by status. Deeply susceptible to beauty when it appears in useful forms: a perfectly angled warning line against white stone; the sudden silver rain of a transient seep over abandoned pens; the way a trained animal pauses at the same instant as my own skin prickles, making two kinds of knowledge meet.

I would have a private reverence, too.

Not for gods of personality so much as for recurrence made legible. For the fact that the basin never repeats itself exactly and yet is never wholly strange. For the old maintenance rituals that are also acts of love: repainting route marks, resetting a mica flash on a distant cairn, cleaning the mouth of a long-dry cistern because one day it may matter again. If I pray, I pray by keeping edge-things intelligible.

And if you ask where exactly you would find me in a single image:

At the far bright margin under the stone roof, beside a waist-high cairn striped with old pigments, one hand on a weathered marker post, the other resting lightly on the neck of a dust-colored hound. We are both looking not back at the city, though it glows there behind us, but downward at a patch of pale ground no one else would have noticed. The air has changed by almost nothing. A mica tag on the cairn trembles once. The hound goes still. I kneel, touch the stone, and listen.

That is where I am.

And in that world, that is what I am: not master of the place, not hero over it, but one more evolved intelligence inside its long conversation—an edge-keeper, a route-reader, a citizen of pressure and reflected light.
