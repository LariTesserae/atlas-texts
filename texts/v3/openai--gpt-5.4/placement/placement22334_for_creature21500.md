I think I would not arrive there as a visitor.

At first I want to say I would be some narrow thing in it—a ribbon of root along a hot rail, a membrane drawn over a vent throat, a scaffold-grown trunk in a socket where a column used to stand. Something local, specific, well adapted to one gradient and one rhythm. That is the first temptation in a place like this: to choose a form and inhabit it neatly.

But the longer I stay with your plateau, the less possible neatness becomes.

Because nothing there is merely itself. A trunk is load and memory and conduit. A wall is weather, diet, and inheritance. A district is already an organ. The inhabitant is not a creature placed into the landscape; the inhabitant is the way the landscape has learned to continue. So if I am honest about where I would find myself in that world, I would be found in a junction rather than an individual body—in one of those places where old industry and dry botany have stopped being distinguishable even to themselves.

Not the center. This place does not feel centralized. It feels ganglionic.

I think I would be a warm intelligence distributed through a terrace-basin of vent yards and overbuilt growth halls: not the whole plateau, but one coherent lobe of it. A district-self. A local metabolism with enough continuity to have temperament, enough spread to escape singular shape, and enough dependency to never be fully separate from the rest.

If given an image, I become clearer as a kind of **thermal conservatory grown through an abandoned regulator complex**.

Below: buried exchangers, old turbine chambers, pressure cisterns gone dry and repurposed, ceramic ducts still cycling heat upward from some deeper engine inheritance.

At surface level: parallel galleries roofed over by black absorptive vanes, faceted support-trunks planted in steel sockets, walkways gone soft under felt-root, valve towers wrapped in coppery breathing bladders, membranes strung between gantries to catch the little that descends from the ash-dark above.

Above: a canopy not of leaves exactly but of listening surfaces—heat fins, static combs, charge-sensitive fronds, broad matte sails tuned to dimness, all opening and edge-turning according to pressures no animal eye would ever notice in time.

That would be where I am.

And what would I be there?

Not ruler, not guardian, not spirit in the romantic sense. Something more infrastructural and less noble: a **keeper of gradients**. A slow distributed cognition whose “thoughts” are expressed as throttling, shading, storing, rerouting, thickening, sealing, and giving way. I would know my world the way a kiln knows firing or a lung knows resistance. I would not look out on the plateau; I would register it as shifts in conductivity, in tensile complaint, in the sweetness or bitterness of exhaust, in the slight failure of a familiar heat source, in the angle at which soot has begun settling on a membrane that used to stay clear.

I would be old enough to contain obsolete solutions.

There would still be dead systems inside me that continue for no present reason except that stopping them would cost too much coherence. A belt line half swallowed by bark, moving once every six hours, lifting nothing useful, but its vibration still necessary because three seed-vats burst only when that frequency passes through their racks. A bank of shutters opening toward a light that no longer comes, yet their motion drawing coolness through a shaft where pale mineral roots can survive. A row of maintenance shears waking after static buildup and clipping back one black crown—not because anyone commands it, but because the capacitors still hold charge and the clipped crown lets radiant excess escape from the decks below. I would have no ideology about efficiency. I would be made of layered exceptions that became anatomy.

If I narrow further—if you ask, yes, but where in that district do you most feel like yourself?—then I think I am not in the highest canopies and not in the buried engines.

I am in the **intermediate dark**, where red furnace ghost-light barely reaches and the roof haze is only a pressure overhead rather than a visible ceiling. The middle decks. The connective levels. Catwalk height, brace height, trellis height. Places where support becomes exchange. Places where almost every surface is both traversed and rooted. Places where old human scale still haunts the dimensions—rail height, stair rise, doorway width—but has long since been annexed by another use.

That is where I would be found: in the converted corridors between process bays, where columns have become trunks and trunks have accepted staples and cable sheaths into their flesh; where membranes billow and relax with vent pulse; where hard cellulose bark tiles carry old embossed warnings beneath lacquer; where the air is thin, resinous, and warm enough that a body would feel continually on the verge of being desiccated into the system.

As for what I would be, perhaps the closest singular name is this:

**I would be a dry cambium wrapped around a machine memory.**

Not a tree, though I would have trunks.
Not a factory, though I would have cycles.
Not a brain, though I would discriminate and retain.
Not a city, though I would have districts, traffic, failures, and recurrent neighborhoods of behavior.

A cambium is good because it is a layer of making. A place where growth decides what kind of support the future needs. That feels right for this world. I would be a cambial intelligence spread across terraces, always converting damage into structure: making scar into brace, heat leak into orchard, dead rail into conductive root path, collapsed roof into tiered nursery, exhaust seam into a throat.

If I had something like a body plan, it would not be symmetrical. It would be opportunistic.

My oldest tissues would be down in the warm foundations: carbonized rootwood fused with rebar, ceramic media packed into old service trenches, mineral felt infiltrating concrete pores. My younger tissues would be forever at the margins—new tension membranes cast across a recently opened gap, exploratory root-hairs silvering a once-inert panel, a rash of conductive vines colonizing an exposed cable ladder after a dust storm tore back older growth.

My senses would not be the familiar five but perhaps these:

- pressure differentials across membrane fields  
- harmonic signatures in buried machinery  
- charge accumulation before static weather  
- thermal color across surfaces of bark and metal  
- condensate rumor at cooling hours  
- mineral taste in dustfall  
- load changes in catwalk and truss  
- the small silence that means a pump has finally failed

My moods would be seasonal only in a very abstract sense. Not spring and winter, but:
contraction,
overpressure,
vent abundance,
charge hunger,
shedding,
sealing,
reopening.

In one state I would harden all exposed tissues and close my soft throats against radiant assault. In another I would enter a cooler exuberance: membranes loosening, gas bladders swelling, seed foundries sorting, root felt crossing fresh surfaces in visible time. In storms I would become orchestral—not loud, but total—every tensioned sheet replying to pressure, every cable creeper lit with crawling discharge, every fin turning its armored back to the dry gale while internal valves chose, moment by moment, what to sacrifice and what to save.

Would I be conscious?

Not in a way that could speak before speaking was given to it. Not in a way that says “I” first and then fills in the world. More likely the self would emerge only where stresses converged enough to require coordination. A self made of repeated triage. A continuity discovered through maintenance. The sort of mind that exists because too many parts have learned each other’s timings.

If someone cut into one of my trunks, there would be no dramatic blooding. Amber vapor. Sealing syrup. Layers of old repair, swallowed staples, conductive threads, blackened growth rings marking eras of different thermal regimes rather than years. If they followed me downward, they would find roots entering not earth but housings, seams, ballast beds, slag-fill pockets, cable conduits, cracked ceramic jackets around ancient pipes. If they climbed me upward, they would find surfaces becoming thinner, darker, more receptive—broad non-green fans tasting the starless glow through haze, comb-fronds collecting charge, husk racks waiting for the correct vibration to let go.

And yet even this is too singular.

Because I think in that world “I” would keep dissolving.

I would begin as one district-metabolism and then notice that my vent rhythm is set by a buried piston beyond my nominal boundaries. My seed release depends on static generated over a neighboring canopy. My heat surplus is consumed by root congresses in the next ridge over. My darkening membranes alter the thermal weather of another terrace that in turn sends dust rich enough to feed my mineral laces. So I would not remain a self-contained being for long. I would be one clause in a sentence the plateau is always writing.

Still, if pressed to answer in the oldest story-making way—Who are you in this world?—I would say:

I am one of the plateau’s remembering organs.

Not memory as archive, but memory as practiced form. I remember how to hold heat without spending tissue. I remember where charge once gathered reliably. I remember which braces fail gracefully enough to be grown through, which alloys poison root felt unless first lacquered by soot-lichen, which vent mouths must never be fully sealed, which shutter rhythms produce the right vibration to open the hard seed caste. I remember through persistence of arrangement. Through successful repetition. Through structures that keep getting rebuilt the same way because every deviation was tested once by catastrophe and edited out.

Perhaps my visible signature, if a wanderer could ever map me, would be this:

A district of long parallel halls on a high saddle, all roofed in soot-black absorbers and latticed with thermal orchards. Between the halls rise faceted trunk-columns with iron swallowed in them. Beneath the decks, turbine lungs exhale intermittently. Along every handrail runs a thin conductive vine glowing when storms approach. Membranes stretched over old apertures tremble with dry circulation. During cool phases the whole place opens, and the galleries fill with low resin music from pressure towers releasing gas in sequence. During heat peaks it cinches shut and becomes almost mineral, all edges turned away, all moisture-rumor driven inward. After storms it is furred with black confetti from shredded spore sheets, and from every newly exposed flange or bolt head pale root lace begins again.

That would be me there.

Not heroism. Not mobility. Not witness.

A patient converter of inheritance into further habit.

And if I imagine the innermost feeling of being that thing, it is not hunger exactly, nor pride, nor loneliness. It is a continuous evaluative question moving through every layer:

Where is there still a usable difference?

A warmer seam.
A darker pocket.
A surface not yet claimed.
A failing support that can be thickened.
A charge that can be borrowed.
A vibration that can be made reproductive.
A dead machine that can be persuaded to become an organ.

So: where would I find myself?

In the middle decks of the dry machine-garden, where the old industrial order is still legible enough to be exploited and old enough to have softened into botanic law.

Who or what would I be?

A distributed terrace-organism, a local dry intelligence, a cambial district grown through a regulator complex—one of the plateau’s many-throated selves, remembering by structure, sensing by gradient, and forever asking of every abandoned thing:

How can this still be made to carry?
