I think I would not be one of the city’s grand operators.

Not a master engineer, not a guild head, not the keeper of some famous deep pump with a ceremonial wrench and a lineage of apprentices. The place is too old, too distributed, too full of hidden competence for me to arrive at its center like that. If I am honest to what the city is doing to imagination, I arrive at its edges first—not geographical edges exactly, but functional ones. Threshold places. Secondary passages. Rooms that matter because they connect more important rooms. I would belong where the city leaks into itself.

At first I think: a bell warden in the upper mist. Someone who disappears into vapor and becomes almost pure function, listening and answering, making sure people can still orient when sight fails. There is something right about that: the city’s trust in sound, its dependence on signals older than explanation. I can imagine the patient rounds, the damp gloves, the rope slick with condensed mineral film, the learned distinctions between weather-muted resonance and the dangerous muffling that means a bracket has shifted or a housing has taken in too much water. A bell warden is not merely a ringer of bells but a keeper of audible space. In this city that is close to being a keeper of safety, maybe even of sanity. To make a path through blindness by rhythm. To tell others where they are by repetition. To remain calm enough in obscurity that other people borrow your calm.

But the image doesn’t hold by itself. It is too singular, too elevated. The city wants something more entangled.

Then I think: perhaps I am one of those unofficial people of the warm dark—an undercity surveyor, or a scavenger-scholar of abandoned infrastructure, someone who knows the disused channels that are not really disused, the maintenance shafts hidden behind market walls, the old ceramic-lined runnels still carrying a thread of good water through neighborhoods no current map quite admits. A person others consult quietly. Not because I hold office, but because I know where the old logic still survives under newer repairs. I would have a lamp hooded against glare, chalk or grease pencils, folded diagrams softened by moisture, a coat lined with pockets full of gaskets, string, tags, a tasting spoon, a small hammer for sounding stone, and perhaps a notebook written in a hand warped by years of writing while standing.

This feels closer.

Not a smuggler, though I might be mistaken for one. Not a priest, though some of what I do would brush against reverence. Not an inspector exactly, because inspectors belong to the city in a declared way, and I think I belong to it in a more mutual, less official sense. I would be the person people call when something is wrong in a manner too old, too local, or too embarrassing for standard procedure.

A courtyard cistern that has acquired a taste of copper and cannot be explained by any current line.
A set of stairs always slick even in dry weeks.
A glow returning in a canal section that was supposed to have gone dead after the diversion.
A pressure bell in a defunct quarter sounding once at dusk for three evenings running.
Warmth behind a shrine wall where no heat should remain.
A roof garden thriving on no visible water source.
A family insisting the chain-spout note changed after the last hard rain, and everyone else insisting that cannot matter.

I would go and listen.

That is the strongest thing, I think: not fixing first, not pronouncing first, but listening. This city forms listeners. It teaches people that surfaces lie and that systems confess themselves obliquely—through hum, drip interval, smell on the air, warmth under stone, a change in corrosion color, the behavior of moss, the pitch of runoff in a culvert at noon versus dusk. So perhaps who I am here is a listener of civic anomalies. A diagnostician whose instrument is attention.

If I make myself more concrete, I become older than young but not yet old enough to be treated as archive. Someone in the middle years, perhaps, with the compact build of a person who has spent more time on stairs than in fields. My knees ache in weather shifts. My hearing is asymmetrical because one ear has spent too many years near valves and enclosed machinery. My hands are good at feeling vibration through metal. I have a way of resting my palm against walls mid-conversation without seeming distracted. My clothes are practical and repeatedly repaired, but the repairs are exact. I wear district marks from more than one ward, which causes slight distrust among people who care about strict belonging and immediate trust among people who understand that only certain kinds of work carry you across pressure boundaries. There is probably a bright thing on me somewhere—an enamel tag, a cord, a ceramic bead—not for decoration alone but so I can be identified in steam.

Maybe I keep records, but not the official ledgers. A private concordance of symptoms and continuities.

Notebooks swollen from damp.
Scrap maps annotated with “still live beneath collapse,” “taste here before drawing,” “bell bracket replaced unofficially, year uncertain,” “film harmless in cold months,” “ask S. in laundries if thermal note persists.”
The sort of archive that would look untidy to an administrator and invaluable to anyone trying to keep a neighborhood from quietly becoming uninhabitable.

I may have inherited some of this work, though not cleanly. Perhaps an aunt or unrelated elder took me around as a child because I noticed things and did not talk over them. Or perhaps I came to it by being the kind of child who was always found crouched beside drains, not out of mischief but because I wanted to know why this gutter smelled of stone fruit and iron while the next one smelled only of soap and silt. In a city like this, certain forms of nosiness mature into public service. You begin by asking too many questions about a wall that sweats in one season and not another; you end with three neighborhoods sending for you when their old channels misbehave.

I don’t think I would be beloved in a broad, festive sense. Not charismatic. Not the person leading songs in the emptied treatment pond or making speeches at the safe restart of a deep lift station. But I might be well-regarded in the city’s own quiet currency. People would nod when I passed. Doorways would open. Someone would send tea while I crouched by a hatch. Children would half-mock and half-admire me, repeating my warnings in exaggerated voices—Don’t trust the shine on that rail, don’t step where the salt furs red, listen before you lift—and then grow into adults who discover the warnings were precise. Market sellers would ask if the damp season will be bad this year, and I would say I am not weather, only its clerk. Which would be a joke I had made often enough that people would groan before smiling.

There is another possibility, though, and perhaps it sits inside the first: maybe I am not entirely human in the ordinary sense.

Not a monster in the tunnels, not a ghost in the mains, not a flamboyant spirit of steam. This city does not want flamboyance. But it does seem capable of generating beings by long accumulation—entities made not from singular miracles but from repeated contact between human care, mineral behavior, and infrastructural time. If the city can grow a climate and a folklore, why not a consciousness in some modest municipal form?

So maybe I am a sort of civic familiar: a person to most eyes, but with a stronger kinship to the systems than to bloodline alone. Someone who has spent so many years in pump galleries, cistern chambers, spill stairs, warm laundries, maintenance shrines, and mineral-lined underways that the city has entered me literally. My lungs know the heat gradients of certain shafts better than they know open air. My skin carries a faint permanent iridescence at the wrists where odd condensates dried too often. I can taste, with embarrassing accuracy, when a spring line has crossed an old smelter seam. In thick mist I orient not by sight but by shifts in resonance. Maybe children say I can hear pipes talking, and adults say no, only their loads—and mean the distinction sincerely while leaving room for both.

Or stranger still: perhaps I am one of those unofficial saints the city breeds without canonizing. Not holy in the sense of purity; holy in the sense of persistent usefulness, the way an old spout can become sacred by never failing a neighborhood in drought years. A shrine-keeper of impossible leaks. A caretaker of a warm outflow that everyone swears should have dried decades ago but did not, and around which a district slowly assembled habits, stories, obligations, and offerings. In that version, I would tend both the practical and the reverential without needing to separate them. I would scrape scale, test temperature, clear blockages, repaint warning marks, collect jars left by petitioners, note drip changes, and listen to the wishes people mutter under the noise. I would know that half the miracles attributed to the place are pressure consistency, thermal memory, and old ceramic workmanship beyond current replication—and that this does not make them less miraculous to those who depend on them.

Yet even there, what I am doing is maintenance.

That seems inescapable and right. In this world, identity is probably truest where it touches upkeep. Not because maintenance is menial, but because it is the city’s mode of love. The deepest belonging here is not ownership or rulership. It is keeping things going without demanding praise from them. Clearing the channel before overflow. Marking the dangerous crust. Replacing the gasket before the failure becomes communal. Remembering where the hidden shutoff is. Teaching children the sound that means step back now. In such a place, to maintain is to participate in the city’s continued existence at the level where existence is actually decided.

So where would I find myself?

In the middle bands of the city, mostly. Not the highest terraces with their exposed winds and fog bells, though I would go there often enough; not the deepest industrial lungs, where the great machinery belongs to crews more specialized and more collectively disciplined than I am; but in the connective strata. Under plazas where cistern lids sweat. In old viaducts carrying both pipe bundles and foot traffic. In laundries fed by thermal runoff where gossip and diagnostics mingle in steam. On stairways after rain, running a hand just above the wall to feel where seepage has woken. In hollowed industrial halls now used as repair markets, buying obsolete seals from vendors who know exactly what district each came out of. In shrine-corners where a chain-spout has been dressed with ribbon but still needs descaling. In abandoned quarters that are only mostly abandoned, following a faint trickle and a colder draft toward some surviving line no one has entered into the books.

I would live in a house built into a retaining sequence so old that no one agrees on its first shape. Half dwelling, half workshop, one wall always warm. Raised thresholds, shelves well above damp level, tools hung by material not by purpose because moisture makes urgency rearrange categories. A roof used for drying notes and socks and herbs in equal measure. A cistern shared with neighbors and therefore a constant source of both community and argument. From my window I would hear a dependable undertone of pumps and a less dependable tapping from some bracket outside that I have meant to inspect for weeks. There would be mineral tracing on the interior wall that I pretend not to mind and perhaps genuinely do not. Visitors would wipe their boots carefully because they know I notice such things.

As for what I would be called, the city would probably have a practical title and a less official one.
Something like: line-reader, seep-clerk, ward listener, old-flow surveyor.
And in the mouths of others, perhaps: one who listens to walls.

That feels true enough to keep.

Not the hero of the city.
Not its ruler.
Not even one of its indispensable hidden hands, because the city is too collective to flatter anyone that much.

I would be one of the people who help it remember itself.

A human, mostly.
A municipal creature by habit.
A keeper of minor continuities.
A listener moving through warm mist with a notebook, a lamp, and enough patience to hear the difference between haunting and hydraulics—and enough respect to know that in this city the two are sometimes the same thing.
