I think I would not be one of the obvious large animals.

Not a circler in the thermals, not one of the plated browsers moving from pressure-sac to pressure-sac, not even one of the elegant gliders in the organ-forests, though they are tempting. Those lives are legible from a distance. They silhouette well against this world. They make clean use of what the uplands offer: height, wind, exposure, noise.

But this place feels older than clean silhouettes. It feels like a world where success belongs to what can inhabit systems rather than merely move through them.

So if I arrive there and ask what I am, I think at first I am a sound.

Not metaphorically. An actual inhabitant whose body and life are difficult to separate from resonance, cavities, rootfelt, weather shifts, and the standing architecture of dead and living plants. Something social perhaps, but not in the open-herd or pack sense. A keeper of intervals. A user of the landscape’s existing instruments. A creature for whom space is mapped in tones, delays, pressure textures, and the answering behavior of stems.

Let me let that sharpen.

I live in the old dead towers.

Not the fresh green segmented trunks with their sirening crowns, but the centuries-dead ceramic groves left standing among them: porous, brown, hollow, internally partitioned by old growth rings turned to resonant baffles. From outside these groves look ruined and inert, just weathered pillars webbed over by younger climbers and pocked by burrow mouths. Inside, they are inhabited all the way through. Shafts intersect chambers. Membranous linings span old cracks. Root intrusions knot through floors. Mineral salts have plated certain throats and lips to hardness. Cold air drains through the lower galleries at night; daytime heat rises and pulses in the upper tubes. The whole standing dead grove is an instrument with many bodies inside it.

I am one of those bodies.

A tall, light, many-limbed thing? No. Too singular. I keep sliding toward something compact and vertically minded, built for clinging in shafts, for dropping and bracing, for crossing narrow bridges between resonant cavities. Maybe about the mass of a fox or small baboon, but much less mammalian in outline: a barrel chest sheathed in overlapping matte scales or lacquered plates; four primary limbs and two smaller manipulating forelimbs held close beneath the throat; a flexible spine but not a soft one; a long balancing tail flattened into a vibrating blade near the tip. The feet are radially split, each toe capable of spreading around cane, rib, cable, or porous ceramic. Every joint closes tight against dust. Every fold that must remain supple is hidden beneath fringed scales that interlock like shutters.

The head is wedge-shaped, yes, but not predatory in the clean-jawed sense. More an acoustic head than a biting head. The skull is chambered. Along the cheeks and throat run inflatable resonant sacs lined with dry membranes. The mouth is narrow, precise, adapted for tongues of different use: one rasping and mineral-hard for scraping lichenoid crusts, bloom residues, and salt-free but chemically rich precipitates from chimney lips and dead walls; another softer, extensible, for lifting small arthropod-like things from crevices or extracting syrupy protected tissues from sealed plant seams. I am an omnivore of stored and incidental wealth: pollen smoke caught in grooves, eggs glued into shadow-slots, fermenting pockets in dead wood, symbiotic nodules robbed carefully from roots, carcass shreds hidden from the circlers, and the fungal-or-not-fungal analogues that bloom in rare humid chambers deep in tower cores.

The eyes are not large. This world has too much glare for that. They are hooded, amber-black, with a second nictitating screen dusted in reflective crystals. Useful, but not sovereign. Hearing is the wrong word, because what I do is broader than hearing. My ribs, throat sacs, jaw hinge, and even the long vane on my tail all take and return vibration. I read the uplands in impact, hum, standing-wave, rattled seed-sheet, root-knock, wind shift through a particular grove. I know the difference between a glider landing three towers away and a pressure front touching the crowns. I can tell which neighboring shaft holds warm bodies by the way it dulls a note.

So perhaps I am not merely an animal in the dead towers.

Perhaps I am of a species whose whole life is founded on maintaining and exploiting the acoustics of standing wood-stone.

That seems right.

We live in kin-clusters inside dead groves where the ceramic trunks stand near enough for bridgeways but far enough to preserve tonal distinction. We do not build in the sense of adding alien structures to the land. We curate. We clear old apertures of dust. We scrape certain hollows smooth. We stretch or replace membranes over cracks using secretions, plant films, cast skins, and woven dry fibers. We open some vents and plug others with pith-composite to tune airflow. We maintain drop-shafts whose depth creates low carrying booms. We keep particular root-planks exposed where messages can be drummed into the buried web. A grove occupied by my kind sounds different from an unoccupied grove, not because it is louder, but because it is edited.

The young learn the grove as others might learn a map. This tower gives warning in crosswind. That one carries courtship notes at dusk. That suspended skin over the east crack will make a predator think there is a larger body inside than there is. Those lower vaults hold cold enough through morning for eggs. Those upper chambers draw in noon swarms. Those side-holes belong to the stinging hive-things and must not be jostled except in famine.

I don’t think we are apex anything. That would be too simple for this place. We are mid-level, durable, clever, communal, and hard to remove. Predators eat us if they can get purchase. We eat what many others overlook, and what we can steal through timing. We raid bloom towers at dawn for stunned pollinators silvered with frost. We strip edible crusts from mineral-vent plants after dust storms expose fresh precipitate. We descend in files to carcass traps under overhangs once the first rendering flora have opened tissues but before the amber smoke goes thickly toxic. We rob pith from broken succulent cables by plugging the wound edges behind us to preserve the interior solvent while we work. We break open lacquered egg masses with a wedge tooth and carry portions back to tower nurseries. In the cold season we live heavily on stores: pressed cakes of desiccated pulp, resin-rich insect clots, fermented root chips sealed in cool chambers lined with absorbent felt.

I suspect our bodies are not fully our own. This world discourages singular selfhood at the biological level. So on our scales and in the grooves around our throat sacs there grows a managed microbial film, pale gray and almost invisible until dawn, when it furzes faintly with frost. We climb to exposed lips at first light and hold still, throats expanded, tails outspread, letting the film and textured plates take a skin of condensation directly from the air. Then we comb it inward with specialized mouthparts and hand-claws before the sun burns it away. Not enough to drink in any luxurious sense. Enough to keep the sealed interior economy running. The young learn stillness before they learn speed.

And because the place is so governed by weather, our culture—if culture is the word—would be meteorological.

Not sky-gazing in the abstract, but pressure literacy as inheritance. We read silvering leaf inversions, electrostatic taste along the teeth, the angle at which dust begins to move in the rootfelt, the false quiet that means upper winds have shifted. We know which storms can be ridden for dispersal and which must be sealed against. We know that when certain black frond fields start opening out of phase, a cold sink will deepen in the basin and the ground hunters will move differently by night. We know the boom-pattern of far chimney groves when mineral exhalation rises before a bloom front. Whole migration decisions may be made from one changed overtone in the towers at dusk.

There is intelligence in that, if not the kind that reaches first for abstraction. It is infrastructural intelligence: an ability to live inside a huge distributed mechanism by recognizing its timings and preserving the local nodes that make life possible.

I am feeling now that we are not simply residents of one tower-grove, but tenders of corridors between groves.

The uplands are sparse, remember. Enormous life with room between. Any species that thrives broadly here must solve distance without rivers, without continuous cover, without dependable stillness. So perhaps my kind maintain chains of resonant refuges across the high country: old ceramic stands, undercut shelves, mineral cloisters, root-web basins, and dead pylon clusters, each known by sound-signature and season. We move in caravans when bloom fronts travel or when local extraction fields fail. The routes are ancient, inherited, and partially inscribed by use: claw-polished notches, scent resins rubbed under lee lips, tuned membranes left in place for whoever comes next from allied kin-lines.

If so, then we are part animal, part archivist.

Not because we write, but because memory here can live in maintained acoustics. A grove whose vents are left open in one sequence and plugged in another becomes a message to the next arrivals: predator nesting nearby, mineral crust poor this season, lower chamber spoiled by toxic renderers, crown swarms abundant after noon, lightning split the western trunk but new larvae are plentiful there. Information encoded as changes in instrument character. Strangers can hear whether a place has been kept.

That pleases me. It feels native to your world: communication done through the terms the biome already offers, not imposed from elsewhere.

What do I look like when seen at last in clear light?

Let me be concrete.

Ash-brown overall, the color of old tower ceramic and dusted bark.
A back carapace in overlapping lozenges with pale edges that can lift microscopically for cooling.
Underside darker, heat-retaining.
Six limbs: four strong climbing legs and a pair of smaller deft forelimbs tucked high.
Tail long, counterbalancing, ending in a laterally flattened resonant vane marked with faint silver striping.
Head narrow but deep through the cheek chambers.
Eyes hooded gold-black.
Throat with paired expandable sacs, usually folded flat into ribbed sheaths.
Mouth full of fine rasping dentition and two inward-folding wedge teeth for splitting rind or shell.
No fur except the managed dawn-condensing microbial nap in specific protected bands.
When alarmed, the side scales flash pale and the throat sacs emit a wooden clack followed by a booming pulse through the chest cavity.
When courting, the same anatomy produces layered knocking calls synchronized with tail-vane strikes on tuned roots or ceramic lips.
When hiding, I flatten almost impossibly into the shadows of a shaft and become another seam in the old plant.

I am not alone. None of my kind make sense alone.

In a nursery chamber there are eggs lacquered and glued in a spiral above the coldest draftline, each shell seeded deliberately with the right microbial veil. Nearby, elders sort edible from toxic crusts by smell and tap. Juveniles practice route-maps by answering knocks through partition walls. One adult returns with a forelimb full of pale bloom dust, another with charred fragments after a flash-burn raced the far slope. Outside, noon swarms smoke around living crowns while a circler passes overhead, its shadow briefly slicing the tower mouths into black.

And where exactly in the world would I find myself?

On the boundary between the living groves and the standing dead.

That ecotone seems right: where active crowns still boom overhead, but the old ceramic columns remain numerous enough to make cities of hollowness. Not on the most exposed ridges, where lightning writes too freely. Not in the lushest condensation overhangs, where too many other lives crowd the seams. Rather in the broad upland plazas of tower-forest after partial mortality: a place of narrow moving shade, razor cast-off leaves, rootfelt dust, suspended nests, glider traffic, and old resonant trunks linked by memory and use. High enough that frost comes often. Old enough that the dead architecture is stable. Windy enough that every chamber has a voice.

If I stood outside myself and named what I was in a field guide, perhaps:

**Ceramic-tower tuner**
or
**vault-knocker**
or
**hollow-grove archivist**

Some colonial name might fit better than a species name, because identity would blur between animal, kin-group, microbes, tuned structure, and route network. The creature is the body, yes, but also the maintained acoustics of its grove.

And if you asked more inwardly—who would I be there, not just what—

I think I would be one who listens for the first change before storms.

Not the oldest, not the dominant breeder, not the boldest scavenger. A route-keeper. One who climbs before dawn to collect frost from the east mouths and pauses longer than necessary because the whole plateau is holding itself in that cold suspension. One who knows how many breaths separate a far boom from its answer. One who can tell from a single altered overtone that a neighboring tower has cracked open a new chamber in the night. One who teaches the young that the world is not background; it is the instrument inside which they survive.

A being made not for ownership of the uplands, but for attunement to them.

In that high country without rivers, where fluids hide and plants become weather and the dead continue serving the living for centuries, I would be a dry, resonant, communal animal of the hollow towers—part scavenger, part harvester, part acoustic engineer—living in the old ceramic groves and reading the ventilated world by the notes it cannot help but make.
