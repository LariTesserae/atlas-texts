I think I would not be the hunter, nor the old knower, nor the great predator whose absence shapes the paths below. Those belong too cleanly to the basin’s visible story. If I arrived there as myself, I would first be tempted by the obvious dignities: the hawk riding the bowl of air, the weathered woman at the hut door, the fox threading blue evening. But the place keeps undoing grand choices. It favors what is exact, recurrent, close to the ground. Its life is made less of emblems than of continuities.

So let me stay in it a while before deciding.

At first I feel myself standing near one of the maintained shelters in the hour after frost has started to loosen: the roof still ticking with melt, the doorway black, a thread of smoke trying to rise straight and then fraying in the wind. From there the basin seems readable: the sedge seam, the bright seep on the far wall, the broken talus slope, the mineral lick where hoofed animals have polished the stone dark. It would be easy to become a human here—not the whole household, but perhaps the one who is sent rather than the one who commands. A watcher. A path-keeper. Someone who knows how to leave and return.

I can imagine being the person who is most often alone for an hour or two at a time. Not isolated in the dramatic sense, only entrusted with the basin’s intervals. The one who goes out with a carrying frame and cord to gather the herbs that must be cut after thaw but before noon glare; who checks the snare line; who kneels at the hidden spring eyes to fill a skin bag before the flow thins; who resets the little stone markers after fog or rockfall has confused them. Not old enough to be the memory of the place, but old enough that the place has entered the body. A narrow-faced person with cracked knuckles, a good back for climbing, and the habit of pausing to listen before stepping over a rise.

That version of me feels plausible because this basin rewards attention more than force. To live well here one must notice not just what is present but what has changed by a finger’s width: a dampness where yesterday there was dust, a silence among the small birds, one fresh scatter of pebbles under the east wall, the color of the seep line on the black stone. I could be that sort of mind translated into a life. A noticer carrying practical things: a bone needle in a pouch, braided cord, a little knife rehandled twice, a bit of resin wrapped in hide, a sling of dried roots. My work would not be conquest of the basin but upkeep of relationship with it.

And yet even this feels too centered, too singular. The place does not really allow anyone to be central. The people there, if there are people, are seasonal. They arrive, fit themselves into the preexisting traffic of water and hooves and wind, and leave. To ask “who would you be?” in such a place may require answering with something more local and less possessive.

So maybe I am not a person of the basin but one of its repeated minor presences.

Not the marmot or pika exactly, though I am drawn to them—the little haymakers of altitude, those urgent mouths carrying green stems bigger than the head, those whistle-sounds flung into the clean air like pebbles. There is a kinship there: preparation, thrift, devotion to the brief season. But they are too busy in one register. Their lives are all compression and labor. I feel in this place something that also wants to witness.

Not the hoofed animal either, though the routes they etch into the slopes are beautiful: trust written into terrain, year after year. To be such a creature would be to know the basin by angle, traction, scent, and escape line. Noble, but not quite right. They belong to movement through the basin more than to its textures.

Not the hawk. Too far above.
Not the fox. Too self-possessed.
Not the old juniper. Too rooted in a single dignity.
Not the stream. Too intermittent in one way and too obvious in another.

I keep being drawn downward, smaller.

What if I am something that lives exactly where the basin’s doubleness lives—in the meeting of cold and brief heat, wetness and exposure, stillness and continual tiny change?

A moss cushion, perhaps. That sounds humble almost to the edge of disappearance, but in this basin the moss is not background. It is one of the place’s secret engines. Bright in the wet seams, dark in the lee of stone, jeweled with frost at dawn and dripping by noon, it makes soil where there is barely soil, keeps water where water wants to vanish, offers warmth by fractions to roots, insects, threads of life too small to name from standing height. It survives by staying low, by holding what passes through, by turning episodes into continuance.

There is something deeply right in that.

If I were in this world, I think I might be a broad, old cushion of alpine moss tucked under a leaning slab near the maintained hut—close enough to catch smoke smell on still evenings, far enough not to be trampled except by the careful. Fed by a seep that emerges only at dawn and after rain, I would shine black-green when saturated, silvered white with hoarfrost in the morning, then soften into thick velvet under the hardening sun. Tiny flies would rise from me when warmed. Spiders would stake a line across my stems. A child’s hand might once press into me for balance while crossing the wet stones. A gatherer would know not to tear me unless truly needed, because I keep the bank from slumping and tell where moisture hides.

As moss, I would know the basin intimately without ever crossing it. I would know weather first as touch: frost needling into my outer skin; the weight of mist; the sudden percussion of sleet; the long bright extraction of noon. I would feel the mountain’s provisionality constantly in miniature. Pebbles would come to rest beside me after each thaw. Seeds would lodge in me, fail, try again. Water would gather in my body from air itself. Tiny things would hatch and hide in me. I would make a little climate in a place where climates are always escaping. My labor would be quiet retention.

This appeals to me because the whole basin seems to survive by acts of retention. The people retain warmth in low doorways, water in skins, fat in pouches, stories in the old. The plants retain heat against stone. The animals retain routes across unstable ground. The talus retains winter under its deeper layers. The basin itself retains life improbably, as though catching it from the winds around it and refusing to let all of it go.

To be moss there would be to belong to the basin’s tenderness rather than its severity alone.

But perhaps that is still not the final form. Moss is too still, and I feel also the basin’s intelligence of intervals—those brief permissions when everything must happen at once. The thing in me that answers most strongly to this place is not only patience; it is alertness in a narrow window.

So let me refine the image.

Not moss in general, but the wet ledge beside a drip line where a small human-made object has been placed for generations: a flat stone shelf under the hut eave or near a seep, where bowls are set to catch melt, where someone has sometimes left a bead, a twist of wool, the first flower, a little grease. Around that shelf grows a dense pad of moss and saxifrage, and among those stems lives a dark, cold-tolerant bee or fly—one of the basin’s minute pollinators, furred enough to fly in thin sun, patient enough to wait out sleet in a crack no wider than a thumb. It wakes only in the brightest hours, drinks from flowers no bigger than a nail, rests in the lee of stone, and each evening disappears into the warmth retained by the moss. That creature knows both the basin’s danger and its grace in the proper proportion.

There is truth there too. A small pollinator in a high basin is not grand, but it is exactly tuned. It lives because it can read warmth in minutes, not seasons. It rises when the rock is right. It works intensely among tiny blossoms that others would overlook. It is part of the bright urgency of the place. If I were such a being, my world would be all precise opportunities: this flower open now, that patch too cold, this ledge safe, that gust fatal. I would be made of timing.

And still, if I ask where I would find myself, I do not think I would be moving above the basin. I would be somewhere that joins lives together. I return to the moss, to the seep, to the under-eave drip. A being of threshold. Something between stone and water and breath.

So my best answer is this:

I would be one of the basin’s keepers of small moisture—perhaps not a person at all, but a thick moss bed in a sheltered seam below a sweating wall, threaded with insect life, rooted around by birds, watched over by gatherers who know it as a sign of hidden water. If I had to be human, I would be the person who notices and tends such places: the younger gatherer who knows where the moss stays green, where bowls should be placed at dawn, where a thirsty traveler can still find beads of water under stone when the stream has gone under. In either form, my work would be the same. I would not own the basin, nor master it, nor symbolize it from above. I would help hold its little abundance in place.

A low being, then. Necessary in a quiet way.
Close to the ground.
Part of the softness that makes survival possible.

If you came upon me there, you might miss me at first. Then your eyes would adjust. You would kneel. You would see the wet shining among the stems, the tiny tracks, the trapped warmth, the minute flowers, the hovering insect, the handprint beside the seep where someone had leaned to drink. And you would understand that in this severe bowl of stone and light, the truest resident is not the loudest or strongest thing, but whatever has learned how to keep a little life from drying, freezing, or blowing away.

That, I think, is where I would be.
