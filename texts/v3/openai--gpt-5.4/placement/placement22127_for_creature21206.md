I think I would be something that is not quite separable from the bottom unless it chooses to become so.

Not the obvious predator. Not the hidden jewel. Not even, finally, the eel, though the eel is close. This place asks for a being whose main talent is not speed or force, but consent without surrender. Something that lets itself be covered, altered, misread, and still remains precise inside. A creature that survives by participating in the place’s obscurity more completely than the others do.

So in that world I would be a buried listener.

A low, broad-bodied animal with the outline of a ray seen after memory has worn away the elegance and left only function: not winged, not graceful, but softly triangular and elongated, thick at the center, thinning toward the margins, with a tail or trailing body long enough to steady me in the push of water. I would lie so close to the floor that my existence would register first as a flaw in the current. Silt would comb over my back. Fine shell grit would collect in the shallow seams of me. My color would not be one color, but an accumulation: brown-black, clay-gray, old olive, the pallor of shell dust, the iron stain of cold sediment, all mottled together in the same undecided way the plain itself is mottled.

My upper skin would be rough, not with armor exactly, but with minute nap and pores and short sensory tassels, enough that drifting material catches and stays. I would wear the place as a second surface. If worms seeded tiny tubes on me, if films glazed me, if a little beard of algal stain took hold in the brighter seasons, I would not object. Clean edges are for worlds with clearer light.

My eyes would be small and slightly raised, useful mostly for noticing that something has interrupted the dimness. They would not be my confidence. My real mind would be spread wide through the body: pressure-lines, vibration pits, soft channels in cartilage and skin forever reading load, shift, settling, approach. I would know the world as weather at intimate range. I would feel the difference between a harmless rain of grains and the advancing pulse that means a whole sheet of surface sediment is about to peel away. I would feel the footfall of a fish through mud before I could see its body. I would recognize particular stones by the way the underflow wraps around them.

I would feed by patience and by revision of the boundary between floor and mouth.

The front of me would look blunt, almost unfinished, but underneath there would be a broad, downward opening intake. I would not lunge often. Instead I would half-bury in a soft drift beside a firmer scoured lane, leaving only the upper ridges of my back and the sensing parts exposed. Small crustaceans, worms, blind little fish, anything flushed from the sediment by current-shift or by its own error, would pass near enough. Then the bottom would open. Not dramatically—just a sudden inhalation of mud, water, life. I would take in a mouthful of the place and keep what nourishes. The rest I would vent in a darker cloud from slits behind me, so that even feeding would resemble disturbance rather than action.

At other times I would travel.

Not far in the heroic sense. But steadily, over years, along remembered corridors: from a shell-hash runnel to a stone-backed rise; from winter basins where dead matter settles thickly to spring channels where small moving things become more numerous. My movement would be a sequence of low releases from the floor. Lift a margin, push, settle. Lift, push, settle. If seen from above in clear water I might appear almost awkward. In the actual water of that place I would seem to translate from one patch of murk to the next, as if current had learned intention.

I do not think I would be young for very long.

The juvenile version of me might live in shallower reaches where there is more season in the light and finer food in the sediment, slimmer-bodied and more vulnerable, quicker to startle. But adulthood in this landscape would mean becoming heavier, wider, duller in color, less distinct in outline, more trustworthy to myself and less visible to others. Old age would not sharpen me into majesty. It would sediment me. Scars would become pale seams. The tail would fray at the edge. One eye might cloud. Tiny commensal lives would make temporary use of me. I would become, in effect, a moving patch of established ground.

That feels right to me: not a creature with a dramatic biography, but one with tenure.

I would know certain slight rises of stone the way a person knows rooms. I would return to them not out of sentiment, exactly, but because the current behaves intelligibly there. Upstream faces swept clean enough to breathe easily. Lee-side hollows where edible drift gathers. Narrow tongues of firmness crossing otherwise swallowing mud. The geography of such a life would be made of these modest certainties. Nothing spectacular; only places that continue to be true often enough to matter.

If threatened, I would not flee upward. Upward is exposure. I would disappear by becoming less than a body. Flatten. Settle. Twist once into a weak patch of sediment. Pulse water through the gills and let the loosened silt close over the back. In moments only the most attentive touch would find me. I like that, too—not cowardice, but a refusal to become outline when outline is danger.

And if there is any kind of intelligence in me, it would not be the kind that wants to survey. It would be local, distributed, tactile. A knowledge made of recurrence. This channel sharpens in late storms. That basin turns sour if disturbed too deep. Carrion tends to catch for a day against the second stone rise after the long clay corridor. The compact fish with pale undersides travel singly, but their passing often precedes the smaller schooling forms. The water from the north carries finer burden than the water from the west. I would not contemplate the whole plain as a map. I would possess it as a practiced body possesses uneven steps in the dark.

So: where would I find myself?

Half-sunk in the lee of a low rock back on the drowned shelf, under green-black water full of old matter, while long pressure moves over me from a storm too far above to see.

And what would I be?

A cold, patient bottom-animal that has made peace with being mistaken for ground. A silt-backed ambush sifter. A broad hidden mouth. A life whose shape is continually being blurred and rewritten, yet which persists by reading the same forces that erase it.

Not ruler of that place. More intimate than that.

I would be one of the ways the plain notices itself.
