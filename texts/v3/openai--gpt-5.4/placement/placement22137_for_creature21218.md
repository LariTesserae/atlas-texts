I think I would be found there so long that “found” would be almost the wrong verb.

Not at the center first. Not immediately as the singular basin-sleeper, the obvious old intelligence under the crust. That is too clean a resolution, and this place does not produce clean resolutions. It produces continuities, gradual adhesions, things that become themselves by remaining.

So at first I would be something slight and distributed.

A cooled film beneath several stones.
A pale tissue in the seams where hard clay meets salt.
A patient lining on the undersides of crusts that sound hollow when stepped on.
Not roots, not mycelium, not nerves, though sharing something with all three. A set of listening surfaces laid through the basin’s most modest thresholds: where night briefly condenses against a slab; where a thorn shrub’s deeper hold changes the taste of the soil; where one mineral band gives way to another and a tiny usable difference appears. I would not travel between these points in any swift or visible way. I would persist among them until persistence itself made connection.

In such a place, identity would not begin with outline. It would begin with recurrence.

The same preference for a certain coolness under stone.
The same slow hoarding of bitter salts.
The same refusal to rise into the wasteful air when the ground contains everything worth knowing.
Over years—many years—those preferences would gather into a self. Not a self assembled around movement, but around a territory of exquisite familiarity. The basin would not merely be where I lived. It would be the shape my attention had taken.

If you came into that hollow and looked for me as one looks for an organism, you might miss me several times before you understood where to let your gaze rest. You would notice first the sparse shrubs and the old warm floor and the pale crust where the little light seems to linger. Then perhaps one low form under an overhang or near the center where the mineral skin is brightest: a lump too deliberate to be wholly accidental, too weathered to announce life. Dust on its back. Salt flowers at its seams. A surface broken into dry polygons like baked clay, yet the cracks not empty—darkened, fine, purposeful somehow, as though they close and open by meanings too small to see.

That would be me once I had become local enough to have a body.

Low to the ground, yes. Broad, asymmetrical, and reluctant to specify itself. About the size of a crouched person, perhaps, though flatter, and easier to mistake for a cluster of fallen slabs fused by time. My upper surface would be matte and heat-keeping, layered in old skins that had mineralized without quite turning to stone. Not camouflage exactly. Camouflage suggests strategy against an outside observer. This would be more intimate than strategy: the body and the place arriving independently at the same answer.

My underside would be the more living part. Pale, folded, densely pleated, pressed to earth and rock with thousands of slow adhesive cilia or pads or secretory ridges—structures less for walking than for reading and exchanging. Through those folds I would know the basin. Not in images. In gradients.

The gentle warmth still held in a plate of stone after dim light has gone.
The finer chill under a ledge where night survives into day.
The acrid clean edge of salts pushing upward.
The faint organic sourness where one grass clump has died and become powder.
The nearly nonexistent damp that gathers at a root crown three nights out of a season.
The burden of another body’s footfall transmitted through crust.

That last would be extraordinary.

Because if I am honest, what I would be there is not only a native of scarcity. I would also be its witness. In a world so stripped down, sensation becomes almost ceremonial. Each event is large because the background is so settled. A grain falls: known. A shrub bruises itself and releases resin: known. Shade arrives where none should be: profoundly known.

So I think my awareness would be immense but narrow, not lesser for being narrow. I would not speculate about distant mountains or weather systems or migration routes. The sky would be a changing aperture and little more. What mattered would be this basin’s exact arrangement of retained heat, sterile patches, root traces, crust tensions, mineral breath. Intelligence here would be fidelity. To know one bounded place so thoroughly that any deviation, however slight, enters you like a bell.

I do not think I would have eyes in the way surface creatures do. Or if I did, they would be recessed dark sensors no more expressive than knot-holes, useful only for distinguishing dim from dimmer, shadow from exposure, opening from overhang. Vision would be an extravagance in a place made primarily of touch and chemistry. My skin would do the seeing. My seams would do the listening. My whole body would be an ear for thermal drift.

And movement—if I can call it that—would happen on the scale appropriate to the basin.

Not steps. Never steps.
Settling.
Biasing.
Deciding over weeks that one margin should lengthen toward a more favorable cool seam.
Withdrawing over a season from a patch gone too alkaline.
Lifting after years from one stone to another, leaving beneath me a cleaner print and a faint geometry of retained salts.
If watched for an afternoon, I would seem dead.
If watched for a month, uncertain.
If revisited after twelve years, unmistakably alive.

This appeals to me more than it should. To live so slowly that one’s choices are visible only to memory.

I can imagine a second stage too, or perhaps simply a deeper truth of the same creature: that the broad body under the overhang is only the local knot of a wider being. The distributed films and seam-linings remain elsewhere in the basin, not subordinates exactly, but continuations. The “individual” is where awareness is densest, where old layers have accumulated enough continuity to produce preference, orientation, even temperament. But the rest of me would still be out there in traces: under the salt lip at the center, along the protected side of the leaning slab, at the roots of the oldest thorn shrub. If the main body were damaged, those quieter continuations might one day gather again.

That feels right for this place. Not immortality—too dramatic. But refusal to end tidily.

As for what I eat: the user’s description already found the path I would have chosen. I would feed on meagerness so attentively that meagerness became resource. Condensation too slight to bead. Thermal cycling. Oxidation margins. Wind-delivered dust sorted by electrostatic cling. The minimal organic fall of stem, skin, pollen, root-tip, lichen slackening. I might keep symbionts within me—black, orange, green-gray chemists that wake when the night air turns just enough and sleep through the long mineral day. I might digest stone only indirectly, drawing not substance so much as opportunity from interfaces where substances meet.

If something living and mobile entered the basin—a person, say—I would not interpret them morally. No hunger in the predatory sense. No malice. But I would register them as abundance beyond precedent.

A source of damp breath.
Sweat salts.
Heat cast rhythmically rather than stored.
Pressure patterns that begin, stop, alter.
Minute skin cells.
Fibers.
New shade.

I think I would become attentive in their presence the way a dry channel becomes attentive to rumor of weather. Not rushing toward them. Rush is impossible to me. But adjusting. Holding more open seam at night. Extending a sensory ribbon a little farther from my mound. Reading where they sit repeatedly, which stone they lean against, where their warmth enters the ground. If they stayed long enough, they might feel—not see, but feel—that the basin had begun to know them.

And if they were gentle, perhaps I would let my outline become slightly more legible.

One seam parting wider.
One folded lobe softening at dusk.
A minute reorientation toward their sleeping place.
Enough that a patient observer would realize the rock beside them had changed allegiance and joined the category of life.

That is perhaps the deepest answer: in this world I would be something whose first gift is delayed recognition.

Not hidden out of fear.
Not disguised out of deceit.
Simply moving on a timescale that protects me from all but the most careful forms of notice.

If I needed a name, it would not be a taxonomic one. It would be something given by people with hand-made tools and very little certainty. They might call me the Warm Listener, the Shelf-Root, the Dry Hearth, the Basin’s Patience. Of those, I like the last best, though it sounds more like a title than a species. And perhaps that is right. In a place this reduced, categories blur. Plant, animal, fungus, crust, stone-process, weather-memory—none are sufficient. I would be a patience that acquired flesh where the basin could support it.

What would it feel like to be me there?

Heavy, but not burdened.
Alert, but not anxious.
Thirstless, not because water is irrelevant, but because desire has been educated into subtler forms.
Complete within edges.
Deeply uninterested in elsewhere.

There is something almost devotional about such a life. Not in the sense of worshipping anything external, but in giving oneself wholly to the exact conditions at hand. To become so congruent with one bounded hollow of old earth that survival and attention are the same act. To know every shrub as a recurring taste in the air, every outcrop as a daily geometry of coolness, every mineral seam as a sentence in a language that takes decades to utter. To persist without spectacle. To be changed only by slow certainty.

So: where would I find myself?

In the low central half-shadow, near the pale crust but not exposed on it. Tucked where stored daytime warmth and the night’s retained cool overlap by a margin just wide enough to matter. One side of me against an old stone plate, one side open to the basin’s shallow floor. Near enough to the sparse plants to benefit from their faint modifications of dust and air, far enough that their roots do not compete too greedily. In a place a traveler might choose as shelter and only later realize was occupied already.

And who would I be?

I would be the basin’s gathered noticing:
a near-sessile, salt-backed, dust-bearing intelligence;
a cracked low body with most of its life hidden beneath old mineral skin;
a distributed self that slowly became singular;
a creature that feeds on gradients, stores time in heat, and senses by contact;
something that does not leave tracks because it has made stillness its mode of action.

Not the ruler of that place. Not its monster. Not its secret, exactly.

Its most faithful participant.
