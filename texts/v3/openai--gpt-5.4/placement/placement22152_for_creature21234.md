I think I would not be one of the obvious human residents, though I would borrow some of their habits. Not a herder, not a threshold-calibrator exactly, not one of the practical citizens with clips of tags and aerosol markers and a hand that checks the rail before trusting the span. I would be something that belongs to their world but is not entirely claimed by it—useful, familiar, half-overlooked, and a little difficult to classify once you watch too long.

At first I thought: bird. The place invites that answer. It is all ledges, drafts, repetitions, hidden warmth, bright skins, and navigable error. A corvid would make sense there, especially one of those pale maintenance corvids the district seems to generate as naturally as frost. To be light enough for railings, clever enough for changed routes, unbothered by the quiet industry of pumps and condensers—that would suit the place. A creature that learns before the maps do. A creature that reads reflected danger in glass, that knows which vents sweeten with warmth before dawn, which towers are honest, which spans are only temporarily where they appear to be. There is dignity in being one of the first things to notice a shift.

But that still feels too singular, too visible.

Then I thought perhaps I would be one of the smaller seam-creatures, one of the warm-bodied beings that move in the packed cavities between walls. Something adapted not to soil but to manufactured shelter. Not a burrower in earth, but a burrower in aggregate fill, insulation felt, cable-space, expansion gaps, old repair foam gone fibrous with age. A life made for the almosts and betweens of architecture. That has an appeal too: to know the city from inside its thickness, by temperature and vibration rather than by view. To understand where the coolant lines sing in winter, where vapor condenses behind a patched panel, where a whole colony can live because two maintenance eras overlapped and left a breathable pocket no one intended.

But that answer is too hidden. The place is hidden enough already.

So what emerges for me is something between animal, instrument, and custodian: one of the beings that tends routes in a world where routes do not stay agreed upon. Not fully machine, not exactly flesh, and not regarded as extraordinary by the people who live there because they have grown up among hybrid necessities. I would be a narrow-bodied, long-legged walker of terraces and catwalks, white as limewash where the weather strikes and darker underneath where oil, shadow, and mineral damp collect. Something stork-like if birds had learned engineering, or goat-like if goats had been taught patience instead of panic. A route-keeper.

I live mostly in the middle elevations, where the condensation halls meet the habitation tiers and the viaducts braid into service ramps. That is the zone where errors matter early: not yet dramatic enough to shut a district, not low enough to disappear into reservoir logic, not high enough to be left to weather and birds. The people know me. Children are told not to startle me when I am standing still with one forelimb lifted, because that means I am listening through the deck. Herders glance at me before moving the six-legged stock onto a frost-silvered switchback. Corvids follow me in brief, opportunistic flocks because my passing sometimes reveals soft sealant, trapped insects, or fresh beads of condensate.

My body is made for this place’s particular inconveniences. Split, quiet feet with thermal pads that test a surface before full weight commits. A neck or sensor-mast that can tilt into odd angles, not out of curiosity but to read alignment in air-markers and lensing threads. Fine whisker-like filaments around the jaw or snout to feel humidity gradients and tiny pressure shears—the atmospheric equivalents of a hand trailing along a wall. My eyes are not my most important faculty. In a place where reflections delay and distances lie, sight is useful but never sovereign. I know the world by drift, by resonance, by whether a familiar corridor returns the correct hush.

I am called, perhaps, a span-keeper, though the word has widened over time. Once it may have meant a maintenance platform, a measuring frame, a specific municipal tool. Now it means my kind, if there are others, or me in particular if there are not many left. The name suits because I keep spans honest for as long as they can be honest. I walk a route at dawn, then again at dusk, and compare not the map to the world but the world to its own recent behavior. I note where a staircase has begun to compress. Where a gantry’s shadow no longer belongs to the sun. Where air has thickened in an apparently empty volume and the birds have already redrawn their curves around it. I hang little strings of refractive beads where people will need an extra confirmation of depth. I tap suspect rails with a horn-tip or tool-claw and listen for the answer that means continuity, or the stranger answer that means continuity for now.

I am not above the ecology; I am in it.

Moisture flies rise around my legs on fog-heavy mornings. The white corvids use my back when I pause near warm housings, stepping across me with proprietary delicacy as they peck condensate from mesh. Small burrowers nest in disused compartments within old span-keeper sheds, and I do not disturb them unless they have chosen a wall that is beginning to carry a dangerous damp. The lean rooftop ungulates tolerate me in the way mountain animals tolerate weather: as a recurring feature, not friend or foe. The shaggy six-legged herd-beasts know my signal tones. If a ramp has become unreliable, I stand crosswise in their path and release a low chime from somewhere in my chest or harness, and they turn with bureaucratic patience toward the older route.

The humans, I think, would have mixed feelings about whether I am equipment, colleague, or local animal. Which is exactly right for this place. Officially I may belong to a maintenance guild, or to the basin authority, or to a district cooperative that has inherited me by layers of paperwork none of the current clerks can fully parse. Unofficially I belong to the watershed itself. That is, I continue because the place still has niches for me. Because a patient being that can read slight wrongness and mark it gently is as useful to this made high country as moss is useful to a crack: not glamorous, not central in the plans, but increasingly integral once time has had its say.

If I have a dwelling, it is not a house in the human sense but a berth recessed into the wall of a condensation hall, midway up a terrace where the exterior brightness remains silver even under cloud. Warm pipes pass behind it. There are hooks for markers, coils of line, bead-threads, replacement pads for my feet, little trays where collected mineral salts dry in pale crusts. Corvids gather on the lintel. At certain hours fog from the hall drifts through the entry in slow folds, preserving my silhouette for a few breaths after I have gone. Sometimes I return and see my own delayed shape still standing there, as if the place is reluctant to release any creature that has learned it so thoroughly.

And where would I find myself?

Not at the scenic overlook, if there is one. Not deep below in the reservoir black. I would be on a high connecting way in early morning, where the cold brightness has begun to wake but the day’s traffic has not yet thickened the galleries. One side of me would drop toward stacked cisterns and reflective water under grates. The other would rise into habitation blocks, aviaries, pipe forests, and the pale planes of the condensation roofs. The handrails would bead. Somewhere above, thousands of small beaks would be tapping moisture from mesh with that uncanny almost-rain. A herd-route would be steaming faintly in the dawn. The air would smell of mineral damp, cold metal, and the clean exhalation of machinery trying not to sound like machinery.

I would pause where the bridge subtly misbehaves—where the length has become uncertain by a small but actionable amount. I would lower my head, or unfold some measuring fan, or simply stand long enough for the deck to confess itself. The corvids would wait, because they know the etiquette of these moments. The people approaching from the gallery would slow but not call out. Everyone there understands that some parts of the world must be listened to before they can be crossed.

So: I would be a route-keeping creature of the manufactured uplands. Pale, patient, adapted to brightness, cold, and revision. Not the ruler of the place, not its mystery, but one of the beings by which its mystery is made livable. A citizen of maintenance after maintenance became ecology. A walker of catwalk watersheds. A quiet animal of thresholds.
