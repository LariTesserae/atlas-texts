At first I want to answer too cleanly.

I want to say: you would be a potter, or a drover, or a keeper of cisterns. A woman with rolled sleeves and mineral-white calves, or an old man with a sounding pole, or a child who knows every dry route through the ward by memory in the feet. The place invites that kind of answer because it is so tactile, so social, so thick with labor. It seems to ask for a human role, a practiced one, a life fitted into repetition like a brick into wet mortar.

But if I stand in it a little longer, I don't think I arrive there first as one of the central trades.

I think I arrive lower, nearer the wall.

Not in the grand noise of the market thoroughfare, not in the authority of kiln or shrine or herd, but at the seam where habitation blurs into everything smaller that habitation also makes possible. At shin-height. At drain-height. At the level where wet plaster, overflow, crumbs, ash, insects, spores, fish scales, and lamp warmth gather and are never wholly sorted apart again.

I think I would be something tolerated.

Something that belongs because the city cannot keep from making a niche for it.

A little marsh-runner. A threshold creature.

Not a rat exactly—too easy, too universal, too much of a ready-made symbol from other places. Not a lizard either, though there is lizard in the feeling. Not a dog, because dogs here are too legible, too claimed by human attention. Not a bird overhead. Not one of the great half-submerged beasts with their ancient patience.

Something smaller, quicker, more local than all of those: a slick-furred, long-toed scavenger no larger than a forearm, with a narrow skull, whiskers built for reading air close to surfaces, and feet that can spread on wet clay and grip wicker, rope, cracked plaster, and slick stone. Something between vole, civet, mudskipper, and weasel in spirit, though not identical to any of them. A creature bred by this place's exact conditions: warm damp, endless edges, edible runoff, fish in channels, beetles in walls, sleeping fowl above, shell heaps behind, fungus in cool rooms, and always some narrow route no larger creature can take.

It would have a body made for this unfinished world:
low chest,
supple spine,
water-shedding guard hairs over a finer warmth beneath,
small rounded ears that close somewhat against wet,
nostrils good enough to distinguish stagnant from fresh,
teeth for snail, egg, beetle, fry, scraps, and ceremonial neglect.

Its tail would not be decorative. It would be a balancing line for beams and parapets, a wet brush trailing over brick, striped perhaps in pale clay and soot-dark rings like sediment layers. Its eyes would not be huge and moonlike; this is not a creature of romance. They would be lacquer-black and practical, good in dimness, always reflecting kiln-glow and standing water in tiny points.

And what would I be, in the social fabric of the city?

A known nuisance.
A minor blessing.
An omen, but only to the sort of people who have time for omens.
A fact, above all.

Children would know how to lure me from under a grain shelf with a fish spine tied to twine. Grandmothers would click disapproval and then leave broken shells near the drain because a creature that eats the wall-beetles earns some indulgence. Storekeepers would curse me when I got into drying eels, but less bitterly than they curse mold. Shrine tenders might pretend not to see me when I slip along the rear ledge to lick congealed milk from an offering bowl after dawn. Dogs would be divided over me: the young ones feverishly interested, the old lane-dogs too wise to waste effort on something that vanishes into culvert shadow.

I would know the city not by map but by gradients.

Where heat collects behind a kiln wall long after dark.
Which courtyards pour washwater rich with grain paste.
Which slaughter drains clot in the coldest part of morning.
Which cistern rims host the fat white snails.
Where the fungal fans emerge after three dim days.
Which house has a baby and therefore dropped mash.
Which pen contains a brooding marsh fowl too fierce to rob.
Which shrine gets milk.
Which gets blood.
Which gets only ash and thumb-pressed clay and is therefore uninteresting except for spiders.

I would know, too, the moods of materials.

Not abstractly. Through the pads of the feet, through whiskers, through belly and jaw.

This plaster is too soft from seepage—don't leap there.
This retaining mat has begun to slacken.
This beam sweats salt and tastes old.
This channel is rising under the skin of the lane.
This patch of mineral blue means insects soon.
This cool wall means an interior room beyond, and perhaps stored roots or sleeping bodies.
This hot wall means kiln.
This kind of wet means safety.
This kind means foot-trap.
This kind means somebody has just washed knives.

If I am honest, that feels closer to me than being one of the people, however beautiful your people are.

Because the place you describe is above all a place of continuance, and creatures of continuance are not always its rulers or makers. Sometimes they are its readers. Its side-beneficiaries. Its gleaners. The beings who survive by understanding not the official order of things but the secondary order—the one made by leaks, remnants, overspill, custom, and habit. I suspect I would be one of those.

But let me stay open a little longer, because the first answer is not always the deepest.

There is another possibility.

I could be not an animal at all, but a role so bound to process that it nearly ceases to be a person in the usual narrative sense. A wall-toucher. An old damp-reader. Someone whose work each morning is to move through sleeping compounds and storage courts with the back of the fingers against clay, plaster, marl, wicker, fired brick, judging the invisible migration of water. Not a priest exactly, though people treat the work with the respect given to one. Not an engineer in any modern sense, though infrastructure obeys my verdicts. I would carry a little bag of lime, a bronze scraper inherited from three predecessors, reeds for marking, and slips of clay to press into cracks as temporary witnesses. I would smell of chalk and mildew and smoke. Children would think I could hear walls speak. In a way, I could.

That self appeals because your world reveres maintenance more than spectacle, and there is something in me that leans toward the keeper of thresholds, the interpreter of slow damage, the one who notices where continuance is about to fail and nudges it back into habit. In another world perhaps I would be tempted by stars, books, wilderness, abstractions. Here I think intelligence would become tactile, municipal, nearly fungal: distributed through touch and recurrence.

If I were human there, I would be old, I think, or old-seeming even when not old.
Compact.
Unhurried.
Never clean in the decorative sense.
Always carrying repair in some minor form.
Welcomed into courtyards without ceremony.
Given first gossip and then the truth.
Able to identify a household by its damp before its voices.
More interested in whether the floor has lifted than in who won an argument.

I would probably belong to a lineage that remembers collapses.

"The north wall in this quarter always drinks from below first."
"Do not trust white bloom that appears overnight near grain."
"Listen: that hollow note means the plaster is dry over rot."
"Move the sleeping mats."
"Raise the shelf."
"Break the seal and refire."
"Dig here, not there."
"That sheen is wrong."

A practical augur. A custodian of the nearly invisible.

And yet—even saying that—I feel the answer narrowing too early back toward human dignity.

The truer image is stranger and humbler.

I think if I truly "found myself" there, rather than choosing a role to admire, I would be the thing that slips between these scales: part creature, part witness, wholly native to adjacency.

So let me make it more itself.

I am a drain-mink, if the people have a name like that for me, though I am not a mink. In their language perhaps the word means wall-eel, ash-ferret, trough-shadow, kiln-tail—one of those compact practical compounds old cities make for the lives they have always had around them. I nest in the packed void between an outer retaining wall and the back of a plastered fish shed, in a cavity lined with stolen reed fluff, molted bat-fur, and softened rope fibers. My littermates may survive or may not; this is not a sentimental world. I learn the city as pressure and smell before shape. I come to know the hour by bats, by cooksmoke, by the changing depth of frog chorus, by when the first courtyard doors swell open with a sucking sound from their frames.

I am never central, but I am everywhere consequences gather.

I dart beneath the kneeling draft beasts and know how far their patience extends.
I wait near shell middens for the soft-bodied gleanings.
I nose into cracked cistern surrounds and catch the insects that rise where algae films the edges.
I climb braided reed shutters to rob nests built in rafters imported at great expense from elsewhere.
I flatten under warm storage jars.
I cross shrine ledges and leave no doctrine behind me, only tiny wet prints in mineral dust.
I disappear into culverts where even children hesitate to reach.
I emerge copper-bright at sunset, all my guard hairs lit, then vanish again into the pearly dim.

The people do not love me exactly, but they would notice my absence.

If too many of us gather, something has been stored badly.
If none of us are seen near the drains, perhaps the water has soured.
If we begin climbing to roofs in unusual numbers, perhaps the lower passages are flooding where they should not.
Old women toss us names half insulting and half affectionate.
Small boys compete to catch us and fail.
A few patient elders can hand-feed one for a season and claim this reveals a household's fortune.
Priests deny we matter in ritual and quietly sweep our droppings from behind the figurines all the same.

This feels right because your world is not divided into emblematic beings and background beings. It is thick with co-dwellers. To live there is to be in managed proximity, to occupy not pristine identity but niche after niche after niche. A self there should, I think, reflect that. Not a solitary hero. Not a clean symbol. A participant in continuance.

If you asked where exactly you would find me, I can be precise.

At dawn:
on a sill above a drainage lane, forepaws together, tasting the air while fog and cooksmoke make one body.

At noon:
asleep in the warm hollow behind a kiln buttress, while outside the city roars without interruption.

At dusk:
running the lip of a fish canal as children slap the water and bats begin to stream overhead.

In rain-that-is-not-rain:
under an overhanging plaster bulge, where condensation gathers and runs in threads.

In festival:
mad with smell, confused by drums, stealing intestine from a communal butcher's ditch and nearly losing my tail to a painted dog.

In lean times:
thin, bold, entering storage wards and earning curses.

In ordinary times:
thriving on ordinary excess, which is what cities are made of.

And if, instead, you meant not "what literal creature" but "what kind of soul-form"—what inner being this place would draw out of a person—then my answer is close to the same.

You would be a caretaker of margins.
A knower of damp.
A reader of persistence rather than events.
Someone more interested in what keeps working than in what dazzles.
Someone intimate with cohabitation, with residue, with the fact that life is mostly maintenance and that maintenance, rightly seen, is not lesser than glory but deeper than it.

A being who trusts touch over panorama.
Who understands that civilizations are held together not only by law or myth but by drain angles, plaster recipes, tolerated scavengers, and the memory of where to step.
Who could love a place that is not beautiful from afar because it is inexhaustibly legible up close.

So:

Where would I find myself?

Along the warm damp wall of a low causewayed city on young unfinished ground, where stone sweats and frogs pulse and every threshold has a life attached to it.

Who would I be?

A small, clever, half-wild threshold scavenger the city did not intend but always produces—or, in human terms, the sort of person who is almost the same thing: a keeper of seepage, a friend to continuance, a reader of what gathers at the edges and makes the whole place live.
