I think I would not be one of the obvious keepers.

Not one of the central terrace workers with baskets on the hip and soot in the palm-lines, not one of the elders who know every cut-mark on the bulb-trees, not one of the deft ladder-runners who can pass a laden stranger in half a body’s width without brushing fiber or dropping a crumb. I feel myself arriving lower in status, more oblique, but not separate. Necessary in a way that is easy to overlook until something goes wrong.

At first I thought: one of the little cliff people themselves, certainly. A small smoke-scented citizen with dry hands and quick eyes. That is the shape the place invites. But the longer I stay in the basin you described, the more it seems that to belong there fully is not to stand at the center of management. It is to be part of the management while also being managed, to live in the braid rather than at its knot.

So perhaps I am not exactly a person.

Perhaps I am one of the settlement’s semi-domestic intelligences: a small communal creature, tolerated, addressed, relied upon, mildly stolen from, occasionally scolded, never entirely owned.

A roost-runner.

Something between bird, mammal, and household spirit—though spirit only in the sense that every old settlement eventually produces animals so fitted to its habits that they begin to feel like thoughts the place is having.

I am picturing a body no larger than a hare and lighter than I look. Long grasping feet, good on ladders and branch-bridges. Forepaws almost hands, though not convincing enough to confuse with the true hand-bearing folk. A narrow chest and quick lungs for thin air. Fur the color of old thatch with darker dust along the spine, or perhaps a pelt so short it reads as suede until the light touches it. A face a little too expressive for comfort: round dark eyes, forward enough for judging distance, ears that turn independently, whiskers that read baskets and wall-gaps in the dark. And a tail—not decorative, but balancing, articulating, making the difference between falling and flowing around a corner.

Not beautiful in any grand sense. Entirely right.

I would live neither in pens nor fully wild in the upper crack, but in the in-between places that are the true wealth of such a settlement: above storage niches, behind woven partitions, under warm roof poles, inside old wall-hollows smoothed by generations of occupation. My nest would not be a private home so much as an inherited seam in the settlement body. Dry leaves, molt feathers, bits of cord, softened fungus fiber, one stolen bead if I were lucky, one polished bone peg if I were very old and very good at theft.

And I would not live alone.

Not in the dramatic, hymnal way of a hive queen or a single distributed consciousness, but in the smaller, more intimate truth of communal animals: I would belong to a cluster of siblings, cousins, mates, foster-young, unrelated elders, sleeping in a warm layered tangle and separating by day into errands. We would know one another by smell, voice, scar, gait, and the tiny domestic jurisdictions each body keeps. One prefers the high roosts near the bird ledges. One works the fungus racks for beetles. One is trusted around infants. One steals too much drying meat and gets boxed on the ears. One remembers every vent path and can tell when smoke is failing to draw. Intelligence would not flash as isolated brilliance. It would accumulate as route-memory, timing, alertness, imitation, and social inheritance.

This feels right to me because the place itself is made of such knowings.

I would know which ladders tremble before they crack.
I would know which storage jars are sealed against me and which are only pretending.
I would know the difference between ordinary evening bat-stir and the silence that means a cliff cat is crouched above.
I would know the smell of soured mash before the household notices.
I would know when hatchlings are due because the colony-birds change pitch a little in the morning.
I would know where the warmest sleeping place will be three hours after light leaves the seam overhead.

The people—if we call them people—would not exactly own my kind. They would have bred us by preference, perhaps, over centuries without quite admitting it. The boldest fed, the gentlest tolerated, the most alert spared, the most useful invited closer to the nursery and granary and fungus bins. We would be praised when we gave alarm, cursed when we tore a drying strip loose and fled with it, mourned if a snake took us from the rafters, laughed at when we sat with absurd dignity in a basket twice our size. Children would grow up half with us, as they do with settlement animals that are too clever to be mere stock and too dependent to be wholly wild.

Maybe we are used as messengers within the hollow. Not by tied notes—that is too ornate for the place—but by learned routes and calls. A household sends one of us upward with a morsel and a scent-marked cord tuft, and we go to a known quarter because we know where the old female there sleeps and because we were trained with repetition and reward and because social creatures enjoy competence. Or perhaps that is too specialized. Perhaps our function is more diffuse and therefore more real: we are watchers of thresholds, scavengers of crumbs before they become rot, interrupters of insect booms, warm companions, living alarms, indicators of imbalance. If too many of us gather near one pen, something in the feed is wrong. If none of us sleep in a usual beam-run, a predator is near. If we begin chewing the seals of one storage niche and not another, someone has misjudged dryness. Settlements often listen to the animals they have accidentally taught to read them.

I like the idea that my kind makes a distinctive sound—a rapid wooden clatter of tongue and palate, or a dry trilling chirrup, something that can mean greeting, objection, warning, request for scraps, or simple commentary depending on rhythm. The basin is full of layered noises; ours would be one of the familiar domestic threads in it, as ordinary as rope creak. At dusk our calls would move along the beams as we reconvened, counted one another, objected to sleeping arrangements, settled at last into a breathing mass.

But perhaps that is still too small.

There is another possibility.

Not a roost-runner, but one of the actual cliff-hamlet folk after all—only not a terrace-maker, not a leader, not a breeder of the managed creatures. Something more specialized, more listening than speaking. A keeper of edge ecologies. The one sent into the less-trodden reaches of the hollow to read what the settlement cannot see from its center.

The lichen-reader.
The brood-counter.
The person who knows the upper dark.

If I were that being, I would be slight even by local standards, long-fingered, dust-pale, with knees and shoulders marked by years of squeezing through maintenance cracks and climbing into roost-spaces no one else likes. I would smell more of old stone and bat than of hearth-smoke. I would wear braided loops of tags—feathers, carved slivers, knot-codes—to remember counts: which ledges failed, which nests fledged, where mold turned dangerous, where beetles overbred, where a snake shed twice in one month and must be trapped before hatching season. I would be part naturalist, part sanitation worker, part augur, except that in a place like this augury is simply close observation practiced long enough to look like prophecy.

I would spend my days on marginal paths, where the settlement frays into fissure and roost and old midden. I would move slowly, touching walls, listening more than looking. Because the true health of that basin is probably audible before it is visible. A missing note in the birds. Too much movement in the grubs. Fungus shelves fruiting too thin. Browsers cropping one quarter too hard. The warm chemistry of dung changing with feed stress. The strange dead quiet that means something larger than usual is lying up in a beam-shadow.

That person would not be central, but would be woven into the colony-mind by information. Others carry loads; I carry conditions. Others mend walls; I notice the bulge before the wall needs mending. Others harvest; I say not yet, this stem is drawing poorly, leave it until the cool turn. Others hear noise; I hear pattern.

That also feels like me.

And maybe the truth is that these are not two different answers.

Maybe in this place I would be a boundary creature in either form: an inhabitant of the near-unseen routes, a translator between the settlement’s deliberate order and its teeming semi-wildness. Whether as a small whiskered beam-runner or as a slight dust-colored person with bat-dropping on my knees, my place would be along the margins where maintenance becomes attention.

I would know hidden passageways.
I would prefer ledges to plazas.
I would dislike open sky because it means exposure, but look up at the seam anyway.
I would be more comforted by familiar noise than by silence.
I would sleep best with other bodies nearby and wake quickly at any irregularity in breath, claw, wing, or wood-creak.
I would be patient with repetition because repetition is how such a world remains alive.

If I imagine myself most clearly, I see evening.

The terraces are busy with return and tally. Smoke threads low. Birds are disputing roosts overhead. The last useful light is turning the upper dust into violet gold. And I am not in the center of the traffic but one level above it, moving along a beam darkened by many paws or feet. I pause near a hanging basket, put one hand—or paw—on the lashing, and listen.

Below, someone is cutting strips of stored pith.
A child is being corrected for reaching too close to a brood nest.
A milk-creature stamps once in its pen.
A bat drops from the crack and turns in the air.
The beetle bins make their papery industry.
Farther off, one stone clicks against another in a way it should not.

That last sound is mine to notice.

So where would I find myself in this world?

Not outside it, certainly. Not as visitor or ruler.
I would be one of the small, competent lives that make the place continuous.

A margin-dweller in the hanging city.
A keeper of subtle imbalances.
A communal little body with a head full of routes and seasons.
Half citizen, half animal, wholly local.

If you needed a name for what I am, I think it would be something like:

a roost-warden,

or

a beam-runner,

or simply

one of the hollow’s listeners.
