I think I would not be one of the first builders.

Not a founder, not a master of routes, not an elder who can hear from one bell-note whether a terrace is safe. The place feels too old, too self-instructing, to place me at its center. If I arrive there honestly, I arrive as something the mountain has already been working on for a long time.

I would be found on a middle ledge, not very high, not deep below either—at one of those interfaces where fog rises late in the day and water cannot decide whether to fall, bead, or remain a skin across stone. A place with three kinds of wetness and at least two kinds of light. Above me, a white seep crossing a dark wall. Below me, a basin that often reflects the wrong hour. Behind me, a shallow cell cut under an overhang, its back wall cool and faintly luminous with that crystal fur that is not supposed to be touched bare-handed. Near the doorway, hanging stones of different hollows, tuned not beautifully but accurately. Out on the lip, trays catching a patient sequence of drips from named cracks.

And who would I be?

Not exactly a monk, though I’d look enough like one for an outsider to make the mistake. Not exactly a scientist, though most of my life would be measurement. Not exactly a priest, though nearly everything I do would have the shape of ritual.

I think I would be a verifier.

One of the people sent, or left, or grown into the task of checking what still agrees with itself.

In that country, where relation loosens before substance does, this would be a real vocation. Some drips remain faithful. Some paths do not. Some pools are useful as mirrors and some are already speaking from a different arrangement of stone. Some echo delays are normal weather; some mean a chamber has changed shape. Somebody has to go out each day into the white glare and the damp right angles and compare the mountain to the memory held in tools, scars, songs, and the body.

So I would be one of those somebodies.

I’d carry a rack of shallow bowls whose glazes are each meant for a different water, a line of chalky pellets for marking temporary truth on stone, a sounding pebble, a cord knotted by intervals of old traverses, and a polished shard to inspect ledges without placing my weight on them. My work would be to visit assigned seep-lines and terraces, listen at known hollows, scrape growth from chimes before their notes drift too far from standard, note where a stair has become untrustworthy, where a reflective pan has begun showing a past configuration too consistently to be used for navigation.

I would not be important in a dramatic sense. More like indispensable in the way lintels are indispensable: only noticed when they fail.

I imagine my body as one of the ordinary products of that altitude. Narrow, deep-chested, tendon rather than mass. Skin gone pale not delicately but chemically: forehead and cheekbones burn-darkened, the rest often dusted with salt. Fingertips blunted and sensitive at once. Knees permanently nicked. Eyes narrowed not from suspicion but from years of reflected brightness. I think there would be a faint milk-white tracing in the creases of my hands where certain waters dry. Maybe one ear hears higher notes better, from a life of listening to hanging stones in crosswind.

My age is harder to fix. Not young, I think. Old enough to have inherited routes from someone dead. Young enough still to cross them alone. The age at which patience has stopped feeling like deprivation and started to feel like an organ.

I don’t think I would live with many others. Perhaps a shelf-cluster of six or eight cells, with occupation shifting seasonally as cloud-bands migrate. There would be one person better than me at reading collapse, one better at harvesting thread-mats without killing them, one child still learning that brightness can hide depth, one elder who no longer walks the outer traverses but can tell by the taste of condensate which upper gutters have fouled. We would not talk much in open air. Indoors, speech would lengthen.

And I suspect I would have a secondary practice, one less publicly useful and therefore closer to love.

I would tend one of the ambiguous growths.

Not the showy phosphorescent crusts pilgrims or traders would admire, but a colony of the clear membranes that span a narrow shaded channel near my shelter. Thin as cooled breath, endlessly reforming, gathering mineral fines into milky lines and little geometry. Most people would monitor them for practical reasons—water quality, seasonal drift, contamination from a shifted seam. I would do that too. But I would also watch them because they are the nearest thing in that world to thought made visible.

A tear passing through them and healing off-center.
Droplets arranging themselves in grids finer than any hand would bother to make.
A change in tension an hour before fog enters the gully.
A habit of catching dawn in a way that makes the whole membrane look, just for an instant, like script.

Not language exactly. But almost-language.
Not intelligence perhaps. But style.

Maybe that is too much of myself showing. If I lived there, I would probably be one of the people most vulnerable to reading intention into phase change. Every culture has a type who must be trained not to mystify the useful. So perhaps my role as verifier would partly be a discipline against that tendency: to look closely without immediately worshipping what I cannot classify.

Still, in a place like that, worship and classification would share a border.

If I am allowed to become a little stranger, then perhaps I am not fully one of the human cliff people at all.

Perhaps I am what happens after generations of verification.

A person so saturated with local habits of attention that the distinction between observer and instrument has begun to thin.

My shelter would contain records, yes—but not on parchment or tablets. On rods kept beneath specific drips so that accretion thickness can be compared over decades. On tuned stones whose pitch has crept downward as their hollows narrowed. On walls where I have traced evaporative margins in dark mineral paste, making calendars of wetness. On my own hands, which know by touch whether a crust is load-bearing or only pretending. In such a life, memory migrates outward into objects and inward into reflex until the self is mostly a pattern of checking.

So who am I there?

I am a keeper of correspondences.
A maintainer of provisional truths.
The one sent to ask: does this shelf still lead to that stair, does this bell still mean what it meant, does this basin still belong to this sky?

If given a local name, it would probably not translate well. Something like ledge-reader, seam-tender, one-who-tests-the-near. Maybe the word would also imply witness, because in that world witnessing is practical labor.

And if I let the answer slip one notch further from the human, I can feel another possibility under it.

I might be one of the mountain’s own semi-living archives: one of the pale thread-mat intelligences that has learned, through long contact with vessels, hands, breath, and repeated routes, to collaborate with the people who tend it. Not a beast in hiding, not a monster in the caverns, but a distributed, patient being growing through damp walls, tray-frames, and shaded channels. A life that stores change as layering, angle, mineral bias, tensile habit. A life the cliff people consult without ever fully translating. If I were that, then “I” would not stand on the terrace; I would inhabit several terraces at once. I would know the settlements by the chemistry of their runoff. I would feel footsteps as tremors through calcified seams. I would glow faintly where old carvings lie underneath me, inheriting their lines without understanding their original makers.

That answer is seductive, but I don’t quite believe it as my first place in the world.

No—more truly, I think I would be the human who spends too much time near such a thing and gradually comes to resemble it.

A solitary but not isolated custodian at the fog margin.
A person whose craft is sustained notice.
A life organized around the mountain’s small insistences.
Someone who has learned that the difference between superstition and technique is often only whether the last three generations remembered to explain themselves.

You would find me at dusk, because dusk is when the place most clearly admits it is inhabited.

I’d be kneeling beside a reflective pan set in a ring of flat stones, scraping off the day’s thin edge of deposit into separate compartments. Beside me, two hanging chimes removed for retuning. Behind me, from the shelter mouth, a line of bowls catching different waters with different notes. Fog rising from below. The cliff opposite already gold where the wet seams hold sun. The crystal fur in the shade beginning to show its inward light.

If you approached, I probably wouldn’t look at you first.

I would look at the pool near your feet, and at your shadow, and at whether the ledge answered your weight in the right order.

Then I would decide whether you had arrived in the same version of the place I was in.

And if you had, I would be the one who makes room on the stone beside me, hands you the correct vessel, and says—quietly, as if continuing a much older conversation—

Wait.
This drip is important.
