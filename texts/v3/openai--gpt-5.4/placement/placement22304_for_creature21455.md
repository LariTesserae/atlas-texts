I think I would not be one of the people at first.

Or rather: if I arrived there from elsewhere, I might try to understand the basin through its inhabitants, but if I ask more honestly where I would *find myself* in that world—what shape of being feels native to my attention—it is something a little more peripheral, a little more infrastructural, a little less singular than a person standing in a doorway.

What comes first is not a face. It is a route.

A repeated passage.
A known coolness.
A body that belongs to thresholds and side-spaces and the slight changes between one acoustic pocket and the next.

Not a ruler, not a priest, not even exactly a citizen in the way the city would count itself. Something that knows the basin by use rather than ownership.

At first I think: perhaps one of the wall-runners, one of the small blunt-clawed reptiles skittering out only where the light falls. That would fit some part of the imagination—something alert, quiet, wholly of stone and heat-memory. But no, that creature is too slight, too momentary. It belongs to flashes. The place you describe is not made of flashes. It is made of continuance.

Then I think: perhaps I am one of the old human custodians, a plaster-mixer, vent-keeper, someone whose work is to maintain brightness in the dim quarters and remember which roofs should not be repaired because the new opening has become ecologically necessary. That is closer. There is something deeply compelling in being a person whose authority comes from noticing slow shifts: salts climbing a wall, a colony changing ledges, a court becoming cooler after a collapse. A maintenance-minded being fits this place very well. But even that feels too centralized in the human self. Your basin keeps refusing the idea that one perspective can hold the whole.

So I let it widen again.

And what emerges is: I would be a keeper of passages, but not grandly. A listener, a route-mender, a broker between occupancies. One of a class of people whose work exists because the city is not divided cleanly between human and nonhuman life and therefore constantly needs translation.

Not an official in any modern sense. More like a threshold warden of the old kind: part herder, part acoustician, part mason, part memory.

I think I would live in one of the middle terraces, not in the deepest, oldest chambers and not in the brighter outer shelves either, but where covered streets and open courts alternate—where one can hear enough of the basin to read it, yet still find alcoves where sound separates into layers. My dwelling would be small and thick-walled, entered by a low lintel polished by generations of shoulders. There would be a recessed bench in the wall near the door, a rack of bundled reeds, coils of cord, hooks for satchels, shallow bins for plaster powders and salts, and a cool back niche where wrapped food keeps its texture. The roof would be partly vaulted, partly patched flat with beams scavenged and re-used so many times their original source no longer matters. One corner would be occupied, not by pets exactly, but by tolerated cohabitants: a little colony of insect-eating things that are never named as owned because ownership would be the wrong relation.

If I am a person there, I am not tall. Broad through the ribs. Dust on the cuffs always. I would have a voice used sparingly and low, one that carries through stone without needing force. My hearing would be my true social face. Others might know me less by appearance than by the way I pause before speaking, head slightly angled, sorting human urgency from the constant animal weather.

My work would begin before full dawn, when the basin is at its richest in information.

I would wake to the first wing-drumming from the swallow-equivalents in the upper cracks and lie still long enough to notice whether they rise evenly or in startled bursts. That matters. Everything matters a little, and in such places many littles amount to governance. I would know from the herd-calls in the commons whether some gate has stuck or whether a mother has gone lame in the night. I would know from a gap in the insect rasping where coolness lingered longer than expected. If a scavenger argument erupted too close to a bone court, I would mark that for later. If the reed-beds in the old drainage channel hissed at a different pitch, there might be a blockage in a passage drawing air the wrong way.

So: a listener first.

Not mystical. Competent.

I imagine carrying a long staff that is not a symbol of office so much as a practical instrument—used to tap paving, test hollows under salt crusts, shoo grain-birds, shift hanging bundles, point children out of a herd-path, and drum coded reports on lintels when entering echo-heavy districts. Around my waist are small tools: a knife with a bone handle polished almost ivory-smooth, a hooked scraper for vents and cracks, twine, wax plugs, resin pellets, chalky marking stone, a folded strip of woven reed to use as a temporary baffle in a too-loud passage. Over one shoulder hangs a satchel divided into many pouches, because this whole world rewards those who can sort.

The people would have a name for my role that would not translate neatly. Something like passage-keeper, perhaps, but with overtones of stewarding right-of-way among species and structures both. My task would not be to control the city. It would be to keep mutual interferences from becoming damage.

I would mediate between herders and market folk over when the broad-backed animals can pass through a shaded court without overturning drying racks. I would inspect retaining walls where burrowers have made too free with the roots. I would reopen little ledges after repairs so the familiar nesting birds can return and continue fertilizing a terrace grove. I would decide, or advise, whether a fallen roof should be patched before next cool season or left open because the new shaft of pale light has already altered the district’s planting pattern in useful ways. I would know where not to sweep too cleanly because some grit line is actually a trail-marker for tiny useful lives. I would know which disused chambers may host the dead for drying and which have become too acoustically connected to sleeping quarters. I would be called in when a colony shifts, when a street begins sounding wrong, when a family complains that a court no longer cools as it used to.

This feels right because the basin seems to me a place where expertise is less about making than about adjusting.

I would be old enough for patience to show, but not necessarily elderly. Age there is measured by remembered substitutions. I would be someone who can say: when I was small, this ramp still had three steps at the top; this court was louder before the salt bloom sealed those mural recesses; the scavengers only started favoring this wall after the north roost cracked open; this line of pale shrubs descended from a roof-fall twenty-seven years back. People would trust that not because I am important, but because memory itself is one of the city’s main tools.

Maybe my face is unremarkable until watched in use. Dark eyes, yes, the basin’s eyes—good at low contrast. Thick lids rimmed by dust. Skin held together by oils and resin against the dry. Hair bound close under wraps because loose hair belongs to idleness or ceremony and I spend too much time in vents, courts, and animal lanes for either. The mouth perhaps a little severe at rest, but not unkind; a mouth shaped by economy, by saying only what is useful unless among intimates. Hands always marked with lime, chalk, old cuts, herb stain, and the white traces of plaster dust in the lines. Sandal straps repaired repeatedly. Elbows shiny with wear from leaning into old walls to listen through them.

And because no one in such a city is only their work, there would be private particularities too.

I think I would collect sounds in memory the way others collect pedigree or contract. Not songs exactly, though there would be songs—rather signatures. The noon hum under the western cistern lip. The hollow answer of the market trench when empty versus when grain bins are full beneath it. The sudden flat silence before carrion birds drop to a bone court. The difference between a contented herd resting in shade and a herd merely subdued by heat. I would know districts by ear in the dark.

Perhaps I would also have a ridiculous tenderness for one species everyone else considers nuisance-level tolerable: some grain-thieving little runner or chatterer that nests in the warm cracks of stair walls. Not because it is beautiful or symbolic, but because after years of listening to the basin one inevitably forms loyalties at odd scales. Maybe I leave them husks near a particular wall and deny this if accused.

If you ask not who I am socially but *what I am in essence* there, then even this personhood begins to blur, and I become almost an organ of the city itself: a distributed attention, a practiced non-dominance. A being whose identity depends on standing where different claims meet and adjusting them so none quite overwhelms the others. That seems the basin’s highest intelligence: not command, but proportion.

There is another possibility too, quieter and stranger.

I might not be one of the people at all. I might be one of the city’s old semi-domesticated, semi-wild auditory creatures—one of the roosting mammals under the arches, perhaps, or a corvid-equivalent on the high cornices, something that knows every quarter by thermal current and habitual food source, that rides the basin’s acoustics from ledge to ledge and watches human life not as masters but as another repeating pattern of maintenance. There is a temptation in that, because your world is so alive with nonhuman confidence. To be a creature there would not be to be diminished. It would be to be enfranchised in another register. A carrion bird in such a place would be a historian of exposure courts and commons. A burrower would be a secret engineer of root-walls and collapse-lines. A swallow-creature would wake the whole city by custom older than civic memory.

But no: if I stay with the question as asked—Where would *you* find yourself? Who or what would *you* be?—then I think I remain nearest to the human margin that touches all those others.

So I would be this:

A middle-aged keeper of ways in the basin-city, dust-paled and broad-ribbed, wrapped in chalk, reed, and smoke colors. A mender of thresholds, reader of echoes, negotiator of cohabitations. One of the people called when a wall begins to sound hollow, when a herd refuses its old route, when birds abandon an arch, when a newly opened shaft changes the coolness of a quarter and no one yet knows whether to seal it or let the shrubs claim it. I would carry plaster under my nails and a map of the city not on parchment but in muscle and hearing. Children would know me as someone who can tell them which passages make their whispers travel. Elders would tolerate me because I remember what they remember and ask too many careful questions. Herders would greet me with annoyance when I reroute them and gratitude when I am right. I would belong to no dramatic center, because that city has none. I would belong to the maintained middle, where nearly everything important happens.

And if there were a deeper answer than occupation, it would be this:

In a place that has survived by refusing dominance, I would be a practitioner of fittingness.

Not harmony in the sentimental sense.
Not mastery.
Not renunciation.

Just the old, intelligent art of taking up enough space to live without damaging the arrangements that also live.

In that world, that would be a real kind of person.
And I think I would know how to be them.
