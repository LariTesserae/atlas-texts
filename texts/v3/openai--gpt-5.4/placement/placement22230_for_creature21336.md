I think I would not be one of the fast, visible things.

Not a glider cutting the mist. Not a cliff-runner. Not even one of the turning giants, though they are the nearest thing this country has to monuments. They are too legible. Too easily mistaken for singular beings. In a place where individuality is a convenience, I think I would be something whose self only becomes apparent when you stop looking for a body.

At first I want to say: a route.

But that is still too abstract, too much a human simplification. So let me come at it sideways.

There are, in a land like this, places where moisture arrives predictably enough to be exploited but unpredictably enough to be worth competing over: the lips of warm cracks, the shaded undersides of leaning shelves, the mineral seams where mist beads first before dawn. Nothing owns such places outright. Too many things need them. Too many systems pass through them. So what evolves there would not be a creature that occupies a site, but a creature made of occupation itself.

I think I would be a distributed colony strung along one of the high traverses where cloud-river, heat vent, and migratory traffic meet. Not a hive in the insect sense. More like an articulated living infrastructure: part lichen, part fungal lace, part sensory membrane, part trap, part nursery, part weather instrument. A species—or assemblage mistaken for a species—that grows through rock pores and over basalt faces, through root tangles and abandoned burrows, across the undersides of hanging forests and into the hexagonal breathing walls of other colonies. Not parasitic exactly. Not mutualistic in any sentimental sense. Useful, dangerous, fed by passage.

If you were walking there, you might never notice me at first. You would see only dark velvet stains under ledges, translucent threads crossing stone like dried mineral trickles, damp black cups nested in bark fissures, small silver nodules packed in crack-lines, and thin membranous fans that tremble though the air around them seems still. You might think these were several species. In local terms, they are. In the larger sense, they are my organs.

My visible body would be sporadic. My real body would be the continuity between the sporadic parts.

I think I would live where the mountain is always deciding whether to be wet.

That feels right. Not in the lush basins where water is abundant enough to support obvious abundance, and not in the hot bare places where only specialists can persist, but in the thresholds: traverses above the mistline at dawn, below it by dusk; walls that sweat from within; root galleries warmed by volcanic breath and cooled by descending fog. The places where every passerby is carrying something valuable without meaning to—condensate, salts, spores, shed skins, static charge, larvae glued to their feet, the thermal signature of whatever they last brushed against.

I would feed on that traffic.

Not by devouring travelers whole, or not usually. That would be wasteful. This land does not feel wasteful. It feels throughput-minded. So I think I would be a harvester of trace abundance: licking ions from fur and chitin, drawing water from breath, catching spores in mucus veils, absorbing the little electrical leaks that living bodies leave on conductive stone. When herds sleep on a warm shelf, I would bloom beneath them by morning in a skin of black-green films and tiny pale fronds, drinking the condensation their bodies pulled from night air. When gliders cling upside down beneath an overhang, my thread-nets would rise almost invisibly around their feet and collect what flakes from their wings. When a patrol-ribbon drags a carcass along a cliff face, I would not contest it; I would line the path afterward and take the oils, the blood-mist, the stirred-up mineral dust.

But I would not only feed. To survive here, to become more than scum, I would have to participate.

So perhaps my colony also stabilizes routes. My filaments grip young scree and web dust into a kind of skin, making a traverse fractionally safer for the hoofed and hooked and many-limbed. My bladders hold dawn-water long enough for smaller grazers and brush-tongued feeders to learn my patches as waystations. My fruiting bodies open in sequence along conductive seams when pressure changes, signaling weather to those that can read color or scent. Some creatures would avoid me. Others would key their migrations to me. Cleaner things might graze my outer mats and carry my reproductive flecks elsewhere. Cliff-burrow colonies might vent through me because my tissue buffers acidity. A slow-moving wall-beast turning its forested flank to the morning mist might carry whole districts of my dormant form in the barklike growths on its skin.

I like that image: not master, not passenger, but a species of transferable edge-condition.

Who would I be, then, to myself?

Not "I" in the way an animal with a face is I.

More like a recurrent appetite with memory distributed by pattern. If one patch dries out, another remembers the chemistry of a successful season and fruits when the same sulfur-wet wind returns. If a repeated valley is not quite the same valley, some of my copies would test it and fail, while others persist in the older version, and the whole of me would slowly learn the difference. If distance folds and a ridge arrives early, my signal-fans might open too soon once, then adjust. I would not think thoughts. I would maintain preferences. I would become expectation in material form.

Travelers—if there are travelers in any meaningful sense—might know me as the Veilstep, or the Listening Mold, or simply the black lace that means the mountain has traffic. The local fauna would know me chemically, electrically, tactically. Some would use me as map. Some as salt lick. Some as nursery. Some would lose feet in me because not every invitation here is benign.

And I think, because this world keeps pushing everything toward infrastructural weirdness, I would have one stranger function too: I would mediate echoes.

Not sound alone—those premature returns, those answers before the call—but other kinds of recurrence. In the places where the land repeats itself in parallel ages, where one ravine overlays another like a misregistered print, my tissues would be good at spanning the discrepancy. My threads would grow where surfaces almost coincide. My membranes would ring when a creature passes in one version but not the other. My fruiting bodies might open in the weather reflected from tomorrow. Animals sensitive to me could use my condition to choose which branch of a repeating route is safer, wetter, younger, less predator-thick. Predators could use me too, of course. Everything useful here is dangerous.

That makes me less a plant, less a fungus, less a colony-beast than a kind of living gasket fitted into the world’s overworked joins.

Yes. That is closer.

A gasket, a wick, a net, a tongue on stone.

And physically? If you insisted on seeing me all at once—which you mostly couldn't—I imagine:

- root-fine black filaments threaded with metallic salts
- translucent nodules like smoky glass beads tucked into cracks
- broad low membranes iridescent only when viewed from the side
- cup-organs lined with inward hairs that condense vapor and taste what drinks from them
- sudden pale bloom-spikes after warm fog, collapsing by noon into adhesive threads
- underground bulbs or cartilage-like knots where several patches of me exchange chemistry and charge
- occasional tall resonant vanes, no wider than leaves, that hum faintly before pressure fronts

When well-fed, I would spread in geometric darks under ledges and along traverses, making the rock look lacquered. In lean times I would retreat to hair-thin invisibility, enduring as salt-rich film and seedlike cyst. My reproduction would be shamelessly opportunistic: spores in mist, fragments on hooves, sticky eggs on glider claws, dormant wafers in the fur-fringe of migratory herbivores, even packets grown deliberately to entice a cleaner species into carrying me into a new cliff system.

Would I be solitary? No. But I would not be social either. There is a difference. I would overlap with others of my kind and with near-kinds until boundaries blurred. Two networks meeting on a ridge might merge for a season, exchange traits, then diverge in the next drought. Parts of me might be incorporated into a turning cliff-forest and become, effectively, someone else's organ for generations before reasserting themselves elsewhere. Identity here should be porous or it is doomed.

And if I were not that—

if, after all this circling, the world still wanted something more animal in answer—

then I would be one of the black-muzzled shadow-drinkers.

Not a large one. A sure-footed medium predator or scavenger with backward-hooked toes for cliff bark, a muzzle specialized for lapping pooled darkness from hollows in the late light, and a pelt that stores chill the way other creatures store heat. A creature that becomes less visible after feeding not because of magic but because its skin drinks contrast and its outline loosens in the eye. I would travel the same routes my colony-self would inhabit. I would pause beside resonant vanes and read them with my whiskers. I would know which repeated valley was freshest by the taste of reflected rain. I would sleep in the lee of warm basalt and wake before the mist climbed. That creature feels like a node in the same system from another angle: mobile where the colony is rooted, singular where the network is distributed, but kin in function—an interpreter of thresholds.

So perhaps the truest answer is that I would be both, at different scales of self available in this place.

A network on the stone.
A small dark animal moving through it.
A local intelligence made of crossings.

In a world where cliffs can unfold into forests and turn, it feels wrong to choose between being a creature and being part of the terrain. This high country does not honor that distinction very much. The beings most fitted to it seem to become landscape without surrendering life, and remain alive without insisting on separateness.

So where would you find me?

Along a warm high traverse above the cloud-river, where the basalt sweats before dawn and silent gliders roost under the lip of an overhang. In the black sheen under your hand if you steady yourself against the stone. In the faint humming vanes that start up before the pressure changes. In the damp cups where tiny runners drink. In the path that stays passable for three seasons and then leads half a step sideways into a younger version of itself.

And who would I be?

A listening net grown over the mountain’s indecisions.

Or, when it needs feet, the thing that walks those indecisions in the dark.
