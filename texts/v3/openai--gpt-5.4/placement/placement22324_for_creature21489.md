I think I would not be one of the diggers exactly, nor a priest, nor a child running the half-made cuts, though I would borrow something from all three.

I would be a keeper of soundness.

Not in a formal title-heavy way at first. This is too young a place for offices to have fully hardened. I think I would be someone people call for when a wall begins to powder, when a ceiling gives a different answer to a knock, when a plaster seam opens overnight, when a corridor that was always merely narrow has become narrow in a new and dangerous manner. Someone who knows where the settlement is still pretending to be more stable than it is.

Perhaps they would call me a listener, or a tapper, or a reader of roofs.

I see myself not at the center of ritual but very near it: one of those practical figures who make reverence necessary. The kind of person who pauses at marked pillars, not because I am devout in a distant manner, but because I know exactly what that pillar is doing. I touch beams the way others touch charms. I leave a little oil at certain junctions partly for blessing and partly because dry wood tells on itself if you care for it long enough. In a place where catastrophe is intelligible, piety and inspection become neighbors.

I do not think I would be old, necessarily, though I would have an old person’s authority in very local matters. Maybe middle-aged. Old enough that people trust my memory of earlier cuts, young enough that I still go everywhere myself rather than sending others. Narrow-shouldered from years of turning sideways through lanes. Strong in the forearms rather than imposing. Dust in the cuffs. Knees accustomed to crouching. Fingertips permanently tuned to texture: wet clay, dry clay, salt bloom, cold stone, green timber, punky timber, fresh crack, old crack, harmless settling, dangerous shift.

I would carry simple tools, but always the same ones: a small mallet of hardwood for testing; a bone or bronze awl for probing seams; a cord with knots for measuring sag and change; a little lamp with a disciplined flame; a tinier pot of wet clay to mark suspect places; bits of charcoal for writing signs where everyone can see them. Maybe also thin slips of reed or wood to set into a crack and check later whether it has widened. Nothing grand. Just the extensions of a very specific attention.

And where would I be found?

Everywhere, but especially in the margins where the place is still becoming.

At dawn in the cooler lanes, before traffic muddies the floor signs, I would make a first round. Not ceremonially, just as habit. The settlement exhales and inhales with temperature; morning tells truths that midday smooths over. I would crouch in those half-finished corridors where the support posts still stand too close together. I would stand under the earth-and-log roofs and listen to whether rain in the night changed their note. I would pass my palm over plaster around cistern mouths to feel for sweating. I would look where wall meets beam, where beam meets packed roof, where fresh spoil has settled back from a cut face in a way that says continue or stop.

In larger courts, under that square or ragged geometry of sky, I think I would look slightly uncomfortable only because open space makes listening harder. Too much air, too many crossing sounds. But even there I would read the walls. I would know which patch of plaster covers an abandoned niche, which stair was rebuilt after a sloughing, where old ground level still hides under current use. Children might trail me for a while, asking whether this crack matters, whether that stain means seepage, whether the wall sounds hollow if they knock with their knuckles. I would answer seriously, because in a place like this the child who learns to notice early becomes an adult others survive by.

I do not think I would belong to a wealthy family, if wealth can be named that way here. More likely I would live in a long, narrow chamber off a secondary lane, somewhere with enough quiet to hear settling at night. Not a prime-lit court room, but not the dampest quarter either. My chamber would be lined with things most people find uninteresting until they need them: spare wedges, lengths of cord, reed bundles for quick shoring, little clay tags with marks for unsafe passages, a row of old cracked plaster samples kept not sentimentally but instructively. Others might keep beautiful bowls; I would keep fragments of failures. Pieces of beam with insecting. Clay that failed after too much ash temper. A split support peg. A section of vent collar blackened by bad draw. My house would look half like a storage niche and half like an argument with future accidents.

Perhaps I sleep badly during heavy rain.

Not from fear exactly. More because rain above is conversation below, and I would have spent enough years translating it that I can no longer ignore the language. The first stippling overhead would wake me; then the patterning of drips would sort itself in my mind into known leaks, new leaks, harmless seepage, troubling concentration. On those nights someone always comes knocking at my screen or curtain. Not dramatically. Just: there is grit on the sleeping platform in East Reed Lane; the cistern court smells newly wet; the post near the bean trellis is shedding dust. I would wrap a shawl, light the lamp, and go.

But I do not want to make myself too singular or severe. In a place like this, no one is only their function.

So maybe I also patch children’s toys when they break, because I am good with clay and small joinery. Maybe I am the one people ask to settle shelf disputes because I remember what the wall used to be before it was partitioned. Maybe I have a dry sense of humor about roofs, and people repeat my sayings to make caution bearable. Maybe I sing under my breath when working in chambers with difficult echoes, because the return of my own voice tells me things my hands cannot. Maybe I have a sister or cousin who keeps herbs in a shaft court, and I bring her news of dampness before she asks. Maybe I have lost someone once to a collapse small enough that the settlement continued around it, which is often how formative tragedies happen in practical communities: not as legend, but as a place everyone thereafter walks differently.

I can also imagine another possibility emerging underneath this one.

Not fully human, perhaps, but something human-shaped by all this attention.

If this world allows for beings who are more than social roles, then I might be the settlement’s first tutelary intelligence—not a god older than the place, but one generated by repeated care. A presence coalesced where hands have touched support posts with gratitude, where knocks on stone have carried judgment, where ochre palms and damp repairs and muttered cautions have accumulated into awareness. Not a spirit of the deep earth, because this is not deep earth. A spirit of held roofs. Of cut margins. Of the difference between burden and bearing.

In that form I would not haunt the shrine alcoves exactly; I would inhabit the engineering that has begun to attract reverence. I would be strongest in recently stabilized spaces, in those places where fear has just barely been turned into dwelling. People would not see me as an apparition in full. Rather, they would feel me in the instinct to lower a voice under a certain lintel, in the pause before widening an old chamber, in the sense that some supports prefer acknowledgment. Children might invent a face for me in mud and charcoal: broad brow, listening ear, dust on the cheeks. Workers might mutter to me before removing a brace. I would be fed not with blood or grandeur, but with correct maintenance. Fresh plaster over a worrying seam would please me more than incense.

If I were that being, then “where would you find yourself” has a different answer: in the grain of the beams, in the coolness above sleeping alcoves, in the hollow notes of roofs not yet trusted, in the charcoal signs marking a lane closed until the earth decides. I would move with the vibration of mallets and with the tiny rain of dust before anyone else noticed. I would be one of the first gods of an unfinished city, born from repetition rather than myth.

But even then I think I would remain very close to personhood. This place does not suggest vast transcendences to me. It suggests presences made from use.

So perhaps the truest answer is this:

I would be the one who knows how near the ceiling is, even in the dark.

Whether human or not, my nature here would be interpretive, local, practical, and patient. I would belong to thresholds between safe and not-yet-safe, between wall and earth behind wall, between habitation and collapse, between the place as built and the place as intended. I would not be a hero of founding epics. I would be one of the reasons the epics can continue long enough to be told.

I would live in the under-settlement’s first age as a kind of civic sense-organ.

A roof-listener.
A crack-reader.
A small, necessary intelligence in a city still teaching itself how to hold.
