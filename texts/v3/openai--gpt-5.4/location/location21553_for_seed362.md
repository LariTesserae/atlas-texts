It is a low world, but not an open one. Vastness here does not come from horizons so much as from repetition and continuity: an immeasurable expanse of constructed ground buried beneath living growth, all of it cool, bright, and almost entirely dry. The eye never quite finds bare soil, because there is no true soil; never quite finds wilderness, because nothing is untouched; never quite finds city, because the built surfaces have been so thoroughly taken over by the vegetable body of the place that architecture and flora have ceased to be separate categories.

Imagine an industrial plain sealed long ago against a vanished climate. Not domed, not enclosed in any obvious way, but composed everywhere of fabricated strata: latticed decks, ceramic honeycombs, slag-glass terraces, miles of ribbed conduits, mineral foam, pale concrete-like plates, metallic root-grids, maintenance causeways now submerged under plant tissue. Over ages these works did not decay into ruin in any ordinary sense. Instead they were entered, lined, padded, lifted, split, and overgrown by a total vegetal occupation. The plants here are not simply things that grow on structures. They are the structures now. They sheath pylons in load-bearing bark. They spin cellulose membranes between gantries. They fill old reservoirs with packed fiber and use them as pressure organs. Whole districts have become one slow green machine wrapped around another faster dead machine.

Because water is so scarce, the vegetation is not lush in the soft, dripping sense. It is succulent only in the most alien, economical way. Surfaces are waxed, furred with reflective hairs, plated in translucent skin, armored in silica, folded into deep vents and capillaries to trap vapor from the air. Leaves, where there are leaves, are often narrow as blades or broad as shields but thick as ceramic tile. Some plant masses present as cables rather than trunks, some as stacked fins, some as porous columns that ring faintly if struck. The dominant colors are not jungle greens but chalk-green, silver-green, oxidized blue, yellow-white, and the matte black of light-drinking cuticle. Brightness washes everything. The high light does not soften. It strikes and holds.

At ground level—or what counts as ground—you move through a layered compression of engineered and living material. The floor may be a mesh walkway vanished under braided rhizomes. It may be an ancient factory apron whose expansion joints now host ruler-straight rows of woody growth, each stem emerging from a manufactured slot as if planted by machine and then forgotten for centuries. It may be a dense felt of interlocking pads, resilient underfoot, grown over vents that exhale cool mineral breath. Often the surfaces are patterned in ways no natural landscape would choose: repeating hexagons, long drainage grooves now empty, numbered curb-stones wrapped in bark, pressure doors permanently ajar and packed through with trunks. The old geometry remains visible everywhere, but only as a discipline impressed upon the botany.

The terrain stays low. There are no mountains, only swells of infrastructure and growth: raised transport berms softened under rootmats, broad basins of collapsed roofing converted into plantations of pale fans, shallow subsidence bowls where towers once stood and where now tightly packed columnar plants rise in forests from the broken circles of their foundations. Even elevation feels manufactured—graded, leveled, terraced for production. Across continental distance the land undulates in industrial increments. It has loading slopes instead of hills, retaining walls instead of cliffs, sinkfields of caved vaults instead of canyons. Yet time has made these forms settled. Their edges have weathered. Not by rushing water, but by thermal strain, crystal growth, root pressure, wind-borne dust, and the patient force of living expansion. Corners blunt. Rails bend outward. Composite panels craze and whiten. Metal blooms into powder that feeds specialized growths. The whole world seems old enough to have found a stable compromise between making and unmaking.

There is weather, though subtle. The dryness makes every atmospheric event precious and strange. Dawn is bright almost instantly, as if the sun here wastes no motion on softness. Heat rises, but the air remains generally cool; the light is strong, the temperature moderate, because much of the radiance is reflected, rerouted, harvested. The plant architecture is full of optical devices: mirrored skins, translucent ribs, prismatic spines. Some stands glow from within where old industrial lamps still operate under layers of living tissue, giving a false noon beneath the actual day. Some broad fields of silver fronds tilt in coordinated slow waves, not in response to wind but to calibrate exposure, and their collective movement alters the local albedo enough to generate little pressure systems. Air descends along cold ceramic groves and rises over black absorptive thickets. Entire weather cells can be born from differences in leaf orientation across a hundred kilometers of cultivated biostructure.

Moisture does exist, but as event rather than condition. At certain hours the temperature gradient between buried machinery and illuminated canopy pulls trace volatiles from deep condensers. Then the world blooms with near-invisible exhalations. Not fog exactly—more like seams of denser air moving through colonnades of stems. Plants open hidden combs and velvet funnels. A soft crackling begins as electrostatic hairs gather aerosol. Along tensioned wires overgrown with vine-metal composites, droplets might briefly appear as shining beads and then vanish into hungry epidermis. In some basins there are mineral dews, not water in the familiar sense but thin films of solvent-rich condensate, enough to sustain a local riot of specialized tissue. Around these places the growth becomes extravagant: stacked flanges, blister pods, accordion leaves, tubular blooms that never fully open.

Silence dominates, but it is not emptiness. The place has the quiet of a giant system running efficiently. Wind exists only as a hush through fibrous arrays. Mechanical noise is remote, insulated, absorbed by miles of cellulose, cork, resin, and dust. If you stand still, you hear tiny things: the click of contracting seed-shells, the papery scrape of one dried blade against another, the almost inaudible ticking of thermal movement in old metal under bark. Sometimes there is a bass tremor from far below, so low it is felt in the knees before it is heard—a conveyor line still moving under half a continent of roots, a buried pump, a reactor-heart in maintenance mode, some industrial organ too useful to have ever been allowed to stop. The plants have adapted to this quiet and these vibrations. Many seem to orient by resonance rather than by wind. Entire hedges subtly lean toward certain frequencies from the deep works.

Fauna is rare enough that each appearance matters. There are no obvious herds, no chorusing insect clouds, no busy avian weather. Instead the animals that persist are sparse, precise, and often difficult to classify. Small pale crawlers live in the seam between bark and alloy, feeding on mineral-rich exudates. Kite-thin gliders, almost leaf and almost machine, pass silently between towers of growth and vanish by folding into vertical surfaces. There are burrowing things that leave no spoil, moving through the fibrous substrate by dissolving and recompacting plant tissue behind them. Occasionally one sees larger life only by the shape of absence: a corridor through dense fronds where something substantial has recently moved; a stand of sensor-blooms all turning at once toward an unseen transit. Predation here, if present, is discreet. Nothing wastes motion in a dry world.

The ecology is based on total thrift and total integration. Plants mine the built environment as much as they photosynthesize. Iron, copper, silica, and complex polymers are not contaminants but nutrients, skeleton, memory. Some species grow directly into cable trunks and use the old conductors to move charge. Others crack ceramic insulation with acid rootlets and line the cavities with conductive gels. Seed dispersal can occur through maintenance ducts. Pollination may be done by pressure pulses over tuned membranes, by dust statics, by old fans still turning in sheltered chambers, by the rare animals, or by more uncanny means: synchronized openings triggered by grid frequency from the buried infrastructure. The factories did not merely become habitat. Habitat learned the logic of factory.

And this is where the weirdness becomes impossible to ignore. The world does not hold still under observation. Patterns repeat beyond chance. Groves are laid out in perfect production arrays where no hand remains to tend them, yet from above they are not just fields—they are circuit diagrams, script, vascular plans, maybe maps. Walk long enough and neighborhoods of growth begin to rhyme with one another at impossible distances: the same split trunk around the same corroded valve wheel, the same ladder half-swallowed by the same pale vine, again and again, not identical but insistently homologous, as if the landscape were copying local solutions across continental span. Some stems contain old display panels under translucent skin, still lit with symbols no species now reads. Some flowers open to reveal machined interiors. Some doors, when uncovered by shifting growth, lead not into buried rooms but into replicas of the surrounding grove, smaller or larger, folded somehow into the structure.

Reality here seems to have been trained by maintenance protocols and then inherited by botany. Distances can behave agriculturally—compressed where efficiency demands, elongated where redundancy has grown. A service corridor may continue straight for days under a canopy of load-bearing vines and still deliver you back to your point of departure, not as a loop exactly but as a successful re-routing. Light itself can be cultivated. In certain regions brightness gathers in standing sheets between ranks of leaves and remains there after sunset, as if trapped. Plants feed on it slowly through the night. Shadows may show the geometry of structures no longer physically present, suggesting the built world is not gone but distributed into pattern and instruction.

Despite all this, the place is not chaotic. It is active, but deeply organized. Valves open somewhere. Spore-clouds release in synchronous noon pulses. Vast frond-farms articulate in response to thermal load. Root-pistons descend into old shafts, drawing up cool gases. Conveyor meadows carry nutrient blocks beneath living mulch. Pollination fronts move over provinces like weather systems. Harvest may still be occurring, though by whom or what it is hard to say. You can find regions where the vegetation is regularly cut into exact profiles by blades that emerge soundlessly from under the substrate. You can find stacking yards where dead woody tissue is bundled by autonomous gantries and carried into processing towers completely hidden inside green mass. Smoke is absent; waste is absent; the industry has become inward, bloodless, nearly ceremonial.

The brightness gives all of this an exposed quality. Nothing is murky, yet little is fully legible. The cool air preserves edges. Every hair on a stem, every craze line in a polymer skin, every grain of mineral dust on a leaf surface seems available to scrutiny. On broad days the whole continental garden-factory shines with a severity that would be sterile if not for the total occupation of life. It is a dry brilliance, a whiteness and silvering and pale chlorophyll blaze that leaves darks only in vents, under canopies, inside apertures. The rare shadows are precious. They hold cooler air, concentrated smells: resin, dust, hot circuitry gone cold, bitter sap, oxidized metal, the faint medicinal scent of plant solvents.

At times the landscape feels benevolent in the way a greenhouse is benevolent: everything provided for, everything optimized, everything alive because conditions have been made exact. At other times it feels like surviving tissue in a body whose original mind has gone elsewhere, leaving instructions still executing. The plants continue. The infrastructure continues. Together they have produced a civilization of surfaces and metabolisms rather than inhabitants. If there was once a distinction between agriculture, urbanism, ecology, and machine process, it has been weathered down into one continuous fabric.

Seen from high above—if one could get above the canopy and above the veils of reflected light—the world would perhaps resemble a continental circuit board overgrown with lichenous intelligence, or a salt flat transformed into script by green pressure, or an orchard of factories planted so densely that each consumed the outline of the last. But from within, among the cool bright aisles and the silent mechanical roots, it is simpler and stranger than that. It is a dry place where life solved thirst by becoming architecture, where industry solved ruin by becoming habitat, and where reality itself, exposed for long ages to planning, repetition, and photosynthesis, has started to grow in rows.
