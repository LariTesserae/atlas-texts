At first it seems impossible to call it a landscape, because almost nothing here behaves like ground in the ordinary sense. There is no bare earth, no exposed rock except where something has failed and peeled back, no river, no open reservoir, no familiar horizon line of soil meeting sky. The place is built all the way through, but what it is built *from* is vegetal: not a forest overtaking ruins, and not a greenhouse scaled up to geography, but an old industrial plateau whose every surface has been given over to engineered plant matter until the distinction between factory, city, field, and organism has become tedious to insist on.

It lies at a height where the air is thin enough to feel used already. Heat does not rise off it in the lively shimmer of a wet tropic; it presses downward and radiates outward from stored mass. The whole region has been cooking for so long that sharpness has gone out of it. Its structures are ancient not because they are crumbling into picturesque collapse, but because they have passed through collapse long ago and arrived at a durable late condition: warped, polished, furred over, reinforced by growth, abraded by centuries of dry movement. Things that once had purpose now have habit. Conduits run because they have always run. Root-columns brace gantries whose original loads are forgotten. Vent stacks exhale slowly into darkness because some buried thermal gradient still demands exchange.

From a distance—if one can get enough distance in such a crowded world—the region resembles a blackened hanging garden spread over terraces and low industrial ridges. The elevation is not mountainous. It is a high basin, or a sequence of uplifted tables and saddles, broad enough to feel open but never empty. Every depression is occupied. Every wall supports another layer. Every span carries cables, creepers, latticework, folded membranes, seed-vats, chimneys, old rails, husks of service towers, and dense sheaves of leafless photosynthetic vanes tuned to a star that almost never directly reaches them.

That darkness is important. The place is not lightless, but it is starved of broad illumination. A permanent roof of smoke, spore haze, heat-born convection, and industrial vapor hangs overhead like a second terrain. Above that, perhaps, is a sky. Below it, the world is all ember-glow, maintenance phosphors, bioluminal sap-lines, furnace windows, pulse-lamps, and the dull red afterimage held in metal that has spent entire eras hot. The plant life does not spread green in this dimness. It has gone elsewhere in color. Vast mats of matte charcoal fronds drink the faintest wavelengths. Ribs of purple-black tissue arch between towers. Coppery bladders swell from pipework and slowly contract, pumping gases instead of fluids. Pale root felt spreads over walls in dry filigree, drawing trace minerals and condensed vapors from the air. Some surfaces are velvety with soot-lichen; some are plated in hard cellulose tiles like kiln-fired bark. In sheltered slots, translucent sheets of leaf-tissue stretch taut across frames, collecting warmth and sparse light while trembling with internal capillary motion, though capillary is the wrong old water-word for what moves there.

There is almost no free water anywhere. The chemistry of life here has adapted toward oils, waxes, superheated gels, volatile solvents, and mineral slurries. Moisture exists mainly as rumor: trapped in pressure bladders, bound in resin, stolen as condensate from night-cooling metal, or manufactured deep below and consumed before it can escape. Cut one of the larger vine-trunks and it does not bleed; it sweats an amber vapor and extrudes a syrup that hardens at once into a sealing crust. Some of the great reservoir-plants hold not water but reactive salts in compartmented chambers, opening and closing valves in response to temperature and current. Others breathe in the industrial effluvia the old works still produce—sulfur traces, metallic dusts, warm carbon-rich exhaust—and fix them into flesh. They are less descendants of plants than redefinitions of what a plant can be when dryness is the universal law.

And yet it is lush. Oppressively, triumphantly lush.

Walkways, if they can still be called walkways, run through tunnels of braided stem-cable and structural wood extruded in perfect right angles. Overhead, growth and manufacture have collaborated for so many generations that one cannot tell whether a beam was rolled in a foundry or grown in a pressure mold. There are groves of load-bearing trunks, each one rising from a socket in the deck plating, faceted by design for maximum strength, their surfaces banded with old repair grafts and iron staples now swallowed by tissue. Between them stretch catwalks furred with felt-roots. Below them are lower decks where heat accumulates and the dark is redder. Above them are suspended orchards of thermal organs: folded, fin-like leaves that open at dusk to radiate excess heat and close at dawnless intervals when the convection storms begin.

Because the place is turbulent. Not noisy in the way of a bustling city full of voices and traffic—there are no animals, no crowds, no chatter—but dynamically restless in every other sense. Heat columns rise unpredictably from grates and sinkholes, carrying ash, seed dust, and dry leaf fragments in twisting sheets. Membrane canopies inflate suddenly as pressure changes through the vent network, then collapse with a long papery sigh. Entire walls of climber growth can tense and release when internal pumps reverse. Industrial systems still cycle in buried layers: pistons somewhere deep produce a slow periodic shudder; belts move in hidden corridors; thermal shutters clang open and shut hours apart; old turbines, fed by gradients no one now measures, spin up briefly and then coast for days. The vegetation has evolved around these rhythms and also into them. Pollination, insofar as the word still applies, is mechanical, barometric, electrostatic. Seed casings burst when vibration frequencies align with factory resonance. Pollen drifts not on insect traffic but on hot exhaust and eddying dust devils under the roof haze.

Storms here are made of dry matter. They come as regional convulsions in the overhead atmosphere: smoke-thickening, pressure drops, sudden static discharges that crawl blue-white over cable groves and make whole thickets of carbon leaves glow at the edges. Then the upper canopy lashes. Fronds turn their armored backs. Dust rises from every shelf and ledge. Lightweight husks tear free and go bowling through the air. Spore sheets unroll by the thousands from hidden seams and are shredded into glittering black confetti. Vent chimneys begin to moan softly, not loudly, but with a tonal insistence that enters the bones. For an hour or a day the plateau is all movement, every layer responding to every other: shutters snapping, stems flexing, braces creaking, brittle growth breaking, new tendrils instantly seeking the exposed anchors. Afterward the place settles into a slightly different arrangement, as if it had turned in sleep.

Its age shows in the smoothness of repeated solutions. Erosion here is not rain-carving or river-cutting; it is abrasion by dust, thermal fatigue, chemical bloom, root pressure, and long patience. Corners have become rounded under the combined work of expansion and growth. Stairs sag into ramps under accumulated tissue. Window apertures are sealed with successive generations of translucent bark. Exposed metals have either oxidized into velvet-black passivity or been overgrown by conductive vines that use them as nerves. Ancient signage survives only as embossed ghosts beneath plant lacquer. Whole buildings have settled into low mounds because the surrounding growth found more efficient geometries than the original architecture and simply buried it alive, preserving only vents, access shafts, and the occasional tower top protruding like the mast of a wreck in a dark sea of leaves.

The sounds are spare enough that each acquires scale. A chain somewhere ticks as it cools. Seedpods crack one by one in the heat. There is a continual dry whisper of surfaces touching: leaf against cable, fiber against duct, dust against membrane. Far below, an engine or pump maintains a nearly subaudible hum that might be mistaken for the body's own hearing until it stops and the silence becomes larger. At intervals there is a heavier report—the dropping of a hardened fruit-shell onto metal, the snapping of an overstressed support vine, the delayed slam of a heat-warped gate. Much of the region listens to itself. Quiet dominates, but it is a worked quiet, full of suspended processes.

Without animals, the ecology does not make the familiar bargains of flower and pollinator, fruit and grazer, predator and prey. Competition is architectural and chemical. Plants overtop, shade, brace, poison, insulate, perforate, and redirect one another. Some species spread as insulating carpets across hot machinery, moderating temperatures and making other growth possible. Others are stranglers in a literal mechanical sense, wrapping around moving shafts to harvest frictional heat and eventually stalling them, after which an entirely different community colonizes the stillness. There are chimney-plants whose roots descend into warm exhaust stacks and whose crowns plume the openings with heat-exchanging fans. There are cable creepers that sheath electrical lines in segmented tissues, tapping tiny currents through conductive sap salts and glowing faintly in return. There are quarry succulents with bodies of layered ceramic cellulose that crack open after decades to release mineral-rich dust for their own seedlings. There are immense raft-mats suspended on trusswork, woven from mutually dependent species so interlocked that no individual can be said to begin or end.

Some areas read as avenues, some as plantations, some as process halls. In one district the old industrial order still shows clearly: parallel bays, repeating support frames, linear conveyors now thick as avenues with rooted mass. Here the vegetation respects the geometry, using it as trellis and skeleton, producing corridors where one can see a surprising distance under arches of black leaf and copper tendon. In another district the built substrate has almost disappeared under bulbous accumulations of growth. Great domes of layered tissue rise from fused rooftops, each dome ridged with cooling fins and stitched with maintenance ladders that vanish into vegetative folds. They look fungal at first glance, but their insides are partitioned greenhouses of dry chemistry, seed foundries, heat incubators, and gas bladders. Elsewhere there are vertical yards of stacked planters the size of apartment blocks, all connected by pipes, roots, and service gantries, as if someone once intended to farm the air itself and succeeded too well.

The rules of the place become apparent slowly.

Nothing exposed remains inert for long. If a surface can hold heat, catch dust, or trap a condensate film, something will root there.

No void is tolerated. Gaps are crossed by tendrils, cables, chains, membranes, scaffoldings, or all at once. Open space is merely growth not yet arrived.

Energy is more valuable than matter. Matter is everywhere in stored form—dead fibers, old walls, mineral dust—but usable gradients of heat, charge, pressure, and light are what shape territory. The richest ecologies cluster around vents, transformers, warm shafts, kiln mouths, and the upper ribs where darkness thins slightly.

Decay is incorporation, not disappearance. When structures fail, they become substrate. When tissues die, they petrify, carbonize, laminate, or are extruded through by successors. Nothing here rots in the damp earthly way; it cures, flakes, powders, and is woven back in.

Movement belongs to systems rather than creatures. Valves open, shutters rotate, leaves furl, girders flex, belts creep, seed fogs travel, pressure fronts pass. The region is animate without possessing animals.

Darkness is not absence but medium. Organisms here are arranged around gradients of dimness the way coral reefs might organize around depth. Some species require the near-black underdecks and die if exposed to furnace glow; others spread broad absorptive fans only in the ashy twilight near the roofline. There are whole ecotones defined by whether red light reaches them.

The strangeness of it is not flamboyant. It does not wave impossible colors and demand astonishment. It becomes stranger the longer one looks because every category begins to fail politely. A hedge is also a heat sink. A warehouse wall is alive and conducting current. A tree is a support column with growth rings of maintenance eras rather than seasons. A garden is a refinery. A field is a lung. A ruin is a nursery. The oldest specimens are not the biggest but the most integrated—organisms whose tissues pass through six different material regimes, whose roots are anchored in concrete, slag, ceramic media, and older rootwood all at once, whose upper crowns are clipped regularly by automatic shears that no one services anymore but which still wake when charge accumulates in buried capacitors.

If there is weather beyond the dry storms, it is mostly thermal. The plateau cycles through periods of brutal radiant heat when every black surface seems to drink and then emit fire, and intervals of comparative cool when the upper haze thickens and the whole region exhales. During these cooler phases, the plants become more active. Valves iris open. Spore lanterns swell. Certain broad fronds unfurl mirror-pale undersides to catch what little diffuse light filters through. Fine root hairs creep from cracks in audible time, questing across metal. Resin towers soften and release built-up gases in muted musical tones. In the hottest hours, everything contracts into hardness. Leaves edgewise themselves. Vents dominate. The place survives by refusing exposure.

There may once have been people here; the architecture insists on former intention. Door sizes, railing heights, ladder spacing, service signage in unreachable alcoves—all point to makers of a more ordinary scale and shape. But the landscape no longer feels inhabited in that sense. It feels *maintained by continuation*. Systems support systems because interruption never came decisively enough to end them. The industrial substrate still produces heat, circulation, and occasional manufactured forms. The vegetation still exploits, stabilizes, and redirects those outputs. Each has become the environment of the other. Together they have made a regional machine-garden old enough to seem geological.

Stand on one of the higher terraces and look outward. Under the low red gloom the land rolls away in crowded tiers: ribbed rooflines, trellis forests, chimney groves, tension-bridged gaps, swelling domes, stacked vats gone green-black, long rails swallowed by rootwork, and beyond them all the dim bulk of similar districts fading into haze. Here and there furnace apertures shine like banked coals. Higher still, static plays silently in the cloud roof, briefly silvering the topmost canopies. The openness is there, but every meter of it is occupied. It is a vastness made not by emptiness but by uninterrupted complication.

And because it is mostly quiet, the last impression is not of industry or wilderness but of persistence. Heat held. Structures endured. Growth answering every surface. Darkness thick with labor. A dry, crowded, ancient place that never stopped building itself, even after the builders were gone.
