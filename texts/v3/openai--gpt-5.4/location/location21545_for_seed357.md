At first it seems like a high marsh laid improbably on a mountain: a wide, hot upland where water does not gather into neat streams so much as sweat out of everything. The ground is never simply ground. It is assembled, terraced, latticed, grown into planes and ledges and long suspended shelves, all of it overrun by plant matter until the distinction between construction and growth becomes useless. A cliff-face turns out to be a retaining wall of fitted black blocks, but every seam is padded with root-flesh. A plaza opens between ridges, but it is flooded ankle-deep with warm green water and roofed only by hanging vines. The place has been made completely, yet not by machinery and not in the way a city is made. It feels built by repetition, by patience, by stacking and weaving and coaxing, by a culture or force that understood masonry and load and channel, then vanished or dissolved into the vegetation that inherited its work.

The altitude is felt less in cold than in exposure. The air is thin enough to sharpen distance, but hot enough to shimmer. Clouds drag low and break apart against the upper works of the landscape: tall partitions, stepped embankments, leaning pylons wrapped in climbing leaves. Moisture condenses on every elevated surface. Walls weep. Undersides of bridges bead with clear drops that fall all day into cisterns choked with reeds. Great sloped roofs of stone or fired clay—or something between those states—carry runnels of water down into hanging gardens and flooded basins. You could stand still here and hear hundreds of small descents: drips into pools, sheets slipping over moss-glazed edges, hollow resonances from water entering hidden shafts, sudden guttering rushes when some saturated mat of roots gives way and releases what it held.

It is not crowded, despite how total the plant life is. That is one of the place’s first oddities. Vegetation does not make a dense forest or tangle every passage shut. Instead it occupies nearly all surfaces while leaving large volumes of open air and long stretches of visible distance. A hillside may be entirely skinned in a single velvet of liverwort-bright growth, interrupted only by the geometric risers of old steps. Vast horizontal platforms support low orchards of columnar succulents, each many body-lengths apart, their canopies more like green candelabras than trees. Curtain-vines descend from high arches but hang in isolated veils, with broad hot spaces between them. Plants are substrate, cladding, upholstery, plumbing, memory; but the arrangement is spacious, ceremonial, almost infrastructural.

Seen from above—if one could gain the rim of the enclosing heights—the whole region might resemble a broken hanging city converted into watershed. Not a city of houses and streets in any familiar sense, though traces of intention are everywhere: radial causeways leading nowhere apparent, amphitheater-like depressions now turned to ponds, stair systems climbing into masses of rooted masonry and terminating at platforms where only wind and orchids remain. Some sectors are rigidly terraced, each ledge holding a different kind of wet growth: fan-ferns in one, red-stemmed reeds in another, broad-leafed pads in the next. Other sectors are chaotic, as if recent upheaval thrust fresh structures out of the earth at angles too steep for original use, after which plants and water simply accepted the new geometry.

The youth of the landscape shows in its edges. Very little has weathered into softness. Corners remain assertive. Slabs tilt with fresh violence. Escarpments expose clean fractures the color of iron, charcoal, and baked cream. New channels cut overnight through embankments and glisten raw. Whole retaining faces bulge and split under root pressure, not yet rounded by centuries. It feels recently raised, recently cracked, recently saturated into life. The high plateau may have been uplifted in some convulsive event; the built substrate may still be settling into itself. In many places the architecture and geology seem to be emerging together, as though foundational walls are not inserted into the mountain but are one phase of its formation.

This gives the region its strangeness: the sense that “built” here is not opposed to “natural.” There are no tools lying about, no hinges, no cables, no worked metals, no signs of engines or habitation in the ordinary sense. Yet nearly nothing is uncomposed. Surfaces meet at decided angles. Water is captured, redirected, elevated, and spilled according to patterns too exact to be accidental. Some towers are hollow and tuned, booming when wind crosses their mouths. Some fields are planted in spirals so large they only resolve from neighboring heights, their leaves different shades of green according to moisture. Some walls are made of interlocked cellular blocks, each pocket filled with soil and roots, so that the wall is both support and garden. It is construction by growth, growth according to design, and design now acted out by climate rather than by hands.

The heat is lush rather than desert-hard. Warm vapor rises from every basin. After rainfall—and there is a sense that rainfall is frequent, abrupt, theatrical—the entire upland steams. The sun returns through moving curtains of cloud, drawing silver breath from terraces and flooded courts. Wet stone becomes too hot to touch by midday. Broad leaves sag under the combined weight of water and heat, then lift again as the air moves. In shaded cuttings the atmosphere turns almost interior: close, mineral, full of leaf-sweat and the sweet-fermented smell of standing water among roots. On exposed platforms the heat has a brighter character, mixed with the chalky odor of fresh fractures and the resinous tang of sun-struck sap.

Without animals, every sound belongs to weather, water, structure, or vegetation itself. This does not make the place quiet. It is loud in a continuous, distributed way. Wind threads through cavities and fluted walls, producing tones that range from low organ-like moans to sudden high whistles. Leaves the size of banners slap against columns. Frond-fields hiss when gusts rake across them. Water pounds unexpectedly inside enclosed channels and then reappears as a broad spill. Seedpods burst in the heat with dry knocking cracks. Hollow stems clatter against one another. Whole suspended root-webs creak like ships. In storm periods—and storm feels less like an event here than a prevailing mode—the region becomes orchestral. Sheets of rain drum on terraces, overflow leaps from level to level, and the built forms amplify everything into a rolling, many-chambered roar.

The absence of fauna changes the emotional temperature. No darting movement. No call and reply. No insects at the waterline, no birds taking the upper air, no burrowing thing to disturb the mud. Pools can lie mirror-still except where rain strikes them. Fruit, where it forms, swells and falls untouched into the water to soften and split and feed only roots and microbes implied rather than seen. Flowers open with no pollinator in mind, or perhaps with wind and water as their only visitors. Some are enormous and pale, cupped to catch condensation; others are waxy ropes along walls, exuding scents stronger at dusk though nothing arrives for them. The ecology therefore feels closed, self-consuming, self-redistributing. Plant matter dies, mats, liquefies in the heat, and is taken back into the woven substrate. Drainage grilles—if they are grilles—vanish under mats of filamentous green and emerge elsewhere as thickened growth. Decay here is not ruin but plumbing.

The large scale of the place reveals itself slowly. A nearby flooded stair looks like a local feature until it is followed by the eye across a valley to a corresponding stair on the opposite side, then another above that, all part of a system spanning kilometers. One may notice a line of upright slabs on a ridge, each wrapped in flowering creepers, and only later understand they are the surviving crest of an immense aqueduct or windbreak. Basins that seem like ponds connect through buried channels into lower cistern-plains where giant pads raft over dark water. Elevated walkways—if walkways they ever were—trace arcs across open emptiness, too high now for use, carrying only ropes of roots and dripping epiphytes. The region is open enough that weather can be watched traveling through it: a gray wall of rain crossing a far terrace district, sun igniting another, mist lifting from a third.

Nothing is stable for long. The landscape is actively revising itself. New seepage appears. Old channels choke with roots and redirect floodwater across plazas. A whole green-clad face of masonry may peel away under saturation and expose chambers behind it, immediately colonized by dangling white rootlets. Suspended gardens collapse and become marsh shelves. Vines bridge gaps between towers, thicken, then pull masonry out of line. Silt accumulates in formal courtyards until emergent reeds transform them into ponds. Elsewhere, pressure from below lifts patterned paving into ridges from which hot water escapes in threads. It is possible that the “built” fabric is not dead material being consumed by plants but a slower participant in the same metabolism: blocks that absorb, swell, exude minerals; columns that sprout buttressing lobes of lichen-stone; channels that heal cracks by growing inward with calcareous tissue. The place behaves less like abandoned architecture and more like a botanical tectonic engine.

Color shifts with moisture. In full wetness the region is deep green, lacquer-black, copper-brown, and cloud-white, every surface darkened to richness. As heat returns after rain, pale salts bloom on ledges. New fractures show raw apricot and ash. Certain mosses brighten to electric chartreuse around seeps. Red bromeliad-like crowns blaze from the tops of pylons. On distant slopes, mats of silver foliage catch the sun so evenly they look like water. At evening the normal light turns kind, and the geometry hidden in noon glare becomes readable: the terraces, courts, channels, and stair-lines emerge from under the luxuriance as the shadows lengthen. Then mist rises from the lower basins and the place partly erases itself again.

If there are rules here, they are hydrological first. Everything serves water’s delay, storage, redirection, lifting by capillary means, release by overflow. Heights do not mean dryness; the mountain is wettest at its crown because the built vegetation catches cloud and wrings it out. Second: every surface is provisional. To stand anywhere is to stand on a phase, not a final form. Third: empty space is preserved. Even in total vegetative occupation, corridors of air, sightlines, and runoff remain. The place does not close into jungle because closure would interrupt its circulation. And finally: life here does not depend on animal mediation. Reproduction, spread, repair, and succession all proceed by rain, wind, collapse, and contact. One plant leans into another and becomes scaffolding; one terrace floods and seeds the next.

There are districts within it. High on the rims lie the condensing gardens, where toothy parapets harvest cloud and feed vertical moss-falls down their inner faces. Mid-slope are the stepped reservoirs, warm and shallow, crowded with paddle-leaf plants rooted in fitted stone cells. In the broad central depressions are marsh courts where old foundations disappear beneath peat and reed and floating bloom. On exposed promontories stand the sounding towers, mostly empty, their interiors lined with climbing roots that turn wind into a voice. In shadowed cuts beneath overhanging retaining walls are the drip cloisters, cool relative to the rest, where translucent fungi-like plant bodies—if they are plants—inflate with moisture and slowly deflate through the day.

The place is beautiful in a nonhuman way, but not hostile. It simply has no use for a visitor. Paths, where they exist, are interrupted by water, by sudden growth, by subsidence. Flatness is often membrane over cistern. What appears solid may be a raft of roots over warm black water. Handholds are slick, steps uneven, ledges newly cracked. Yet there is also a persistent impression of invitation, inherited from the original intentionality of the construction. Stairs still lead. Arches still frame views. Reservoirs still receive the sky. Even overgrown beyond any recognizable plan, the landscape continues to stage itself.

Perhaps that is its deepest strangeness: it is a made place whose makers are no longer separate from what was made. Their logic remains in the terraces and channels, but their presence has become botanical, aqueous, atmospheric. The mountain keeps building in their style. Every rain extends the design. Every root interprets structure. Every collapse is also an addition. And across the open, steaming heights, under ordinary daylight and tumultuous weather, the whole region goes on remaking itself loudly, patiently, with no witness required.
