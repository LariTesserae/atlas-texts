At first it seems like a mountainside city, the sort of place terraced into a steep country until masonry and scaffolding have replaced the idea of slope. But the longer you look, the less it resembles a city built on a mountain and the more it seems that the mountain itself has been remade, completely faced over in human surfaces until no original ground is visible anywhere. Stone is present, but cut and laid. Soil exists, but in beds, troughs, retaining basins, roof-gardens, suspended planters, and green ledges sewn into the architecture. Water is not absent, only disciplined: it beads on pipes at dawn, threads down gutters after the brief rains, gathers in cistern courts, mossed tanks, and channels so narrow they look like polished seams stitched through the district.

The whole region rises in gigantic steps. Not cliffs, exactly, though there are sheer drops; not towers, though there are towers too. Think instead of an old escarpment translated into construction. Long retaining walls hundreds of meters high hold broad inhabited shelves. Bridges leap the cuts between them. Inclined rail lines and freight lifts move diagonally up the face. Above one band of habitation hangs another, and above that another still, until the upper levels fade into bright haze. Because the elevation is so severe, air and perspective are always working strangely together. A courtyard may be open to the sky yet feel buried; a market lane may end in a parapet from which an entire neighboring district is visible across a gulf full of cables, vapor, and greenery.

Everything is made, but not everything is hard. The place has had time to mellow into its own completed state. Concrete sweats and supports ferns. Riveted water towers wear curtains of vine. Brick viaducts hold root mats in their joints. Factory roofs are bright with planted beds, and the undersides of stairways have become hanging gardens in miniature, draped with warm-climate epiphytes fed by condensate dripping from ducts and cooling coils. Wherever two surfaces meet and moisture can linger, something green has entered the design—not as invasion, exactly, but as accepted resident. There are gardeners here, certainly, but much of the vegetation feels like a long accommodation between maintenance and exuberance. Pipes are rerouted around trunks. Catwalks bend to preserve a shade tree growing out of an old loading gantry. Window grilles are built wide enough to admit creepers and birds together.

The warmth is constant enough that the place does not seem to brace against weather so much as receive it. Mornings begin with dampness collecting on metal handrails, cable housings, glazed tile, broad leaves. By noon the brightness is almost architectural in itself, making whitewashed service walls flare and turning every wet thing into a mirror. Rain comes in episodes rather than regimes: a passing shower drumming on sheet roofs, vapor rising from heated terraces afterward, runoff clattering briefly through vertical drains and then gone. In the long hours between, the city-mountain lives on stored water—cistern reserves, pumped mains, rooftop reservoirs, fog catchers stretched between upper structures, condensing fins mounted where warm air meets cooler currents off the heights.

Because there is no natural substrate visible, even the ecologies are infrastructural. Birds nest in vent housings, bell recesses, truss nests, cavities behind ornamental screens. Lizards bask on transformer boxes. Insects cloud around spill-light and flowering balcony orchards. There are domestic animals too, but adapted to altitude and ledges: lean goats with excellent balance on maintenance walks, doves and chickens in rooftop pens, cats moving through the green service alleys between machine rooms. Higher up, where access is more difficult and the gardens get wilder, monkeys sometimes appear—not great troops, but neighborhood-wise bands who know which fruiting trellises are least defended and which skylights can be pried at. In the damp interior courtyards there are frogs, surprisingly many of them, living in cistern rims and drain gardens, their calls emerging in the evenings once the machinery settles to a lower register.

The abundance of life is possible because the built fabric is so dense. One surface makes shade for another; one gutter feeds a planter; one exhaust warms an alcove where certain flowers bloom nearly year-round. The place stacks habitats the way it stacks workshops and homes. There are foundries or machine halls on the lower shelves, where deliveries arrive on broad viaducts and smoke can disperse. Above them are warehouses converted into apartment warrens, schools, textile rooms, repair courts, shrines inset into retaining walls, bathhouses built around storage tanks, cooperatives of gardeners tending tier upon tier of edible greens. On upper terraces, long conservatory galleries catch light and collect humidity, growing bananas, taro, medicinal vines, peppers, citrus trained flat against sun-walls. Not wilderness, but a fertility of use.

The sounds carry oddly over such vertical distances. Nothing here is ever truly quiet. There is always the broad body of industrial noise: generators with their patient thrum, lift chains knocking in rhythm, belts whining, fans chopping the air, pumps starting and stopping, the metallic report of some distant yard handling sheet stock or pipe lengths. But because the landscape is so articulated—courts, shafts, alleys, bridges, balconies, open-sided halls—sound arrives layered and delayed. A shout from far below rises softened by vegetation. Rain on corrugated roofs rattles across a whole district like a single instrument. Birds answer factory whistles. Monkeys overturn something in a high greenhouse and the clatter cascades down through three levels before ending in laughter from a balcony cafe. In the late day, when shifts change, the stairs and ramps fill with footfall and voice, and the mountain of construction becomes unmistakably inhabited, not merely operated.

It is bright here, almost insistently so. The light is sharpened by elevation and multiplied by pale materials: limewash, glazed ceramic, galvanized metal, mica in old concrete, panes of greenhouse glass, reservoirs tiled in sky-blue. Even under shade structures the brightness leaks in reflected from opposite walls. Plants respond by becoming thick and assertive—big leaves, waxy leaves, climbing stems, explosive blossoms the color of warning paint. Laundry strung across service courts contributes its own flags of color. Safety markings, temple murals, company logos faded on warehouse sides, market awnings, painted cistern lids: all of it sits under the same generous illumination. Only the deepest underworks—beneath the freight terraces, inside vented retaining galleries, in the old drain tunnels—escape it.

The age of the place shows less as ruin than as confidence. Erosion has already had its argument with these structures and been answered. Corners are rounded where countless hands have passed; runoff has stained walls into maps; channels have been lined where water insisted on flowing; buttresses have been thickened where some old settling once occurred. There are no tentative edges left. Every stair knows its traffic. Every parapet is polished to the height of resting arms. Even the newest additions imitate this weathered assurance: poured concrete made with aggregate chosen to match older walls, steel pre-rusted to harmonize with generations of oxidation, planters cast directly into facades because everyone knows roots will come.

The rules of the world here are simple and pervasive. Nothing is left unused if it can bear a load, catch water, hold heat, cast shade, or grow food. Verticality is not scenic but logistical; every ascent and descent has a purpose. Moisture is precious enough to be harvested but common enough, in little forms, to support green abundance. Wildlife is tolerated until it threatens storage, and then redirected rather than exterminated. Maintenance is a civic art. Growth is expected everywhere and therefore framed, trellised, contained, guided. Because change is slow, people build for decades, not seasons. A pipe run may be rerouted around a fig tree that will outlive the engineer. A cracked stair may remain in use for years, shored and patched, until the district agrees on the proper reconstruction. Whole neighborhoods feel like they have been revised rather than replaced, each generation adding another layer of adaptation to a form already dense with decisions.

From a distance the region reads as a green-struck massif of terraces, mills, reservoirs, and suspended neighborhoods rising through warm clear air. Up close it resolves into a thousand interdependencies: moisture silvering cables at dawn, parrots in the transmission towers, diesel and jasmine in the same breath, children racing along a shaded aqueduct walk, orchids tied to the rails above a machine shop, a freight car climbing slowly past a wall of papaya, frogs calling from an overflow garden while somewhere below a turbine keeps turning as it has for years.

There is little here that is untouched, and almost nothing that is unlived in. Yet it does not feel sterile or overmastered. It feels settled into—a complete artificial landscape that has learned, over long time, how to host warmth, brightness, industry, and life without pretending to be anything else. A manufactured mountain, yes, but one that now has weather, habitats, neighborhoods, seasons of flowering, local sounds, favored perches, old stains, familiar breezes. Not nature overwritten, exactly. More like construction so total and so longstanding that it has become a terrain in its own right.
