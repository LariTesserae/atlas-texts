The high country begins below the clouds.

Not metaphorically—though it invites metaphor—but literally, because the clouds here are not the ceiling of the world so much as one of its middle organs. They move through the land instead of over it, threading ravines and pouring between black ridges, snagging on forests that grow out of raw stone. Above them rise further escarpments, fresh as if thrust upward yesterday: walls of iron-dark basalt, pale volcanic glass, pink and sulfur-yellow seams not yet weathered into modesty. The mountains are too young to seem permanent. They have edges. They still look like decisions.

Everything is elevated, and almost nothing is settled.

The terrain spreads at a scale large enough to breed its own climates in neighboring folds. One range may be dry and hot, all warm mineral wind over clinker slopes and knife-edged spurs; the next, only a few leagues away across a gulf of vapor, can be dripping with condensed mist, every ledge slick, every root beaded with water. Moisture is not abundant in the simple way of a rainforest or marsh. It appears in pockets, in catches, in ambushes. There are hanging basins where rain gathers for half a season and vanishes in a day. There are blind canyons lined with stone that sweats. There are mornings when every thorn and blade and membrane in the land is jeweled with condensate, and noon when the same surfaces rasp dry against one another. Water comes as a visitation and leaves traces more elaborate than its volume ought to allow.

Because what grows here has learned to seize anything.

Vegetation is lush without being soft. It crowds, layers, interlocks. The lower saddles and vapor channels are jungled with rope-thick creepers, fern towers, mats of warm moss, broad leaves lacquered dark as oil, and pillar-plants whose ribbed trunks rise in clusters from cracks in newborn lava. Higher up, where the slopes are steep and the stone still unstable, the greenery changes character rather than retreating. Trees root in impossible angles from near-vertical faces, their root systems spread over rock like hands gripping the lip of a moving cart. Shrubs armored in glossy scales bloom overnight after mistfall, then harden by afternoon into thorned cages full of stored damp. There are cushions of silver-green growth clinging to pumice fields, and whole forests of translucent stalks in sheltered calderas, each stalk lit from within by a sluggish amber pulse as if passing weather were being absorbed and digested.

From a distance the land can look sparsely watered, all heat-hazed ridges and fractured shelves; up close it is crowded with reservoirs: bladders, tubes, nodules, cups, folded fronds that trap runoff, bark that wicks dew inward. Leaves overlap leaves, vines braid through branchwork, fungal fans sheet the shaded sides of boulders. Even bare places are not truly bare. Film-life slicks the stone. Lichens spread in geometric stains. Hair-fine rootlets web dust into skin.

This density is not only botanical. The place is busy with living things, and yet remains mostly quiet, because few of them are built on loudness.

Movement is constant if you stop expecting spectacle. A ridge that seems still is full of gliding shapes crossing between steam plumes, low-slung grazers nosing through succulent scrub, insects flickering in and out of visibility as though their wings are slicing different layers of air. The abundance of fauna expresses itself in occupation. Every vertical surface has tenants. Every pocket of shadow contains some folded, patient body. Every pool, however temporary, blooms with larvae, transparent eels, paddle-legged skimmers, gelatinous strings of eggs attached to stone by noon and hatched by dusk. Herds migrate along impossible high traverses, not in open grasslands but in braided procession through root galleries and shelf forests. Predators hunt by pressure change, by scent of wet mineral, by sensing electrical leaks in the fog.

Birds—if bird is the word for all feathered and near-feathered things here—are numerous, but many fly silently, on broad powder-dark wings or on membranous sails stiffened by cartilage and metallic fibers grown rather than manufactured. There are cliff-nesting colonies whose members hang upside down in rows from overhangs, facing the abyss in sleep. There are six-limbed gliders that feed on condensation, licking rock faces at dawn with brush tongues. There are heavy-bodied mountain runners with frilled throats and heat-pale eyes, moving through underbrush with the austere speed of poured sand.

The sounds of the place, when you first enter it, seem sparse: a distant mineral clatter from somewhere unseen, leaf-rub, occasional drips, the soft abrasive hiss of fog moving through needle canopies. But the quiet is layered, not empty. There is always some very small thing clicking. Stone contracts with little pops after sunset. Frond-banks whisper against one another. Far below, in hidden trenches, subterranean water booms once and is still. Sometimes a whole valley emits a low hum with no source visible, only for the mist to thin and reveal a plain of tall reedlike growths all vibrating in sympathetic resonance with wind too high to be felt at ground level. There are hours when the loudest sound is the passage of shadow.

And then turbulence reminds you where you are.

This is not an old equilibrium. The world is actively, restlessly making and unmaking itself. Ridges calve into ravines. Warm updrafts rear out of nowhere and lift flocks, leaves, dust, spores, and loose skin from the land in spiraling columns. Mists condense with impossible speed, forming cloud-walls that roll through forests and leave behind dripping silence and stunned insects. In some regions the slopes crawl—not metaphorically but geologically and almost biologically—as fresh extrusions rise under crusts not yet committed to being mountain. Entire groves lean together as the ground beneath them slowly bulges. Basalt fins crack open overnight, venting hot mineral breath. Ponds appear where no depression existed the day before, because a sheet of stone has warped upward around a trapped lens of cold vapor and pinched it into water.

Scale magnifies this instability. Storms are born in one massif and die in another. Valleys have local calendars based not on season but on recurring convulsions of fog, ash pollen, thermal inversion, and what passes here for rain: slanting falls of condensed droplets shaken loose from the undersides of cloud-banks by pressure fronts from above. There are places where the atmosphere itself seems stratified into moods. Climb a hundred meters and the air shifts from warm green damp to dry copper heat; descend into a chasm and find a stagnant blue chill where luminous moths pack the walls like scales.

The dimness of the landscape does not come from perpetual night. The sky is often bright somewhere, but light has trouble arriving cleanly. Too much of it is caught, scattered, absorbed, bruised on the way down by smoke-veils, high suspended moisture, canopies, cliff overhangs, and atmospheric peculiarities that no longer quite behave like weather should. Dawn leaks instead of breaking. Noon often resembles late afternoon, all muted gold and pewter haze. Under the forests, colors deepen toward black-green, rust-red, wet violet, and the electric pallor of fungus or exposed mineral. In open places the dimness becomes stranger: not shadow, but a slight failure of certainty, as if distances are being calculated by a distracted mind.

This is where the industrial quality of the place first becomes legible.

There are no buildings. No roads. No towers, cables, platforms, refineries, mines, or wrecks. Nothing made in the ordinary sense. And yet the land behaves with an industrial intensity, as if some principle of scaled production has emerged directly from ecology and geology.

Certain valleys process wind. Dense stands of hollow stone-grass, each blade taller than a mast and edged in conductive salts, draw charge from storm friction and discharge it into root-webs that run for kilometers through the ground. Whole hillsides pulse in sequence, storing and releasing energy with the timed regularity of a switching yard. Vast fungal shelves spread across warm fault faces in repeating arrays, converting mineral seep and heat into blooms of fatty tissue harvested—by whom? by what?—within hours by swarms of pale, eyeless grazers that move in lanes and return along others, as disciplined as conveyor lines. In volcanic basins, bead-vines inflate with sulfur gas and drift upward in bunches, only to be punctured by hovering filter-creatures that strip their contents and let the skins fall into catch-forests below, where decomposition proceeds in neat, heat-generating rows.

Nothing was designed, and everything seems organized.

There are migration routes so regular they resemble freight schedules. There are cliff faces drilled by burrowing colonies into repeating hexagonal apertures, hundreds of thousands of them, each occupied, vented, serviced by cleaner organisms and defended by ribbon-bodied guardians that patrol the facade in shiftlike rotations. There are plains of nodular black growth that split and reveal interior cogs of cartilage and silica, turning slowly to grind lichen and pumice into nutrient dust. There are riverless channels where polished stones roll uphill in files during specific atmospheric conditions, gathering static and distributing it to ridge-top nurseries. You can watch these processes for days and begin to suspect intention, then watch longer and realize intention may be too small a word. The place has throughput. It has metabolism at megascale. It manufactures weather, fertility, migration, pressure, glow.

The warmth of the highlands contributes to the sense of generated work. Heat rises from the ground in many regions, geothermal and fresh. Some plateaus are bare enough to expose broad plates of dark rock that drink the day’s weak light and re-emit it slowly through the dim hours, so that sleeping animals lie in mosaics across them. Steam enters the ecology not as grand geysers so much as innumerable exhalations: cracks breathing warm vapor into root tangles, fumarole gardens surrounded by thick, fleshy leaves held out like hands around a stove, cloud-chambers in lava tubes where hot mineral air meets seepage and creates hanging condensate forests upside down from the ceiling.

The weirdness begins at the edges of explanation and then keeps going.

Distances misbehave first. A neighboring ridge may remain the same apparent size for hours of walking, while a mountain thought remote suddenly looms overhead after a turn through fern-dark ravines. Echoes arrive before sounds. Rain can be seen falling upward in the mouths of certain canyons, coalescing back into a low ceiling of moving gray. Some valleys repeat. Not resemble—repeat, with the same split boulder, the same angled tree burdened by orange epiphytes, the same troop of long-snouted herbivores crossing left to right—except that each recurrence is at a different stage of age or damage, as if the landscape is trying out versions in parallel.

Life participates in this uncertainty. Herd animals sometimes shed not fur or scales but excess outlines, leaving behind flickering afterbodies that continue grazing for minutes before collapsing into steam. Predators enter mist and emerge overbright, overjointed, briefly too detailed for the eye to tolerate. There are groves in which every leaf shows the wind conditions of a different place. Pools reflect weather not currently present. Some flying creatures bank against inclines in the air no one else can see and vanish into a fold of space with the casualness of swallows entering reeds.

In the highest regions, where the fresh mountains rise into thin warm light and cloud seas drift below like illuminated slag, the rules loosen further. Gravity may become directional rather than absolute along certain mineral seams; entire stands of hook-rooted trees grow horizontally out from cliff walls and then curve upward only at their tips, making hanging avenues in open air. Boulders ring softly when passed by animals that have never touched them. Shadow pools in hollows and can be drunk by specialized black-muzzled fauna, after which they become briefly invisible except for their wet footprints. Lightning can crawl slowly over the ground for hours, tame as grazing.

None of this feels magical in the decorative sense. It feels infrastructural. Reality has become overtasked here, stretched by the scale and speed of ongoing formation, by the density of interdependent systems, by heat and uplift and charge and the voracious opportunism of life. The world is not broken so much as operating beyond normal tolerances.

There are no human rules here because there are no human works, but the place has rules all the same, and they are strict in their own alien way.

Moisture will collect where surfaces offer the right invitation, regardless of apparent exposure. Anything still for long enough becomes substrate. Exposed softness is harvested. Heat gradients are pathways, borders, and feeding grounds. Height does not mean emptiness; the highest ledges may be the most crowded. Quiet often indicates density rather than absence. Stability is local and temporary. If a thing repeats, do not assume it is the same thing. If a route remains easy, it is leading somewhere that benefits from traffic. Brightness is suspect. Open water is never merely water; it is nursery, lure, lens, membrane, or mouth. The youngest rock is often the most inhabited.

And despite all this motion, all this industrial-scale metabolism and ontological slippage, the overwhelming impression is not frenzy but poised concentration. The landscape seems to be listening to itself.

Stand on a high shelf at what passes for evening, with warm mist moving below you in broad pale rivers and the upper ridges emerging from it like cooling metal. Around you the vegetation holds the day’s last damp in a million fine droplets. Silent gliders cut between the trunks. Somewhere in the cloud underworld, a colony shifts and a whole canyon gives one soft clacking sigh. Far off, beyond several ranges, a storm blooms from within, lighting the mist from inside in slow pulses of copper-white, but no thunder arrives. Beneath your feet the young mountain emits a warmth like breath.

Then one of the nearby cliffs, which you had taken for stone all day, unfolds a forest from its face and begins very slowly to turn.
