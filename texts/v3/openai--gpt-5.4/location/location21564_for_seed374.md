The place lies low and broad, as if the world here has only recently decided to stop being sea and begin being ground. It is not old land. That is the first thing it tells you. Nothing has been worn into elegance. Nothing has had time to soften. The earth sits in blunt planes and rounded swellings, in lifted shelves of clay and pale stone that look pressed up from beneath rather than carved down from above. Edges are not sharp from fracture and not smooth from age; they are merely unfinished. The whole district has the look of something set out to cure.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming grandeur. There is no deep river valley, no dramatic coast, no cataract. Instead the wetness lives close to the skin of the place. The ground gleams in patches. Shallow channels, no older than habit, thread between packed earthen rises and foundation-like ridges of exposed marl. Pools gather in depressions and remain there as if they have always been there and always will, mirror-dull under the dim sky. Stone sweats. Walls bead with moisture. In the mornings, and often all day, the air seems only half committed to being air at all; it hovers near condensation, heavy and warm, and every surface carries a darkened edge where damp has climbed into it.

There is almost no greenery to break this mineral body. No grass fuzzes the flats. No roots prise open cracks. Whatever life once covered earth elsewhere either never arrived here or was refused. The nearest thing to vegetation is a skin of pale films in the wettest corners: bacterial iridescence, maybe, or mats so simple they read as stain before they read as living matter. Along the bases of walls, around troughs, across the lips of standing pools, there are glistening sheens of white, amber, and bruised blue where minerals precipitate and microscopic life feeds on the chemistry of wet stone. In shade, one finds soft, low fungal fans no larger than a hand, colorless as old bone. They do not make the place feel inhabited by plants. They make it feel inhabited by processes.

And yet it is crowded with life.

The city—if city is the right word—is made from earth, fired clay, rough-cut stone, wickerwork, hide, rope, and timber brought from elsewhere in such a disciplined quantity that the absence of local wood becomes part of the architecture. Buildings rise not in towers but in compact masses, shoulder to shoulder on low embanked islands and causewayed ridges. Their walls are thick, often damp, and slightly bulged, as though built atop ground that still shifts in minute, patient ways beneath them. The streets between them are narrow enough to trap heat and noise, and broad enough, in the main thoroughfares, to let herds pass. Nothing gleams with advanced craft. Metal is rare and treasured where it exists—a hinge, a knife, a hook, a bell tongue. Most things bear the marks of hand labor: thumb-smoothed clay bricks, adze-chopped beams, braided reed shutters, wheel-rutted ramps hardened by long use.

The settlement spreads farther than first sight suggests. Because the land is so low and the air so thick, distance folds in layers. Beyond one district of kiln-yards and livestock pens there is another district of cistern courts; beyond that a tangle of market roofs; beyond that raised sleeping compounds; beyond that the glimmer of salt pans or clay flats or fish ponds—whatever this culture can coax from persistent shallow water. It is open country in the sense that the horizon exists, but dense in the way coral is dense: every apparent gap fills, on approach, with some other structure, enclosure, pen, shed, embankment, bridge, wall, trench, trough, or clustered moving body.

The sound reaches you before the shape of things resolves. Deafening is not an exaggeration here. This is not a place of occasional loudness. It is built on a permanent acoustic flood. Animal voices dominate it: the bellowing of swamp cattle or some broad-hoofed equivalent; the shriller crying of penned fowl; the clatter and snort of tusked beasts used to drag sledges through mud; frogs in impossible number pulsing from every wet margin; insects rasping from the walls at dusk and dawn and all through the dim middle hours; waterfowl screaming over the ponds; dogs, or doglike camp followers, erupting into arguments at every pass. Layered over this are human sounds multiplied by confinement—hammering on stone, paddles slapping water in the canals, potters turning wheels, kiln vents roaring, ropes groaning, babies crying, traders calling, sandals and bare feet striking packed causeway, the thud of grain pounders, the slap of wet hides, the steady low liturgical chanting from some courtyard shrine.

Even the water contributes not with rushing but with persistence: dripping from eaves, sucking under planks, glugging in culverts, tapping from overflow spouts into jars, lapping at retaining walls. In enclosed passages the whole city seems to breathe through damp throats.

The warmth is not tropical lushness but stored body heat. The land radiates upward. Mud walls radiate sideways. Animals crowd pens and lend their own weather to the alleys. The dimness overhead comes from a habitual lid of cloud or vapor rather than storm. Light diffuses through it and settles evenly on the place, with little drama of shadow. Days are bright enough to work in, but the sky never fully opens; it is pearl-colored, beige at the horizon, sometimes violet with pending rain that may or may not ever arrive because the air already feels like aftermath. At sunset, what little angle the sun finds turns every wet thing to beaten copper. At dawn, the city appears to condense out of fog rather than emerge from sleep.

Because erosion is barely at work, the surrounding terrain feels geologically fresh and socially overcommitted. A ridge might prove, up close, to be less a natural hill than a new extrusion of clay-rich sediment stabilized by woven retaining mats and walked flat by generations. The channels are too regular in places to be called natural and too irregular in others to be called engineered. This is a landscape in negotiation with itself. Not changing, exactly—that is the strange thing—but still unfinished. It has the stillness of an equilibrium reached early. Water stands where it has found standing works. Mud rises where pressure lifts it. People build exactly on the lines that remain dry enough to bear them. The animals know the routes and keep to them. Nothing here feels temporary in the ordinary sense. It feels primordial and settled at once.

The fauna give the place its true architecture. Since plants do not, animals fill every niche of movement, labor, waste, appetite, and attention. Herd-beasts with broad splayed feet churn the outer flats but leave the elevated lanes hard-packed and polished. Long-legged marsh birds stalk among the drainage pools inside the city itself, accepted as scavengers. Fish live in every canal that can hold them, and children scoop them by hand from overflow basins. Snails cluster thick on shaded damp stone. Beetles swarm dung-heaps in lacquer-black sheets. Lizards bask on kiln walls. Bats—or something very like them—hang in black felted bunches from the ceilings of storage vaults and emerge in streams through ventilation slits at the same hour every evening, so punctually that people time chores by them.

And there are larger creatures too, ordinary enough to belong. Massive draft animals kneel into harness at the foot of earthen ramps. Semi-domesticated marsh grazers lie half-submerged in stagnant pools beyond the walls with only nostrils, horns, and patient eyes above water. Lean scavengers shadow the slaughter courts. Every threshold seems to host some tolerated cohabitant: a lizard, a dog, a basket of chirring insects, a tied monkey, a pile of shellfish, a rack of drying eels. Life here does not express itself as foliage but as metabolism. The city is fed, in every sense, by mouths.

The rules of the place are practical, old, and probably obvious to those who live there. Build high, even if high means only the height of three stacked bricks. Keep damp away from stores and invite it toward waste. Raise every floor from the ground if you can. Fire clay whenever possible: fired surfaces survive what raw surfaces slowly surrender to. Share walls. Roof broad. Drain inward to cistern, outward to ditch, and never trust a flat yard unless you laid it yourself. Animals are not kept apart from settlement because settlement depends on them too intimately. Water is not drunk where it stands unless it has stood in the right vessel. Stone that sweats is useful; stone that crumbles is not. Nothing organic left exposed remains yours for long—not because of weather, which hardly changes, but because something living will claim it.

Time here is measured less by season than by cycles of use. The world does not dramatically alter. It remains warm, damp, dim, loud. Pools neither vanish nor spread much beyond their habit. Walls darken and are replastered. Channels silt and are scooped clear. Kilns fire. Herds breed. Fish are trapped and consumed. New buildings are raised against old ones, not replacing but thickening. The city grows the way mineral deposits grow around a drip: incrementally, concentrically, according to available surfaces. Stability breeds density. Density breeds custom. Custom hardens into infrastructure.

If you leave the busiest wards and walk outward over the causeways, the urban mass thins into worked wetlands and then into open flats of clay, shell, and seepage. Even there, the sense of habitation does not quite stop. Marker posts stand in lines over the marsh. Fish weirs comb the shallows. Low shrine mounds rise from the wet ground, each carrying a ring of fired figurines glazed by mineral mist. In the distance are extraction pits where people cut blocks of clay from the earth like slices from a cake. The pits fill with water almost at once, becoming new ponds, and the spoil banks beside them become new dry ground, and on that new ground someone eventually lays a wall. The landscape and the settlement are not enemies. They are versions of the same material arranged to different purposes.

There is little beauty here in the airy sense, but a great deal in the tactile one. Damp brick under the hand. Smooth worn thresholds. The satin skin of pooled water clouded by the touch of insects. Salt rimming a trough. Smoke from low fuel fires trapped beneath the hanging vapor and turning the upper alleys blue. The ochres, umbers, chalk-whites, soot-blacks, and occasional greenish mineral blooms of the place make a restrained palette, and within it every wet highlight matters. It is a world of surfaces that remember contact.

Stand still long enough and the deafening noise resolves into constancy. That may be the deepest character of the landscape: not wildness, not construction, not marsh, not city, but continuance. The same cries from the pens. The same drip from the eaves. The same slow exhalation of warm damp from the ground. The same low horizon carrying the same dim light. A place barely eroded, barely aged, but already complete in its habits—a young earth with an old routine, thick with animals, built by hands, and held together by mud, water, and repetition.
