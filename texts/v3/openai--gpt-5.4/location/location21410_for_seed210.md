The place sits high enough that clouds do not so much pass overhead as lean against the land and stay there. It is not a vast country. It is a contained upland basin, or something very like a basin: a shelf of dark stone cupped among sharper rises, perhaps no larger than a village would have been if villages existed here. Its boundaries are easy to feel even when they are hard to see. Sound returns from them. Mist pools against them. Paths made by animals drift toward them and stop, as if the place has agreed on its own circumference.

Everything is wet, but there is almost no open water in the ordinary sense. The ground shines with it instead. Water stands inside moss, beads under lichen skins, collects in leaf axils, threads along seams in rock, and breathes out of the earth in slow cold exhalations. The stone sweats continuously. Ferns have droplets hanging from every serration. Broad, low plants, thick as if grown in a greenhouse but chilled blue-green by the altitude, hold little private reservoirs in their folded centers. When rain comes, it changes almost nothing. The basin already lives in the state just after rain.

The terrain is young in the way a fresh break is young. It has not been rounded into patience yet. Ridges rise with clean decisions. Black escarpments split along pale veins. Slabs lean where they should have fallen but never did. There are cracks everywhere, narrow and geometric at first glance, but on looking longer they are too regular in some places and too unruly in others, as though the rock was cooling from a cast rather than eroding from an age. The ground under the vegetation is shallow: a skin of peat, rootmat, and soaked mineral grit stretched over recent stone. In places the skin parts and shows the body beneath, glossy and dark, like iron left in weather.

That resemblance is not metaphor only.

Nothing has been built here. No wall, no road, no cut, no fastening, no tool mark. Yet the basin is industrial all through, with an industrial confidence, an industrial repetition, an industrial logic of intake and output. The machinery is not separate from the ecology. It is the ecology, or has been long enough that no one could draw a border.

Some of the rock faces are ribbed like heat exchangers. Some of the trees — if trees is the word for the tall sparsely spaced growths rooted among the ledges — rise on trunks banded with concentric metal-gray bark that flakes in shavings thin as swarf. In the dimness these shavings gather at their roots in bright curls, but when touched by drifting condensation they soften, darken, and unfold into liverwort-like fans. Whole stands of brush are arranged in repeating intervals, not planted but self-spacing, each clump exactly far enough from the next to leave lanes of bare wet stone between them. The moss grows in directional nap, all fibers laid one way, as though combed by a current no one can feel. Vents open and close in the cliff with the slow rhythm of pistons. They are made of basalt, not steel, but they move with engineering exactness.

The light is poor and permanent. Day never fully commits here. The basin receives a diluted illumination that seems filtered through cloud, water, and stone all at once. The colors it permits are deep and selective: bottle-green, bruised purple, black with hints of blue, the silver of film on water, the pale luminescent white of fungal membranes. Brightness occurs only in small industrial accents: a seam of mineral that catches and holds a cold sheen; beads on spider silk suddenly lit; the wet flank of an animal passing between fronds; the brief internal glow of certain seedpods that pulse as if status lamps were buried in them.

Because the place is dim, sounds arrive before shapes. It is louder than it should be for such a small enclosed land. There is no windstorm, no waterfall, no sea. The loudness is layered and continuous: drips amplified by hollows under stone, the conveyor-belt chatter of insect colonies, throats clicking in the reeds, the wet rasp of leaves dragging against one another, periodic deep thunks from somewhere beneath the basin floor, long tremulous humming from the root systems during heavy condensation, wingbeats, chittering, the clank-like report of seed husks opening, and under all of it a steady mechanical drone that has no source because every source contributes to it. The basin sounds the way a factory might sound if all its machines had become shy and gone to live under moss.

Fauna are everywhere, though the spaciousness of the place prevents it from feeling crowded. Presences are scattered, each with room around it, each sharply itself against the dimness. On a single ledge one might see only three things: a pair of long-legged marsh ungulates with velvet-black hides drinking from a crack in stone; a wheel of white fungus opening and closing around a trapped moth; a patient many-eyed bird standing motionless as a valve. Yet every patch of ground is worked, tasted, patrolled, sifted, grazed, tunneled through, pollinated, watched.

Many of the animals seem designed for both wetland and machinery. Beetles with segmented carapaces interlock into chains to bridge runnels, then break apart without hurry. Snails carry shells like pressure gauges, translucent and spiraled with moving dark fluid. Small mammals with fan-shaped ears sleep against warm vent-rock and wake in shifts, emerging with condensation on their whiskers. There are eels in the moss — not metaphorical eels, true slender bodies moving under the saturated green skin of the ground as fish move beneath duckweed. When they surface they do so vertically, tasting the air, silver-black and expressionless.

Higher up, among the sparse crooked trunks, the dominant life is avian, though “bird” strains. They perch on branch tips too fine to hold them and are somehow held. Some have plumage that resembles oil on water: not glossy, but iridescent in dull light, color moving where no brightness is available to produce it. Some communicate by making sounds exactly like distant metal stress: pinging, cable-singing, the light hammer note of cooling plates. Flocks do not wheel; they assemble and disassemble in modular patterns, as if drafted and redrafted overhead.

The plants, lush as they are, do not form a closed jungle. They occupy the basin in productive clusters, leaving breathing room between masses of growth. A shoulder of stone may carry a lavish cape of clubmoss, orchids, fungus shelves, and dripping rootlets, then end abruptly in ten bare yards of dark mineral slick with condensation. A hollow may be packed with gigantic horsetails and broad fungal fans, each leaf and cap trembling with life, while the adjacent rise supports only a few low red shrubs set far apart like punctuation. This spareness gives every colony the air of a workstation. Each patch seems responsible for a process.

And the processes are real. Water gathers in leaves, then drains through capillary fibers into porous rock. Rock warms around hidden seams and exhales vapor. Vapor condenses on suspended filament webs, where tiny crustacean-like grazers feed and excrete mineral pellets. The pellets dissolve into clear acidic trickles that etch shallow channels in the fresh stone. In the channels grow mats of black algae that shine faintly and release a smell like wet batteries. Grazers come for the algae; hunters come for the grazers; scavengers come for all the rest. What would elsewhere be decomposition feels here like manufacture carried on in reverse: disassembly so precise it becomes another form of production.

The strangest thing is that none of this changes.

Not in the human sense of weather passing, flowers opening, carcasses rotting, young becoming old. Motion exists, but only inside a fixed arrangement. Drips fall, but from the same points into the same cups. Animals feed, but remain somehow within a stable count, a stable spacing, a stable age. Mist thickens and thins but never arrives or departs. A bird lifts from one branch and lands on another branch that seems to have always contained that landing. A beetle shell cracks; the crack has the feeling of an event repeated so many times that it no longer belongs to sequence. If you stayed long enough — and one suspects many things have, without meaning to — you would begin to notice repetitions too exact to be seasonal. The same call every forty-seven breaths. The same plume of vapor from the same crevice after the same number of drips. The same heron-like creature stepping into the same ribbon of water with the same raised foot, forever not because it is trapped in a single frozen instant, but because the basin has settled into a looped equilibrium so deep that time only circulates inside it.

This is where the reality-break begins to show itself plainly. The industrial quality is not simply analogy, and the stasis is not just ecological balance. The basin appears to be running.

Running what is hard to say. It takes in mist, seepage, mineral salts, dim light, flesh, spores, noise. It outputs heat in slight increments, rearranged matter, patterned calls, polished bones, and the persistent hum under everything. There are moments when the ground transparency shifts and you can see, not beneath the soil exactly but beneath the assumptions of soil, a deeper order: root systems branching like wiring diagrams; fungal nets laid in grids too orthogonal for chance; pools whose surfaces show not reflections but cross-sections of lower strata where pale things move in synchronized transit. At the base of one cliff a waterfall seems forever about to begin. Water bulges from the rock, beads, and retracts, never crossing into flow. Behind the bead is the sound of immense pumping.

Elsewhere, on a lichen-bright wall, there are circular growths that resemble bolted access plates. They are only colonies of fungus until one of the large grazing animals brushes by, and every circle rotates a fraction in response, shedding water in a ring. The animal does not react. It has brushed that wall forever.

Nothing looks ruined. Nothing looks new. “Young” here means unweathered by uncertainty. The sharp edges have not yet learned that they are temporary, perhaps because in this place temporary forms are continuously reissued. Broken stems regrow to the same break. Mud keeps the same hoofprints while remaining soft. Feathers caught on a thorn do not decay or blow free; they remain fresh, wet, and newly caught. Even death participates in the rule. In one fern hollow lies the skeleton of a fox-sized thing with too many forelimbs, overgrown in moss and tiny white flowers. The bones are clean, the flowers in bloom, the moss bead-bright with moisture. None of these are stages. They are coexistent aspects maintained together like parts in inventory.

The air is cold enough that breath shows, if you are a thing that breathes warm. It smells of peat, stone, green sap, fungal bloom, and an intermittent tang like struck metal. Put your hand on certain boulders and they thrum. Put your ear to the ground and there are subsonic rotations below hearing that you hear anyway, translated by bone. Stand still near the center of the basin and eventually the local life will resume around you, because your stillness allows you to be sorted. A red-backed carrion bird will land nearby and watch. Eels will move under the moss around your boots or paws or roots. The long-legged drinkers will return to their crack in the stone. Somewhere in the cliff a chamber will close with a damp, final-seeming seal, and immediately another will open.

The rules of the world here seem simple and impossible:

Nothing is made, yet everything is manufactured.  
Nothing develops, yet everything is active.  
Nothing leaves, though many things circulate.  
Water is always arriving from surfaces, never from sky or stream.  
Every lifeform is also a component.  
Every component is alive enough to adapt, but not enough to escape function.  
The basin is small, but contains more recurrence than a mountain range.

If there is a center, it may be a marsh no wider than a house would be, had houses occurred. It is a low place crowded with reeds of translucent cartilage and broad floating leaves furred in silver down. Beneath the leaf cover the water is black and shallow-looking, but no probe reaches bottom. The surface bears occasional geometric ripples, squares and hexagons appearing for an instant among the natural circles of drip impact. Frogs live there, if frogs can have glassy throats lit from within and calls that sound like valves releasing pressure. Insects skate over the black water in straight, measured routes. At night — if there is night distinct from day, and one is not sure there is — the marsh emits a diffuse glow from under the surface, enough to silhouette the reeds and turn every hanging droplet into a tiny suspended lamp. The surrounding animals gather at its edges, never in panic, never in celebration, simply taking their places.

And perhaps that is the deepest character of the landscape: not wildness, not serenity, not menace, but placement. Everything is in place with such total conviction that the place no longer needs history to justify itself. It does not feel ancient, though it may have persisted beyond measure. It does not feel primeval, though no hand has touched it. It feels operational. Self-willed. Complete in a way that excludes narrative. You do not enter it expecting revelation; you enter it and gradually understand that revelation is what the basin is doing all the time, repeatedly, to itself.

A cool, dripping upland hollow of fresh black stone and lush divided growth; a marsh-edge without a marsh, a factory without fabrication, a living engine with no inventor; loud with hidden labor, dim with held-back daylight, crowded with animals but spacious in arrangement; unchanged not because it is dead, but because it has found the exact shape of its own continuation and will keep producing that shape forever.
