The city begins as stone before it becomes construction.

It stands high, though not in the clean way of a mountaintop. This is elevation made broad and habitable: a long uplifted basin or shelf of dark mineral country, miles above whatever lowlands birthed the rivers, but worn so old and so thoroughly by weather that steepness has been mostly negotiated into terraces, escarpments, gullies, and patient ramps. The land has had an immense amount of time to come to terms with gravity. Nothing here is raw. Every cliff is stained, every ledge rounded, every exposed face softened by cycles of dampness and warmth. Water has not carved dramatic youth into the place; it has taught it repetition.

The first impression is of wet stone and occupation.

The ground is almost nowhere dry for long. Not because rain falls constantly—though showers do move across it often enough—but because water lingers close to the surface, gathers in seams, condenses overnight on cooler walls, beads along cables and railings, sweats from the pores of old masonry, drips out from overhangs with the calm persistence of a body at rest. Streets are slick more often than not. Between blocks, in cuts where service roads descend, there are channels with only a finger’s depth of running water, and these have worn their beds smooth over ages. Retaining walls weep. The undersides of viaducts carry rows of droplets like strings of glass. In the morning the whole place often seems to have exhaled in its sleep and dampened itself.

Yet there are almost no plants.

This absence is not sterile in the abstract, not the vacancy of a laboratory. It is more particular and more complete than that. No grass in cracks, no windblown weed at stair edges, no moss fur on shaded joints, no rooting tree to break a wall. Open ground is rock, aggregate, fired brick, slag tile, poured mineral composite, iron darkened by age, and expanses of old ceramic paving. Surfaces collect mineral bloom instead of lichen: pale veils of salt, rust-colored runnels, black manganese skins, sulfurous yellows around vents and drains. In places there are mats and curtains of things that almost tempt the eye into calling them alive—soft ash-gray films around warm exhausts, translucent jelly along condensation gutters, threadlike pale colonies around animal wastes and carcasses—but if there is life there below the threshold of easy naming, it keeps to the logic of chemistry rather than greenery. The city has no leaf-shadow. Light falls uninterrupted by canopies and reveals everything.

Because fauna has taken the abandoned ecological space and then some.

There are animals everywhere. Not one dominant species ruling the skyline in theatrical numbers, but an entire pressure of life, a crowding and layering and occupation of every cavity the city and the rock permit. They swarm roofs, ledges, culverts, reservoirs, galleries beneath warehouses, warm pipe trenches, dry lofts, drainage mouths, bridge undersides, quarry pools, cable forests, and abandoned stations. Their abundance is less like a forest and more like a reef built on architecture.

At a distance one sees movement before one identifies bodies. Whole parapets ripple. Thousands of small, warm-colored creatures huddle on sun-touched concrete in the morning and flow away at noon. Long-backed, many-limbed scavengers work slowly through alleys in files, their shells clicking against grit. Blind, pale amphibious things fill the runoff channels and retreat only reluctantly when traffic shudders overhead. On higher towers, broad-winged carrion fliers circle not because death is frequent—though in such abundance it always is—but because the updrafts are reliable and the city produces a constant geography of smell. There are burrowers in the embankments, nest-clusterers in duct arrays, slick-bodied grazers that rasp mineral films from damp walls, and intelligent opportunists that have learned latches, timetables, and the zones of greatest human carelessness.

The noise they make is one of the signatures of the place. It is not wilderness birdsong. It is a civic animal noise: calls bounced off facades, drumming feet in service corridors, the collective hiss and murmur of bodies packed under grates, chitter from utility voids, low mating hoots from cistern chambers, sporadic shrieks from rooftop disputes, the splash and slither of things in drainage canals. Mixed with this are human and industrial sounds: motors idling under load, the deep belt-thrum of conveyors, pumps cycling, warning bells from freight yards, clanged metal gates, tires on wet pavement, compressed steam venting in brief forceful exhalations, public announcements blunted by humidity, and always the trickle and tap of water. Loud, but not frantic. The city has found a volume at which it can continue indefinitely.

Its built form suggests long negotiation between geology, industry, and infestation.

There are districts of heavy stone construction so old they seem quarried out of the plateau rather than assembled upon it. Their streets are narrow, their walls thick, their arches low and damp. Water stains map every facade. Above and among these rise later industrial additions: riveted towers, foundries with sawtooth roofs, tram lines, gantries, tank farms, processing halls, blocky worker barracks, vent stacks, pumping stations, freight elevators climbing cliff faces, and broad platforms cut into the slopes where machines once required level ground at any cost. Not all of it is active, but enough is active that the city still feels metabolically complete.

Infrastructure has been layered rather than replaced. A drainage tunnel becomes a pedestrian underpass becomes an animal rookery becomes a conduit chase. A retaining wall from some early period is jacketed in newer concrete, then webbed with pipes, then bridged with steel catwalks. Reservoir basins are roofed over and converted into cool warehouses while new cisterns are driven deeper into the bedrock. Staircases and ramps proliferate. Elevation inside the city is rarely solved elegantly; it is managed by persistence. You climb, descend, cross, descend again, pass through a machine hall warm with engines, emerge onto a wet terrace full of feeding animals, and realize you are still within one continuous district.

The materials are all chosen under the assumption of dampness and time. Stone that can take a stain gracefully. Brick fired dense enough to resist spalling. Iron so thick that rust becomes a phase rather than a failure. Ceramics for channels and gutters. Concrete mixed with local ash and aggregate, dark and fine-grained. In newer industrial sections there are enameled panels and sealed glass, but even these are quickly fogged, spotted, or edged with mineral crusts. The city does not win against water; it incorporates water's signatures into its surfaces.

Because erosion is so advanced and the landscape so old, the boundaries between natural and built topography have blurred. Entire neighborhoods occupy former water-cut shelves. Quarry scars have softened into amphitheaters. Excavations become ponds. Spoil heaps are now weather-rounded hills with service roads spiraling up them. The cliffs beyond the city are pocked with ancient entries—mines, storage vaults, old shrines, ventilation adits—whose mouths are dark with generations of animal habitation. Even untouched rock nearby bears the same vocabulary as the architecture: vertical streaks, ledges populated by clustered bodies, shallow basins of rainwater, polished passages where things have moved the same route for centuries.

The atmosphere is warm, but the warmth is not desert warmth. It has weight. Air arrives carrying mineral scents, animal musk, wet dust, machine oil, hot metal, stale feed, and the cool iron smell of recent rain. Heat accumulates in masonry and radiates back after sunset, so nights do not entirely clear the day away. Mist forms in low cuts and under bridges at dawn, especially where warm effluent water meets cooler runoff. At times the entire high city can seem to float on its own exhalation, towers rising from pale vapor, the tops of cranes and chimneys clear in ordinary daylight while streets below are briefly reduced to sounds and moving silhouettes.

The light itself is straightforward, almost mercifully normal. No alien spectral drama, no perpetual eclipse. Sun, cloud, reflected brightness from wet surfaces, long evening shadows from towers and escarpments. This normality makes the rest of the place feel more believable and more strange by contrast. You can stand in full afternoon light and watch a million small lives moving through the gaps of an industrial quarter with no vegetation in sight, and the mind has no atmospheric excuse to retreat into fantasy. It must accept the place as a functioning world.

And it functions by rules that have become obvious only over time.

One rule is that empty space does not remain empty. Any sheltered volume of suitable temperature and moisture will be occupied: by animals, by droppings, by nests, by mineral accretions, by salvage, by pipes, by condensation, by sound. Maintenance here is less about cleanliness than about channeling occupation. Gratings are designed not to exclude fauna entirely—that would be impossible—but to admit only the sizes that do least damage. Feed stores are elevated and skirted in polished metal. Public seating is sloped, ridged, or warmed to discourage nesting. Certain bridges hum faintly at frequencies unpleasant to particular flocking species. Whole municipal departments likely exist just to negotiate breeding cycles.

Another rule is that water always reveals hierarchy. The richest districts are not the driest but the best drained. Water is valuable, omnipresent, and troublesome all at once. Those who control cisterns, pumping stations, purification works, and runoff gates govern more materially than those who merely own facades. Rooftops are shaped to catch, divert, or shed; courtyards dip into collection basins; every large building has a visible relationship to storage. In poorer quarters, stairwells drip and lower rooms remain permanently damp; in better-kept ones, the same water arrives disciplined in tiled rills and covered tanks.

A third rule is patience. This is not a place of rapid transformation. Even industrial activity feels settled into cycles too old to surprise anyone. Furnaces are fired, cooled, repaired, and fired again according to rhythms older than living memory. Animal populations bloom and crash seasonally but at such volume that absence is never total. Buildings are not demolished so much as repurposed layer by layer. Failures happen slowly first: a stain, a sag, a persistent leak, a change in which species gathers on which beam. Catastrophe, when it comes, is usually the final visible moment of a very long conversation between material and environment.

Beyond the central districts, the vastness of the setting becomes clearer. The city is not a compact mountain settlement but one node in a spread of habitation and works extending across the elevated country. Causeways run over shallow marshy pans of mineral water. Freight rails thread along escarpment edges. There are pumping fields, terraced extraction sites, distant processing towns joined by lines of pylons, reservoirs stepped down valley after valley, and broad tracts of bare rock humming softly with transmission. Weather moves across this high realm visibly: curtains of rain dragging under cloudbanks, sun laying silver on wet plateau, storms building from lower horizons and spending themselves against the uplift. The city’s sound does not end abruptly; it feathers outward into infrastructure and then into animal colonies inhabiting the margins of utility.

Without plants, color comes from other systems. The palette is iron red, basalt black, concrete gray, ocher runoff, white salt bloom, glazed brick blue, dull copper, oily rainbow on puddles, bone pale in heaps where animals gather and die, and the brief warm tones of fur, hide, shell, and flesh in motion. After rain, every surface deepens. Dark stone becomes nearly purple. Rust glows. Pools mirror sky between factory stacks. The city can look rich then, almost ceremonial, until one notices a drainage stair crowded shoulder to shoulder with feeding creatures and remembers that opulence here belongs as much to biology as to architecture.

There are places of relative quiet, but they are chosen rather than given. High service roofs where only the wind and distant machinery reach. Old inspection galleries cut back into dry rock. Heat towers in the lull between shifts. The uppermost edges of retaining walls at noon, when many species withdraw to shadow. In such places the vastness is easier to feel. Looking out, you see tiers of built mass stepping over geological age, all of it embedded in a plateau that long predates the city and will outlast its current arrangement. The settlement has become successful not by mastering the landscape but by making itself another durable stratum within it.

What is most unusual is not any single spectacle, but the coexistence of so many crowded things in a place that remains fundamentally bare. Wetness without greenery. Warmth without lushness. Urban density without the soft buffer of parks or gardens. Overwhelming animal life on a mineral, weathered highland of industry. It feels at first like an omission, as though something should be growing and is not. Then, after time, the absence becomes structural. Plants are not missing; they are irrelevant to how this world has solved itself.

So the city persists: dripping, echoing, occupied.

At dusk, lights come on in bands and clusters across terraces and towers. The wet streets reflect them in fractured lines. Steam rises from vents. Flocks wheel black against a still-bright sky. Down in the channels, blind things disturb the shallow water. Somewhere pumps begin their evening cycle, heavy and regular as a second heartbeat under the stone. And all over the high plateau, in walls, roofs, culverts, cisterns, lofts, and ledges, innumerable animals settle, shuffle, call to one another, or wake for the night shift, while the old masonry gives back the day’s heat and the city glistens as if it has just emerged from rain, even when no rain has fallen at all.
