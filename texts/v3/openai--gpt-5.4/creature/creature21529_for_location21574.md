What lives there is a people who have learned to behave less like conquerors of a place than like one more long-evolved species inside it.

At first they might seem austere simply because the city is austere. In that pale mineral brightness anything soft, loud, or extravagant would look temporary, almost foolish. So the inhabitants would likely appear lean in outline and economical in movement, shaped by ramps, stone dust, cool air, and a culture that has had to notice consequences. Not fragile—nothing that remained here for generations could be fragile—but spare, tendon-clear, balance-conscious. They would walk as if every surface has a history, because it does. Even children would probably place their feet well.

I do not think they are a people of broad gestures and careless noise. Stone has trained that out of them. They would speak quietly when they speak at all, and much of their life would happen in signs, touches, glances, little directed clicks or taps that do not carry far. Not because they are secretive by nature, exactly, but because loudness would feel crude to them, a kind of civic rudeness and perhaps a physical danger. In such a city, restraint would not read as repression. It would read as competence.

Their bodies may bear the marks of living in bright shade rather than in sun. Skin perhaps in a range of pale browns, umbers, grays, and chalk-dusted darks depending on ancestry and labor, but all of it altered by the place: less weather-cracked by wind and heat than surface peoples, more often powdered with lime, salt, and stone flour. Faces with eyes adapted not to darkness but to subtleties of diffuse brightness—good at reading sheen, fracture glint, changing translucence overhead. Maybe the iris colors that matter among them are not startlingly vivid but precise: slate, amber, diluted hazel, mineral green so rare it acquires significance. Or perhaps eye color matters less than the practiced motion of the gaze: upward to the roof, sideways to a warning cord, down to a dust cone, always reading.

They would likely dress in layers suited to constant adjustment. Not heavy robes dragging in grit, but wrapped and belted garments that can be vented in a warm draft, tightened in a chill pocket, tucked for climbing, loosened for work. Felted fiber, woven reed or imported plant cloth where available, leather softened by long use, fungal textile if they know how to make one, hide aprons for masons and cistern-keepers. Their clothing would carry color deliberately, because color means something in a pale city. A sash might identify district, vent-right, water duty, clan, trade corridor, mourning state, roof-watch obligation. Dyes would not be scattered casually; they would strike the eye the way a bell strikes the ear. Children might wear brighter markers than adults because in all that brightness and stone, to be seen is safety.

Jewelry, if they have it, would probably favor significance over gleam. Bone toggles carved with fault patterns. Soft-metal wire used sparingly and repaired often. Polished shell brought from trade and worn as inherited wealth. Little mica plates that flash when one turns. Charms not to attract luck in the abstract, but to remember practical truths: the shape of a safe echo, the map of a family seep, the sign of a dangerous roof tone. It feels like a place where adornment and mnemonic would blur.

Their hair—if they are human or humanlike—would be managed around labor and dust. Bound close, braided, wrapped, greased against grit, threaded perhaps with cords whose knots signify household or task. Long loose hair would be beautiful in ceremony because it is impractical in work. There may be professions recognizable by headwear alone: reflector-tenders in pale hoods against glare, animal drivers with padded caps, deep-vent workers with mouth-cloths and oil-darkened wraps, cistern keepers marked by white mineral bands at wrist and throat.

But what most defines them would not be appearance. It would be temperament shaped by continual low-grade instability. They would be patient, but not placid. Alert without theatrical anxiety. Habitually adaptive. A people for whom changing plans is not evidence of failure but of paying attention. If a courtyard becomes a seep-field, then of course the baskets move, the children’s route changes, the ward-signs are repainted, and the old women who remember the previous seep there come to supervise. Their pride would lie not in permanence but in aptness: in making the right adjustment quickly, elegantly, with minimal waste.

This would make them excellent rememberers.

Not merely literate or oral in a generic sense, but trained in layered practical memory. They would remember not only names and ancestors, but drips, crack notes, roof seasons, the years of warm wind, the taste of certain cisterns before a mineral shift, the old route around a chamber that collapsed three generations ago, which wall used to sing before it sheared. Their historical consciousness would be topographic and acoustic. A child might be corrected not by “that is forbidden” but by “that is what the stone did last time.” In such a culture, elders need not be rulers to be indispensable. Anyone who recalls an older behavior of the basin carries power.

They might organize themselves less by abstract neighborhoods than by relationships to systems: those of the upper light, those of the cistern chains, those of pack ramps, reflector lines, vent mouths, insect towers, herd courts, drift flats. A “family” may be both blood and maintenance unit. Some lineages would belong to a particular fault or roof panel the way surface peoples belong to a field or riverbank. One household knows the mica mirrors. Another reads the bone chimes. Another breeds the tunnel-hounds. Another can hear, from a hammer blow, whether a stair pin has begun to fail. Prestige might attach not to accumulation but to trustworthiness under pressure.

Their religion, if that word applies, would be difficult to separate from municipal practice. They would almost certainly ritualize attention. Offerings to vents, yes, but perhaps these are also air-readings. Processions around old boundaries, yes, but perhaps these are inspections encoded as sacred tradition so they are never neglected. Their gods, if named, may not be sky-fathers or underworld queens in simple form. More likely powers of compression, seepage, balance, reflected light, patient stone, migrating hunger, stored water, warned danger. Or else they revere ancestors who first learned to hear the basin correctly. They may pray by maintaining. They may worship by recalibrating mirrors and cleaning channels before a holy silence.

That silence matters. A people living here would know collective listening as an experience more intimate than collective shouting. Imagine a whole district pausing—animals stilled, tools laid down, children hushed—not in fear exactly, but in concentration, each person attending to the mountain’s tiny declarations. Such moments would shape psychology. They would produce people who are not uncomfortable with quiet, who do not rush to fill it, who understand that what is not said may be structurally important.

This does not mean they are somber all the time. In fact I suspect their humor would be sharp, dry, physical, and highly local. Jokes about districts defined by bad condensation. Teasing imitations of different vent accents if the air changes how voices sound in certain wards. Playful competitions in echo-reading among children. Courtship through the exchange of cleverly chosen color markers or through offers to share a favored light patch at working hours. Laughter would happen, just not wastefully. It would be compressed and intense, like much else there.

As for governance: whoever lives here must be excellent at distributed authority. No single ruler could micromanage a city whose conditions alter by the hour. So perhaps councils of ward-keepers, cistern measurers, animal-route masters, fault listeners, light stewards. Decisions would likely emerge from those who monitor flows rather than those who merely command persons. Water law would be ancient. Vibration law even older. Property itself might be conditional in a way surface societies find strange: one may possess a dwelling, but not the draft passing through it; one may inherit a reflector angle, but not if it deprives a lower hall; one may maintain a seep niche only while it seeps.

Their children would grow up preternaturally capable in ways outsiders might miss. Able to carry a brimming jar across polished stone without spilling. Able to identify domestic animals by hoof-scrape alone. Able to tell, from a parent’s hand pressure on the shoulder, whether to freeze, duck, reroute, or keep walking. They would play with pebbles, bones, cords, little ceramic channels, miniature pens for insects, balancing games, drift-racing in airflow. They might learn counting through jar chains and herd tallies, geometry through retaining walls and reflector angles, ethics through water distribution. Perhaps every child at some age is taught to sit alone in a safe gallery and map the sounds of an hour.

Because of the animals, the inhabitants would smell faintly of shared economy: hide, wax, milk, dust, mineral damp. They would be deeply knowledgeable about nonhuman behavior. A twitch in a pack beast’s ears, a shift in the ceiling colonies, a silence among the insect towers—these would all be read as environmental data. They may trust animals in some ways more than instruments. Certain breeds might be half-sacred not because they are adored sentimentally, but because generations of survival have made their sensitivities invaluable.

And the people themselves, in turn, would seem a little animal to outsiders—not in savagery, but in attunement. Quick to test the air with nostrils and tongue. Quick to rest a palm on a wall. Quick to pause mid-conversation because some minute change overhead has entered awareness. Their city has trained them into an elegance of responsiveness.

They are probably hard to surprise but easy to startle, and the distinction matters. Surprise is conceptual; startle is bodily. Their nerves would remain close to the surface, not from weakness but from long usefulness. Yet because they are practiced, the startle becomes action almost at once. A shouted warning might not even be necessary: one person glances up, two others begin moving jars, a driver turns a herd aside, a child starts gathering marker stones. This is a society that has turned coordination into reflex.

Would they welcome outsiders? Carefully. This is not a xenophobic place by necessity, but it is one where ignorance is dangerous. A newcomer who stamps, shouts, wastes water, startles herd trains, or meddles with colored cords could kill people without intending to. So hospitality would be instructive, bounded, supervised. Guests might be marked visibly until they learn route customs. Children would be told not to follow them into unstable wards. Trade would be valued, especially for timber, fibers, metals, medicines, dyes—but trust would accrue through demonstrated attention.

In the far reaches, where the city thins, there may also live those who prefer less entanglement: cairn-keepers, scavenger-hunters, salt harvesters, listeners stationed at lonely margins. They would be considered eccentric but necessary, touched by too much brightness and emptiness. They know the wandering fauna and the moods of abandoned pens. They return with warnings before others hear them. Some may be revered; some gently mistrusted; all are part of the larger metabolism.

If one seeks a single phrase for what lives there, perhaps it is this:

A bright-under-stone people, pale with dust and vivid by choice, descended not merely from settlers but from maintainers. Quiet-handed, animal-wise, exacting about water and sound. Citizens of seepage and reflection. A civilization of listeners whose oldest art is adaptation, and whose deepest belief is that survival depends on entering into correct relationship with pressure, light, memory, and the living mountain.

And around them, with them, because no such city belongs to one species alone, lives a whole companion ecology of managed and half-managed creatures: gliding roof-flocks, rasping shellbacks, burrowers in the damp grit, wax insects in towers, tunnel-hounds, milk herds, dust-footed pack beasts, pale scavengers at the margins. The people are the city’s mind, perhaps, but the animals are its circulation, nerves, appetite, warning system, and moving wealth.

Together they make something rare: not simply life surviving underground, but an old urban ecosystem that has become so fitted to stone-bright instability that its inhabitants—human and otherwise—would look, to anyone arriving from elsewhere, less like residents than like expressions of the place itself.
