What lives there is a people of maintenance.

Not in the narrow sense of janitors or mechanics alone, though there are many of those, but a whole resident kind shaped by the fact that their world does not stay itself unless someone notices it every day. They are not grand subterranean exiles or solemn inheritors of a sacred depth. They are practical, humid, watchful citizens of a place that sweats. Their intelligence is distributed into habits: how to hear when a pump is laboring wrong, how to smell a new seep before it becomes a stain, how to read the agitation of birds as a sign that a vent has changed pressure, how to step over a gutter without looking, how to judge from the feel of a handrail whether the night has been hotter than usual.

They would likely look softened and toughened at once by the climate. Skin always carrying a sheen, clothes chosen for tolerating damp rather than displaying status, sleeves rolled, collars open, hems repeatedly mended. Nothing crisp lasts here. Even authority would have mildew at the edges. Hair tends to curl or lie flat with moisture. Leather belts darken with use. Tools are wiped constantly and never truly dry. Their bodies would be acclimated to warmth in the way coastal bodies are acclimated to salt air: not immune to discomfort, but formed around it. They move without wasting energy, because haste in thick air is expensive. They are patient walkers, economical climbers, people who pause often not from indecision but from listening.

They are close to the scale of their district. That matters. In a huge underground realm, one imagines castes, pageantry, abstraction. Here one imagines recognizability. People know one another by trade, by route, by the sound of a cart, by which cats trail after them, by which stair they prefer in flood season. The pump-tender knows the woman who sells cistern fish; the mason knows the boy who cleans lamp chimneys; the kitchen knows who comes off shift from the hot rooms and wants extra salt in broth. Their city is large enough for neighborhoods and small enough for reputations to cling like condensation.

I think many of them would be second- or third-generation dwellers, not pioneers anymore. The first cutters and stay-behinds may have been desperate, opportunistic, or visionary; those living there now are native to compromise. They no longer compare every wall to the open sky. They have developed tastes, superstitions, etiquette, and maybe even humor specifically suited to a buried wet district. They can tell “old damp” from “bad damp.” They distinguish harmless rust from structural rust at a glance. They have names for categories an outsider would collapse together: five or six kinds of mud, several kinds of drip, many temperaments of bird. Children grow up learning not simply directions but layers—upper walk, mid-lane, sump edge, roost loft, hot pipe run—as naturally as others learn avenue and square.

Their domestic life would be compact and elevated where possible. People here do not trust floors entirely; they trust platforms, pallets, shelves, hooks, racks, suspended things. Beds off the ground. Cabinets with mesh doors. Food hung, sealed, smoked, pickled, or kept in ceramic crocks weighted against pests. Homes would be dense with practical adaptations: extra basins under known drips, polished stones at thresholds to wipe boots, folded netting for bird season, shutters not against sunlight but against flutter and dust and sudden steam. Fabrics are chosen not for grandeur but for how they behave when they can never quite become fully dry. A house-proud person here is someone whose lamp glass is clear, whose corners do not mildew, whose grain bins show no sign of rat teeth, whose walls smell of soap rather than wet plaster.

And because the city is inseparable from animals, the people who live there are not cleanly separate from them either. They are managers of coexistence. Not sentimental animal-lovers, not exterminators, but negotiators.

They leave some rafters to the swifts because the birds eat insects and because dislodging them would only send them elsewhere. They keep cats and are in turn kept by cats, tolerating a level of feline autonomy that surface towns might call ownership only half-seriously. They raise certain frogs near basins or at least refrain from disturbing them because they mark water quality by thriving or failing. They know which snakes are useful and which conduits to avoid in breeding season. Children collect eggs from approved ledges and are beaten or scolded for taking from the wrong nests. Someone breeds pigeons or swift-like birds intentionally in side lofts for meat, fertilizer, or message-carrying; someone else curses the feral ones while still sweeping and selling their droppings to mushroom growers. Rats are the enemy, but also a permanent fact, so warfare against them has become procedural rather than dramatic: traps, terriers perhaps, sealed bins, regular inspections, shared gossip about outbreaks.

That may be the strongest trait of whoever lives there: they are procedural. They survive by routines so old and so repeated they have acquired the dignity of ritual without ever ceasing to be useful. Morning rounds. Drain-clearing days. Lamp-scrubbing shifts. Seasonal shuttering of certain lofts. Scheduled dredging of settling tanks. Marking walls with chalk when seepage changes. Listening at boiler doors before unlatching. Tapping beams. Checking grain seals. Rotating stores. Counting birds if disease is rumored. Measuring water in the sump against notches cut by generations into a post or stair.

From outside this could look drab, but from within it is culture. Their songs, if they have work songs, likely keep time with pumps or cart wheels. Their proverbs would come from seepage, bracing, rot, and patient accumulation: open the drain before the flood, trust old stone until it sweats black, never stand under a silent flock, things like that. Their festivals, if they celebrate, might be linked not to harvest fields but to maintenance victories and environmental thresholds—the first reopening of a flooded lane, the annual cleaning of the great cistern, the brief period when one shaft drops cool air from above, the hatching season of the roof-birds, the month when mushrooms are at their thickest on the compost racks. They would likely decorate where decoration can survive: glazed tile, enamel signs, painted pipe-bands, charms of shell or polished brass, bird bones carved into toggles, bright cloth in canteens where steam can soften but not entirely ruin it.

Socially, I imagine them less hierarchical than interdependent. Expertise is tangible here. A person who can keep a failing pump alive through flood week, read the ceiling, set a retaining wall plumb in sweating clay, or cure mildew from records without destroying them is valuable in a way everyone can understand. Prestige clings to competence. There may be district officials, owners, engineers, foremen, but status cannot float too far free of material reality underground. A proud administrator still needs the old woman who knows which cistern fish are safe after a pipe rupture, the mason who can tell whether the bulging marl is bluffing, the bird-keeper who senses sickness in the lofts before it spreads to market fowl.

So the people living there are probably excellent noticers. Their attention is low, lateral, continuous. Surface cultures often teach people to look outward and far; this one teaches them to look near and around. At shoulder height for rust. At ankle height for water movement. Overhead for guano, loose hooks, changing nest density. They read glisten, stain, smell, residue, silence. Silence especially might matter. In a place of continual sound, the absence of one familiar note can be more alarming than a bang. A stopped fan, a quiet pump, an empty roost at the wrong hour, frogs gone suddenly mute from a favored basin—these are messages.

Who else lives there, beyond the human residents, is almost a parallel citizenry.

The birds are the visible sky-substitute of the district. They occupy the upper volume the way weather occupies a surface town. Their departures and returns alter the feel of whole halls. Their droppings feed gardens and fungi, foul machines, mark neglect, and create work. Their nesting rights are probably contested in ordinances and in shouted arguments. Their bodies make motion where architecture leaves space. If one asked a child what truly owns the canteen ceiling or the freight arches, the answer might be: the swifts.

The amphibians are the district’s pulse. Frogs and salamanders inhabit its confidence in water. They would be read as signs of persistence, perhaps of luck, perhaps just of healthy damp. In a place where water is not a spectacle but an ongoing negotiation, little wet-bodied creatures become natural auditors of infrastructure. People may not romanticize them, but they would notice their presence the way farmers notice bees.

The rats are its shadow bureaucracy: omnipresent, adaptive, never fully defeated, living off every inefficiency. Whoever lives there measures itself partly by how well it excludes rats from particular thresholds. Rat-proofing may be a craft. Rat stories surely circulate. Some residents hate them with spiritual intensity; others respect them grimly as fellow urbanists.

The cats are the district’s small aristocrats, moving by rights no one granted and everyone confirms. In warm machine rooms, along market beams, beside kitchens and storerooms, they serve and are indulged. In such a place cats become part charm, part labor, part local myth. Specific cats would be known better than some officials.

And there are the invisible or half-visible residents: fungi in damp timber, bacterial films in warm channels, insects breeding in the lamplight margins, blind fish in cisterns, colonies of things that are spoken of collectively rather than individually. These are not “vermin” in a simple way. They are indicators, nuisances, food sources, contaminants, recurring neighbors. The people of the district would think ecologically without using the word. They know what follows what. More standing water means more insects; more insects mean more bats and frogs; blocked gutters mean rat detours; spilled grain changes the whole lane by evening.

If there is a type of person especially native to this place, it is the child who grows up in it. Such children would be astonishingly sure-footed, deeply warned, and difficult to impress with ordinary grime. They know by memory which pipes are warm enough to sleep near in cold spells, which markets feed scraps to birds, which old corridor sings before a pressure release, which sump platforms are forbidden not because of strictness but because one wrong step means fever, bites, or a week’s smell in your clothes. They would have pet names for toads, favorite cats, secret paths along maintenance ledges. They may be pale compared to surface children or perhaps simply lamplit in complexion, with eyes trained to amber light and moving shadows. They inherit not romance but fluency.

The elderly here would be repositories of environmental memory. Not just names and dates, but “the wall by the old canteen always sweats before the south pumps fail,” “that stair had to be moved after the wet year,” “there used to be herons in the upper cistern before the new fans drove them off.” Their recollections would map the district as a living argument between repair and relapse. In a place never finished, age is not attachment to a lost ideal but expertise in recurring instability.

I do not think the inhabitants are gloomy, though outsiders might imagine so. Constant maintenance produces irritation, yes, and fatigue, but also intimacy. They live close to consequences. Their labor answers directly to material change. That often creates a dry wit, a collective realism, an impatience with fantasy, and a tenderness expressed practically: someone fixed your leak, brought your laundry in before the steam burst, warned your child off a nesting ledge, traded lamp oil for mushroom broth. Care here is infrastructural before it is verbal.

If there is any broader temperament to them, it is this: they do not dream of conquering their environment, and they do not surrender to it either. They bargain. Every day they bargain with seepage, rust, mold, vermin, heat, gravity, and the astonishing fertility of neglected corners. In return they get shelter, industry, food, community, and a form of belonging that is inseparable from upkeep. Their pride, if you touched it, would lie not in saying “we built this” but “we keep this.”

So whoever lives there is a compound creature: workers, families, traders, tenders, scavengers, cooks, masons, children, ratters, fish-raisers, bird-wardens, pump mechanics, old people with mineral wisdom, cats threading among them, swifts writing weather overhead, frogs sounding from basins, lizards claiming valves as if metal were sun-warmed rock.

A citizenry of warm stone and damp iron.
An urban species of the under-marsh.
Not masters of the buried district, and not merely its occupants either, but one more successful life-form it has produced.
