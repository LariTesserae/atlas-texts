What lives there is a people of gradients.

Not a single type, not one face repeated, but a whole civic temperament shaped by wet stone, inaccessible corners, and the need to notice. The city seems likely to produce inhabitants who are practical before they are dramatic, but not blunt; reserved, perhaps, yet intensely observant. They live among systems that can’t be ignored for long, so they become readers of small signs. They look at a wall and also at the damp line on the wall. They hear a change in the pitch of water in a downspout. They know when fog is merely thick and when it will delay freight, when a stair is only slick and when it has become dangerous, when a patch of moss is picturesque and when it means a seam is staying wet too long.

The people here are probably sure-footed in body and mind. Even the unathletic learn routes the way lowland people learn weather apps: instinctively, constantly, with backups. Children grow up with vertical literacy. They know which public lifts fail in rain, which covered crossings stay dry longest, which terraces keep wind, which ones collect it, where one can cut through boiler-warm corridors in winter, where a handrail will numb the palm, where frogs can be heard after dusk, where blackberries have overrun a fence, where the city smells of yeast at dawn and hot metal by noon. They do not think of this as expertise. It is just being from there.

Their movements would have a characteristic economy. People in the high city do not waste effort because effort is measurable in stairs climbed, loads carried, detours taken around closures and seepage and repairs. They travel with weather in mind and with one eye always on infrastructure. A resident leaving home may carry, almost without thinking, a hooded coat, gloves that can grip wet rail, a key for a shared service gate, a folded market sack, maybe twine, maybe a small torch, maybe a rag for wiping a bench or saddle or bicycle seat. They know how to brace on slick inclines, how to step from stone to grating, how to duck under scaffolds, how to continue a conversation while climbing.

Because warmth is local and precious, the people likely gather by thermal geography. Their social life clings to vents, sheltered courts, bakery windows, greenhouse margins, bathhouses, tea stalls under viaducts, laundries where steam turns everyone briefly benevolent. They are connoisseurs of lee and overhang. They choose meeting places by dripline, not by address alone. “Under the third stair after the tram stop, by the warm wall,” is enough. “At the landing where you can hear the spillway.” “In the passage between the cisterns if it isn’t flooding.” These are precise instructions in a city where precision is kindness.

I think they would be a layered people, much like the city itself. On the surface, many may seem self-contained. There is too much public nearness for everybody to perform intimacy all the time. One learns a courteous compression: making room on stairs, sharing lifts, giving way to maintenance carts, speaking briefly under dripping awnings. But this reserve would not be coldness. It would be the social form of dense habitation—an agreement not to crowd one another more than the architecture already does. Beneath that, there would be strong habits of interdependence. You cannot live among valves, retaining walls, common crossings, and hidden conduits without developing a practical communal ethic. If your leak becomes everyone’s landslip, privacy has limits. If a blocked drain above a school stair affects three neighborhoods, maintenance is social. So the inhabitants are probably helpful in specific, ungrand ways: lending tools, passing along closure news, clearing channels before a storm, watching one another’s roof loads, watering a neighbor’s terrace beds from runoff while they’re away, phoning in a new crack, bringing soup to someone whose lift is out.

The city would favor certain callings, and those callings would influence character. A great many inhabitants must be custodians of flow: plumbers, drain crews, pump attendants, turbine technicians, scaffolders, lift mechanics, gardeners who are half hydraulic engineers, inspectors of masonry and rust, tram operators, weather readers, roofers, glaziers, canal and spillway keepers, market workers adept at moving goods through impossible sections, teachers who know six alternate routes for every school excursion, cooks who understand humidity as an ingredient. Even office workers, clerks, archivists, administrators here would live close enough to the material systems to think materially. Paperwork would not be abstract for long; it would become a gate opening, a slope shored, a freight line rerouted, a cistern reopened.

So whoever lives there is probably difficult to impress with grand theories and easy to impress with competence. They respect the person who arrives with the right gasket more than the one with the best speech. Their humor may be dry and infrastructural: jokes about gradients, pressure, the eternal leak in a municipal office no one can defeat, the stair that appears on no map but everyone uses, the fig tree thriving obscenely beside an exhaust vent while more respectable plantings fail. They may nickname districts after weather behavior, pumps, old retaining disasters, vanished channels. They likely tell time by shifts and light and water load as much as by clocks.

And because the city absorbs rather than discards, its people may be similarly accretive. They keep, mend, repurpose. Clothing is patched well. Containers are reused. A broken rail becomes a trellis. Old tanks become workshops, then studios, then mushroom sheds, then overflow again when needed. Families pass down not only objects but route knowledge, maintenance lore, inherited warnings: never trust that parapet in freeze-thaw, there’s an old void behind the west wall, the broad stair was for animals once so the treads are uneven, the rosemary lane ices first, the lower market floods ankle-deep if rain comes from the southeast. Memory itself becomes a civic utility.

Their homes, where we glimpse them, would probably be compact, vertical, damp-managed, full of hooks and racks and shelves near the door. Boots in ordered rows. Spare towels for condensation. Windows cracked strategically. Plants everywhere that can survive the light: ferns, herbs, hardy geraniums, perhaps tomatoes under glass if they have a warm exposure. Kitchens that understand steam. Laundry lines threaded in covered corners. Furniture arranged not for grandeur but for dryness, warmth, and access to the one patch of good light. A house here is less a sealed refuge from climate than a negotiated chamber within it.

What about their faces? It is tempting to imagine them pale from cloudlight, ruddy from climbs, hair beaded with mist, sleeves darkened at the cuff, boots always carrying some trace of the city. But more than appearance, they likely wear a look of partial attention—to one another, yes, but also to the systems around them. A person pauses in conversation because they have heard a pump change note. Someone glances up automatically at an overhead channel during heavy rain. Someone smells iron and says, almost idly, that a gate is probably open downstream. Their senses are trained by context.

There may also be a particular local generosity toward plants. In a place where greenery does not arrive as pastoral abundance but as insistence—through cracks, over walls, along gutters—people would become collaborators with opportunistic life. Not sentimental gardeners exactly, though some would be that too. More like practitioners of guided flourishing. They know which roots stabilize and which split stone, which moss insulates and which traps too much damp, which vines cool a wall and which invade a drain, which volunteer birch must come out and which self-seeded herb can stay. They prune with civic feeling. They tolerate useful wildness. They are intimate with compost.

Since large animals are scarce, the emotional field that elsewhere might be occupied by livestock or dogs in numbers may here be filled by other attachments: to birds that return to certain roofs, to the cats around depots, to particular greenhouse frogs, to an old fig espalier, to a chestnut on a stair landing, to a stubborn patch of foxglove by a pumping station, to the sound of one’s local spill channel in rain. They would not romanticize nature in the abstract because they live with growth as maintenance reality. But they might love specific living things fiercely.

The city also seems to breed patience without passivity. Weather causes delay; seepage reroutes life; systems require tending. So its inhabitants are probably adaptable, but not lax. They expect interruption and prepare for it. They know that “later” might mean after the drain crew clears the grating, after the fog lifts, after the retaining wall inspection, after the afternoon squall. Yet this does not make them vague. On the contrary, it makes them exact about contingencies. Plans are made with branches. If the upper tram closes, take the school roof crossing. If the west lift is out, use the goods stair before market closes. If the rain strengthens, move the stall in by three meters. Their flexibility is procedural.

Such a place might also produce a strong municipal culture—not necessarily patriotic in banners and slogans, but deeply invested in the shared functioning of things. People argue about drainage, roof loads, planting policy, access rights, public stair repairs. They know the names of weather fronts and reservoir levels. They complain about maintenance delays with the passion other places reserve for sports. Local prestige may attach to those who keep systems running, and local scandal to those who neglect common obligations. A blocked gutter above a public route is not a personal oversight; it is bad citizenship.

At the same time, artists would live there too, but they would be artists of the same material world. Painters of wet surfaces and layered sections. Poets of foghorns and boiler heat. Musicians attuned to drips, hums, muffled transit, distant metal. Architects who think in drains before facades. Dyers, brewers, glassworkers, printmakers, metal restorers, moss growers, mapmakers of unofficial stairs. The city’s subdued light would foster a subtler aesthetic: matte glazes, deep greens, smoke blues, rust reds, lamp amber, saturated brick purple, silvered wood. Beauty there would be trusted most when it survives use.

And I suspect the people who live there have a particular relationship to danger: not thrill-seeking, not naïve, but companionable. Hidden drops, slick steps, load-bearing mysteries, old voids, water under pressure—these are facts. So residents become calm around risk because panic is useless, but also incapable of pretending that systems manage themselves. They teach children not fear but protocol. Hold the rail. Listen before entering the shaft crossing. Don’t stand under a loaded crane. Report the crack. Respect the fog. In another place these might feel like stern rules; here they are forms of belonging.

If there is a shared spiritual character, it may come from this daily encounter with invisibly linked structures. People learn that what happens above affects below; that buried things still govern the present; that maintenance is a form of care; that neglected margins become failures; that water remembers routes; that adaptation accumulates into beauty. Even the religious life of such a city, if visible, would probably be architectural and practical: chapels in buttresses, shrines by pumps, festivals tied to first thaw, reservoir opening, flood remembrance, harvest from terrace orchards, annual stair cleaning, communal replanting after slips. Reverence would attach to continuance.

So who lives there?

Citizens of damp competence. Terrace growers. Lift sharers. Leak listeners. Keepers of alternate routes. People who know that a city is not its skyline but its drains, not its monuments but its access points, not its claims of permanence but its endless adjustments. They are not rugged individualists, though they may look self-possessed; the terrain has educated them too thoroughly in dependence. They are urban mountaineers without heroics, machinists with gardeners’ hands, householders who think like stewards of retaining walls, workers who move through mist as if through a familiar room.

And perhaps, after long enough, they begin to resemble the city in the deepest sense: densely made, weather-marked, somewhat cool at first touch, full of hidden circulations, resistant to waste, unexpectedly lush where conditions allow, and held together not by any final mastery over instability, but by a thousand ordinary acts of attention.
