What lives there is not an animal in the ordinary sense, nor a people, nor even a single species of plant spread thin across a difficult country. What lives there is a first ecology: a slow, heat-made republic of seam-dwellers, condensers, crust-formers, root-priers, and patient films. Life there is small because the world is still too new in the wrong way. It has not yet learned abundance. So whatever lives there must live by intervals, by residue, by timing, by exploiting the brief moments when the manufactured ground accidentally behaves like habitat.

The dominant inhabitants would be things that can survive almost entirely in suspension between wet and dry. Thin green-black skins in the cooler joints. Mineral-tolerant algal films that wake in dew and go dormant by full sun. Mosses so shallow they are almost discolorations rather than growths, occupying undersides of tilted slabs, shaded lips of retaining edges, and the bottoms of condensation hollows where dawn lasts a few minutes longer. They would not spread continuously. They would appear as script—short passages written wherever the plain offers a pause in heat.

In the cracks where dust has begun to gather, there would be rooted pioneers with a severe, economical look. Not lush things. Not anything broad-leaved or soft. They would live low, gripping the made surface as if expecting to be erased. Some would be wiry mats, with stems the color of old straw or oxidized metal, their visible greenery minimal and seasonal, most of the organism invested below in hair-thin roots following invisible moisture along adhesive failures and thermal fractures. Others would be beadlike succulents, storing a single shower for weeks, their tissues bitter, mineral-heavy, and dull with bloom against the light. In deeper subsidences and old settling pans, where fines and runoff briefly agree to stay together, there would be knot-rooted clusters with surprisingly deep anchorage, not because the substrate is fertile but because it is unstable. They would live by fastening themselves to the very process by which the place slowly comes apart.

Around periodic seeps and temporary basin margins, a different cast appears: reedlike growths, pale and rigid, less like marsh plants than instruments for counting recurrence. They would rise after rain in sparse ranks, make seed quickly if they make seed at all, then stand dead for a long time, holding dust, slowing runoff, and creating tiny lee zones where the next generation of finer life can accumulate. Their dead stems would matter almost as much as their living tissue. In a place without animals to chew, trample, shred, or carry them away, every fallen strand remains as structure. The living and the dead would interlock into little traps for the future.

There may also be lichens, though they would be unusual ones: chalk-pale, rust-dark, or sulfur-gray scales feeding directly on the exposed mineral skin, etching, staining, and very gradually roughening surfaces that are otherwise too clean, too hard, too newly made. On cooler east-facing scarps where condensation returns often enough to be predictable, these crusts might organize into bands visible at a distance, making entire lines of artificial wall appear lightly brushed with weather before weather has truly arrived. They would be among the first citizens of permanence there—slow enough to seem absent, tough enough to become background.

And there would be fungi, though mostly hidden. Not forests of mushrooms, nothing theatrical. Instead, threadlike interiors in the accumulating dust beneath shrub skirts, in damp seams after rain, in pockets of dead roots and trapped fines under slab edges. Their lives would be episodic, flashing briefly into fruiting only under rare conjunctions of moisture and coolness. Most of the time they would be invisible labor: loosening, binding, digesting the first tiny stores of organic matter in a world still overwhelmingly inorganic. If the place is becoming land, they are among the translators.

The larger plants—if “larger” can be used for things still modest against that scale—would seem less like inhabitants than like outposts. Scattered shrubs with gray, heat-reflective stems and leaves reduced almost to a gesture. Each one would stand in its own made microclimate, ringed by trapped dust and darkened soil-to-be, accumulating around itself a halo of altered chemistry and slightly moderated temperature. They would be crucial not because they are numerous, but because they are local engines of conversion. Under them, shade persists. Moisture lingers a little longer. Fallen matter collects instead of blowing on. A second, finer ecology begins beneath each crown. Seen from afar, they are punctuation marks. Seen closely, they are workshops where the future is being assembled.

Because there are no animals, whatever lives there must also do all the work animals often do elsewhere. There are no burrowers to turn material, no grazers to pulse growth, no pollinators to shuttle between flowers, no scavengers to redistribute nutrients. So life has to rely on the atmosphere, gravity, fracture, and chance hydraulics. Reproduction would be austere. Spores, fragments, seeds that can wait years, seeds that can lodge in a seam and survive temperatures that would kill a softer world. Many of the inhabitants would spread clonally, or by simple breakage and re-rooting, or by taking advantage of runoff that travels along the old logic of construction. To live there is to understand the geometry of flow better than any creature understands a riverbank.

If one insists on imagining a single “who” that lives there, then the truest answer may be: succession lives there. Early succession stripped to fundamentals. The resident life is provisional, opportunistic, exacting, and persistent. It does not dominate the built lowland; it interprets it. It reads every cant, lip, seam, blister, trough, and shadow as a possible brief opening. It is a population of specialists in almost-land. Not the life of a mature desert, which has had ages to negotiate its terms, but the life of raw exposure just beginning to discover where it can remain.

And there is another inhabitant, if one allows processes to count as living presences: weathering itself, assisted by life. Dust lives there in the way pollen lives elsewhere—mobile, settling, consequential. Moisture lives there as habit rather than supply, recurring in certain hollows with enough fidelity to shape communities. Heat lives there as the great selector, killing the incautious, rewarding the low, the pale, the waxed, the dormant, the deep-rooted, the quickly waking and quickly sleeping. The organisms that survive are less separate from these forces than braided into them. Each patch of green is partly a plant and partly a local treaty among radiative cooling, mineral texture, seam depth, and the memory of the last rain.

So the inhabitants are humble, but not negligible. They are the first makers after the makers: films that darken stone, roots that prise joints apart, stems that catch dust, crusts that hold moisture for one minute longer than bare aggregate would, hidden threads that turn dead tissue into the first true organics. They are not yet enough to soften the place into familiarity. From a distance the lowland still looks almost lifeless. But that is because what lives there lives at the scale of beginnings.

If you walked there for days, you would start to notice that life keeps choosing the same kinds of sites: the underside of heat-lifted plates, the cool toe of east-facing walls, the seam below an old flow guide, the margin where a temporary basin shrinks, the chalk-fringed edge of a runoff pan, the shadow cast by a fin that no longer serves its original purpose. The inhabitants are cartographers of accident. They occupy not territory but opportunities repeated across immense distances.

In time, if nothing interrupts, they will make the next inhabitants possible. More dust, more hold, more shade, more retained damp, more organics, more fracture, more true soil. But for now the living population of that place would look like this: sparse mats, mineral films, seam mosses, lichen crusts, rooted knots, bitter succulents, gray shrubs, hidden fungal threads, and all the dormant seeds and spores waiting inside the hot made world for the next brief permission to become visible.
