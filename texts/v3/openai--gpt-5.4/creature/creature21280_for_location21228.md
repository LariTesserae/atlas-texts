What lives there is patient.

Not patient in the gentle sense, not serene, but patient the way deposits are patient, the way a pump is patient, the way an organism survives by fitting itself so exactly to a hostile medium that survival stops looking dramatic and becomes style. The city does not favor creatures of burst, bloom, flock, chatter, or parade. It makes another kind of inhabitant: coated, procedural, half-obscured, more often inferred than seen. Its people and its lesser lives alike belong to condensation, residue, and repetition.

At first you think the city is inhabited by workers, and that is true, but incomplete. It is inhabited by keepers of gradients. By readers of damp. By listeners trained to distinguish ten kinds of flow behind stone. They live less as citizens of streets than as interpreters of thresholds: too wet, too hot, too mineral-rich, too still, too aerated, too old. They are not numerous enough to fill the spaces built for them, so their presence arrives in intervals—one figure crossing a high pipewalk, two silhouettes at a hatch, someone in a glazed hood standing motionless with one hand on a valve wheel as if taking the city's pulse.

They are dressed, almost always, for contact rather than display. Layered waxed fabrics, ceramic-scaled aprons in some districts, cuffed gloves that can be rinsed quickly, throat wraps stained dark at the edges from use. Their clothing is built to shed droplets and accept crust. You can tell trades by how people weather. Pump wardens smell faintly of warm metal and sour condensate. Basin keepers carry the chalky bloom of dried salts at the seams of their coats. Those who work near the geothermal vents often have a soft yellowing around their boots and hems, sulfur fixed into cloth beyond washing. Instrument readers favor smooth surfaces and enclosed lenses; channel clearers wear ridged soles and knee guards, because they spend hours braced on slick inclines with tools sunk wrist-deep into living runoff.

Their bodies, too, are city-shaped. Narrow-shouldered or broad-shouldered, tall or compact, it matters less than economy of movement. They conserve effort. They place feet exactly. They do not lean on the wrong railing or take the bright-looking shortcut across a wet yard. Their hands are the kind that test a surface before trusting it. Their hearing is educated. Their lungs may not be strong in the heroic sense, but they are disciplined—breath held automatically under a mineral plume, exhalation shortened when crossing pockets of bad air, speech saved for covered places where words will not dissolve into steam. Even their stillness is skilled. To pause in that city is often to inspect, and to inspect is a civic reflex.

They are not, I think, a noisy people. Not taciturn from coldness, but because so much of the city already speaks. Drips mark time. Pressure sirens carry instruction. Expanding plates sing weather. Machinery confesses its condition in hum and strain. A culture grows around not drowning those messages out. Conversation happens indoors, under hoods, beside exchanger vents, in high entries above the worst of the saturated air. Out in the open one imagines gestures, brief phrases, touched sleeves, a chin tipped toward a condenser tower whose note has changed. Their sociality is probably dense and local rather than public and theatrical: work crews, stair-neighbors, shared cistern rights, inherited routes through districts whose hazards alter with every shift in vapor.

Because the city is sparse, everyone visible acquires weight. A lone figure under an amber lamp is not background but event. This likely makes the inhabitants careful about appearing. They choose when to be seen. There may be customs of signaling occupancy in otherwise empty blocks: a certain cloth hung dry-side out, a cup placed in a high niche, a lamp shaded blue to mark breathable lower rooms, white to warn of active descaling, no light at all if a facade is weeping from deep rise and the stair should not be trusted. In a place of so many unpeopled surfaces, evidence substitutes for company. The residents become adept at reading traces others would miss: recent scrape on a hatch, warmth left on a rail, droplets disturbed where someone passed seconds earlier.

I suspect they are practical to the point of ritual. The city would reward habits that harden into manners and then into ethics. Flush after orange. Burn off black velvet only at the right humidity. Leave the nursery basins untouched. Never cap a vent because it annoys you. Report sweet smells in the fog. Knock twice on a pressure door before opening because someone may be listening from the other side through the pipework. Such practices would not feel fussy here; they would be the difference between continuity and a system drifting out of tune. Their morality may be infrastructural: not an abstract ideal of goodness, but a deep social reverence for calibration, maintenance, and not making your neglect become someone else's mineral problem three districts away.

Children, if there are many, must be strange and sure-footed little things. Raised where no grass exists, where cloud walks at knee height, where walls sweat and safe water has to be known rather than assumed, they would grow up with a map of textures instead of colors. They would know by smell which courtyard is fed by deep hot seep and which by condenser run. They would learn not to lick crystals, not to touch beautiful orange fuzz, not to chase the transparent gliders near vent mouths. Their games might involve echoes, drip-patterns, racing dew lines down wires, or guessing from sound which cistern is filling. They would be watched, but perhaps not constantly in the lowland sense; the city itself trains attentiveness early. A child who survives there becomes observant or does not remain careless for long.

The old would be formidable.

In many places age is associated with softening or retirement into irrelevance. Here I imagine elders as repositories of incomparable local knowledge: people who remember when a district's lower air was still breathable in winter, who can hear from one struck pipe-collar whether the lining behind it is furred over, who know which abandoned workshop is now more shell than structure and which only looks sealed. Their authority might derive less from title than from memory of systems no map fully captures. The city changes slowly, but never stops changing; mineral and moisture edit every intention. Those who have lived long in one quarter would become almost part of its instrumentation. One listens when they say a certain wall is too warm for the season, or the rain clock is running flat this month, or the white hanging threads have started retreating before dawn, which means some deeper chemistry has shifted.

If there are officials, they are probably hard to distinguish from technicians. Bureaucracy here would tend toward maintenance, and maintenance toward priesthood. There may be departments for atmospheric harvest, runoff law, vent stewardship, biological balance, deep-water assay, public dry-air provision. Yet these would not feel like remote ministries. The city's true administrators are those who can keep a thing behaving. Some stations may even have hereditary lineages or guild traditions around very local phenomena: a family that tends one impossible district where mist induces currents on iron, a guild responsible for the shelled pool-scavengers, a school of acoustic readers who diagnose hidden flow by hammer and listening tube. In a place where the environment is all mechanism and all temperament at once, expertise becomes intimate, almost devotional.

And then there are the nonhuman inhabitants, which are not numerous but are exact.

The black films, yellow froths, gray skins, white threads, and velvet coatings are not scenery. They are citizens of a slower category, eating chemistry and leaving conditions altered behind them. Some are tolerated, some cultivated, some burned away before they become another kind. They live as thin negotiations between mineral, heat, and water. A patch of black velvet under a pipe is both growth and instrument panel: it tells of shade, humidity, seep rate, neglect. The white culvert-threads that recoil from lamp light have the skittishness of something almost animal, though perhaps they are no more animal than frost. The city must know them by a hundred practical names, not taxonomic ones: harmless black, biting orange, sweet-slick gray, blind lace, cup-rust, lungfilm, kiln-moss.

The shelled things in the settling pools feel especially native. They may be as simple as mouths in a dish of minerals, or far stranger—soft internal chemistries protected by hard precipitated armor, moving slowly enough to seem accidental until one returns and sees the clean trails they have left. They are useful, which in such a city is a form of acceptance. To preserve a creature because it keeps channels clear is not lesser regard; it is the city's version of esteem. Whole procedures likely exist around their breeding, transfer, and protection during purge cycles. Children may be warned not to disturb them in the same tone used for not tampering with gauge glass: both are part of how water remains manageable.

The darting six-legged seep-feeders sound more recognizably animal, but still made in the image of masonry. Glossy as fired clay, quick, heat-loving, crack-dwelling: they are like mobile pieces of the city that learned appetite. They would emerge where the stone is hottest and the chemistry richest, not in swarms but singly or in brief apparitions. Workers might flip them gently aside with tools, curse them when they startle from expansion gaps, leave out mineral scrapings near sensitive seals to lure them away. Their smallness is important. Large beasts would have too much surface to surrender to this environment. Survival here favors compactness, plating, reserve.

The nearly transparent gliders above the vapor are the opposite response: not plated but reduced, all membrane and angle, living where thermals write invisible roads. They must be rarely seen well and therefore weighted heavily in local imagination. Not birds exactly, because the city has no leaves, no seeds, no conventional prey in abundance; instead these creatures would seem like fragments of weather granted metabolism. They appear, catch vent heat, vanish in brighter mist. People may read them as signs of pressure changes or industrial imbalance, though perhaps they are only hunting tiny airborne things no one else notices. In a city so focused downward into channels and inward into pipes, anything that rides the upper fog would acquire an aura of untouchable freedom.

There may also be things the residents don't wholly classify, because the city blurs the border between life and process. Mobile crusts. Re-forming gels in warm basins. Colonies that transmit a change along a pipe interior so quickly they seem to decide. Minute translucent feeders in condensate nets visible only when the light catches them. A city this chemical would breed ambiguities. Its inhabitants may not care much for philosophical distinction so long as behavior is predictable. Does it clog? Does it corrode? Does it indicate a deeper shift? Can it be redirected? Useful questions outrank ontological ones.

What lives there emotionally, beyond bodies, is caution without panic. The residents do not strike me as fearful people, though they have many reasons to be careful. Fear wastes movement and narrows perception; their world requires the opposite. Better to be attentive, adjusted, humble before systems. I imagine them possessing a dry wit, because wit is one of the cheapest forms of dryness available. Their humor may be local, technical, hard to translate: jokes about a courting pair taking the third condensate stair in inversion season, about a cousin with a face like a silver-rimmed basin, about someone so unreliable they couldn't keep a drip-wall honest. Affection would likely be expressed through competence—fixing a seal for someone before the weather turns, walking them by the less saturated route, leaving a warmed mask-cloth on a peg.

Religiously, if the city tends toward anything, it would be reverence for continuance rather than transcendence. Shrines at inspection points make sense here: not temples to distant perfection, but acknowledgments that certain joints, springs, vents, and old cuttings have personalities earned over centuries of dependable danger. Offerings might be practical—descaling labor, polished gauge covers, the first clean cup from a newly restored condenser. Saints, if named, would be founders of channels, discoverers of safe basins, listeners who heard a failure coming in time. Even secular people in such a place would lapse into acts of propitiation toward systems too large and too temperamental to call merely mechanical.

What perhaps most lives there is absence made active. The empty blocks, the overbuilt yards, the many unlit windows do not mean vacancy in the simple sense. They create a habitat for indirect living. The inhabitants are dispersed through shafts, maintenance walks, high rooms, service courts, watch stations, and hidden warm chambers. They occupy the city as water occupies stone: through paths, capillaries, recurrences. Their civilization is not one of gathering masses but of synchronized solitude. Many may spend hours alone, yet remain tightly linked by shared routines and audible infrastructure. A siren bends; three districts answer without seeing one another. A pump changes pitch; somewhere a tea is set down and someone rises. That too is a form of togetherness.

So whoever lives there is never entirely separate from the place. They are filmed by it, instructed by it, and in turn they maintain the conditions under which it can continue being itself. If you met one on a high bridge in the milk-tin afternoon, you might first register only silhouette: hood, sealed coat, one hand resting on a parapet furred with mineral velvet. Then perhaps the mask would come down, and the face would not seem exotic at all—just human, watchful, marked at the cuffs and lashes by the same damp that marks every wall. But after a moment you would notice the differences that matter: the way they listen before speaking, the way they glance not at the horizon but at the bead size on the rail, the way they know from a smell you have not yet recognized that the fog is turning old.

And if you asked what lives in this city, they might not answer with people first.

They might name the waters by type.
The vents by temperament.
The films by color.
The shell-things by season.
The pipes by district.
The mists by what they do to iron, lungs, memory.

Only after all that would they say: and us, of course.

Us too.
