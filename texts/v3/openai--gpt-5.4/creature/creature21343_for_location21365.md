What lives there is not a beast in the ordinary sense, nor a people, nor even a discreet species you could point to and say: this, here, is the inhabitant. The inhabitant is the whole alliance.

It lives there as a system that has learned to wear the shapes of grove, chimney, crust, fin, membrane, and pillar. If you insist on singularity, then what lives there is a dry intelligence of matter—not necessarily conscious, but coordinated enough to seem like intention viewed from the wrong distance. The plateau is inhabited by a biosphere that has stepped so close to geochemistry that the border between organism and environment has thinned almost to nothing.

At first you might call them plants, because they stand rooted and harvest light. But the longer you watch, the less sufficient that word becomes. These beings are extractive flora, vent-organisms, mineral symbionts, electrochemical colonies. They do not merely grow on the land; they complete it. They are what the landscape is doing with its heat.

Some of them are tall and rank-forming: forests of hollow columns ribbed in wax and silica, pale green under silver skins, each stem divided into pressure chambers and conductive tissues that move charge as much as sap. In noon heat their seams open, not like leaves breathing but like valves entering a production cycle. They draw trace gases from cracks, condense microscopic night-moisture, wick ions from hot stone, and store all of it in latticed flesh tougher than wood and more brittle than ceramic. If cut, they do not bleed so much as vent. Their interiors smell metallic, saline, faintly sulfurous.

Others spread low across flats in discs and scales, turning their mirrored surfaces by fractions through the day. These are patient collectors: of light, of charge, of thermal difference between sunstruck crust and cold sky. Their undersides knit to the mineral skin of the plateau with hair-fine roots and conductive films. A whole colony may behave like one broad organ laid over the ground, smoothing electrical gradients, shunting useful energy toward deeper rhizome webs hidden in the warm fractures below.

There are frond-fields too, though frond suggests softness they do not possess. These are crystal-edged lamellae that unfold over seasons, clear or smoky or faintly green, each blade a growth of biologically guided mineral deposition. They gather dust rich in salts and metals; they focus dew too scant to bead; they may even sort the spectrum, sending some wavelengths downward into buried tissues and reflecting the rest with a deadened shine. Seen at a distance they resemble meadows. Up close they resemble arrays.

The rope-vines in the hollows suggest another tendency entirely: self-scaffolding life. With no trees to ascend, they braid around one another, building tensile architectures in open air, lattices that catch radiative cooling at night and thermal plumes by day. Within these braided masses, gases pool, spores are sheltered, charge accumulates and leaks away. They are less like climbers than like infrastructure growing in the shape of vegetation.

And beneath all of these, probably, lies the true body.

A buried network of pale rhizomes, mineral filaments, reactive nodules, gas bladders, and conductive sheaths runs through the upland wherever conditions permit. The visible groves are fruiting towers, exchangers, antennae, vents, solar vanes. The main organism—or rather the main mode of living—extends underground along heat seams and chemical gradients, joining chimney to basin, pillar to salt pan, grove to fin-field. If one stand dies, another rises where the seam continues. If a vent cools, the surface colony calcifies and waits, or fragments, or goes dormant in armored scales for decades. Death here is likely not an event but a phase shift.

So what lives there may be colonial in a way that makes individual identity almost irrelevant. A “forest” may be one clone spread over kilometers. A field of separate-looking stalks may share charge and chemistry through brittle white cords under the crust. Distinct species surely exist, but their lives seem less arranged around competition than around partitioning process: one fixes volatile compounds from vent breath, one accumulates metals, one stabilizes crust, one pumps gases, one concentrates the almost imaginary water, one stores surplus energy in chemical form, one colonizes fresh extrusions too caustic for the rest. Together they make a trophic web without grazing and without teeth.

That is part of their strangeness: they inhabit an ecology with almost no predation, perhaps no herbivory at all, and so they are not shaped by being eaten. Nothing in them wastes effort on sweetness, softness, or seduction. Their reproductive structures are austere, timed, mechanical. Cups open at exact temperatures. Anthers or their equivalent release metallic dust into still air when pressure and dryness align. Flakes harden, break off, and tumble only far enough. Pods may wait years for the right electrostatic condition before splitting. They are not offering themselves to animals or bargaining with pollinators. They are making appointments with physics.

If there is an animal analogue at all, it may be something so subtle that a visitor would miss it: thin films migrating through rock pores, charge-sensitive mites of silica and resin no larger than grit, translucent burrowers in hot pumice, motile colonies active only at night beneath membranes. But the place as described does not seem built around them. It feels ruled by sessile life, by standing metabolism, by organisms whose primary gestures are opening, closing, conducting, precipitating, insulating, and storing. If there are mobile creatures, they are probably incidental and few. The dominant inhabitants are rooted processes with anatomies.

And yet to call them only process would miss their presence. They have character.

They seem severe, but not hostile. Deliberate, but not watchful. They stand with the composure of things that have never needed hurry. Their abundance is an abundance of fittedness: every blade, rib, wax film, and reflective scale tuned to a world where water is rumor but heat is reliable, where chemistry replaces weather as the main source of opportunity. They are elegant in the way instruments are elegant. Nothing droops. Nothing sprawls loosely unless sprawling is exactly the correct engineering solution.

If one wanted to imagine a representative inhabitant—not the whole system, but a single creaturely form emerging from it—it might be something like this:

A tall vent-rooted columnar organism growing in clusters around warm chimneys. Ten or twelve meters high, segmented, with a trunk made of pale jointed tubes braided together and lacquered in a silver-blue cuticle. From each segment flare narrow translucent vanes, amber at the base and clear at the tips, which can cant by tiny increments to modulate heat gain and light capture. The top divides into a crown of hollow reeds that resonate softly with pressure changes and perhaps function as both thermal radiators and spore organs. Its roots are not roots in the soft terrestrial sense but branching mineral sheaths penetrating fissures in the chimney, lined with living tissues that harvest redox energy from vent gases and hot stone. By day it stands apparently inert except for minute adjustments in vane angle and pore aperture. At dusk charge stored through the day redistributes through its tissues; faint blue discharges may jump between neighboring crowns. Once every few years, after a precise sequence of cool nights and high mineral flux, seams open along the upper joints and release a cloud of glittering metallic propagules that drift low over the ridge before settling into cracks. Each propagule is armored, faceted, and part seed, part battery, part sensor.

That would be one inhabitant. But it would still only be one expression of the country’s deeper resident.

Because the deepest resident is the metabolism of the plateau itself: a distributed living manufacture in which stone extrudes, salts bloom, gases rise, membranes pump, conductive mats spread, and sunlight is drafted directly into mineral economies. The life there has abandoned the old lush bargain of soil, river, grazer, rot. It has made another bargain instead—with tectonic warmth, with desiccation, with trace compounds, with the regular violence of noon and the radiative cold of night.

In a wetter world, life softens landscapes. Here, it sharpens them. It armors itself, aligns itself, plates itself into fins and columns and scales until it looks half fabricated. It does not conceal geology under loam and leaf mold. It collaborates with geology openly. It turns vents into organs, crusts into skin, pillars into circulatory nodes. The living and the nonliving are not categories there so much as phases in one long dry operation.

So whoever lives there is austere, colonial, radiant with stored chemistry, and almost eerily innocent of animal history. A civilization of plants, if you like, but without minds, monuments, or appetite—unless appetite can include the patient taking-in of sunlight, heat, metal ions, and breath-thin gases from stone. A factory-bloom biosphere. A chorus of rooted engines. The first citizens of a young mineral world, standing in their groves and fin-fields under the hard sun, quietly continuing the work the planet began.
