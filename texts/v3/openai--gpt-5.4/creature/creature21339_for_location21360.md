What lives there is a people of maintenance, pressure, and patience.

Not a frontier breed, not romantics of height, not even exactly “industrial workers” in the old familiar sense, though they work among industry. The inhabitants feel like something shaped by continuity more than ambition. They are the human answer to a land where nothing dramatic needs to happen for power to be immense. They belong to pumps the way lowland peoples belong to rivers. Their civilization would not think of itself as heroic. It would think in terms of load, seepage, intervals, condensation, access, grade, redundancy, safe footing, stable yield. Their deepest cultural image may not be the tower or the road or even the city, but the sealed joint that holds.

At a distance, one might imagine sternness first. And there is sternness: heavy clothes, practical silhouettes on catwalks in weather, faces reddened by cold vapor, the habitual care of people who know wet metal can kill the inattentive. But up close that first impression probably softens into something more exact and stranger. They are not hard because they worship hardness. They are careful because the world around them is permanently half-fluid. They live amid leaks that are not failures, fog that is not weather but infrastructure, warmth appearing in rock and cold appearing indoors along badly insulated seams. Such people would grow skilled at reading conditions too subtle for outsiders to notice. A hand on a handrail. A glance at bead size on a valve housing. The smell of iron versus peat versus mineral warmth. The pitch of a cable in morning frost. The halo around a beacon. They would seem preternaturally observant not because they are mystical, though some might be, but because their survival and competence depend on noticing gradients.

Physically, they may have the look of people made compact by climate and architecture. Not necessarily short, but efficient in movement, economical in posture, inclined to conserve heat and effort. Layered garments, always layered: waxed outer shells, felted inner collars, gloves with fingertips adapted for tools, boots with soles made for grating, slime, frost, and vibrating metal stairs. Their clothing would blur categories of civic and industrial life. Here there is little difference between what one wears to commute, to repair a sluice gate, to cross a roof in fog, or to visit family three terraces over. Everything must tolerate moisture. Everything must hang to dry near some vent or warm wall. Hoods, clips, straps, reflective stitching, mineral stains that no washing fully removes. In children, perhaps tiny versions of the same competency: knowing not to trust the mirror-skin pools, knowing which moss on concrete means slickness, knowing how to listen before stepping into mist.

Their homes would be warm inwardly and damp at the edges. The people who live there would become connoisseurs of interior climates. They would arrange domestic life around gradients of heat and condensation: drying alcoves near service risers, fungus shelves in warm utility corners, sleeping rooms set away from drumming vents, kitchens tuned to reclaim heat from pipes or neighboring machinery. They might cultivate dense indoor gardens not as luxury but as extension of the world’s one thriving kingdom. Moss trays, edible mycelia, vine walls on mineral mesh, troughs of greens under lamps. With so few animals in the outer landscape, their idea of life’s company may settle on leaves, films, spores, rooted things. A household may feel alive because something is growing along every lit surface.

And because there are almost no wild moving creatures, the humans themselves may move differently. In most landscapes, human motion is one pattern among many. Here it would be nearly the only voluntary large-scale motion visible to the eye. That confers a certain gravity. A solitary worker on a viaduct in fog becomes as visually significant as a deer on a hillside would elsewhere. A cluster of children crossing a suspended walkway would read like an event in the landscape. The people may therefore learn either to minimize their visual signature—to fold into the built lines, to move in measured practical ways—or, conversely, to use visibility deliberately through bright hazard colors, signal lamps, coded gestures. In a world where the land itself scarcely stirs, any moving body becomes message.

They are almost certainly communal in a distributed way. Not village-intimate in the pastoral sense, not urban-anonymous in the sprawling sense, but node-bound. Each pumping station, escarpment block, quarry district, marsh-ring settlement, rail shelf, and condensing field would have its own local knowledge and temperament. People would identify each other by district acoustics and water chemistry the way others do by accent. Someone from the warm cliff districts might smell faintly of mineral steam and copper. Someone from the upper dry ridges carries static in clothes and dust in seams. Someone from a marsh-basin pump town has peat under their nails no matter what their profession. They would all belong to the larger plateau, but locally, fiercely, to a specific pressure point where construction meets terrain.

This suggests a culture rich in maintenance castes, guilds, or lineages—not necessarily hereditary by blood, though perhaps partly, but hereditary by apprenticeship. Drainage keepers. Rail listeners. Lift tenders. Fog wardens. Moss cutters. Pipe laggers. Reservoir skin-watchers. Quarry tuners who can tell from the ringing strata whether a wall is safe. Condensate harvesters who collect those strange glass-filament growths from high arrays before noon dissolves them. Surveyors trained not only in geometry but in the patient lying of their instruments over ground that subtly “breathes.” There may be engineers, but in such a place “engineer” is too broad. Competence would be more ecological than abstract. One does not merely know machines; one knows machine-weather-stone interactions.

The strange elements you describe would not make the inhabitants flamboyantly occult unless we insist on outsider drama. More likely they have become domesticated by long use, and so the people who live there are intimate with phenomena other cultures would call miraculous. They attach taps to warm sweating rock and argue about maintenance schedules. They mark maps for the dependable pools with impossible surface tension and teach children never to throw themselves onto them as if onto ordinary water. They string listening wires across ringing quarry faces and derive practical forecasts from tone shifts. Their strangeness is therefore deeply anti-theatrical. They are a people who make regulations for wonders. This can produce a distinctive mentality: reverent without being dazzled, empirical without being disenchanted. To them, the uncanny is not the opposite of the useful. The uncanny is a utility class with special handling procedures.

Religiously or philosophically, they may center less on gods of creation or conquest and more on continuance, balance, recurrence, and accommodation. Their sacred emotions would be tied to persistence under pressure, to systems that hold, to the daily conversion of seepage into supply, fog into water, heat into shelter, stone fracture into usable geometry. If they have shrines, they might stand in infrastructural places: niches near warm rock seeps, maintenance chapels in turbine halls where the floor hums, memorial rails along lift shafts, small lamp-altars in drainage tunnels where generations have worked. Their dead might be remembered not with pastoral metaphors but through service language—who kept which district dry, who stabilized which cliff, who restored power after a freeze, who knew the old resonances of a flooded quarry. Ancestors in such a culture are probably not imagined as distant watchers in heaven, but as accumulated competence in the systems still running around the living.

Their art would likely favor pattern, interval, and texture over dramatic fauna or pastoral scenery. Since the landscape already supplies scale and atmosphere in abundance, the human contribution may become one of notation and reframing. Sound art would flourish here: tuned resonant chambers, siren harmonics, chime arrays grown from condensation filaments, compositions built from pumps and delayed cliff echoes. Textiles might mimic seep patterns, talus fans, mineral curtains, rail diagrams. Ceramics and glass would be prized, not as fragile luxuries but as proper responses to water and cold. Colors might stay mostly in the dark and mineral range of the environment, with sudden, culturally meaningful uses of acid green, sodium gold, beacon red, insulator white. Public beauty may emerge from infrastructural elegance: a beautifully drained stair, a reservoir roof laid out as a civic garden, a condensation gutter shaped so runoff sings at different rates in different weather.

Emotionally, the inhabitants may seem restrained to outsiders because they are used to high continuous stimulus. In a land where the baseline is already full of sound, vapor, and scale, one does not need to dramatize oneself constantly. Their social style could be low-amplitude but deep-running. Humor may be dry, local, technical, and very good. Affection may be practical: replacing a boot seal before someone asks, leaving a thermal flask at the right maintenance hatch, sharing map updates on unstable bog paths, tuning a room’s warmth for a guest. Their versions of intimacy might rely on trust in conditions: walking together in fog, speaking over turbine hum without raising voices, knowing another person’s route timings by ear. Love there would likely be less about escape from the environment than co-navigation of it.

Children raised there would inherit a very unusual normal. They might learn distance first by delayed echoes and train intervals. They would draw cities hanging from cliffs as casually as other children draw houses under trees. They would know that surfaces lie: a grassy mat may be marsh, a still pool may resist a thrown pebble and then swallow a pole, a warm wall may hide dangerous pressure. They may also feel the absence of animals less as tragedy than as a clean blankness at the edge of awareness—until they encounter an outsider’s world and realize what is missing. Then perhaps a cultural tenderness appears: not nostalgia for birds they never had, but a sharpened attachment to the forms of life they do know. Moss becomes charismatic. Fungus becomes festive. Human traffic itself becomes a kind of migration.

Politically, such a people might organize around networks rather than centers. Since the settlements are strung and anchored rather than spread, authority probably follows infrastructure corridors. Control of pumps, lifts, reservoirs, rail grades, and maintenance labor would matter more than ceremonial capitals. A city hanging from a cliff may look dominant, but if its cisterns depend on a marsh-basin pumping complex three basins away, power is relational. This can produce a society that is both hierarchical and mutually constrained. No district is self-sufficient. No district can afford romantic separatism for long. Interdependence would be audible every day in the shared sound floor.

Do nonhuman things “live” there as well? Very much so, if one allows the word a little reach.

The real co-inhabitants are systems that have crossed the line from machinery into environment. Pump arrays with rhythms known like weather. Rail lines on breathing ground. Warm cliffs whose harvested heat has probably shaped neighborhoods for generations. Condensate fields that grow filaments. Marsh pools with anomalous skins. Ringing quarry walls. These are not just resources; they are residents of a sort—slow, repeating presences with behaviors, moods, danger conditions, and reliable habits. The people may speak of them in near-personal terms without fully anthropomorphizing them. The South Face is sulking today. The Upper Skin is taking rain badly. Third Lift has a temper in fog. The red quarry is singing low, storm by evening. Such expressions would not be poetic excess but practical shorthand in a culture where matter has recurring character.

If one wanted to name the dominant inhabitant in singular form, though, it might be this:

A keeper-species lives there.  
A human kind specialized not in expansion but in sustained attendance.

They do not tame the plateau so much as accompany its chosen stabilities. They inherit installations the way others inherit orchards. They trust schedules but never entirely. They are literate in water, stone, resonance, and industrial time. They are probably healthier with routine than with disruption, but not because they fear change absolutely; rather because they understand how many things must remain continuous for life on that high wet roof to remain possible at all. They would be excellent at long responsibility. Poor perhaps at frivolous mobility. Deeply attached to known routes. Capable of immense endurance in bad visibility. Uneasy in places where the ground is dry and overfull of animals.

And if something stranger than human also lives there—something distributed through the pressure-warm cliffs, the tensioned pools, the breathing railbeds, the ringing strata—then the people have likely made room for that too, not as conquerors or worshippers alone, but as neighbors sharing utility. In a world like this, the line between phenomenon and presence thins. The inhabitants would sense that. They may never say it loudly. But they would live as if the plateau notices being used, and responds best to those who listen.

So what lives there?

Wet-mossed cities bolted to escarpments.  
Fungus gardens in service heat.  
An old continuous machinery that has become landscape.  
Plants in every margin water can hold.  
And among them, a population of disciplined, observant, infrastructure-born humans whose genius is not speed or dominance, but the art of staying in accord with a cold elevated world that is forever fresh, forever damp, forever humming, and never in a hurry.
