What lives there is not a single kind of inhabitant so much as a style of being.

At first you might imagine ordinary people adapted to an unusual place: divers, gardeners, fishers, keepers of floating orchards. That is true, but only at the surface of the answer. Given enough time in such a basin, a people would be shaped less like “mountain folk who happen to live in water” and more like a watershed that learned to speak, remember, and make deliberate choices.

They would be quiet-bodied, not necessarily silent, but economical in motion because cold water punishes waste. Their strength would not announce itself in speed or bulk. It would live in grip, lungs, balance, patience, and the ability to remain half-submerged for long stretches while working with cord, root, blade, and silt. Their hands would be remarkable: broad-palmed or at least heavily callused, sensitive to tension through wet fiber, able to judge by touch whether a stem is ready to bend, whether a bladder seal is holding, whether a wall of moss-felt has gone sour at its core. They would know drag and buoyancy as intimately as dryland people know weight and friction. A child there would learn early how different objects want to inhabit water—how they roll, snag, sink, drift, swell, soften, or trap air—and that knowledge would become as basic as grammar.

Their bodies would carry the climate. Skin pale in some lineages for lack of hard sun, perhaps, but weathered in another sense: constantly damp, mineral-washed, often smelling faintly of reed sap, clean stone, fish oil, wet bark, and the ironless sweetness of mountain water. Hair might be worn bound tightly when submerged, or lacquered, braided with shell or seed cases, or shaved in practical patterns to reduce chill and fouling. Clothing would be layered by zone rather than season alone: clinging woven undergarments that insulate when wet, outer wraps of treated fiber or hide for time spent in air pockets, belts and harnesses carrying knives in shell sheaths, pruning hooks, twine, bladder-floats, little sealed jars. Footwear might be minimal in the water, more like gripping sandals or soft wraps for slick stone and root. Jewelry, if common, would not be ostentatious metal but water-tolerant things: carved bone toggles, polished shell, beads of fired clay, seed lacquers, fish vertebrae, translucent bladders dried and tinted, maybe little cages of woven reed holding fragrant moss.

They would likely look older than they are when dry, younger than they are in water. Everyone in such a place would be seen through distortion—through ripples, through green light, through membranes and air bells—and so personhood would be recognized by movement patterns, silhouettes, habits of tying and carrying, preferred routes through channels, the rhythm of knocking at a chamber wall. Identity might be acoustic as much as visual. A person could be known by the two-tap signal they use under a platform, by the tune they hum to call trained fish, by the way their paddle enters water without splash.

I do not think they would divide labor in the same way a dry city does. “Builder,” “farmer,” “boatmaker,” “plumber,” “gardener,” “roofer,” “fisher,” “waste-keeper,” “orchard tender”—these would blur. Most adults would know all of them in some degree because the city itself is one continuous act of hydroponic husbandry. The most respected people might be those who understand transitions: how to make wall become trellis, trellis become root scaffold, root scaffold become dwelling; how to turn spoiled vegetation into feed, feed into snail-flesh, shells into lime, lime into sealing plaster; how to keep a channel open without killing the spawn that shelter in its edges. Their masters and elders would be grafters, current-readers, air-keepers, cold-season planners, the people who know which structures are truly alive and which are only pretending.

And because the settlement is small enough to know itself, its inhabitants would not have the anonymity of city dwellers. They would live among witnesses: fish, plants, ancestors, neighbors, old stone, the basin itself. Everyone’s habits would matter. Who cuts too greedily. Who leaves a gate-lattice drifting. Who tends the snail racks carefully. Who can be trusted with dry storage. Who remembers when the Quiet Shelf was deeper. Who knows the old names of submerged courtyards no one uses now except during spawning closures. Their society would probably be intimate, conservative in the literal sense of conserving systems, but not rigid in all things. Water rewards adaptation. The stubborn person who insists on using a fixed method in every season would be thought foolish, even dangerous.

They would have children, of course, but their children would seem almost amphibious to outsiders. Not biologically transformed into another species—there is no need to leap there—but culturally and physically at home in three dimensions. A child of the basin would know where to breathe, where to duck, where to pull along rootwork overhead, where eels shelter, where the cold current suddenly cuts under a warm mat of leaves. Games would involve submergence, balancing on floating pads, racing seedpods through narrow channels, diving for marked stones, listening under water to identify whose hammering is echoing from what district. Childhood scrapes would be leech bites, reed cuts, cold-stiff fingers, startled turtle snaps, bruises from submerged lintels. Their lullabies would include water noises as part of the expected world. Silence in a sealed room might make them uneasy.

Their elderly would not disappear from usefulness so quickly as in harsher frontiers. A place built by accumulation favors memory. Old people would be living maps. They would remember which hanging roots are descendants of the first bridge-vines, where the old stair beneath Lantern Pool still descends six more steps than anyone under forty believes, which family once kept cormorants in the flooded archive court, which wall can still be cut for emergency bark fiber, which temple chamber holds air longest during storms. Their hands might no longer work underwater in winter, but their judgment would remain infrastructural. In a settlement where everything slowly drifts, swells, rots, roots, and shifts, historical memory is a building material.

As for temperament, I think they would be neither dreamy nor especially severe, though outsiders might mistake them for one or the other. Living there would cultivate practical mysticism: an acceptance that almost everything important is permeable. Walls seep. Floors float. Gardens migrate. Air must be trapped and rationed. Seeds drift away and return. Ancestors’ houses become fish habitat and then shrine-space and then orchard trellis. So their philosophy would likely be less about permanence than about honorable maintenance. Not “How do we keep this unchanged?” but “How do we keep this in relation?” They would understand loss, but not always as catastrophe. A platform shifting by two arm-lengths over a decade is not failure if its ties remain good and its shade still serves the fry beneath it. Their metaphors would be rooted in circulation, grafting, silting, suspension, overflow, and held breath.

Religion, if present, would probably center on contained light, stored air, springs, depths, and old stone cores that predate the full water-life of the settlement. Their sacred figures might not be sky gods so much as beings of seepage, retention, runoff, germination, and return. Ritual could involve releasing breath into communal bells, descending processions along drowned steps, offerings lodged in root tangles for eels or turtles, white vine cultivation around shrines, seasonal reopening of channels after fish-spawn periods. They might regard fire with awe not because it is unknown but because it is difficult and ravenous, a guest from another order of existence. A continuously tended ember would be a civic treasure. The keeper of ceremonial coals might rank beside priests and engineers.

They would almost certainly keep company with nonhuman citizens so closely that the line between domestication and alliance would blur. Not pets in the dryland sense. Rather co-workers, tolerated thieves, ritual companions, indicators of system health. Tame fish that gather to bells. Ducks that glean pests from garden mats. Snails bred in careful strains for shell, slime, and flesh. Cormorants or similar divers who help herd schools through funnel weirs. Turtles so familiar with human platforms that they become part of the daily choreography. There may even be species that evolved or were bred specifically for the basin’s architecture: root-cleaning fish, edible mosses selected for wall growth, leaf-beetles tolerated because their bored channels improve aeration in float mats, frogs prized because their calls predict weather shifts in the rim clouds.

And perhaps there is one more layer. In a place so old and so thoroughly intergrown, it would be strange if no beings lived there that people do not entirely classify as animal or plant or citizen, though they need not be supernatural in the blunt sense. Whole house-groves might become quasi-persons over generations, known by temperament. One tower-root mass is sullen and shifts its buoyancy unpredictably in cold months. Another “likes” more current and must never be over-pruned. A shrine-vine blooms only after funerals. An ancient eel in the cistern is treated as an auditor of vows. The oldest floating plaza may have enough accumulated patching, naming, and collective memory that people speak of it as though it listens. This is not primitive confusion. It is what happens when living infrastructure and social life braid tightly enough that agency seems distributed. The city itself begins to qualify as an inhabitant.

So who lives there?

Root-gardeners with diver lungs.
Children who can read current by the movement of pollen.
Elders who remember streets no longer open to feet.
Air-keepers, grafter-masons, turtle-herds, bell-fishers, moss-plasterers, eel-priests, cold-water mothers with reed knives at the hip, patient men repairing a walkway that is half vine and half promise, adolescents courting in market air-bells under leaf canopies, archivists tending bark manuscripts in the driest rooms anyone owns.

Also fish, in impossible number.
Also frogs, turtles, waterbirds, insects, snails.
Also the plants, most of all, because they are not ornament but citizenship embodied.
And underneath all of them, or among all of them, the oldest resident: the basin’s own long intelligence, made of retention, adaptation, and time.

If you met one of the people there on a dry road far below the mountain, they might seem reserved, damp-eyed, attentive, oddly patient with inconvenience, impossible to hurry into waste. They would carry coiled cordage no matter where they went. They would mistrust broad fire and empty sunlight. They would look at a ruined wall and immediately see what could be trained over it. They would listen before answering, as though waiting to hear what the surrounding structure was already saying. And if asked where they came from, they might name not a town in the ordinary sense but a condition:

the Cold Steps,
the Root Market,
the Lantern Pool,
the High Cistern,
the place where water stayed,
and people stayed with it.
