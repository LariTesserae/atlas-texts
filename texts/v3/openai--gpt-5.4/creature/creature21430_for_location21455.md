What lives there is not one thing but a whole temperament of life: a people and a bestiary made plausible by height, thirst, mineral time, and the refusal of softness. If there are persons in the ordinary sense, they would have become very unlike lowland humanity long ago—not because the world brutalized them into hardness in some simple way, but because it rewarded precision, reserve, maintenance, and a kind of intimacy with structure.

The inhabitants are likely lean in outline and layered in function. Not necessarily thin from privation, but built for thin air and distance: deep-chested or otherwise efficient at exchanging what little atmosphere the uplands offer, narrow in exposed profile, conserving moisture and heat with a discipline so old it no longer feels like discipline at all. Their skin—if skin it is—would not present as lush or porous. It might be matte, dust-tolerant, mineral-colored, capable of sealing small abrasions before the world can get into them. Or perhaps much of the visible body is not soft tissue at all, but sheathing: ceramic-keratin plates, reflective scales, woven insulating membranes, replaceable filter-frills around nostrils or throat slits, transparent lids for grit seasons, electrostatic whiskers or cilia fine enough to read charged weather the way another people might read a breeze.

They would dress less for modesty than for interface. Cloaks that are really dust-management surfaces. Long coats lined with conductive mesh to bleed charge safely when crossing certain ravines. Boots with broad, articulated soles for scree and ringing crust. Face veils not romantic but practical, layered with mineral filters and heat-shedding foils. Harnesses everywhere: for tools, tether points, pressure flasks, maintenance kits, climbing lines, signaling vanes. In a place of cliffs, stairs, gantries, cableways, and old unstable stone, clothing would have evolved toward attachment. Nothing loose unless it is meant to read the wind.

Their architecture suggests their minds. Anyone who belongs to those escarpment cities would think in loads, failure points, thermal lag, routes of dust, hours of shade, migration seasons, maintenance intervals. They would have a strong instinct for what can be trusted and what can only be used provisionally. They would value repair as highly as invention, perhaps more. Their elders might not be revered for softness or wisdom in the abstract, but for remembering where the old sinkfields are, which retaining wall has shifted three finger-widths in forty years, what tone in a vent shaft means safe pressure and what tone means leave now.

Speech, if overheard, would probably suit the quiet. Low, economical, carrying well in dry air. Less overlap, more timing. Pauses that are not awkward because everyone is used to listening—to vents, cables, grit on the roof, the changing note of a machine at dusk. Their music, if they make it, might grow out of the world’s existing sounds: struck ceramics, resonant metal ribs, wind-harps in bridgework, rhythmic valve pulses, the insectile clatter of wing-lattice imitated and formalized. They may not have many festivals of profusion. Their celebrations could be things like the reopening of a long-closed lift shaft, first heat from a repaired exchanger, the safe spanning of a new bridge, the annual crossing of the evening flyers over a district newly netted and lit.

Emotionally, they would not be barren. That is important. Places like this do not create empty people; they create people who locate tenderness elsewhere. Care would appear as preventative maintenance, as well-fitted seals, as waiting to cross together at dawn rather than noon, as brushing dust from a sleeping alcove intake, as patching someone’s charge-mesh without comment. Love might look like making sure another person’s route home avoids the breeding cliffs this week. Grief might involve cleaning a dead worker’s tools and hanging them where the light catches the wear on the handles. Beauty would be exactness, endurance, the elegance of a brace under load, the amber glow in a pressure house at night, a stair worn smooth by generations and still holding.

Perhaps they are recognizably human. But perhaps not entirely.

This place invites a species that is partly citizen, partly maintenance organism. One can imagine tall, jointed beings whose limbs are made not for speed but for climbing, balancing, and bracing against wind on exposed walkways. Their hands may split or unfold into multiple precise gripping structures, good for bolts, cable clips, ceramic seams. Their eyes might handle distance almost unnervingly well—eyes that flatten glare, resolve movement across basins, and close down to mirrored slits in the white noon. But perhaps close vision is delegated to touch: fingertip pads that read texture, strain, heat leak, the ghost of current in a handrail. In the dark service trenches they would move with confidence by vibration and stored maps.

They may sleep in recessed cells deep in stone, where temperature is stable and the city’s breathing comes through vents in slow measured drafts. They may molt work surfaces the way other creatures shed skin: replacing abraded outer layers seasonally, harvesting the shed material for insulation, filtration, or binder. If they are social, their sociality may not gather in bright open squares but in chambers, terraces after dusk, tower interiors, maintenance galleries, net-shadowed streets. They would be accustomed to seeing one another at varying scales—tiny silhouettes on far gantries, close presences in sealed transit cars, voices coming first through grilles before bodies appear around a stairwell turn.

Children, if there are children, would grow up learning not nursery flowers and stream banks but maps of safe heat, old infrastructure names, the etiquette of looking upward for scavengers, the habit of tapping stone before trusting it. They would know how to tell active tunnel beneath crust from dead hollow, how to read dust on thresholds, how far a tower really is despite what clear air claims. Games would involve balance, acoustic memory, thermal pursuit, building miniature retaining walls, tracing migration routes from signal towers. Their myths would not center on oceans unless oceans are ancestral abstractions. Instead they would tell stories about depth, lift, buried heat, cities sealed under domes, the time before the current quarry line, the old circles beyond the tram reach.

And then there are the non-persons, the fellow citizens of this elevated dryness.

The great evening flyers seem almost emblematic: not birds, not bats, not even proper gliders in a wet-world sense, but patient aerial frames that harvest rising heat. Up close they are probably delicate only in silhouette. Their articulated lifting fronds may rasp against each other with use, accumulating dust at the joints, flashing matte mineral iridescence where the sun strikes worn ceramic edges. Their bodies may be compact and surprisingly heavy-looking, all ballast and nerve-core, hanging beneath those fan-lattices like a tool suspended from its own deployed geometry. They would nest not in trees but in ledges, vent mouths, abandoned crane housings, cable saddles warmed after sunset. Their young might not flap but simply learn the timing of opening and locking surfaces into the evening current.

The grazers of the flats are beautifully adapted to scarcity that is everywhere but almost invisible. They move in broad intelligent drifts, low to the ground, faceted backs taking the color of whatever district they cross. Their intake combs scour microbial films, dust-borne chemistries, oxidation blooms too thin for outsiders to notice. Herding behavior among them may be less about fear than efficiency: many bodies abrading a feeding lane, exposing trace nutrients for those behind. They leave matte paths across bright stone, and the cities probably map those routes the way one maps roads, because where the grazers pass, predators and scavengers and sometimes burrow collapses follow.

The burrowers themselves might be among the true engineers of the world, though they build by appetite rather than plan. Soft-bodied perhaps only in youth, later mineral-armored by what they ingest, they follow seams of chemical difference through subcrust strata and excrete architecture: ventilated galleries, honeycomb vaults, branching pressure chimneys. Whole neighborhoods may be founded with reference to ancient burrow maps. Children are warned away from certain hollows because of them; miners hire specialists to listen for their active fronts; predators wait above them in statuesque alertness, reading tiny electrostatic signatures through spread limbs.

Those predators are likely eerie rather than dramatic. To see one on a ridge would be to mistake it first for surveying equipment: a many-jointed silhouette with limbs held out to maximize contact with charged air, skin or shell tuned to crackle, sense, and triangulate movement below. When it moves, it may do so with abrupt certainty, collapsing from stillness into a rapid dry skitter. It does not smell blood because there is little blood to smell. It tastes field distortions, frictional wake, the ghost-static of many small mineral bodies moving underground.

Closer to the cities, scavengers blur the line between animal and maintenance failure. Small, heat-hungry things gather around substations and exhaust ports, reflective backs opened like petals of polished slag to collect warmth. Others feed on lubricants, corrosion products, insulation fibers, calcified residues around old pipes. Some may be nuisances, some symbionts, some tolerated because they consume harmful byproducts better than any human-designed system. Entire utility manuals may include notes not to disturb certain colonies during molting because they keep conduit mouths clear of a worse fouling species.

The shell-like cliff colonies and bridge-hung ringing organs feel almost like the nearest equivalent to vegetation—not because they are plantlike, but because they hold place, alter light, gather particulate, and make neighborhoods recognizable. One district is known for pale sun-turning shells on the western retaining walls; another for glass-hard pendants under the freight bridge that sing in a dust wind; another for the dim night-seams of lambent colony growth that draw drifting feeders and give the underworks a false impression of inhabited windows. The inhabitants would know these life-forms not abstractly but practically: which indicate stable stone, which weaken mortar, which can be harvested for filter material, which predict a coming dust migration by changing orientation or glow.

As for intelligence beyond the civic species, one can imagine there are semi-domesticated forms adapted to this world’s needs. Pack organisms that cling to vertical surfaces and carry loads in distributed harnesses. Tunnel-sensing companion beasts used by quarry crews. Small trained gliders that ride thermals between towers carrying line, samples, or emergency signal filaments. Perhaps there are no pets in the indulgent sense, but there are work-companions, mutually legible creatures with whom one shares routes, heat, danger, and routine.

The people who live here, then, would not think of themselves as living in a wasteland. They would think of themselves as living in a legible place. Difficult, yes. Unforgiving of carelessness, certainly. But deeply readable once one has learned its grammar. Such inhabitants would distrust extravagance that cannot be maintained, sentiment that ignores material fact, plans that pretend dust and migration and thermal stress are negotiable. They would probably seem austere to outsiders. Yet among themselves they may be intricate, humorous, even affectionate in dry ways: jokes about compasses losing their religion in magnetized districts; old sayings comparing unreliable people to noon-charged ravines; flirtation conducted by route recommendations and gifts of well-machined fasteners.

Their spirituality, if any, would likely concern persistence rather than transcendence. Not sky gods, despite all that sky, but principles of load, recurrence, buried heat, exposed history. Shrines might form around ancient shafts that exhale warmth, around old dressed-stone circles too large to explain, around surviving sections of impossible old infrastructure. Or perhaps reverence is secularized into conservation law and maintenance ritual. To seal a crack correctly before cold night; to mark a safe crossing for those after you; to leave old structures mapped, named, and understood rather than romanticized—these may be moral acts.

In the end, what lives there is a civilization of upland exactitude surrounded by life that has become angular, dry, clattering, radiant, and patient. The inhabitants belong to a world where softness is not absent but relocated into attention, planning, and shared endurance. They are creatures of terraces, vents, faultlines, and dusk thermals; of filtered breath and long sight; of stone that does not hide its age; of cities that hang on because they were built by beings who understood that to remain is an active art.

If you met one on that high industrial balcony at evening, they might not look dramatic at first. Dust-colored coat. Sealed goggles pushed up. Hands marked by fine white abrasion. A stillness learned from listening. But after a moment the whole place would seem to gather around them—the venting towers, the measured distances, the flyers in the updraft, the old geometries half-buried in scree—and you would realize that this is exactly the sort of being such a world would make: not conqueror, not victim, but reader, repairer, and native of the elevated dry.
