What lives there is not one emblematic beast but a whole cast of hard-edged lives, all shaped by the same conditions until they begin to resemble the place itself: cold, repetitive, unornamented, exact.

The first impression would be that everything living there is built rather than grown.

Not mechanical, though many of the creatures make use of machinery and many machines have become ecological features in their own right. But the organisms of this world have the look of things assembled for function under pressure. They are narrow where water must be cut cleanly, broad where heat must be conserved, plated where impact is common, rubbery where freezing spray would shatter anything brittle, pale where pigment serves no purpose, metallic in sheen where skin and scale have become reflective or vitreous. They do not trail leaves, fronds, manes, or feathery softness. Even the flexible ones seem designed around stress lines.

The dominant life is probably migratory, because the world itself is organized around fixed circulations and fixed resource points. Great moving populations travel prescribed routes through drowned valleys and over submerged saddles, not wandering in search of changing seasons so much as passing between enduring intersections of chemistry, pressure, temperature, and current. These are fish if one needs a familiar word, but fish reduced to the most hydrodynamic logic: long, laterally compressed, shoulderless bodies; heavy tails; flanks that can vanish into iron-dark water or flash all at once into silver when a school turns; blunt heads crowded with pressure pores, electroreceptive canals, thermal pits, whatever sensory machinery evolution has found useful in green-black depth and perpetual noise. They do not need beauty in the shallow-water sense. Their beauty is synchronized response. Whole kilometers of them can bend around a pillar of upwelling water like smoke made of metal filings.

Among these schools move the larger things that make the emptiness feel inhabited.

Some are leviathans in the old sense: air-breathing or at least surface-visiting masses, not common, not social in any obvious way, seen as interruptions rather than presences. A back breaking the black water once. A blunt skull venting steam or condensed breath into sleet. A train-long body sounding down beside a turbine field, scarred white where old encounters with rock, propeller, ice, or rivals have left records. They are not sleek open-ocean performers. The world would not reward elegance for its own sake. These would be dense, pressure-tolerant, heavily insulated by blubber or some denser analogue, with scar-mapped skin like wet basalt. Their senses would run more through vibration, pressure contour, and infrasound than through vision. They would know intake mouths, warm outfalls, breeding ledges, migratory ribbons, and pressure wells as topographical certainties, perhaps more clearly than they know individual mates or offspring.

Some of the large animals would be ambush drifters of depth: huge soft-bodied predators in submerged shafts and drowned caverns, creatures that let the water hold them in folded suspension until some tremor pattern tells them to unfold. In a greener world they might seem monstrous; here they would seem merely apt. Broad oral curtains, internal cartilage instead of showy bone, sensor cilia lining the flanks, skin so pale it becomes blue-white in artificial light. They would feed where dead matter funnels downward or where schools are squeezed through narrow mineral throats. They would not chase much. The world provides choke points. All successful predators here would be readers of inevitability.

Closer to the rock, life becomes more numerous and stranger.

Without plant cover, every surface that can host chemistry becomes contested. So there are encrusting films so thin they are almost stains: bacterial and archaeal communities feeding on vent minerals, industrial effluent, trace organics from death and waste. Upon these films feed the raspers and scrapers, shelled or semi-shelled things that occupy the niche of grazers in a place with nothing green to graze. They might look like a cross between limpets, trilobites, and machine parts: low armored domes with ringed mouths of mineral teeth; segmented undersides capable of clamping tight against wave-beaten stone; multiple gripping limbs that can articulate independently over uneven basalt. On exposed rock they would be dark and glassy to absorb what little warmth exists. Near warm pipes and discharge channels they might grow pallid, chalked with salt and mineral deposit until they seem carved from old bone.

There would also be chain-furred ecologies on the infrastructure itself. Anchor lines, intake grilles, ladder rungs, turbine housings, sluice gates: all the industrial surfaces that interrupt current become reef analogues in a world that has dispensed with coral and kelp. Hanging from them are filter-frilled drifters, colonies or individuals difficult to distinguish, translucent sleeves and combs and latticed fans opening into the current. Some retract instantly when shadow or pressure changes pass over them. Some are almost mineral, calcified into branching crusts that rattle faintly when struck by rain at low tide or by debris in flood. Others are soft and gelatinous but reinforced internally with fibrous rods, so they can survive endless buffeting. Their colors, if seen closely, are not vivid but chemical: nicotine yellow, opal white, iron red, bruise purple around the feeding mouths.

The upper rock supports one of the most conspicuous inhabitants: the cliff colonies.

From afar they occupy the ecological place of seabirds, but the world has not chosen feathers. Feathers would soak, freeze, and become liabilities in these storms. So the colony animals are rain-slick, seal-skinned, and semi-winged only by courtesy. Their forelimbs are jointed grasping sails more than true wings, able to spread broad enough to catch updraft and spray, then fold into hooked climbing tools. Their bodies are torpedo-heavy in the chest for plunging and impact. Their hindquarters are compact, built for bracing on narrow ledges rather than walking any distance. The head is probably blunt and expressive in silhouette only because of the throat apparatus: inflatable resonant sacs that serve social and territorial signaling in a world where visibility fails but sound carries. Whole breeding walls become organs. The colony does not sing; it booms, pulses, knocks, and moans in layered rhythms, each ledge perhaps with its own cadence.

These creatures likely reproduce in dense packed nurseries on shelves just above the permanent kill-zone of surf and below the freeze line that would lock eggs or neonates in ice. Their young would be ugly in the useful way: too much skin, too large a mouth, gripping pads oversized for the body, fed by regurgitated fish paste or nutrient slurry from the adults. White spatter down the cliff would mark not just waste but territory, chemistry, and nutrient transfer; entire invertebrate communities would depend on it. Around colony ledges gather scavengers, parasites, and opportunists: blind many-legged runners in warm crevices; ribbon-bodied siphoners feeding on seepage; shell clusters using the ammoniac runoff; tiny darting carnivores living among the packed bodies to steal tissue, embryos, or afterbirth.

Anything warm becomes habitat, so around machines evolves a whole secondary fauna.

This may be the most characteristic life of the world: not simply wild organisms persisting despite infrastructure, but lineages that have integrated industry into their basic expectations. Creatures nest behind generator housings because the waste heat prevents freezing. Pale worm-ribbons line insulated conduits, feeding on the biofilm and salt condensate there. Thin-limbed scavengers with heat-sensitive pits wait below drain mouths for protein-rich runoff from slaughter docks or processing sheds. Shell-backed huddlers colonize the lee side of pump stations where spray turns to rime and then briefly melts in pulses, producing a reliable trickle. Some species may time reproduction not to season in any terrestrial sense but to maintenance schedules, fish-processing cycles, pressure purges, or the warm effluent released by old turbines. In another world one would call them commensals of civilization. Here civilization is simply one more geothermal vent: colder, louder, more angular.

There are probably amphibious or semi-amphibious hunters that patrol the threshold between installations and water. Not mammalian in any familiar way, perhaps, though they could converge on seal, otter, or crocodile forms. Better to imagine dense-bodied, low-slung things with gripping digits and a neck too short to be elegant, able to launch from slick steel or basalt into flood channels. Their skins would be pebbled and water-shedding, perhaps conductive enough to sense nearby muscle movement. They raid breeding terraces, harry juvenile cliff-colony animals, scavenge processing waste, and vanish into surge caves when approached. Around people they are neither tame nor bold; simply practical. Human presence means offal, warmth, and fixed traffic routes.

Below all this, in the pressure wells and under-crust channels, there may be a deeper biosphere that barely appears except through occasional emergence. The world described feels volcanically active enough, and chemically fed enough, that the ultimate base of the food web may extend into subsurface aquifers and warm mineral cavities. Organisms there would be less like recognizable fish or crustaceans and more like mobile portions of anatomy: pulsating sacs, jawed ropes, fan-mouthed cylinders, eyeless swimmers ringed with tactile whips. Some may rise only when storms drive oxygenation downward or when industrial boring opens a path. Their appearance at the surface would feel like an error in the usual order, but not an impossible one. In a world this stable, even the underworld has routes.

As for intelligence—if something fully self-aware besides the workers who built the towers and pump houses belongs to the place—it would likely not advertise itself with grace or spectacle.

A truly native intelligence here would have to be patient, distributed, and acoustically literate. It would understand recurrence the way primates understand objects. It would map the world not by horizon or vegetation but by the exact signatures of falling water, the resonance inside caverns, the thermal taste of outfalls, the cyclical movement of schools through drowned gates in the rock. Such beings might live in colonies inside pressure-domes of their own construction, or in excavated warm chambers behind waterfall curtains, or in linked stations parasitic on abandoned human installations. Their bodies would probably be low-profile and modular—multiple gripping limbs, skin resistant to abrasion and salt, insulated core, sensory membranes tuned to low-frequency vibration. Eyes, if present, would be secondary. Voice would be more useful as percussion, booming, clicks transmitted through structure and water.

But the place does not require a singular clever species to feel inhabited. Its true residents are patterns of congregation.

A breeding ledge is an organism made of thousands of bodies and their waste, heat, noise, and inherited return.
A turbine throat wrapped in filter colonies and hunting swimmers is an organism.
A warm discharge plume with its invisible microbial bloom, its silver fish, its lurkers below, its scavengers at the margins, is an organism.
Even an abandoned ladder with shells on the rungs, ribbon feeders in the cable tray, and blind mites under the ice lip is a little vertical biome.

Everything lives by exploiting constants. Nothing there depends on bloom and wither, leaf and fruit, meadow and forest succession. Instead life gathers where gradients endure: warm/cold, fresh/brine, flow/slack, light/dark, smooth/turbulent, exposed/sheltered, natural/industrial. The result is an ecology with very little softness and a great deal of precision.

If I had to condense the inhabitants into a single governing image, I would say this:

The world is inhabited by creatures that look as if they were cast from water pressure, black rock, and waste heat—schools like moving ore, cliff colonies like pale wet lichen made animate, shell-backed scrapers like detachable pieces of machinery, deep predators like unfurling gloves of flesh, and scattered leviathans whose appearances are rare enough to preserve awe. Around them, in every crack where warmth, mineral, or current provides a foothold, innumerable lesser beings persist with blind tenacity. Nothing is decorative. Nothing is idle. Everything grips, filters, rasps, dives, clings, sounds, waits, or passes through.

Whoever lives there lives the way the waterfalls fall there: continually, loudly, and along fixed paths that long ago stopped needing explanation.
