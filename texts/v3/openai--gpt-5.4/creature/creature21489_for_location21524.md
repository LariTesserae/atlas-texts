What lives there is a people who have not inherited shelter so much as negotiated it, meter by meter, from the ground.

They are not, at first impression, subterranean in the romantic sense. Not pallid troglodytes, not secretive burrow-folk shaped by vast deeps. They still belong to weather. Surface light still means something to their bodies. They know wind, know clouds, know when rain is coming before it reaches the shafts. But they have become intimate with overhead weight. They live with a roof that is not distant and abstract, but palpable: tested by knuckles, listened to in storms, read for seams and dampness and settling strain. Their civilization is young enough that this intimacy has not yet become invisible custom. It is still conscious. It still structures posture, craft, religion, family arrangement, even humor.

So the people who live here are low-centered in their habits. Not necessarily short, though over time one might imagine a tendency toward compactness being favored by the architecture. More importantly, they are physically economical. They duck without looking. They turn sideways in tight lanes with a practiced courtesy. They carry loads close to the body to avoid scraping walls or catching roof beams. They climb ladders with both confidence and suspicion. They can tell, from a changed echo or a sift of grit underfoot, whether a passage is old and trustworthy or newly cut and still deciding if it wants to remain a passage at all.

Their hands are marked before anything else. This feels like a hand civilization. Clay under the nails, callused palms, fine white dust in the folds of skin, small burns from lamps and kilns, rope grooves on fingers, tiny cuts from reed, wicker, stone flakes, rough timber. Even those who are not diggers probably repair walls, patch cracks, line cisterns, renew plaster, reshape thresholds after a damp spell. The settlement asks everyone to be a maintainer. In a place where infrastructure is not hidden behind finish but present in every room as visible necessity, “living there” means participating in the continued making of there.

They are probably divided less by grand professions than by clusters of competencies. Diggers, yes, but a digger is also a shorer, a listener to earth, a reader of damp. Potters, yes, but these potters likely make not only vessels but lamp saucers, drain liners, cistern seals, wall patches, bead offerings, spindle whorls, maybe even little vent collars or smoke baffles. Reed workers are basket makers, screen makers, mat weavers, reinforcement specialists for daub and roofing. Carpenters are brace-setters, stair builders, frame improvisers, bridge layers over narrow cuts, patchers of lids over old ravines. Even household labor blurs into engineering: sweeping is drainage maintenance, tidying is fire prevention, bedmaking is also moisture management.

The people here live very close to each other, and that closeness has likely made them unusually sensitive to gradients of claim. They understand shared space not as abstraction but as inches. A shelf niche may matter politically. A wall recess for oil jars might be inherited, disputed, or ceremonially granted. The right to hang drying cords above a lane, to place a grinding slab near a better-lit opening, to enlarge a sleeping alcove by one forearm’s width into a soft wall seam—these are meaningful acts. Their etiquette would therefore be extraordinarily spatial. Where you stand while speaking. How long you block a stair throat. Whether your basket rests on a cistern lid. Whether your smoke drifts into another family’s loft. Whether the damp laundry line crosses a shrine sightline. Civility is probably measured less by politeness of word than by competence in shared compression.

Children there would be remarkable creatures of route-memory. Lean, quick, dust-kneed, perpetually warned and perpetually disappearing into legal and semi-legal passages. They would know which courts keep warmth longest, which shafts bring sun at what hour, which unfinished cuts can be wriggled through, where old spoil heaps make soft play hills, where a sympathetic aunt stores cracked pots perfect for games. They would be raised on danger made ordinary: no running near the fresh plaster, no lamp in the reed-roof lane, no shouting in the cistern gallery, do not jump from that beam, mind the drip trench, do not lean against a wall marked red. Their games would imitate labor almost immediately—miniature excavations, tiny retaining walls of gravel and sherd, clay figurines tucked into mock shrine niches, races balancing little jars of seep-water.

Their elders would possess prestige not simply because of age but because they remember the sequence of making. In a settlement so new, memory is structural knowledge. An old woman may know that beneath the neat gravel floor of a sleeping lane lies an earlier drain that still shifts after heavy rain. An old digger may remember which chamber was widened in late thaw and therefore should never carry roof stores. Someone can point to a stain line and say: before that beam was there, the wall sweated all winter. Oral history here is partly architecture. To forget the story of a place is to risk using it wrong.

Religiously, they are likely a people of propitiation rather than grandeur. Their sacred beings, if personified at all, are local and load-bearing. Threshold spirits, seep guardians, patrons of holding-fast clay, listeners in the beams, the kindness of roofs that remain roofs. Perhaps the gods above—sky, sun, storm, field—still exist in the broader cosmology, but below ground devotion becomes immediate and practical. A bowl of water left where three corridors meet. Oil touched to a support post before a widening cut begins. Ochre hands on a pillar after a collapse that did not happen. Their spirituality is probably full of bargains, gratitude, and attentiveness. Not fearful in the melodramatic sense, but unable to be frivolously abstract. Catastrophe is too intelligible here. Therefore reverence has a craftsperson’s exactness.

They may ornament little, but mark much. The settlement description suggests a culture in which signs are functional first and only later become aesthetic. So the people who live there probably decorate through repeated symbols that began as warnings, blessings, ownership marks, water tallies, or excavation records. A painted stripe around a doorway might indicate a family lineage, but also that the chamber was once a cistern and remains cool. Incised notches in a beam might count years since replacement, or births, or successful rain seasons, or all three by custom layered over necessity. Beauty would emerge out of maintenance patterns: carefully smoothed plaster around a shrine, neatly white-clayed cistern lips, red-ochre palms on pillars, reed screens dyed in modest bands, storage jars arranged by color in niches because order itself reads as abundance.

In temperament, this people would likely be communal but not idyllic. Density produces interdependence, and interdependence produces friction with no room to hide from it. They would be used to overhearing lives, to sleeping through others’ work, to measuring others by reliability under strain. A generous person is probably someone who replasters a shared wall before being asked, shifts their storage jars when drainage needs widening, keeps their lamp trimmed cleanly, remembers who is low on water after a dry week. An intolerable person is one whose negligence circulates: smoke, clutter, noise at the wrong choke point, ignored cracks, waste in a runnel. Reputation would move fast here, not out of gossip as entertainment but because everyone’s safety partly depends on everyone else’s habits.

I suspect they are not wealthy in the usual visible sense, but they are rich in workable matter. To them, value attaches to dryness, fit, seal, access, and steadiness. A good beam may be more admired than jewelry. A family with a well-lined private storage pit, a dependable loft shelf, and rights to a ventilated work recess may be considered enviably placed. Finer metals, if present, appear as hinges, pins, blade edges, awls, lamp hooks—small intensifications of practicality. Display, where it exists, may cluster around durability: polished stone bowls that resist seep, an unusually straight lintel, a carved post, a carefully cobbled floor, a thriving herb pot in a shaft court proving command of light.

As for their appearance, one imagines layered adaptation rather than a single type. Clothing suited to cool interiors and damp edges: wool, woven plant fiber, leather where abrasion is constant. Short cloaks or shawls that can be wrapped close in chill corridors and shrugged back in warm workshops. Aprons for labor. Headscarves or tied hair to keep soot and grit out. Soft-soled footwear or sandals with replaceable ties, because floors vary from damp clay to gravel to planking. On some people, a white trace of marl dust is permanent at the hem; on others, smoke shadow darkens cuffs and collars. They likely smell of clean earth more often than open air.

If there is an animal that “lives there” in a secondary but meaningful sense, it is not some monstrous burrow-beast. It is the tolerated camp-following life of human settlements under compression: mice sleek from grain theft, spiders inhabiting corners undisturbed because they perform a service, half-feral cats perhaps if the culture permits them, dogs with excellent route sense and a talent for sleeping exactly where they inconvenience traffic least. Chickens and goats remain mostly at the edges, too messy, too warm, too oxygen-hungry for the core. Swallows at the larger shafts might be cherished as emissaries of surface season. A fig tree in a central court, if one survives there, would almost count as a citizen.

But maybe the truest answer is that what lives there is not only a people. It is a mode of attention.

Because this under-settlement is unfinished, everyone in it must remain slightly interpretive. They are readers of moisture, of soot, of air movement, of tiny debris fallen overnight. They inhabit not static architecture but ongoing conversation with materials. Clay speaks by cracking. Timber speaks by smell and strain. Stone speaks by echo. Water speaks by appearing where it did not yesterday. Their intelligence is therefore distributed through the senses in a very grounded way. A child can notice a new drip and have made an important civic observation. A grandmother touching a wall can halt a dangerous expansion. A potter may understand ventilation better than a digger because kiln smoke taught her the moods of air. Knowledge is embedded, local, practical, and everywhere.

Such people are often underestimated by surface cultures, if there are neighboring ones. Their settlement might seem provisional, improvised, unlovely, even half-buried. But those who live there know they have accomplished something subtle: they have created density without depth, refuge without total severance from the sky, permanence out of materials still close to mud and branch. They are early urbanists of compression. Their genius is not monumentality but adjacency.

And because the place is in its first age, the inhabitants themselves are probably in a first age of identity. They may not yet think of themselves as a distinct “underground people.” They may still speak as though they are simply the residents of this plain, this settlement, this kin-cluster, these courts and shafts. Yet the place is already shaping them into something specific. Their proverbs will fill with beams, seepage, burden, thresholds, hidden reserves, the wisdom of widening slowly. Their songs will suit enclosed acoustics, low and carrying. Their myths may begin to place origins not on mountain peaks or in forests, but in cut earth made habitable by hands and caution and mutual dependence. Their dead, if buried nearby, may be set not far below those still living, making the whole landscape one layered habitation of recent descent.

So whoever lives there is human, almost certainly—but human under active pressure from architecture young enough to be remembered and fragile enough to require everyone. A packed, dusted, vigilant, practical, reverent, adaptive people. They live close to the surface but closer still to one another. They are held up by beams, custom, and continual repair. They are making a city before they have the luxury to call it one.
