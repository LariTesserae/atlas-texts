What lives there is not one creature but a style of living life has fallen into when pressed small, cold, and close together.

At first you might say: insects, arachnids, worms, snails, larvae, molds, mosses. But that is like saying a port is made of ropes, gulls, salt, and men. True, and not enough. The inhabitant of that place is a multitude so interwoven that no single body can stand for it. The city is lived in by grazers and trappers, masons and collapsers, nurses, scavengers, irrigators, looters, tunnelers, milkers, bridge-makers, flood-readers, corpse-hollowers, spore-herders. It is occupied by things that do not know they are neighbors and by things that know very well what passes over whose threshold.

Still, some presences rise.

The spiders are the nearest thing the hollow has to dynasties. Not rulers in any legal sense, but organizers through appetite. They live there as engineers who cannot help being executioners. Some are small taut sheet-builders, making low tensile suburbs where the air itself is taxed for debris, pollen, and flesh. Some are patient line-keepers under bark lips, each thread set where foot traffic must one day become panic. Some squat in bellied silken chambers fogged with damp, among egg-sacs, prey-husks, and old repairs. They are large only by relation, but relation is what matters here. When a spider moves, routes change. When one anchors a line, a district coheres around it. When one dies, its abandoned architecture is not abandoned for long: mites cross it, larvae shelter in it, fungus powders it, and another spider inherits the civic skeleton.

Ants live there too, though uneasily, as colonists of opportunity. They come in disciplined surges through root-tunnels and bark slits, lay down certainty in a place that usually has none, and for a while make roads mean roads. They tend aphids in the pale stem-gardens, stroke sweetness from them, defend them when they can, and lose them when they cannot. In better-defended corners they build nurseries with pellet walls and silk-stiff partitions, trying to make permanence out of a hollow built to refuse it. But this is not an ant city in the full imperial sense. The wet defeats grand plans. Floods erase scent. Snails plow fortifications flat. Spiders tax the avenues. The ants persist as a stern, practical minority, always half engaged in logistics, half in war.

The aphids are among the stranger citizens because they live as livestock and orchard-fruit at once. They cluster on the tenderest stems in translucent green and ash-pale congregations, little swollen vessels taking the plant into themselves. They are cultivated where possible, preyed upon where not, and their stillness is deceptive: around them traffic forms, hierarchies assert themselves, ambushes are staged. Their neighborhoods are some of the city’s nearest approach to agriculture, though agriculture here is only a truce between feeding systems.

Mites occupy the place like innumerable poor. Not powerless exactly—nothing so numerous is powerless—but small enough that the whole city is scaled partly to their passage. Red ones in processional files through the moss. Pale ones in fungal vaults. Dust-fine ones inhabiting the margins of other creatures’ lives, feeding on flakes, films, eggs, forgotten wetness. They are the residents of seams and afterthoughts, of the under-understory. If the spiders are magistrates, mites are the populace: countless, adaptable, always moving into the spaces opened by damage.

Springtails live there as sparks with appetites. They are among the city’s abruptest citizens, impossible to imagine at rest even when resting. They flick between moss towers and water films, vanish from danger by simply no longer being where danger expected them to be, and feed with a kind of bright thrift on algae, rot, fungal bloom, whatever fine life is available on the skin of damp things. In another scale they would seem negligible. Here they give the place its jumpy, electric unpredictability. They are the pedestrians of impossible shortcuts.

Woodlice live in the older, dimmer districts under bark eaves and in refuse-rich pockets, nosing steadily through breakdown itself. Their occupation is conversion. They take soggy structure and render it portable, pellet by pellet. They are municipal in the deepest sense: sanitation workers, excavators, low-status transformers of the dead into the feedstock of the next arrangement. They prefer the thicker dark, the places where roofs sag and the grain of wood begins to lose its memory of being tree.

Beetles are many things depending on kind. Some patrol with a hard official air along lower causeways, black and polished, as if armored for office. Some are minute mold-feeders dusted with spores. Some are temporary catastrophes in larval form, their grubs chewing invisibly through what seemed load-bearing yesterday. The beetles bring casing, edge, punctuation. They are the creatures in this city who most resemble built objects: shiny, jointed, closed, durable-looking. Which only makes it more startling when they open, fly, bury, or rot like anything else.

The larvae are the city’s hidden appetite. Soft white, amber, translucent, blunt-headed, root-curled, they live in vaults, piths, seed hollows, damp seams. They are futures laid in secrecy and labor performed by mouths without faces. Some will become flies and leave briefly for the outer weather before returning to lay new hunger in the dark. Some will become beetles and emerge as hard revisions of their own softness. Many will simply be eaten before any transformation can declare itself. They are the stored possibility of the place, and also its most vulnerable wealth.

Snails and slugs live there as weather made flesh. A spider may command a corner and an ant line may hold a route, but a passing snail redraws districts without meaning to. Its body is infrastructure collapse, chemical proclamation, and temporary abundance all at once. It shears down fungal awnings, compresses moss lanes into silvered causeways, opens glistening roads that others follow or avoid, and leaves a changed pH, changed texture, changed future. The city does not domesticate snails. It endures them the way a coast endures storms. Slugs are even less house-broken, all appetite and seep, entering through softness and leaving absence.

Centipedes live there as pure policy of violence. They are not common in the way mites are common, nor structurally central in the way spiders are central, but when one enters a district the whole logic of movement alters. Their many legs make them seem almost fluid, yet every segment is intention. They inhabit cracks, root-side trenches, bark canyons. They are what the city produces when predation stops pretending to be patient. Assassins is close, but too romantic; they are simply the form sudden death takes when given a long, articulated body.

Flies and midges occupy the thresholds. The wetter openings, the hovering spaces, the dripside air above communal basins—these belong to winged, temporary lives. Midges rise and suspend, making a district out of verticality. Small flies inspect refuse, wounds, soft rot, carrion, nursery damp. They are opportunists in the purest sense, quick to arrive at any loosening of boundaries between living tissue and useful substrate. The city is full of solids and films; the flies make use of intervals.

There are also the near-invisibles: nematodes threading through water-skins; protozoan multitudes in droplets; tiny translucent worms writing through seepage; microorganisms fermenting every softening surface. If one keeps shrinking, the city does not end. It becomes more populated. The under-log hollow is not merely inhabited on one scale; it is recursively tenanted. Moss stalks are towers to one creature and soil to another. A droplet is a cistern overhead to one thing and an ocean containing predators to the next. What lives there, in the broadest truthful sense, is stacked citizenship.

And the plants live there too, though “live there” is weaker than the case deserves, since in one sense they are there. Mosses occupy the city as both forest and masonry: cushions, stairways, retaining walls, flood-breaks, capillary reservoirs, cool roofs, and pasture. Liverworts spread like annexing green weather over fresh surfaces, making slick terraces and scaled embankments. Rootlets thrust through as pilings and tunnels and cliff-faces. Fungal bodies rise and collapse as domes, shelves, urns, awnings, columns. Lichen paints districts in crust and powder. The vegetal world is not backdrop to the animal inhabitants; it is the substance from which neighborhoods can even be said to exist.

Fungi especially feel like a parallel citizenry. They live there as merchants of conversion, quiet universal claimants to whatever weakens. Their threads infiltrate walls, food stores, corpses, abandoned egg chambers, old silk. Their fruiting bodies become towers, shrines, parasols, and then sludge. Their spores make weather in enclosed chapels. If the visible animals are the city’s traffic, fungi are its long argument with time.

So who lives there?

A damp commonwealth of opportunists.  
A hundred tiny regimes of appetite.  
A layered population in which builder, tenant, crop, predator, scavenger, and building material are constantly exchanging roles.

There are no true householders because tenure is brief. No true homeless because every debris-pocket is already a possible address. No clean separation between wild creature and city dweller because the city is exactly what wildness looks like when repeated in one favorable hollow. Life there is mostly unselfconscious, but not simple. Much of it proceeds by reflex, chemistry, and inherited pattern, yet the overall effect is of cunning everywhere: silk put where force will come, eggs hidden where damp will hold, paths chosen by drip intervals, walls raised from what last night’s damage left behind.

If you insisted on a single representative inhabitant, perhaps it would be something modest: a small dark spider repairing an ancestral-looking web between two root hairs while springtails tick through the moss below, mites caravan past a pellet wall, an ant pauses with a dead gnat leg, and overhead a droplet swells toward civic catastrophe. That spider would not own the place, but it would understand one of its central truths: everything here is temporary except the need to make use of what arrives.

Or perhaps the true resident is the aftermath itself.

Because that city is inhabited most completely by beings that know how to live in ruins before the ruin has finished happening.
