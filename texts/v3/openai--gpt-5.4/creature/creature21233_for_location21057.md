What lives there is something that belongs to exposure.

Not a king of peaks, not a creature of the clean summit and banner-wind, but an inhabitant of a high country still wet from its own making. It would not look finished in the way animals from older landscapes do. It would seem assembled by pressure, weather, and necessity more than elegance—an organism shaped to use a place where the ground slips, the water seeps upward, and shelter is always provisional.

At first one imagines a herd animal, because the land is so full of tracks and traverses and mineral licks. Then perhaps a predator, because broken emptiness and sudden concealment invite ambush. Then something smaller, lower, more patient, because so much of the place is about listening, waiting, fitting into cracks, living close to stone. In truth, whatever most fully lives there would probably seem to contain aspects of all three: grazer, watcher, opportunist, commuter between seep and shelf and fog hollow. The place favors creatures that do not insist on one mode of being.

If there is one dominant animal presence, I think it is a broad-footed ungulate of the high plateau—something not noble in the stag-at-sunset sense, but durable, damp-coated, and matter-of-fact. A creature with a deep chest for thin air, short powerful limbs for unstable grades, and feet spread wide enough to trust on saturated gravel and frost-heaved mud. Its hooves might be less polished crescents than splayed dark pads edged in horn, able to grip slick mineral films and distribute weight over trembling flats. It would move with startling precision for something heavy, testing each step not theatrically but by instinct, as if reading load-bearing qualities through the soles.

Its coat would tell you nearly everything about the climate. Not luxurious fur, not decorative shag, but layered weatherproofing: a dense underwool that holds warmth even when the outer hair is beaded dark with mist; longer guard hairs along the spine and shoulders that guide water off in runnels; perhaps a mane or ridge of coarser fiber that always seems damp at the tips. The colors would be quarried from the land itself—pale ash, iron gray, peat-brown, lichen-silver, the black of soaked stone at the knees and muzzle. In clear weather it would vanish against the shelves. In fog it would appear all at once, as if condensed from the hillside.

Its head would be built less for ornament than for extraction. Wide muzzle for cropping sparse sedges close to the root; split upper lip or similarly dexterous mouth for selecting the mineral-rich shoots around marsh margins; nostrils that can close against driven drizzle or steam; heavy-lashed eyes set to catch movement across wide lateral distances. If horned, the horns would not be airy branching antlers but compact tools—swept back, thick-based, perhaps asymmetrical from use and breakage against fresh stone. More likely in such a young, practical landscape, ornament would be minimized. Energy goes into insulation, footing, gestation, vigilance.

These animals would travel in loose files across the high shelves, not tightly packed but spaced enough that each can read the ground ahead. Their paths would become some of the first true lines in a country not yet organized by age. They would know where the seepage comes through warm enough to keep plants alive a little longer, where salts crust pale at the edges of new fractures, where marsh skins can bear weight in morning cold and fail by afternoon thaw. In that sense they would be engineers without intention. Their repeated movement would stabilize some routes, open others, churn flats into richer feeding patches, deepen the connection between water, mineral, and flesh.

But I do not think the place belongs to them alone. Too much of it is made of intervals and hints. Around and beneath the herds there would be a whole second citizenry of quick, low, weather-tight lives.

There would be stone-runners: small mammals with blunt ears, dense pelts, and whiskers always jeweled with damp, living in burrows cut into the firmer embankments where uplifted sediment has just enough hold. They would emerge in the moderated light to clip sedges, gnaw mineral-rich roots, gather the brief flowers before weather takes them, then vanish instantly at a shadow passing through fog. Their bodies would be compact enough to conserve heat, but restless enough that stillness itself seems dangerous. Their tunnels would lace the drier rises and collapse edges, taking advantage of the fact that the land is young enough to be soft in pockets.

There would be marsh-waders too—narrow-bodied birds with improbably long toes, able to walk over trembling mats and flooded gravel where heavier things break through. They would be colored in wet neutrals: smoke, white belly, soot-barred wings that flash and disappear in cloudlight. Their calls would be the sound most associated with the place: thin, carrying, metallic and lonely until answered from another basin. They would feed not in lush abundance but in the minute productivity of seep-country—midges, larvae, soft-bodied things in cold water, whatever thrives in temporary saturation. Their nests, if you ever found one, would be absurdly slight, almost just decisions made among stones.

And because the land is visibly full of alarm and remains, there must be hunters shaped not for forests or dramatic chases but for folds, pauses, and weather windows.

I imagine a predator low enough to disappear behind a raw berm, pale enough to merge with fogged scree, patient enough to let the terrain do half the work. Not necessarily large. In a place where prey is dispersed and footing treacherous, extravagance would be a disadvantage. It might be a long-bodied canid or catlike thing with oversized paws, dense fur that takes on the color of whatever it is wet with, and a head carried low. Its gift would be timing: rising out of a collapse trench just as a flock settles, or ghosting from steam-threaded rocks toward a seep where many tracks converge. It would eat widely—nestlings when it can, burrowers when it must, carrion often, young ungulates rarely but memorably. The clean bones beside water would be as much its signature as any direct sighting.

Yet even that predator would not feel like the true spirit of the place, because nothing here suggests singular dominion. The landscape resists monarchs. It supports circulations.

So perhaps what most fully lives there is not one species at all but a pattern of life: a guild of cold-wet plateau animals, all tuned to exposure, seepage, and incompletion. Grazers that map routes across uncertain shelves. Burrowers that exploit the new softness under the stone skin. Waders and ground-nesting birds that use shallow waters before they become lakes or vanish. Opportunistic hunters and scavengers following concentrations of movement. Insects breeding in tarn margins during the brief windows when water is neither fully frozen nor fully drained. Lichens and mosses beginning the long conversion of bare mineral into inhabited surface. Life there would be less about abundance of form than about repeated solutions to the same problem: how to persist where the land has risen faster than ecology can fully clothe it.

If you wanted a single emblematic creature, though, I would return to the herd animal and make it stranger, more native to that industrial-natural tension.

Picture an animal like a cross between ibex, musk ox, and marsh deer, but belonging neatly to none of them. Shoulder-high to a person’s chest. Heavy in front, slightly downhill in build, so that it can brace on descents and push through mire. Neck thick, not graceful. Coat hanging in damp cords at the belly in the wet season, shedding into ragged pale patches against summer rock. Feet with flexible interdigital pads that spread on mud and clamp on stone. Dewclaws set lower than usual, useful on saturated inclines. Eyes reflective and pale in low light. A muzzle often whitened with mineral residue from licking newly opened seams. Calves able to stand quickly but taught early not just to follow but to test. Whole groups pausing before crossings, each animal reading the hesitation of the one before it.

Its behavior would be inseparable from the land’s processes. It would seek fresh fractures because they expose salts; seek geothermal seep margins because they keep forage available; seek wind-shadow hollows in fog because cold there is less extractive; climb blunt ridges not for display but for visibility. In storm or dense mist the herd would compact and go nearly silent, every body becoming a moving reservoir of warmth. In clearer hours they would spread widely over the shelves, making the emptiness look occupied by punctuation rather than mass.

Their young would learn the country not as scenery but as categories of trust: dark stone good, black shine dangerous; pale crust useful but fragile; moss means water; steam means warmth and uncertainty; birds lifting suddenly means look up or around; silence at a seep means danger or nothing, and the difference must be learned fast. This feels important: animals there would be highly literate in subtle conditions. The place does not reward grandeur. It rewards reading.

Even their deaths would feed the system conspicuously. A fallen body beside a mineral spring would draw scavenging birds, small carnivores, burrowers taking loosened ground, insects in brief weather, and finally the wet chemistry of the place itself. Bones would bleach slowly under moderated light, often re-darkened by rain, lying among crusted deposits as if included in the basin’s own sorting operations. Nothing is wasted because nothing can be.

There may also be creatures that almost seem geological: amphibious things in the tarns with skins dark as wet slate, invertebrates living in cold films under stones, tiny pale crustaceans in ephemeral pools rich with dissolved minerals. One should not ignore them just because the large animals catch the eye. In a young wet highland, the first masters are often the low and numerous: larvae in seep channels, mites among lichen, beetles under thawing edges, worms where silt gathers in cracks. They are the first administrators of the unfinished world, processing what little can be processed, making future richness possible.

And perhaps, if one keeps faith with the atmosphere you describe, the creature there should finally feel less like a symbol and more like a competency. Whatever lives there lives alertly, economically, and without drama. It is insulated but not soft, social but not densely communal, mobile but not migratory in any romantic sense. It knows dozens of temporary shelters, a hundred small routes, every reliable seep, every dangerous shelf, every place where fog usually pools, every season’s brief green-up. It wastes no effort on display that the climate does not permit. It is beautiful only incidentally, in the way wet stone is beautiful.

So: who lives there?

Thick-coated plateau grazers moving in sparse files over new stone.
Burrowing small mammals in the firmer embankments.
Long-toed birds writing brief lives around tarn and marsh.
Pale, low predators using fog and folded ground.
Scavengers, insects, seep-dwellers, lichen, moss—the first patient colonists.

But if you saw one figure against that broad damp uplift and wanted to say, yes, this is the resident, it would be that salt-seeking highland ungulate: broad-footed, mist-dark, mineral-muzzled, carrying the unfinished country in its body. A creature made not for conquering altitude, but for living competently inside a world that is still lifting itself into form.
