What lives there is not one sovereign monster, nor a hidden people in any simple sense. The district is too reciprocal for that. Nothing rules it outright. Instead it has produced a kind of citizenship suited to seepage, dimness, old maintenance, and negotiated danger: creatures that understand thresholds, pauses, mineral patience; people who have learned to behave like one more careful species among the pipes.

If there is a primary inhabitant, it may be attention itself. Not metaphorically, but almost biologically: everything that thrives there is good at noticing slight changes. A pressure drop. A new smell in the water. A ladder rung more slippery than yesterday. The difference between harmless humming and the hum that means stay back. Life in that quarter favors sensitivity over force. Even the bolder animals move as if they understand procedures.

The most obvious residents are the small urban creatures gone half-subterranean and half-sacral through long acquaintance with the place. The rats are not merely numerous; they are established. They have routes, factions, habits tied to chamber type. The pale ones in the old wetted stone are less frantic than their surface cousins, slower in gesture, almost contemplative when seen at a distance with whiskers lit silver by a lamp. They know ledges no human would trust and flooded cuts where air remains trapped in pockets. The dark workshop rats are brisker, municipal in temperament, glossy from scavenged fat and engine warmth. They seem to understand shift changes. They appear where food wrappings appear, vanish before boots arrive, and travel the cable bundles overhead with the assurance of linemen. Both kinds carry the place in their bodies: rust smudges on the belly, lime dust on the paws, ears full of old echoes.

The cats are something more than predators and less than guardians, which is exactly why everyone listens to them. They are the district’s unofficial inspectors. Damp-furred, broad-eyed, underfed only in appearance, they have the composed severity of creatures that know they are useful. They sleep on warm housings, in coils of rope, on folded tarpaulins in pump alcoves. They patrol parapets over black water with tails held level for balance. They are fed by many hands and owned by none, and so they belong perfectly. A surface cat might be whimsical; these are procedural. When they block a way, they do it with laborious certainty, not drama. When they stare at an empty section of tunnel, people tend to wait. Their kittens, when there are kittens, learn young to test metal before committing weight and to drink only from certain runnels. Their coats seem always to hold the smell of wet mortar.

There are fish that have become less like fish than moving indicators of the district’s hidden logic. In the shallow channels they live in densities that make the water seem self-thinking, every turn of their bodies registering a current too slight for a human shin to feel. Some are silver to the point of disappearance, visible only when they tilt and briefly become coins. Some are pale and nearly eyeless, nosing through mineral clouds where the old sections merge into something cave-like. Others carry a lambent quality in the head or lateral line, not enough to light anything, only enough to make a watcher question whether the glow was real or merely reflected relay-light. They feed on films, larvae, each other, offerings from above, and perhaps on subtler things such as the rich bacterial life that any patient wet machinery accumulates. They are a public resource in the old sense: watched, caught carefully in some places, left absolutely alone in others.

The eels feel older than the other fauna, not necessarily in years but in category. They move through overflow grates and sluice shadows like lengths of mobile wet cable, making every drainage map seem provisional. One imagines they remember connections no blueprint records. Their skins take on the colors of the district—black, bronze, mineral green. They appear where there should be no access and disappear through apertures too small to credit. The workers probably speak of them in the same tone they use for recurrent electrical faults: troublesome, respected, somehow foundational.

The amphibians are the district’s little congregation of soft pragmatists. Toads under dynamos, frogs in warmer chambers near steam runs, small newts where cleaner seepage gathers: these seem to benefit from the place without ever altering it. Their presence gives the undercity a peculiar legitimacy. A tunnel full of pipes still feels man-made; a tunnel where toads have chosen specific warm bricks begins to feel ecologically ratified. They sit in mute satisfaction beneath warning labels and rusting switches, as though old industry had always intended to provide amphibian alcoves.

The insects and lesser invertebrates may be the truest natives because they can exploit scales of damp too fine for larger bodies. There are beetles that treat condensation as weather, emerging after a drier interval to browse the fresh bloom on algae-filmed stone. Midges hatch in invisible clocks from sump water and gather around the dimmest lamps rather than the brightest, as if over-illumination is itself suspect. Water boatmen row through pockets of stillness in cistern corners. Pale mites move through fungal nap like herds over pasture. The whole district has the sense, if examined closely enough, of being granulated with tiny purposeful lives.

Then come the stranger things, and here the district’s character clarifies. It does not generate flamboyant aberrations; it fosters creatures that seem to have continued a little farther than ordinary taxonomy expected.

The translucent crustaceans in the mineral pools under the pump hall feel as though the place dreamt them directly out of lime, machinery, and seepage. They are not beautiful in any ornamental sense. They are too jointed, too candidly anatomical, with their glassy shells revealing pale organs and dark food-tracks. Yet they suit the pools exactly. They click over calcified ledges with the dry precision of tools laid down on stone. Their antennae test both water and vibration. Seen in a lampbeam, they look like animated fragments of discarded mechanism that have somehow learned appetite. They probably feed on bacterial films, shed matter, whatever settles from the slow chemistry of those rooms. They are one of the ways the place blurs manufacture and life without ever announcing that it is doing so.

The lampreys are worse, or better, because they imply connection. They say the district is not self-contained no matter how intimate it feels. If lampreys can cling to sluice gates in chambers supposedly isolated from any river, then the undercity is threaded into a larger body than the caretakers fully know. Their mouths make people imagine mouths elsewhere. Their existence adds depth not by size but by hydraulics.

The ribboning worms that answer barometric change seem almost like an organ of the place itself. They emerge before rain, pale and glistening in masses from cracks and trough edges, knotting and unknotting in ways that are repellent only until one sees how precisely their appearances correspond to weather. Then disgust gives way to consultation. Someone who knows the district might look at a wall alive with these threads and say, simply, “Storm by evening,” and be right. The worms convert atmospheric information into spectacle; they are one of the quarter’s many gauges that happen to be alive.

And in the deepest cistern there is, perhaps wisely, only an outline in behavior. Something large enough to displace water. Something patient enough not to need being seen. The district almost requires such a resident—not because every underworld needs a beast, but because an old hydraulic neighborhood with unemptied depths will eventually produce or admit one larger metabolism than the rest. It may be a giant fish, an eel gone improbable with age, a relic amphibian, an escaped thing naturalized beyond recognition. More likely, in imaginative terms, it is the district’s reserve capacity made flesh: all the unspent darkness and inaccessible volume gathered into one breathing body. The important fact is not what it looks like. The important fact is that people know it is there, know it surfaces, and have incorporated that into ordinary caution. That is how fully inhabited the place is: even the unknown has become procedural.

As for humans, those who live there full-time are not numerous enough to be called a community by census, but they are a community by maintenance. They are the kind of people who become legible only after prolonged exposure to a single difficult environment. Their clothes tend toward repetition: waxed canvas, patched wool, rubberized capes, old municipal uniforms inherited across departments and decades, belts heavy with keys, chalk, folding knives, insulated tools, twine. Nothing crisp lasts long down there. Even a new coat quickly learns the district’s creases. Their skin carries the undercity in small ways—mineral dryness on the knuckles despite all the damp, lamp-smoke at the cuffs, a permanent coolness to the hands. They speak softly not out of reverence but because loudness is acoustically stupid.

The pump-tenders are perhaps the closest thing to householders. They belong to rhythms rather than addresses. They know which flywheel hesitates in cold weather, which valve handle must be approached with the shoulder set just so, how long to wait after a pressure change before trusting the gauge. They keep little nests of personal order in break alcoves: mugs, tea tins sealed against damp, calendars with years that no longer matter, a screwdriver no one else is to borrow. Their work is practical, but long service makes them custodians of lore whether they like it or not. One does not spend twenty years below the street without developing opinions about which corridor dislikes haste.

The inspectors are leaner in spirit, if not body: map-minded, note-taking, careful with edges. They carry brass keys and chalk and maintain the grammar of the district. Their marks on brick are not decoration but temporary law. They know what has shifted a centimeter, where a calcite lip now catches the left boot, which hatch should no longer be trusted despite looking sound. They are the ones most likely to speak about the place as though it were cooperating, because their job requires negotiating with material moods every day.

Electricians down there are a special breed, half skeptic and half reluctant mystic. Anyone who works with old current in wet chambers becomes so. They understand grounding, corrosion, relay fatigue, phantom loads. They also know there are boxes that arc when no circuit diagram justifies it, and gauges that answer to storms or tempers better than to pressure lines. Their professionalism consists partly in not over-romanticizing this and partly in not denying what repeats. They are very good at saying, “It does that,” in a tone that is neither surrender nor explanation.

Naturalists, fishers, mushroom gatherers, scavengers from the brighter margins—these are peripheral residents, but the district seems to tolerate them if they behave. They are the gleaners of its slower abundance. The mushroom people know which timbers fruit safely and which growths taste of copper or sickness. The fishers know which channel mouths produce blind silverlings after heavy rain and which pools should never be netted no matter how full they look. The naturalists come in with jars and leave with notes that sound absurd aboveground and entirely reasonable below.

What none of these humans are is conquerors. Even when they repair, clean, reroute, drain, or restart machinery, they do so as participants in an older conversation. They live there by compliance more than mastery. Their dwellings, if they have them below, would not be domestic in the conventional sense. A cot behind a pump office, a locker room improved over decades, a dry shelf in a warm chamber with a blanket roll and a radio that only sometimes works: these would be enough. To inhabit that quarter is to accept adjacency in place of ownership. The district itself is the real dwelling; individuals occupy niches in it.

There may also be, difficult to separate from the workers, a type of person who has simply drifted down and stayed: a self-appointed watcher, a semi-employed caretaker of cats, a widowed keeper of keys no department now officially recognizes, someone who knows every vent and unofficial shortcut and can tell from the smell whether the upper streets have had rain. Such figures arise naturally in old infrastructures. The city above forgets to account for them while continuing to rely on their memory. They become human equivalents of the rounded brick corners and polished ladder rungs: proof of long use stabilized into necessity.

And beyond all these there is perhaps one more inhabitant, not supernatural exactly, but emergent: the district’s own temperament. Enough systems have persisted there long enough that the place behaves like a resident among its residents. It sulks by delaying sound. It communicates by condensation, by inexplicable gauge movements, by cats refusing a threshold, by water taking offense at a sequence of closed valves. It is not conscious in any human way one could responsibly claim. But it has preferences, habits, sensitivities, zones of ease and zones of intolerance. The beings who thrive there are the ones able to live with that fact without needing to name it too grandly.

So what lives there?

A municipal ecology of damp-minded creatures.
Cats with procedural authority.
Rats with inherited routes.
Fish that read currents like text.
Eels that keep the map honest.
Toads, bats, beetles, worms, and translucent pool-things.
A large unseen breather in the oldest water.
Caretakers who have become part tradesperson, part listener, part minor priest of valves and thresholds.
And the place itself, if a place can be said to live when enough water, darkness, memory, and maintenance have entered into agreement.

Altogether they form not a hidden kingdom but a neighborhood: small, dim, exacting, and fully occupied by lives that have learned how to prosper where infrastructure softened into habitat and every important rule was written jointly by masonry, seepage, and time.
