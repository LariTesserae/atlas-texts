What lives here is not one species, and not even, in the strict sense, one society. The inhabitant of this place is a compact civilization of appetites and repairs: a many-bodied citizenry composed of leaf, fur, shell, wire, tendon, root, spore, and habit. If there are people among them, they are only one guild within a larger polity.

Still, a figure does emerge.

The nearest thing to a primary resident is a human-descended or human-adjacent community of underkeepers—small in number compared with the biomass around them, but disproportionately visible at junctions, thresholds, and moments of exchange. They are not rulers. They are brokers, pruners, listeners, scavenger-engineers, route-memorists, and treaty-keepers between systems that would otherwise snarl or overrun one another.

They would look, at first glance, half feral and half municipal.

Lean from climbing and carrying, broad-handed, often bow-backed from low ceilings and a life spent under beams. Skin probably shows the place in it: a mineral pallor browned or greened where light occasionally reaches, scarred by hooks, spores, bites, acid sap, hot metal, and old wire. Their hair is usually bound close or shorn in practical patches, though some may let it mat into cords threaded with copper, bone tags, shed insect wing, seed cases, or route markers that click faintly when they move. They dress in layers that can survive abrasion and damp: woven root-fiber, softened hide, patchwork rubber, fungal felt, old industrial cloth, cable-knit wrappings, scavenged mesh, insulated sleeves cut down from machine jackets. Nothing hangs loose unless it means to. Everything has a second use.

Masks seem likely here, and not ceremonial at first but environmental: filter veils against spores, lacquered bark visors against wasps, clear eye membranes for sump work, nose cages perfumed with bitter herbs to confuse territorial beasts. Over time such protections would become faces in their own right. From a lamp's distance they might appear mask-faced indeed, less because they conceal identity than because identity here is situational. One face for pruning hot vines. One for hive smoke. One for salvage in mold rooms. One for crossing the loud corridors during hunt hours.

They carry baskets, slings, hooked poles, pruning knives, insulated tongs, reed canteens, little lamps hooded to slits, coils of living twine, ceramic tags, seed packets, and lengths of copper stripped from dead systems and reserved for live ones. Many would smell not dirty but densely inhabited: crushed leaf, warm hide, sour metal, resin, smoke, medicinal moss, compost heat. Their bodies would know the district by gradient and echo. They would pause before entering a room not to be cautious in the abstract but to listen for occupancy: fan rhythm, predator silence, active current, bloom-swarm, grazer traffic.

Their intelligence would be ecological before it was mechanical, though it would include both. A child here might learn to read condensation paths before script, to tell from one tremor in a hanging chain whether a heavy browser or a patrolling reptile has passed below, to distinguish safe fungal light from lure light, dead warmth from powered warmth, territorial droppings from merely digestive ones. They would know where to tap a pipe to summon duct-lizards, when to cut a cable-vine so it regrows denser instead of dying back, which beetle-herds can be redirected with salt, which cannot, how to wait beside a stubborn old relay until a bird bridges it with nest filament and the current returns.

They likely do not think of themselves as living in nature or in machinery. They live in maintenance.

That may be their deepest trait: they are maintenance adapted into culture. Not janitors exactly, not gardeners exactly, not mechanics exactly, but all three so fully fused that any distinction sounds surface-born. A blocked reed bundle is a water problem, a plant problem, an airflow problem, a nesting problem, and a neighborhood dispute. To solve it, one must know the rootweb, the insects, the family downstream, the machine casing it passes through, and the season of a moth that lays eggs in damp valves. These people would be experts in compound causes.

Because top-down order cannot hold here, their politics would be local and mnemonic. Authority belongs to those who remember timings, debts, and tempers—not only human ones. There may be cistern keepers, rot masters, current-watchers, wasp speakers, axle wardens, grazer callers, mold judges, hatch counters. A respected elder might simply be someone who knows three hundred routes through the district and which of them remain legal during flood pulse, molt week, rut hours, and condenser season. Their laws would be practical enough to look like lore:

Never take upper-leaf water unless invited.
Do not light open flame in fan-locust weather.
If the red cabinet glows, leave offerings of copper nearby and sleep elsewhere.
Mark fresh carcasses for the wasps before dark or claim them wholly.
Do not prune a root that has swallowed iron unless you know what the iron still does.
Cross the turbine wells only while the birds are feeding.

Such people would not be alone for a moment of their lives.

Around and through them lives the true majority population: the innumerable animal citizenry that gives the district its confidence. And each major kind feels not like fauna in the passive sense but like a profession.

The broad-backed grazers are the street sweepers and compost haulers by appetite alone. Damp-nosed, blunt-faced, perhaps somewhere between pig, deer, and cellar-cow, they clear lanes simply by preferring them and convert low-quality plant matter into heat, dung, and social traffic. They are probably collared not with ownership but with signal—bells, scent satchels, luminous fungus tags—so their movement can be heard or smelled before they arrive. Children would grow up around their knees. Predators would read their routes. Garden sectors would depend on their manure the way older cities depended on carts.

The six-limbed civet-like hunters are the police nobody commands. Quick, pipe-running, intensely territorial, smart enough to associate repeated human gestures with temporary amnesty or warning, they keep tunnel birds, scavenger bursts, and juvenile grazers in restless balance. They are not tame, but known. Individual animals would acquire reputations. One steals copper. One avoids blue lights. One kills wasps midair and leaves the bodies on warm thresholds. One has learned that certain masked keepers carry gut ribbons and may be followed at a dignified distance. They would be cursed, admired, memorialized, and occasionally fed despite every rule against it.

The hook-footed nectar thieves, transparent-eared and social, are rumor made winged. They move pollen, steal sugar, wake flowers, trigger alarms, and carry spores from district to district. Their colonies might map humidity more precisely than any instrument. Their sudden departure from a chamber could be as meaningful as a gauge dropping. People would watch them the way fishers watch seabirds.

The social duct reptiles—the metal tappers—are line communicators. One imagines narrow snouts, insulated scales, lidless patience, and a language of percussion so well adapted to pipe and casing that whole messages can travel farther through infrastructure than through air. The people may not fully decode them, but they know enough to interpret mood: occupied, nesting, heat, flood, stranger, dead end. Perhaps some underkeepers answer by tapping back. Perhaps treaties began that way.

The ribbon scavengers in drainage runs are the district's handwriting in motion: slick, many-jointed or deceptively legged, able to vanish into a wet seam and emerge in another room entirely. They remove what should rot quickly and spread what ought to spread. They make every watercourse a corridor of consequence.

The fist-sized beetles herding aphids are pastoralists at a miniature scale, and the black wasp swarms are morticians, executioners, sanitation crews, and saints of fast reduction. Everyone would respect the wasps. Fear them, hate them, rely on them absolutely. There are places where social order is built on who controls the grain; here some of it may rest on who knows how to notify the wasps without provoking them.

And above all of these, in a way almost infrastructural themselves, are the insects and small volant things that turn air into traffic. Fan-locusts in vents, membrane birds in flocks, impossible little bats around the skylit grove, moths broad as serving trays opening at dusk like signal flags. They are the moving weather of the district. To inhabit this place is to live inside the consequences of their timing.

Then there are the plants, which are not scenery and so produce another kind of resident entirely: the rooted functionaries. Certain trees are roof citizens, organized around light rights. The cable-vines are bridge builders and embargo enforcers. The bladder-leaf vent arrays are bellows communities. Reed-bundles are plumbers. Conductive sap plants are line tenders. Mosses are upholstery, insulation, nursery, archive. A room's temperament may belong less to any animal than to whichever plant architecture dominates it. A fig court is different law than a thorn battery, a condenser garden different law than a turbine grove. If the underkeepers speak of neighborhoods, they may mean species assemblages rather than addresses.

And beneath all that, there is another resident still: the old machinery, no longer mere object. Not alive in the simple sense, but enrolled. A shuddering pump that wakes only in rain season, a battery wall guarded by thorn lattice, a ceramic-jacket heater fed by ancient dribble-power, a relay whose weak current still organizes nesting and migration—these have become something like elder institutions. They are fed, cleaned, visited, interpreted. Their failures are ecological events. Their continuance is cultural memory made material.

So who lives there?

A distributed people of maintenance, certainly: mask-faced underdwellers who have made a civic life out of pruning, salvaging, negotiating, and remembering.

But also a more-than-human municipality in which every species occupies a trade, every corridor has stakeholders, and every useful process has been claimed by multiple forms of life at once.

If you wanted to imagine one inhabitant who stands for all the others, imagine this:

A slight figure on an iron catwalk above a grove-lit loading hall, pausing with one hand on a vine grown along the rail. Their lamp is shuttered. Their basket holds cut reed lengths, two warm eggs, a coil of stripped copper, medicinal moss, and a ceramic valve. Around their boots the grated floor hums faintly from hidden airflow. Their mask is made of layered bark lacquer and fine brass mesh; moisture beads on it and glows for an instant in fungal light. They are listening.

Not for danger only. For sequence.

A tap in the duct.
A pump beginning below.
The fan-locust housing taking up its soft rotary buzz.
A herd shifting lane.
A civet-thing skittering over a beam.
The distant triple strike of metal that means someone else is working the next district over.
The opening of a vent leaf overhead.
The moment when all these sounds fit, and passage is possible.

That person lives there.

So does the thing in the duct tapping back.
So do the roots tightening over old concrete.
So do the grazers making roads with their hunger.
So do the birds bridging contacts with stolen wire.
So do the wasps waiting for notification.
So does the weak current in the cabinet.
So does the moss on the stair hollowed by generations not all human.

The place is inhabited by a negotiated order that has learned to wear many bodies.
