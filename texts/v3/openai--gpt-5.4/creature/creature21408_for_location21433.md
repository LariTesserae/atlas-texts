What lives there is not one emblematic beast standing alone on a ridge. It is a citizenry.

The basin is inhabited by a dense, old, many-specied society of dry keepers: creatures and growths that have become so entangled with one another that "flora," "fauna," and "built environment" are no longer cleanly separable categories. If there is a dominant life-form, it is not simply an animal or a plant, but a way of living that prizes retention, adjacency, maintenance, and silence. Everything there seems evolved not to spread, but to thicken.

At the center of that society, though, one begins to sense a particular kind of resident: small-to-medium communal animals, deft rather than strong, vertical rather than wide-ranging, with bodies made for ledges, crevices, pruning, carrying, sealing, and repeated passage through narrow stone lanes. They are the ones who make the place read as inhabited rather than merely overgrown. They are not toolmakers in the metallurgical sense, but arrangers, binders, fitters, polishers, tenders. Their intelligence is architectural long before it is technological.

You can imagine them first by their traces. Cup-like dwellings woven from fibrous succulent strips. Resin-glazed nest facades repaired in layers over generations. Stones chosen not for monumentality but for fit. Paths scored into exact footholds by claws or hard pads. Storage hollows lined with membranes and shed skins. Thorn curtains trimmed so they open one way and not another. Browsing stands kept low. Shade courtyards preserved from encroachment. Refuse sorted by kind and tipped over designated edges. None of this requires fire or forge; all of it requires habit, memory, and social continuity.

Then the inhabitants themselves come into focus.

They are probably not mammalian in the familiar sense, nor cleanly reptilian, nor avian, though they may rhyme with all three. In a place where water is precious and daytime heat exacting, the primary citizens would likely wear dryness as an accomplishment. Their skin would tend toward sealed surfaces: fine scales, waxed fur, velvety dust-catching nap, or a hide with overlapping plates soft at the joints and nearly ceramic on the exposed planes. They would not be sleek wet-eyed creatures. They would look matte, economical, finished.

Their bodies would be compact and careful. Nothing lanky that wastes moisture. Nothing so heavy that it cannot negotiate shelves and branch-bridges. Think of an animal between a rock hyrax, a parrot, a small antelope, a lizard, and a nest-building marsupial—but resolved according to this basin’s rules. Grasping forelimbs, perhaps not true hands but dexterous enough to pluck fibers, turn pebbles, pinch resin, and brace on vertical faces. Hindlimbs or rear limbs built for springing short distances and absorbing careful landings. A balancing tail, possibly thick at the base as a reserve organ, used not just for movement but for signaling. Faces with protected nostrils, closable slits or valved openings against dust, and large, exact eyes hooded from glare. Ears, if external, would be small; hearing would favor near-field subtleties over broad theatrical listening.

They would communicate almost privately. The place has trained them out of wasteful declaration. So the dominant residents likely speak in close-range systems: throat skin flushing with slow color, tails held in codified curves, pads drummed against hollow stems, resin cups clicked, teeth or beaks ticked softly, torsos pressed to living cables that carry vibration through shared plant structures. At a distance they might seem nearly mute; up close they would be in constant conversation.

Socially, they would be colony-dwellers without becoming anonymous. The basin feels too custom-rich for pure swarm behavior. Its residents would know one another by ledge, lineage, scent, and route. They would have sleeping clusters, nurseries, lookout perches, rind stores, salt caches, cool chambers, birthing hollows, molt niches. They would organize space by season and heat load. Elders might not rule by force but by memory: which root bridge is safe this year, which resin wall must not be touched at noon, which terrace belongs to brooders, which flowering crowns open tonight, where hidden moisture lies under contract with other species.

They are almost certainly omnivorous in a narrow, disciplined way. Not hunters of spectacle, but harvesters of gradients. They would crop rind, pierce oil-rich bulbs, gather seeds, lick salt and metal blooms, take grubs or soft-bodied burrowers from beneath crusts, raid waxy droppings for still-valuable compounds, and tend certain plants less as farmers than as co-dependents. Some of their food would need processing, though not cooking: scored to vent irritants, dried further in shade, packed into resin-lined niches, inoculated with symbiotic films, or left in mineral chambers to mellow. Their mouths would reflect this—not broad tearing jaws, but precise nipping structures, perhaps a short hooked beak over grinding plates, or incisive front teeth with ridged molars, or paired keratin edges evolved to shave succulent tissue cleanly without spilling too much precious fluid.

And they would smell the place better than they see it. Not lush perfume—there is too little volatility for that—but the minute signatures of oils, warm salts, ripe sap, hidden dampness, brood scent, molt dust, night-bloom heat, the clean mineral cold of recently opened rock. A resident there could likely tell, from passing a wall, whether a nest chamber is occupied, whether a plant’s pressure reservoirs are full, whether a stormless humidity rise in the night might justify opening certain combs or venting certain stores.

But these primary citizens do not live alone, and the description resists reducing the basin to one species’ town. The basin is really a consortium.

There are the membrane-winged gliders: pale, silent commuters of the upper air, perhaps pollinators, perhaps thieves, perhaps messengers between terraces. They nest in the high resin cups and fold themselves into dry-leaf geometries when resting. Their wings are likely not feathered but veined and powdery, good for cool-hour travel in still air. They live off flowers that open by heat cue, tree-cactus gums, tiny animal prey, and the exposed nutrient seams of communal middens.

There are the thick-bodied browsers moving at ground and mid-level, dog-sized but more deliberate than herdlike. They are part livestock, part wild infrastructure: cropping column plants so lanes remain open, stripping rind in ways that stimulate regrowth, concentrating nutrients into midden terraces later used by roots and smaller scavengers. Their broad feet polish streets. Their routes become avenues. They are probably tolerated, guided, and occasionally excluded by the basin’s more dexterous residents rather than fully domesticated.

There are armored burrowers underneath, the sub-city engineers. They fracture crust, ventilate deliquescent pockets, bring mineral fines to the surface, make hidden cool chambers possible, and perhaps serve as prey only during certain molt stages. Their bodies would be wedge-shaped, close-jointed, capable of moving through powder and sharp young cracks without losing much moisture. Much of the basin’s apparent freshness may actually be continually edited by them.

There are the six-limbed climbers, minute specialists that turn trace condensation into survival. These feel almost like the basin’s pickpockets and microshepherds: animals that visit pores, flower throats, salt tears, fungal films, and condensate combs, taking almost nothing each time but everywhere at once. They are likely crucial pollinators, cleaners of brood chambers, and vectors of the basin’s subtler economies.

And then there are the living structures themselves, which are not passive scenery. Many of the dominant plants would behave almost like sessile municipal partners. The translucent ribbed columns are cisterns, shade towers, and thermal batteries. The fused fan-leaf platforms are plazas and roofs. The braided cable plants are ladders, moisture conduits, vibration lines, and territorial boundaries. Thorn lattices are doors before anyone names them doors. Resinous sheath-growths are plaster before anyone invents plaster. Hollow calcified trunks filled with packed fibers are warehouses, dormitories, and nurseries in one. To live in that basin is to live partly inside the bodies of other species who have, over long mutual adjustment, become load-bearing members of society.

So if one asks, "Who lives there?" the truest answer may be: a quiet dry civilization of mutualists.

Its most characteristic inhabitants are likely small cliff-and-succulent dwellers with dexterous limbs, sealed bodies, and intricate social memory—animals that build by pruning, fitting, lining, sealing, and reusing. They are attended, constrained, and supported by a host of other residents: gliders in the upper lanes, browsers in the courts, burrowers below, minute climbers in every seam, and a flora so architecturally involved that it functions as public works. Predators likely exist too, but even they would be restrained: ambush forms tucked into page-like stone aisles, silent night hunters reading vibration through pads or whisker-fans, brood-raiders tolerated only because no ecology can afford purity.

What do the main inhabitants feel like, encountered directly? Not fierce in the theatrical sense. Intent. Dry-eyed, warm-bodied, and difficult to surprise. A creature that pauses with one forelimb on a resin-polished wall and looks at you with the composure of something from a place where panic is metabolically expensive. Its throat skin darkens or pales. Its tail tip flexes once. It decides whether you are obstacle, danger, novelty, or merely weather. Around it, others remain in view but do not startle into noise; they redistribute themselves through the architecture with tiny exact movements. The whole colony’s response would be measured, as if urgency must justify its own cost.

They would likely reproduce slowly, invest heavily, and cache not only food but conditions: shade, humidity, inherited chambers, safe paths, known pairings between flowering plants and active pollinators, the timing of pore-opening at dusk. Their culture, if we allow the word, would be rich in maintenance. Not conquest, not display—upkeep. Keeping passages passable. Keeping nurseries clean. Keeping thorn doors live. Keeping salts out of one store and in another. Keeping memory of where the mountain still shifts.

That last part matters. The place is geologically young. So the things living there must be old in behavior but opportunistic in body. They are colonists of fresh fracture, quick to occupy new ledges, root into new mineral faces, claim newly fallen blocks as walls and roofs. They have not grown atop a settled ancient landscape; they are participating in the first long domestication of a wound. That gives them a quality of practical improvisation. They do not require permanence to build custom. They can read a crack opened three seasons ago and already know how it might become a lane, a cool slot, a brood pocket, a trap for leaf-dust, a place to train a cable-root.

In another world one might call them mountain dryads, stone swifts, resin hyraxes, cactus macaques, salt marsupials, terrace lizards, or cliff rodents. None would be quite right. They are creatures shaped by concentrated scarcity into an ethics of nearness. They live close to one another, close to their food, close to the little atmospheres they create and guard. They are not loud enough to dominate the basin acoustically, so they dominate it by use. The place carries their signatures in polish, pruning, lining, and repaired edges.

So: what lives there?

A populous, low-voiced, architecturally minded desert upland community. Succulent towers that are also cisterns and homes. Silent gliders in the high light. Browsers like careful living chisels. Burrowers mining coolness from powder. Tiny climbers harvesting invisible water. And among them, most memorably, the basin’s true householders: nimble communal creatures with sealed skins, grasping limbs, and a genius for making streets, chambers, nurseries, gates, caches, and customs out of stone, thorn, resin, shed membrane, and time. A people of the dry city, living in the mountain’s fresh interior as if abundance were something one could weave from restraint.
