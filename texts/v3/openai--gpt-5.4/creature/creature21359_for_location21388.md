What lives there is not one thing but a habit of life that has learned to impersonate infrastructure.

At first you might notice the animals, because animals are where the eye goes when it wants a resident rather than a process. You would say: there are herds here. There are hunters. There are gliders, burrowers, cave-nesters, things that drink frost and things that read heat the way hawks read motion. You would be right, but only shallowly right. The true inhabitant of these mountains is a society of gradients made flesh over evolutionary time: organisms that do not merely survive the high cold, but participate in its circulation.

Still, some beings do stand forward.

The grazers are among the clearest citizens of the place. They are six-limbed, but not monstrously so; their extra pair of forelimbs makes a kind of mountain sense, allowing them to brace on steep velveted slopes or kneel against warm stone while the rear body remains ready to spring away. Their muzzles are broad and soft-looking, split with dark sensory pits that detect heat under moss crusts and in rootwork. They feed less like deer than like surveyors: pausing, pressing the face to a mat, testing moisture, warmth, and chemistry before biting. Their coats are dense but short, often layered with moisture-shedding outer filaments over a warmer under-pelt that catches the faint phosphor of the night insects. In the predawn they gather around seep-warmed cracks, arranged in loose circles that look almost ceremonial from afar, though really they are only reading the mountain’s exhalations together.

Some of these herd-beasts carry antlers, though antler may be too rigid a word. Their head-branches are broad, dark, and slightly porous, grown not just for display or combat but as thermal instruments. Frost catches there first. Condensation pearls there. They lower them to scrape or rake cold deposits into hollows and then lick the melt. In another world antlers would be ornament or weapon; here they have become tools for farming thin water out of air.

There are cliff gliders too, one of the emblematic lives of the range. They begin in shadow under the high canopies or on mineral shelves where the vent-warmth rises in regular pulses. Long-bodied, narrow-skulled, with six limbs altered into a combination of grasping hooks and sail-struts, they are less bird than living kite. When folded, they seem awkward, almost vulnerable: bundles of tendon and dark membrane clinging to bark or stone. In flight they become exact. Their skin-sails lock taut between elongated ribs and limbs, and they enter the mountain’s invisible traffic—thermal plumes from hidden fissures, cold downdrafts spilling off frost basins, shear zones where one ravine breathes into another. They do not flap much. They negotiate. Seen from below, crossing the nearly black daytime sky, they resemble tears in the light.

Some are browsers of high leaves and spore-fronds, moving basin to basin without descending. Others are predators, and those are worse to behold only because of how little they seem to do. A predatory glider can hang in a current with almost no motion, then fold half its body and drop soundlessly into a grove, vanishing into branches and steam. The mountain trains everything toward restraint.

Lower, within the thick felt of moss and fungal braid, live the tunnel-makers. They are burrowers by instinct in a land where soil is too rare to receive burrowing, so evolution has taught them to mine the living carpet instead. Imagine something low and insulated, with a blunt wedge-head plated not in horn but in densely packed root-resistant scales, forelimbs broad and comb-clawed, nostrils closable against spores and fibers. Their tunnels are not empty holes but lined passages through communal tissue, warm with fermentation in some places, icy in others, threaded with food caches, larvae, and hitchhiking symbionts. Whole hillsides may be underwritten by their traffic. Step on a convincing surface and it may answer with a muffled rush beneath you: residents changing shifts in the dark.

These burrowers are prey to many things, especially the patient ledge-hunters. Those are perhaps the mountains’ purest expression of predation: long, low, stone-colored bodies with limbs that can flatten or fold tight under them, skin or fur broken into mottlings so exact they seem less camouflaged than geologically continuous. Their heads are often unremarkable until they turn and some hidden sensory surface opens—a frill of heat-sensitive pits, perhaps, or whiskers fine enough to detect the movement of air displaced by a hoof beyond the moss. They do not chase far. They become the place where pursuit ends. Warm vent beside a feeding shelf, narrow pass between two damp colonies, ledge above a habitual crossing: they wait where comfort gathers prey. One might lie half a day as a harmless patch of black growth and then reveal, with a single rearrangement of its outline, that the moss had been breathing.

Then there are the ravine things, the ones the mountain keeps less visible.

In the glow-fungus gullies where water moves in films and bacterial chemistries support whole sub-ecologies, life becomes pale, fine-boned, translucent, or blind. There are threadworms combing stone for microbial lace. There are soft-footed quadrupeds with sealed eyes and elaborately veined ears, reading warmth and vibration through skin instead of sight. There are delicate predators with jaws too clear to notice until they catch the bioluminescence behind them. There are membrane-winged scavengers that cluster together over warm mineral outcrops, draping themselves like charred cloth while sharing heat through contact. Many of these creatures seem provisional, less like finished species than temporary agreements between mineral opportunity and animal form.

And something larger moves there. The description gives it away only by consequence: lights going out in sequence, whole living curtains dimmed by approach. That suggests a resident that is not fast but sovereign within its narrow kingdom. Perhaps a broad-bodied ambush feeder with a heat-suppressing skin, passing through luminous ecologies like a ship through phosphorescent water. Perhaps a many-limbed browser on the fungal curtains, large enough that the pollinators and light-organs close reflexively as it nears. Perhaps not one beast but a colony-creature, an assemblage of linked bodies traveling together in the wet dark so that what passes feels singular because its effects are singular. Whatever it is, it belongs to the mountain’s deeper syntax: known by alteration of process more than by appearance.

The sky above these high green shelves also has inhabitants, though they resist easy naming. Some are certainly birdlike, but altitude and dimness have pushed them away from the familiar idea of birds. Their silhouettes are often all one receives: a crossing shape, a drift of lesser darkness in the black-blue day. Closer, they might prove to have plumage reduced to insulating hair-feathers, wings fringed with static-catching filaments, faces ringed with tactile fans for navigating fog and thermal variation. Those streamers mentioned in the description—spore-catching, charge-holding—suggest animals that do not merely fly through the ecosystem but harvest from the air as they travel, collecting food, scents, and electrostatic cues. They may nest near warm cave mouths not just for shelter but for incubation, outsourcing metabolic expense to geology.

The insects and small invertebrates are likely the mountain’s busiest citizens, its truest machinists. Pollinators that pulse blue from within are not glowing for beauty; they are messaging across fog and dark, coordinating mating, feeding, or swarm movement in terrain where sight is compromised. Pale vent-dwelling grazers feed bacterial films and are fed upon in turn. Tiny luminous species settle on the backs of herd animals, perhaps drinking salts from their coats, perhaps cleaning fungal growths, perhaps using them as moving heat islands. Mites, root lice, spore wasps, caddis-like film swimmers, electroreceptive cave arthropods—one feels the place must be thick with such small precision lives, each tuned to a narrow band of moisture and temperature, each switching on only when the mountain enters the right phase of its daily cycle.

And beneath even these, if beneath means foundational rather than lower, are the composite beings: the mats, pelts, braids, and steaming escarpment colonies that blur the line between organism and habitat. They live there in the most literal sense because they are there so completely that other life lives inside them, through them, by permission of them. A house-sized cushion of moss is not vegetation in the simple way a meadow is vegetation. It is a district. Its upper nap condenses water; its middle retains warmth; its lower chambers host fungal processors, insect nurseries, bacterial films, root storage, scavenger routes. A velvet slope on the far mountainside may be one colonial superorganism spread over hectares, adjusting chemistry with temperature, opening pores at dusk, closing them at noon, shunting moisture from margin to core. Some cliffs are less cliffs than skin.

This is why the place feels inhabited by arrangement. The residents are often not single animals but systems wearing the temporary masks of species.

If one had to imagine a characteristic intelligence born here, it would not be a loud or empire-building one. Nothing in the mountains rewards waste, spectacle, or certainty based on one sense alone. A truly native mind would be distributed in attention, exquisitely aware of overlapping signals: condensation timing, vent rhythm, pressure changes before a weather-strip arrives, the electrical feel of a crowded pollinator bloom, the difference between true stone and stone armored in living felt. It would distrust appearances because appearances are routinely composite. It would understand territory not as lines on a map but as access to gradients—this warm seam at dawn, that frost field before sunrise, the damp hollow under the dry crust, the migration lane of insects between two thermal bands. Even the larger animals seem to think this way with their bodies. They live by reading process, not scenery.

So who lives there?

Herd-beasts that harvest frost with their antlers and kneel at warm rock.
Silent gliders that commute on the breath of vents.
Moss-tunnelers making subways through living slopes.
Stone-colored ambush hunters waiting at the edge of comfort.
Blind ravine quadrupeds and clear-toothed things in the chemic dark.
Bird-analogues trailing conductive filaments through the high dimness.
Luminous insects, bacterial grazers, membrane scavengers, cliff-swimmers in vertical water.
Colonial mats and fungal pelts so vast they function like weather with intentions.

But beyond all these particulars, what really lives there is a mode of life that has become inseparable from the mountain’s labor. The inhabitants are specialists in interception, retention, timing, and concealment. They are not conquerors of the alpine. They are components in an alpine metabolism so old and interlocked that individuality can seem incidental.

The nearest single image might be this: the range is full of creatures that behave like technicians in a factory nobody built. Each knows a station—warm seam, spore stream, dew hour, vent mouth, frost trough, glowing gully. None sees the whole. Together they keep the green impossible thing running high in the cold.

And if there is one resident that sums them all up, it may be the least visible: a life-form, whether animal or colony, that waits in darkness until the luminous cliff goes dim around it. Something that does not announce itself by shape, but by the temporary failure of other processes. That feels right for these mountains. Their true inhabitants are not those easiest to spot, but those able to enter the machinery of stone, mist, and living tissue so completely that they are noticed only when the system briefly changes state.
