What lives there is what the city has had to make in the absence of everything else.

Not citizens in any ordinary sense. Not animals adapted upward into strange forms, because the place has made a law of their absence too completely for that to be a temporary condition. If anything lives there in the active, self-willed sense, it must be something that does not interrupt the city’s great existing arrangement—something that can belong inside constancy, inside repetition, inside saturated sound. Something that does not need seasons, prey, migration, sunlight in regular doses, or even straightforward space.

At first one imagines the inhabitants as hidden people: a water-priesthood gone root-thin and patient, dwelling behind porous walls and coming out only through concealed doors. The architecture invites that idea. Galleries, processional stairs, cistern courts, terraces that feel prepared for assembly—everywhere there is evidence of planned movement and communal life. It would be easy to populate it with silent custodians in dark plant-fiber cloaks, skin glazed with condensation, moving by routes the eye cannot hold onto. They would have learned to speak through touch, perhaps, or by changes in water flow, since no voice could matter against that roar. They would tend no crops in rows, because the city already grows what it wants; instead they would cultivate balance, trim channels, lift root masses from drains, guide living lattices back over broken spans. One can almost see them.

But the place resists that answer.

Anything animal-shaped feels too recent, too contingent. A people would imply hunger rhythms, waste, tools set down, smoke, nesting, remains of struggle, some little friction against the total ceremonial stability of the city. Even ghosts of a people seem too dramatic. This is not a haunted city in the usual sense; it has no appetite for apparitions reenacting loss. Its silence is not grief. Its emptiness has passed beyond loneliness. So whatever lives there now must be less like survivors and more like the city’s own continuation: inhabitants grown from pattern rather than merely sheltering in it.

What lives there may be the water itself, though not as metaphor.

Not sentient streams in the folkloric sense, not faces in cascades, but distributed beings with no single body—hydric intelligences laid through the city’s circulation the way mycelium lies through soil. They would exist wherever water is compelled into recurrence: in the same drip forming on the same lip forever, in the aqueduct that feeds itself, in the pool that reflects elsewhere, in the runnels cut with obsessive geometric care. A human mind wants an organism to begin and end somewhere. This place does not. Its resident life could be hydraulic and architectural at once: the inhabitant of a terrace is the terrace’s pattern of wetness, the inhabitant of a shaft is the resonance of vapor rising through it, the inhabitant of a cistern is the memory held in its level surface.

If such beings perceive, they may perceive pressure before shape. Duration before event. They would know the city as gradients, mineral tastes, root incursions, condensations, deviations measured against an immense baseline of sameness. A new leaf dipping into a channel might be, to them, what a hand on the shoulder is to us. A blockage in an overflow might be pain. The opening of a calcified seam into a fine new spray might be birth. They would not need eyes, though the city provides a thousand. Every reflective film, every still pool, every bead of water clinging to a fern tip could be one temporary organ of awareness. Their thought—if thought is the word—would be massively parallel, local and continuous. Not language, exactly, but recurrence recognized by recurrence.

And yet even that may be too diffuse. The description asks not only for what animates the place, but who or what lives there. Something can be encountered.

Then perhaps the inhabitants are the rooted figures the city grows at thresholds.

Not statues, though they might first be taken for them. Not plants, though much of their substance would be botanical. Imagine forms that emerge where architecture and vegetation have had the longest time to compromise: under archways, beside reservoir mouths, in the sheltered angles of stair turnings, along bridges where mist condenses most steadily. They begin as thickened root-knots and mineral accretions, masses of white root-hair, black moss, porous stone, and glossy water-swollen tissue. Over ages these accretions organize. Weight settles into stance. Hanging cords gather into veils or limbs. Basalt bulges suggest shoulders. Vascular channels line up with carved grooves in old masonry. Water chooses a path through them and keeps choosing it. A figure results not because anyone sculpted one, but because the city repeats certain solutions until they resemble anatomy.

These beings would not move often. Perhaps “move” is too strong a word; they would re-posture over hours, over days, over the long almost-time in which the city permits change. One may stand in a colonnade and think a dark pillar is merely overgrown stone, then later realize that the root mass has inclined slightly toward a new leak, or that the pale tendrils hanging where a face might be are parted differently, exposing vesicular hollows that gleam with trapped water like patient eyes. Their locomotion, if it exists, might happen in stages too slow for direct witnessing and too coherent to dismiss afterward. A figure once rooted in one alcove is later found attending another, with a damp trail of newly accepted moss along the wall between.

They would be nourished by everything abundant there and everything absent. Water, obviously. Mineral dissolution. Heat venting from below. The ceaseless rain of spores. The soft collapse of fruit into root-webs. They would not eat in the predatory sense; there is nothing to chase. Instead they would absorb, wick, distill. Hollow bulbs nested in their torsos might store cold water within warm air. Fans of leaf or gill-like stone lamellae might spread under their arms to gather condensation. Fine white feeding filaments might enter the carved seams between paving blocks as delicately as nerves entering flesh. They would live as orchids, cisterns, and retaining walls live: by arrangement.

Their senses would be suited to a city where visibility lies. Sound would be useless as distinct signal because of the total roar, unless they hear through vibration conducted by stone. That seems likely. One can imagine them feeling the full map of the city through their points of contact: the bass load of interior rivers, the lighter tapping of upper drips, the tremor where an impossible stair loops into itself. They might detect contradiction physically, the way a spider detects tension in a web. A place where inside opens onto ravine and outside onto neighboring wall would not confuse them; it would simply have a particular pressure signature. Reflections that show elsewhere might be as informative to them as windows are to us. If a pool presents a lower district, perhaps one of these beings can drink from it and thereby know the condition of those terraces directly.

Would they have individuality? Perhaps only at close scale. From afar they would all seem instances of one broad life process, like mangrove trunks or fungal fruiting bodies. But nearby, distinctions would emerge. Some are columnar and austere, all black basalt plates furred with emerald moss, with long pendant root-beards combed by runoff. Some are bulbous, translucent in parts, with pale blue sap or water-glow moving slowly through visible channels. Some wear crowns of sulfur-yellow flowers that open in the mist and close again with no audience. Others are almost all stone, their living tissues hidden deep within, betrayed only by occasional fresh green at seams and by the fact that water descending them divides too neatly, too consistently, as if guided by intention.

They might gather, though “gathering” in that city would not look like assembly. A plaza slick with permanent reflective water could contain six or seven of them at its edges, each fused to a planter, a wall, a stair-foot, a cistern lip. They would not face one another; they would orient to channels, vents, and descending curtains of water. Yet something would pass among them through shared hydraulics, pressure fluctuations, synchronized openings of pores, changes in internal luminescence. In the fungal-lit hours they may answer one another with slow brightenings: one pale organ-tower in a lower cistern glows, then another terrace far above glows in the same blue, then a whole vertical neighborhood takes on a submerged pulse, as if a thought were climbing through plumbing.

If they remember the vanished builders, they probably do not remember them as we would. Not as faces, stories, reigns, or catastrophes. They would remember them as original channels. As the first cut that taught water where to go. As warmths once concentrated in doorways. As oils from hands darkening lintels. As periods when roots were repeatedly lifted from the same drain. Perhaps the city’s old inhabitants did not disappear so much as surrender, gradually, into this newer mode. A priest kneeling beside a cistern generation after generation becomes impossible to distinguish from the mossed figure always found there. A lineage of gardeners, then canal-keepers, then listeners to shafts, then never quite people anymore. Not transformed suddenly, but accepted—like architecture accepted by vegetation, intention accepted by climate.

That would explain something important: why the city feels not abandoned but fully itself. Its inhabitants did not leave. They changed category.

There may also be a rarer form, less rooted and more ambulatory: the veil-bearers of the wet bridges, if one wants to name them. These would be slender, high-jointed, draped in root-filament and mineral lace, passing through spray curtains where the eye cannot hold outline. They are the nearest thing the place has to walkers. One would glimpse them only where paths contradict themselves—half-seen crossing an aqueduct that later proves to feed itself, or standing on the “same” landing reached by two stair branches at once. Their bodies might be little more than organized humidity around a framework of vine, cane, and polished black bone-like stone. Perhaps they form only where the city’s spatial inconsistencies are strongest, as if contradiction itself condenses a caretaker. Their role, if they have one, may be to preserve equivalence: to ensure both stairways remain the same stairway, that the twelfth terrace counted from below remains the twelfth from above, that no overflow ever accumulates into flood. They would be less inhabitants than agents of the city’s calm impossible maintenance.

These are the beings one might, very rarely, meet directly. If you stood in one of the courts open to the pale warm light and watched the perpetual drop form and fall from a stone lip, perhaps eventually another rhythm would emerge within the lawlike repetition: not a disruption, only a second order. A root-curtain at the far side of the court would thicken. Water there would briefly take on shape because something had entered its path. Then the figure would be standing where moments before there had only been an arrangement of moss, vine, and dark wet wall. It would not approach with animal alertness. It would incline, perhaps, as a frond inclines under runoff. To be seen by it would feel less like being noticed than being incorporated into local conditions.

Would they be dangerous? Not in the predatory sense. But they would likely be fatal to ordinary assumptions. Follow one, and distance would stop behaving. Reach toward one, and your hand might come away wet with cold water from a reservoir not physically nearby. Sleep in their presence, and your breathing might unconsciously synchronize with dripping patterns until waking and dream share the same architecture. They would not attack. The city does not need aggression; it has patience. A person could simply be learned by the place, slowly, and in being learned become difficult to separate from it again.

There is another possibility, smaller and stranger: that what lives there is primarily not the figures but the intervals between them. That the true inhabitants are colonies of relation—networks among roots, runoff, fungal films, mineral veins, and repeating geometries—while the visible forms are only temporary knots where that life becomes legible. In that case every terrace is populated already. Every damp wall is inhabited. Every curtain of white roots is social tissue. The city is crowded, but with a mode of life too distributed to register as crowding. The absence of animals then becomes not emptiness but clearance, the removal of one entire kind of interruption so that this quieter empire can extend uninterrupted through stone and water.

If I had to say plainly what lives there, I would say this:

The mountain-city is inhabited by slow chthonic gardeners made of root, moss, porous stone, fungus, stored water, and time—beings grown from the city’s own maintenance systems after the disappearance of animal life. Some are nearly immobile and architectonic, standing in alcoves and cistern courts as if the masonry itself had learned posture. Some are wandering condensations of vine and mineral that move along impossible routes, tending contradictions the way other keepers tend fires. None speak aloud. They sense through vibration, wetness, pressure, reflection, and the chemistry of flowing water. They feed by absorption. They reproduce by division, grafting, and the patient organization of overgrowth around repeated forms. Their society is hydraulic, their memory geological, their attention continuous but local. They are not separate from the city in any firm way; each is a temporary emphasis within its larger living system.

And underneath even them, pervading and coordinating all of it, lives the oldest inhabitant: the city’s own water-borne mind, a distributed intelligence conducted through cisterns, runnels, shafts, reservoirs, films, drips, roots, and reflective surfaces. The rooted beings are its hands, if hands are needed. The veil-bearers are its balancing reflexes. The gardens are its slow thoughts made visible.

So the answer is not one creature, but a civilization after zoology: a populace of hydrobotanic stone symbionts in permanent communion with the cataract-consciousness of the place itself.

The city hangs where a mountain should have been. What lives there is what a mountain, given architecture, endless water, and enough time, dreamed in place of people.
