What lives there is, first of all, a people who have learned to behave like a pressure system.

Not metaphorically in some decorative sense, but in the practical, bodily way that an environment writes itself into muscle and reflex. They are not broad-gestured people. They do not waste motion, breath, heat, or attention. They move with the ingrained caution of those for whom a dropped tool can become a death two hundred meters below, and for whom standing in the wrong place can mean blocking a ladder, interrupting a load path, fouling a vent line, shading a gauge, startling a flock that was telling everyone something. Even when at rest they are positioned rather than merely sitting: shoulder to wall, feet braced on grating, one hand free, eyes flicking to crossings and cable runs and the behavior of the air.

They are probably compact on average, rope-strong rather than massive, shaped by ladders, steep stairs, cramped housing, and work done in harnesses or on narrow platforms. Big-framed bodies can live here of course, but the city seems likely to reward those who fit through maintenance gaps, crouch comfortably in service culvices, sleep in compartments built by compromise, and balance instinctively on moving catwalks. Their backs, knees, and hands would tell their biography. The palms will be ridged, fingertips scarred or thickened, nails often broken short. Faces will show dryness first: salt at the lashes, split lips if one is careless, skin protected with waxes and mineral salves against dust, cold, and abrasive air. Hair, where worn, is probably kept bound, wrapped, braided close, shorn, or tucked into scarves and caps; loose hair belongs to belts and fans and static and grime.

Their lungs may not be quite like ours if they have belonged to this place for many generations. Or if they are still human in every ordinary anatomical sense, they will nevertheless have the look of people whose breathing is a trained economy. They speak in brief sentences because speech is acoustically unreliable and socially expensive; they may favor taps, whistles, coded light flashes, hand signs, rail-knocks, and habits of eye contact over raised voices. A child there would learn early that to call out is crude and sometimes dangerous, while to place a palm flat on a bulkhead and feel a cycle begin is ordinary literacy.

Their clothing would be layered but dry in character: quilted vests filled with shed heat-moth membrane, ceramic-fiber aprons, graphited gloves, sleeves reinforced at the forearm, soft caps under helmets, hook loops for tools, masks hanging ready at the throat, goggles clouded by fine scratches. Color comes less from fashion than from function at first—warning orange, salvage blue, unit stripes, family marks, reflective stitching—but because people are people, function will have become style without admitting it. The seam tape on a jacket becomes a pattern. Hazard chevrons are painted with extra care. A ladder-rig earns decorative knots. Brace plates are engraved on hidden surfaces. Respirator housings get saints, jokes, names of dead mentors, little painted eyes to make the machine look back.

They are probably a stratified people in the old literal sense: altitude matters. Upper-rim watch crews, middle-tier mechanics and registry clerks, lower-foundry workers, bridge runners, vent technicians, scavenger children, animal keepers, brace inspectors, sealwrights, flock scrapers, unofficial cableway dwellers, extraction planners, furnace-priests if anyone permits the title, and those necessary citizens who move between categories because the shaft is too precarious to allow rigid classes for long. Yet there will still be prejudices. Upper-tier people may smell of outside dust and electrostatic filters, lower-tier people of hot ceramic and iron. Middle-tier residents may think themselves the real city because they occupy the densest human layer, where canteens and schools and offices are. Those who work in the dark below may regard them as soft-handed clerks who forget what load-bearing means. Those near the rim may carry the austere authority of gatekeepers, because seals and intake are the difference between refuge and death.

Children in such a place would be uncanny to outsiders—not joyless, but precise. They would play by traversing what others fear to stand on. They would know flock behavior before they know abstract weather. They would collect droppings, membranes, dead switchbox darts, magnetic filings, broken ceramic teeth, lengths of colored wire. Their games would involve route memory, balancing, knot speed, identifying machine cycles by sound, and daring each other to touch marked-cold stone before the thermal hounds notice. They would be taught not merely manners but civic survival as instinct: never kick debris through a grating; always look up before opening a hatch; count ladders automatically; know three ways home in blackout conditions; feed cleaner beetles and respect their boundaries; if a glider storm starts, cover the eyes first and the mouth second; if someone says “door,” move.

Old people would not necessarily disappear from labor. In a city like this age might shift one’s niche rather than end one’s usefulness. The elderly become listeners to ducts, keepers of maintenance histories, sorters of salvaged parts by touch, readers of stress gossip in stone, tellers of where the first braces went in and what cracked the year the fourth lower gantry was lost. A person too stiff for ladders may still sit in a canteen alcove and identify by ear which hoist is off-balance. Another may breed cleaner colonies, trim heat-moth enclosures, etch identification tags, recalculate old maps against new collapses. Memory itself is infrastructure here. A city always editing itself needs inhabitants who remember previous drafts.

Their morality would be harsh in one register and deeply communal in another. Not sentimental, because the shaft punishes sentiment when it interrupts maintenance, but tightly woven around reciprocity, traceability, and practical mercy. You help because someday the load shifts under your own feet. You report the crack because stone does not respect private grudges. Theft is probably judged on a gradient defined by what was stolen: ornament may be forgivable, fasteners are almost sacrilege, sealant nearly worse, water worst of all. Hoarding warm space, blocking ladders, lying about vent conditions, faking inspections, or neglecting a cleaner colony until wiring fails—these would feel not just criminal but profane. If they have religion, it will be impossible to separate from upkeep. A saint of bearings, a martyr of pressure doors, a shrine built into a patched support where three people once held a bridge until others crossed—these would not feel fanciful here.

Their dead may not go into open earth, because open earth is both unavailable and symbolically wrong. Perhaps they are rendered into useful compounds with solemn accounting; perhaps ash is sealed into wall niches, mixed into ceramic memorial tiles, pressed into tags hung near family doors, or added in tiny portions to structural plaster in a gesture that says one remains a support. Perhaps a name is painted beside altitude numbers and load limits. In a place where matter is scarce and symbolism has to bear weight, burial customs would likely be inseparable from reuse, which outsiders might misread as coldness though it is really an exact form of belonging.

As for what they look like in the face: watchful, certainly. Slightly underlit always. Their eyes may seem older than their years because they are constantly adjusting between lamp glare, furnace orange, and dim pewter ambient light. They will have the habit of looking not at the center of a scene but at edges—where dust moves wrong, where a cable tremor starts, where an animal emerges, where a patch plate is newer than the wall around it. Smiles there are probably quick and slanted, often private, because broad laughter travels and because open-mouthed hilarity in gritty air has practical disadvantages. But humor would be abundant, dry and exacting. A people surrounded by machinery and risk become connoisseurs of understatement. They likely nickname tools and cracks and recurring flock vortices. They mock panic and pomposity alike. They may have entire comic traditions based on absurdly precise maintenance failures or the social shame of misreading a vent.

Their art, like everything else, attaches itself to surfaces already doing another job. Painted saints on patch plates. Family genealogies etched inside locker doors. Altitude songs whose rhythm matches stair flights. Chimes that double as draft indicators. Lullabies that teach alarm sequences. Tattoos made with mineral pigments—perhaps route maps, unit signs, memorial ladders, little glider wings under the wrist, load symbols over the shoulder, the outline of a pressure door over the sternum. Ornament would likely favor durability and smallness: punched metal, wirework, polished slag, bits of silvering mineral, lacquered hide from shaft fauna. Precious things are those that survive abrasion.

And then there are the nonhuman citizens, because this city is not merely inhabited by people with pests around them. It is inhabited by a whole dry parliament of creatures that have entered the civic arrangement.

The shell-backed scavengers that click along cable bundles are the shaft’s mites and monks both: tiny janitors turning waste films into edible continuity. They live in seams where grease accumulates and in the warm shadows behind relay housings. Their bodies must be almost all armor and economy, each shell plate glossy with mineral dust, each little mouthpart adapted to scraping what a wetter ecology would have dissolved. People curse them when colonies overreach and strip insulation binders or foul contacts, but they also seed them deliberately in acceptable zones. A healthy home has cleaner traffic. There may be household customs around tapping a vent cover before opening it, not to scare vermin but to avoid crushing a working colony.

The gliding creatures under platforms feel like one of the shaft’s truest native notes. Not native to the mountain perhaps, but native now to the thermal architecture humans made. They are all hinge, membrane, claw, and folded patience until they are suddenly movement: dropping from the underside of a beam into a warm exhaust ribbon, opening into a taut little geometry, veering with fan tails through columns of dust. Their pale eyes do not need sun; they know furnace mouths, signal bulbs, maintenance strips. They probably sleep in communal clumps, wrapped around each other for heat stability, and wake in waves as air changes. To a shaft-dweller, seeing them launch from under a catwalk might be as ordinary and as diagnostic as seeing swallows rise before rain.

The flocking handspan flyers behind heat exchangers are more than wildlife. They are weather instruments with appetites. Their dry wings making that paper-storm sound when thousands lift at once—this would be one of the city’s great signatures. Children harvest their powdery droppings because trace compounds matter; technicians watch the timing of their swarms because drift patterns reveal temperature inversions; poets, if the shaft allows poets, compare whole marriages or labor disputes to the behavior of these flocks in a vent reversal. They turn excess heat into living motion. They make the vertical void look inhabited even when human crossings are sparse.

The long-legged thermal hounds are perhaps the closest thing the shaft has to beloved animals. Hairless, ash-gray, all tendon and alert nostril, they would move lightly despite their size, threading ladders and open grating with impossible certainty. Semi-domestic packs suggest a bargain older than memory: humans permit them warmth, scraps, sleeping niches near vents; the hounds in return patrol for hot spots, electrical smolder, insulation rot, maybe even certain gases. They are likely not affectionate in the lush pet sense. More selective than that, dignified, professionally interested in people. A child might grow up desperate for one to sleep by their compartment door even once. Adults would pretend not to care while quietly setting aside favored scraps. When a thermal hound refuses a familiar route, no sensible person laughs.

Then there are the blind hopper things in ore bins, blunt little opportunists that convert trace organic contamination into troublesome biomass. They are the kind of creature every industrial ecology invents: ugly, resilient, impossible to eradicate because the system itself keeps making their niche. They smell wrong before they are seen. They pop in the peripheral light. Workers hate them with the weary hatred reserved for recurrent inconvenience. And yet somewhere, surely, someone keeps them in a jar to study their digestive grit, because anything that lives on what little organics reach ore bins may be chemically useful.

The polished-black dart creatures in switchboxes seem almost folkloric: glossy, quick, electric little scavengers feeding on residue and warmth. A child catching them for money tells you everything about the city’s economy of intimacy. These are not distant beasts of wilderness but creatures extracted from the joins of infrastructure, part vermin, part resource, part rite of passage. One imagines little mesh jars under bunks, children comparing catches by sheen and speed, adults warning not to let them breed near important panels because they chew the wrong sealants when crowded.

The outside drifters, the thin-bodied kites battering softly against upper nets at slack-cycle night, are emissaries from whatever dead-looking world surrounds the shaft. They are perhaps the one recurring reminder that life exists beyond the wound in the mountain, though in a form no less austere. Their collection at dawn as food gives them a ritual air: morning harvest from the hostile air itself. They must be fragile-looking and stubborn, all cartilage vanes and skin membranes, creatures of the thin ash-colored sky who meet the city first as filtration burden, then as protein.

There are surely other life forms not mentioned directly but implied by such a place: pale egg masses in warm cavities that children are told not to touch; threadlike colony organisms in slag honeycombs; microbial films in sealed coolant loops with names only specialists know; heat-moths bred not because they are beautiful, though perhaps they are, but because their discarded membranes matter. The shaft would produce distinctions outsiders would miss: not “bug” but separator moth, cable-mite, salt licker, grate louse, vent lace, furnace flea. Taxonomy grows where contact is daily.

What is most striking is that none of these beings—human or animal—feel ornamental. They all occupy a use-space. Even beauty is functional byproduct or tolerated surplus. The people live there because the shaft offers minerals, shelter, enclosure, and a controllable atmosphere in a dead world. The fauna live there because the people made gradients of heat, dust, residue, shelter, and predation niches. The city is a vertical reef built not in water but in the near-total absence of it. Every inhabitant is therefore some kind of dry specialist.

If I had to say who lives there in one breath, I’d say:

A population of spare, watchful, maintenance-devout humans, probably several generations adapted in habit and maybe in body to cold, dust, height, and scarcity; alongside an entire ecosystem of crevice scavengers, thermal gliders, vent-flocking flyers, ash-gray fire-sensing hounds, hopper vermin, switchbox darts, heat-moths, harvested drifters, and innumerable half-tamed opportunists that treat industry as habitat. They are all creatures of ledges, drafts, and residue. None of them trust abundance. All of them read heat better than weather, vertical distance better than horizon, and the difference between a stable hum and a dangerous one better than speech.

They live there the way lichens live on stone and mechanics live inside engines: intimately, unsentimentally, and with complete dependence disguised as competence.

And because the shaft is still being born, its inhabitants probably share that condition. They are not a finished people in a finished city. They are becoming a species of citizen specific to a wound in a mountain—part miner, part rigger, part herder of small austere lives, part keeper of seals, part listener to metal, part tenant of a machine that has not yet decided whether it is a refuge or an appetite.
