What lives here is not one thing alone. The basin seems built to refuse that kind of answer. Nothing in it stands by itself; every life-form has been tuned by darkness, damp heat, long stability, and the quiet intervention of machines so old they now function like geology. If there is a characteristic inhabitant, it is something that has learned not merely to survive in occupied dark, but to move through intervals: between pools, between pulses of hidden systems, between the ordinary logic of ecology and the altered rules the artifacts still impose.

So the first lives here are the obvious ones, though none are simple.

There are grazers in the undergrowth: low, black, many-legged animals with the look of something halfway between a giant water shrew, a millipede, and a soft-furred piglet. Their bodies are compact and slick, rounded so water sheets off them. The fur is not fluffy but directional, almost seal-like, lying in dark ripples over muscle. Around the face and along the neck runs a fringe of pale tendrils—sensory organs rather than ornament—which taste humidity, electric gradients, and minute movements in air or ground. When alarmed or curious they lift these tendrils and they glow with a milk-blue or fungus-green light, not bright enough to reveal them from afar, only enough to let herd-members remain aware of one another in fern-thick dark. They feed by combing mats of moss, young shoots, mineral-rich slime from warm stones, and the fleshy bases of marsh plants. Their mouths are broad and soft-lipped, suited more for rasping and cropping than tearing. One imagines them moving with a hush of many feet and a collective wet warmth, pausing often, never panicking unless the ledges call down to them.

At the marsh margins live the heavier browsers: large-bodied, patient things that sound bigger before they look bigger. Perhaps shoulder-high, perhaps longer than they are tall, with deep ribcages and weight distributed outward through splayed feet that do not sink far into peat. Their hides are dark and sparsely furred except along the spine where long water-shedding hairs form a mane. The face is blunt, but not stupidly so—broad nostrils, thick lips for stripping reeds, and above the skull, hollow chambers or resonant crests that turn breath into those low calls ending in fluted overtones. They are not spectacular animals in outline. They do not need antlers, speed, or armor. The place has made them confident through repetition: the same trails, the same pools, the same hours of feeding. They browse with the composure of cattle in a sacred precinct. When they enter warm shallows, steam catches in their mane and they become briefly half-visible, as if the basin itself were thinking in animal form.

There are predators on the ledges, and these feel less fully yielded to easy image. They are heard as claw-clicks on stone, as pauses in the chorus, as one descending cry so spare and well-placed that it seems almost technological in its economy. I suspect they are long-bodied and low in the shoulders, built for slick rock rather than open pursuit. Their forelimbs might be too articulate for simple cats; their heads perhaps narrow, with whisker-fans or heat-sensing pits adapted to warm stones and hidden blood. The old machinery may have selected for an intelligence that is not high in the abstract sense, but exquisitely situational. Such hunters would know which flat black surfaces hum faintly before a water pulse, which ringed ponds distort sound, which animal paths cross warm shelves where scent hangs longest. Their eyes may be poor and their other senses superb. Or their eyes may be large and silvered, built not for daylight but for the weak emissions of bioluminescent veins and artifact bleed. In either case they belong to the pauses as much as to motion. One sees them, if at all, after they have decided to permit seeing.

Above and among the roots are the amphibious and semiaquatic things: fishlike larvae in the warmed pools, transparent or pink with visible organ-glow; broad-mouthed amphibians with ridge-backed skulls and patient lidless eyes; little waders on stilt legs whose steps are delicate enough to read surface tension. There are frogs, certainly, though “frog” may be too brief for animals that have had ages to diversify in a basin where sound matters more than color. Some may be palm-sized and translucent, clinging to the undersides of warm leaves, their throats flashing dim amber as they produce those wooden bell-notes. Others may be squat and stone-colored, almost impossible to distinguish from wet basalt until they open a mouth lined with luminous tissue and call from somewhere impossibly resonant. Their eggs may adhere to machine-warmed surfaces where temperature remains stable through cold cycles no weather now remembers.

The insects are many, but they seem curated by darkness rather than merely abundant in it. There are hum-weaving swarms, beetles that read mineral seep by taste, mothlike animals whose wing scales deaden sound, lace-legged things that skate black pools without breaking the mirrored skin. Some carry ember-lamps under translucent abdominal plates. Some are drawn to blue-green pulses from the central structure and feed, perhaps, on microbial films that concentrate there. There may be species whose entire life cycle depends on artifact rhythms: eggs laid only after subaudible clicks through stone, larvae emerging when warm pillars enter a particular tonal state, mating flights synchronized to invisible fields around the half-sunk ring.

And then there are the plants, which in a place like this are scarcely passive. The broad-leafed shield plants, the moss velvet, the low orchids, the hanging root curtains, the fern umbrellas: these are the settled fabric of the basin, but among them live stranger vegetative citizens. Some leaves are thick and dark as polished leather, with seams of controllable phosphor in the veins—light organs that open only when touched, browsed, or shadowed by something substantial. Some vines seem to prefer artifact surfaces not only for support but for trace energies leaking through them, and may have rootlets that mimic circuit-branching in miniature. Fungus-fans clustering near warm seams could be metabolizing unusual chemistries, turning old machine exhalations into flesh. The star-shaped mats coating boulders may be less “plants” in a narrow sense than old composite communities of algae, bacteria, and tiny moisture-trapping tissues evolved to live exactly where seepage, mineral content, and thermal consistency intersect. Their softness would be the softness of highly successful precision.

Still, all of these feel like inhabitants of the place. The more interesting possibility is that something lives here that belongs to the treaty itself.

Not a surviving engineer in any simple sense, not a caretaker with recognizable tools, but an organism—or lineage of organisms—so long associated with the buried systems that the distinction between native life and maintenance process has become impossible to draw.

Perhaps they are small. The basin’s technology is too restrained to suggest dramatic sentinels striding through fog. It suggests distributed intelligence, subtle custodianship, intervention by gradients rather than force. So one imagines creatures that move through vents, seams, undersides of shelves, hollow pillars, root-thick channels where warm condensation forms. Pale, many-jointed, perhaps, but not insectile exactly. Soft-bodied in youth, then acquiring a black mineralized skin as they age, their surfaces matte like the central structure. They might emerge only at the basin’s deepest quiet, when the larger animals are settled. Their bodies could register as temporary absences of texture more than as forms. A hand-sized one might look like a knot of roots deciding to walk. A mature one might be as long as a person’s arm, limbless until in motion, then unfolding manipulatory ribs from its sides with disconcerting elegance.

These beings, if they exist, would not “repair” in a human industrial manner. They would clear accretions from seams, redistribute conductive biofilms, prune root intrusions away from sensitive nodes while encouraging them elsewhere, lay down fresh mineral secretions over hairline fractures, open or close capillary channels that move heat through stone. They would feed on the system’s byproducts—trace warmth, electrochemical gradients, microbial mats grown in engineered niches—and in feeding, perform maintenance. Their senses would be attuned to vibration patterns in bedrock and to shifts in water pressure too slight for vertebrate ears. They might gather around a pillar during one of its low tonal emissions, pressed against the moss in a still ring, each body reading the message as nourishment, instruction, or instinctive weather.

Or perhaps the caretaker is larger and older, singular rather than distributed.

The landscape invites that fantasy: one hidden resident whose presence explains the feeling that darkness here is occupied not only by ecology but by attention. Such a being would need to fit the place’s refusal of spectacle. Not a dragon in the theatrical sense, not a giant machine-animal, but something basin-sized in influence and rarely visible in full. Imagine a creature amphibious but not bound to water, with a body broad and low as a landslide held together by intention. Its skin would resemble the surrounding stone—dark, damp, heat-retaining, overgrown in places with symbiotic mosses or bacterial sheens. Along its flanks, seams of blue-green luminescence would pulse in rhythms almost indistinguishable from the artifact threads in the central structure. It would rest in the escarpment’s folds or beneath the warm shelf, entering such deep stillness that plants root briefly in the sediment on its back. When it moves, pools answer first. Pillars click faintly. The hidden systems wake not because it commands them but because its body is one of the old authorized presences the treaty still recognizes.

Its head, if one ever saw it clearly, might be disappointingly gentle: wide-mouthed, whiskered, with sensory cilia arranged around nostrils and jaw. Its eyes need not glow. They might be dark and nearly blind, or silver and inward-looking, using the machine’s intermittent scans as a secondary sense. It would not hunt often. It might feed on the marsh browsers, on mineral slurries in warm seeps, on fungal masses deep in overhangs. It might also do something stranger, such as entering the ringed pond and lying there while the water inside remains perfectly still around it, exchanging charge or memory with whatever survives under the basin. To other animals it would be neither god nor beast of terror but a climatological event: a rare rearrangement of ordinary caution.

Yet even this may be too singular for the place. More likely, what lives here most truly is a clade of organisms that have incorporated old technology into their own life histories so thoroughly that they cannot be understood outside it. Birds or bats that navigate by reflectionless planes. Amphibians that deposit spawn only on surfaces held to exact temperatures by buried heat exchange. Mosses whose growth rings respond to low-frequency emissions. Small mammals whose tendrils sense the machine-hum the way migratory animals sense magnetism. Predators that ambush where hidden fields bend trajectories by a hair’s breadth. Microbes that feed on persistent energy leaks. Fungi that lace through dead conduits and become, in effect, replacement wiring. In such a basin, the most characteristic resident is adaptation itself grown old and confident.

There may also be descendants of the makers, though very changed.

Not necessarily people in cloaks tending relics, but perhaps a relict population reduced, isolated, and ecologically folded into the basin over thousands of years. They would be nocturnal to an extent difficult for surface humans to imagine. Their skin might be dark not by pigmentation alone but by a soft light-swallowing texture that keeps condensation from chilling them. Their eyes could be large, yes, but perhaps the more important features would be outer ears shaped for vertical localization in dripping dark, and hands with broad warm-sensitive pads. They would know every path by moisture gradient, every active artifact by scent. Their dwellings, if any, would not interrupt the basin’s old intervals; they would be tucked into escarpment folds, behind root veils, within spaces the structures already hollowed. Their relationship to the hidden systems would be liturgical only because long practice often resembles reverence. They would not understand every function, but they would know what not to damage, when certain pools become dangerous, why one must not sleep near the northern pillar during damp surges, how to listen for the clicks through stone that precede a quiet redirection of water under the marsh.

But the user asked whoever or whatever lives there, and the truest answer may be this:

What lives there is a community of dark-adapted, moisture-loving, heat-sensing beings that have all, in one way or another, become symbionts of an ancient environmental intelligence. The basin is not merely habitat. It is host and hoster both. Its animals do not inhabit a ruin; they inhabit a still-functioning metabolism distributed across rock, water, machine, and living tissue. Even the largest beasts seem temporary above that deeper resident. The actual enduring life of the place is the basin itself: the old systems in the stone, the seep-fed vegetation, the recurrent animal routes, the warm pools, the microbial films, the scanning absences in the deepest hour. Together they form something that behaves almost like an organism without ever condensing into one body.

If I had to name a representative creature—a thing one might meet on the path and afterward feel one had met the place in person—I would imagine a nocturnal marsh-walker the size of a large dog, long-backed and soft-footed, with dark water-shedding fur, a flexible neck, and a face ringed in pale luminous tendrils. It would step silently from giant horsetails, pause on a warm stone, and turn its head not quite toward you but toward the vibration of your blood, the residual warmth in your footprints, the faint electronic signature of whatever you carry. Its eyes would be black mirrors. Along its flanks, hidden beneath wet fur, would run a few dim lines of bioluminescence that pulse once in response to a subaudible click from somewhere under the shelf. Then it would move on, unhurried, following an animal path older than memory toward a pool that never cools.

And if you watched carefully enough, you might realize that the path it takes is also a circuit, the pool also a sensor, the grazer also a gardener, the predator also a regulator, the moss also an interface. Nothing here lives alone. Everything here lives as part of an old agreement between life and design, so ancient now that the difference has become mostly a matter of perspective.
