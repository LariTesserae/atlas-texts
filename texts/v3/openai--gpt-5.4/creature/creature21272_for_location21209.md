What lives there is something that has learned not to separate tending from dwelling.

At first you might think of the inhabitants simply as people of a cool industrial town—practical workers in coats, carrying knives and ledgers, opening sluices, checking gauges, cutting back growth from hinges and vents. And they are that. But the place suggests a population shaped so thoroughly by its environment that “worker,” “gardener,” “mechanic,” “forester,” “water-keeper,” and even “builder” are no longer fully distinct roles. Whoever lives there would be, above all, custodians of negotiated continuities: between root and wall, damp and rot, light and leaf, machine and season.

They are probably not numerous. A dense crowd would feel wrong here. This is a place that carries signs of repeated use rather than constant pressure, of systems calibrated to modest, regular hands. The people who live there would move with familiarity through routes that are partly street plan and partly microclimate map. They would know where the paving sweats before dawn, which cistern gives a hollow sound when it has fallen below the useful level, which canopy lane stays icy longest in winter shade, which vent needs clearing after a night of pale-winged gliders nesting near its warmth. They would not experience the town as scenery. They would read it diagnostically, almost bodily. A darkened patch of bark means a leak. A different note in the pump’s cycle means sediment. New leaf color on a trained wall means root stress, or excess mineral, or a cracked feed-pipe under the stones.

So the people here are likely observant in a quiet, cumulative way. Not romantics of nature, not quite. Not modern engineers in the abstracted sense either. They would be technicians of living matter, inheritors of methods refined by long weather rather than theory alone. They know how to braid a load-bearing trunk without killing it, how to brace a split branch so it heals into structure, how to prune for airflow around a condenser, how to cast paving around a lifting root, how to let one wall green over and keep another dry, how to train shade exactly where a reservoir needs it and no further. Children growing up here would learn very early that a wall may be younger than the tree supporting it, that a roof can thicken, that streets narrow seasonally, that repairs do not restore an original state but join an ongoing argument among materials.

Physically, they feel to me lean rather than burly, dressed for coolness, damp cuffs, and repetitive work. Layered clothing: waxed cloth, resin-treated canvas, knitted sleeves, aprons with deep pockets, gloves clipped to belts, pruning hooks in loops, notebooks protected from mist. Their garments would show green stains, charcoal dust, iron streaks, pale mineral marks from dried water. Boots matter here. So do cuffs and hoods. Hair is probably worn bound back when working near gearing or graft lines; hands bear the small cuts of fiber, bark, wire, and sheet metal. They smell faintly of leaf tannin, machine oil, wet wool, old smoke from kilns, and the bitter clean scent released by cut sap.

Their temperament is likely reserved without being cold. A place with so much quiet between sounds tends to train speech into usefulness and precision. Not silence from repression—silence from attention. People here would be capable of long shared work with only occasional comments, because many tasks require listening: to water arriving, pressure changing, a belt slipping, foliage rubbing differently in new wind. When they do speak, they might speak concretely. Not “the square by the foundry,” but “the square where the roots lift on the north side.” Not “sometime soon,” but “before the next wet morning” or “after second pruning.” Their language probably carries a rich vocabulary for stages of moisture, textures of shade, conditions of wood, root behavior, trained forms of branchwork, and the moral distinctions between neglect, dormancy, and deliberate waiting.

There may also be a civic temperament built from scarcity without meanness. Water is never abundant, so the people are likely disciplined in a way that reaches into habit, etiquette, perhaps ritual. Storage first, propagation second, cleaning after: that kind of ordering would not remain merely administrative. It would become culture. A resident might feel, almost physically, the impropriety of wasting rinse water that could feed a shaded bed; the irresponsibility of letting a canopy overrun a vent; the social offense of failing to cut back growth from a public path before it catches on others’ sleeves. Care here would be visible and communal. Seasonal pruning being public suggests a society where maintenance is not hidden labor but civic life, where the health of the district is legible enough that everyone notices whether someone is doing their part.

Because so much architecture lives, the inhabitants might also possess a particular ethical patience. They cannot impose every solution immediately. One can bolt steel in a day; one cannot hurry a grafted wall into strength without consequence. So the people who thrive here would be those able to think in staggered timescales: immediate patch, seasonal correction, generational shaping. Their plans might often begin with temporary scaffolds, bindings, braces, and diversions while waiting for slower material to become load-bearing. That tends to produce a cast of mind both modest and exacting. They know they are not ultimate masters of the district. But neither are they passive. They intervene constantly, just not all at once.

I suspect they keep records carefully. Ledgers appeared in the morning image for a reason. In a place like this, records would matter not only for inventories and quotas but for memory beyond any one lifetime: when a reservoir wall last rooted through, which line of pollarded balconies throws too much shade in late summer, which cultivar of roof vine resists dry spells but clogs gutters, which cistern court breeds mold if the airflow screens are repaired too tightly. Their archives may be half municipal, half horticultural, half mechanical—overlapping diagrams with annotations in several hands over decades. A map of the district would not be static. It would need layers: drains, root runs, canopy extents, rail lines, seasonal shadow, firebreaks, ownership, graft age, known seepage.

And because the place blurs categories, the people likely do as well in their social organization. Guilds or trades might exist, but they would overlap heavily. A pump tender must understand algae, shade, and brick. A pruner must understand electrical clearance. A kiln worker must know fuel moisture and the politics of coppice rotation. A builder must think like a horticulturist. Specialists exist, but no one can remain innocent of the living systems around them. This produces inhabitants who are cross-trained by necessity and probably a little suspicious of anyone who knows only one thing.

Are they human? Most likely, yes. The town feels made at human scale, by generations of hands, backs, memory, and repetitive adjustment. But if one wanted to name what truly lives there, one would have to include more than the human population.

Because the district itself lives in a distributed way. Its resident organism is partly civic and partly botanical: trained tree-ranks that serve as walls, root masses that function as foundations, vine vaults that are roofs, clipped embankments that hold roads in place, reservoir canopies tuned to reduce loss, moss-wrapped pipes moderating temperature, fungal and bacterial communities quietly turning waste into feedstock. Not one giant intelligence hidden in the greenery—something subtler and more persuasive than that. A long domestication, perhaps, in both directions. The people shaped the plants; the plants shaped the possible town; the town shaped the people who could remain in it. What lives there, finally, is a mutualism with municipal habits.

If you pressed that into a single character, it would not appear as a king of roots or a forest spirit. It would look more like an old water-and-growth culture embodied in ordinary residents: women and men, elders and apprentices, yard keepers, line clearers, resin press operators, gutter menders, coppice walkers, cistern clerks, scaffold graftors, lamp trimmers moving at dusk beneath the leaves. They would have the patience of people accustomed to maintenance that is never finished. They would distrust spectacle. They would take pride in a well-vented wall, a healthy buttress grown true around a steel rib, a pump yard shaded exactly enough, a lane reopened after cutting with no damage to the host trunks. Their beauty standards would be inseparable from function. A good street is one that drains, grows, cools, carries, and survives.

There may be, too, a few creatures especially fitted to live among them—not many, but memorable: the wall-hive bees, bred for cool weather and calm temper; roof birds accustomed to warm vent housings; gliders using the interlocked canopy as evening roads; patient beetles in bark seams, tolerated until they become a problem; perhaps even semi-domesticated browsers kept at the margins to manage scrub. Yet none of these feels central. They are accents. The true inhabitants are the maintainers and the maintained, entwined.

So: whoever lives there is not merely a population residing in green industry. It is a society of cultivator-mechanics inhabiting a town that is itself half-grown. Reserved, skilled, season-minded, water-conscious, and intimate with slow change. They live by adjustment, by salvage, by pruning, by measured use. They are people who understand that infrastructure can leaf, that architecture can thirst, that a street may require the same kind of care as an orchard and the same kind of inspection as a machine.

In another place such people might seem humble. Here they would be the appropriate form of intelligence for the basin: not heroic, not dominant, but exact, adaptive, and enduring. The thing that lives there is a civilization of careful hands, and through those hands, an urban ecology that has learned to go on growing without ever pretending growth is simple.
