What lives there is not merely a people adapted to darkness, but a people who have let darkness into the shape of their minds so completely that they no longer meet the world by looking at it at all. If they have eyes, the eyes are vestigial in culture long before they are vestigial in flesh. Vision may persist as a private, nearly useless sensation in the young or in those who climb highest toward the less-dark vents, but it would not organize meaning. Meaning would come through the hand, the ear, the lips, the soles of the feet, the membranes of the nose and throat. Their world is one of contact and response. They would think in pressure gradients, in dryness, in resonance, in the minute moral distinctions between living bark and dead bark, ripe pod and sleeping pod, structural creak and dangerous creak.

So the inhabitants are likely slight of gesture but immense of attention.

Physically, they would be shaped less by speed than by economy. Dryness forbids waste. They would be people who move without spilling themselves into the air. Narrow-bodied perhaps, or at least close-held; every motion tucked, every garment fitted to avoid catching on thorn-ribs and wasting fiber. Their skin would probably be thickened where the city most often touches them: fingertips, palms, knees, shoulders, the outer forearms, perhaps the forehead if greeting a wall by leaning to it is customary. Lips sealed when not speaking. Noses efficient, maybe prominent internally more than externally, with labyrinthine passages that reclaim moisture from exhalation. Voices low not because quiet is required, but because sound carries too well to squander. Or else highly trained—able to place a tap, hum, whistle, or phrase with exact acoustic intent.

I do not think they would seem ghostly, though at first one might imagine pale cave-dwellers. The place would not make ghosts; it would make conservators. Their bodies would bear the signs of constant maintenance work: resin under the nails, fine dust in the skin folds, old thorn scars gone smooth, calluses polished almost silky by rope and bark. Hair, if they keep it, would likely be bound, braided, waxed, or cropped close—not from austerity alone but because loose filament catches, drinks precious oils, and confuses touch. They may scent themselves intentionally, not ornamentally but legibly: household resins, district pollens, camphor-crusts from sacred precincts, the bitter trace of quarry dust. Identity in darkness needs edges other than silhouette.

Their clothing would not be draped splendor. It would be layered drought-craft. Felted fiber wraps, barkcloth softened and re-softened over generations, woven pod-silk if any plant can be induced to spin such a thing, segmented guards for shin and forearm, gloves of exquisite importance. Gloves especially: some rough for hauling, some resin-coated for pollination rites, some thin and almost ceremonial for reading delicate growths. Clothing would carry information in texture. A civic caretaker might wear a belt of dry pod-caps that click in a recognizable pattern when walking. An elder responsible for water accounting might have a garment lined with absorbent threads and sealed pouches for gathered condensation. Apprentices may be known by the smell of fresh-cut pith and the faint scrape of unsoftened work wraps.

Yet if one stops with practicalities, they remain too simple. The place implies not just survival but intimacy with an artificial ecology so old it has become ancestry. These inhabitants would live among plants the way coastal peoples live among tides: not as users of a resource only, but as readers of moods and obligations. Their society would produce specialists, certainly, but the specializations would all still be kinds of listening.

There would be binders, who know how to persuade split trunks to heal around braces.

Pruners, whose cuts are legal acts.

Tapkeepers, who judge whether a reservoir stem can be punctured without dishonoring future thirst.

Echo-mappers, who can stand at a junction, click their tongues or strike two thorn pins together, and hear not merely distance but occupancy, humidity, structural fatigue.

Pollinators, perhaps revered and faintly feared, carrying the right oils and frequencies to wake fertile tissues in season.

Dust-wardens, who manage drifts of husk and pollen so that decay can feed growth without choking pathways or swallowing moisture.

Chord-keepers, who tune the wind organs and the hollow towers by shaving, plugging, scoring, or wrapping them, because in such a city sound is part clock, part weather report, part liturgy.

And over them, or among them, those who remember lineages of growth: not kings exactly, nor priests in the distant sense, but custodians of continuity. People who can say this stair-vine was trained by my mother’s mother’s mother; this plaza vault must not be burdened in cold months; this white tendril grove once found a vent that saved the southern district in the dry generation.

They might not call themselves a people by any word equivalent to “humanity” at all. They may define themselves relationally: the walkers, the binders, the warm-breathing, the mobile tenders, the listeners among roots. In a world with no animals, no rival quick life, they are the only moving intention in the dark besides air and falling debris. That would change self-understanding profoundly. They would not learn “life” by watching prey and predator. They would learn it by stewardship and response. The central contrast in their thought might not be animal versus plant, but rooted versus roaming, dormant versus tending, stored versus spent, cut versus healed.

Children there would be astonishingly competent by senses outsiders barely trust. A child could identify district, season, and family by wall texture and ambient pitch. Could tell from one breath whether a chamber has held too many sleepers. Could distinguish eight kinds of useful dryness. Could be sent through absolute black with a basket of sealed seeds and never once put a hand where a sleeping thorn fan might open. Their games would train civic senses: echo-chasing, knot-reading, vent-finding, silent races across suspended walkways, pod-arranging messages for others to decipher by touch. Mischief would involve misbinding a texture-marker or shifting a chain of pods at a corner so someone emerges laughing and lost in a neighboring quarter.

Old age would carry unusual authority here, because time and patience are not abstractions but tools. An elder may not be strongest, but they know by palm-pressure that a column is hollowing too fast, by smell that a seed vault has taken sourness, by the lag in a wind note that a vent-screen needs clearing. Their memories are infrastructural. To lose such a person would be like losing a district map, a legal code, and a season’s forecast at once. I suspect the dead are not hurried away. In a world where all material is slowly reclaimed, the body might be given into specially prepared rootwork, not as disposal but as return to the same living architecture one spent a lifetime maintaining. Perhaps names remain attached not to graves but to growths fed by them.

Temperamentally, they would appear patient to the edge of severity. Not humorless—darkness sharpens humor because sound and touch are intimate media—but incapable of casual waste. They would notice habits outsiders barely know they have: unnecessary throat-clearing, wet cooking, loud footsteps, compulsive cutting, the vanity of making things merely because one can. Their ethics would feel botanical. A wrong act is one that stresses shared structure, steals future resilience, introduces rot, interrupts circulation, or confuses the tactile legibility on which everyone depends. Secrecy itself would be difficult in such a place unless one controlled scent and sound with great discipline. So virtue may involve transparency of movement and permission. To cut is to announce. To harvest is to account. To alter a public wall texture without sanction is close to lying.

But against this discipline there would also be tenderness, because everything fragile is communal. Imagine how gently such people must touch one another. They cannot afford bruising work among themselves when the world already abrades. Greeting might be done with wrist to wrist, brow to shoulder, palm to palm held long enough to exchange humidity and scent. Lovers would know each other as topographies in the dark, every scar and callus a remembered landmark. Parenthood would be deeply tactile: infants carried against wrapped chests in humidity-saving slings, soothed by throat-song matched to the wind organs, taught the city first through guided hands on public textures. Consolation would not need witness by light. A whole community could gather around grief as a ring of warmth, breath, and answering voices.

Their art would be inseparable from function. Since visual display has little purchase, beauty would be judged in resonance, texture sequence, perfume release, thermal surprise, the elegance of a joint hidden under living growth. A masterwork might be a staircase whose rung-scars produce a rising scale under the hand. Or a communal chamber where one clap returns as five soft pulses from five trained hollows. Or a wedding garment woven so that the two wearers can recognize one another in any crowd by the whisper it makes at the knee. Poetry would likely be dense with tactile and acoustic metaphor—less “bright as—” than “true as old bark under fresh binding,” “hollow as a thirsty tower,” “near as resin warming under the thumb.”

Religion, if it exists, would almost certainly concern endurance and rightful cutting. Not fertility in the lush, overflowing sense, but continuity under constraint. Their sacred stories might speak of the first bindings, the first taught trunk, the first condensation net, the generation that learned not to drain a reservoir stem dry. They may revere patches of exposed ancient mineral because those surfaces are the only things in the city that do not answer touch by living change. To lay one’s hand on bare stone would be to meet a prior world, the body beneath the body. Perhaps they hear in that cold smoothness a kind of divine indifference, against which all their careful civic tenderness shines more sharply.

And they themselves might be a little plant-like without ceasing to be people. Not literally green or rooted, but culturally photosynthesis has been replaced by cohabitation. They may spend long communal hours nearly motionless in breathable nests that reclaim condensation from their sleep. They may plan on scales of decades so instinctively that urgency appears adolescent. Courtship may involve tending the same growth together for years. Citizenship may be measured by maintenance contributed rather than lineage alone. Exile, if such a thing exists, would be terrible not because of loneliness only, but because outside the known textures one cannot read the world’s permissions.

I do not imagine them as frail. Dry places often produce hardness mistaken for austerity. These would be durable people. If threatened, they would fight with terrain, not bravado: cutting selected load-vines, opening dust sinks, sounding disorienting chambers, weaponizing thorns, drought, and darkness itself. They know every resonant hollow. An intruder would be deaf and blind among them while they move by remembered slope and hand-mark. Even their tools—root-heartwood splints, silica flakes, pegged thorn grids—would seem humble until used by those who know exactly where to place them.

Still, perhaps the truest thing living there is not any individual inhabitant but the partnership between them and the city. The city has trained them as much as they have trained it. Generations of touching, pruning, breathing, listening, and binding have produced a composite organism: mobile caretakers threaded through rooted infrastructure. Remove the people and the place does not die quickly, but it loses intention; paths blur, ducts clog, pollination timings drift, textures become unreadable. Remove the city and the people are not merely homeless—they are partially unmade, deprived of the external memory in which so much of their culture resides.

So whoever lives there lives as a second nervous system for a wooden, root-bound, drought-hoarding metropolis in eternal night.

They are, in the end, not cave people, not plant worshippers, not post-apocalyptic scavengers in any simple sense. They are citizens of maintenance. A dry subterranean republic of hands. A lineage of listeners who have inherited an agreement with stubborn life: that nothing will be wasted, nothing cut without answer, nothing kept alive by accident alone.

If you met one in the dark, you might first know them not by face but by the pause before they touch a wall—respectful, practiced, almost conversational—and by the fact that when they finally place their palm against the living surface, the gesture looks less like using a city than greeting another resident.
