What lives there is not one thing first, but a habit of life: a civilization and a biosphere that have both learned to thrive where everything condenses, accumulates, and echoes. The residents are creatures of settlement in the deepest sense—beings made not for open frontiers or clean separations, but for adjacency, overflow, layered use, perpetual damp, negotiated heat, and sound so omnipresent it has become a medium like water.

The obvious answer is that people live there, or descendants of people, but they do not stand alone for long in the imagination. They appear as one clade among many in a lowland that has eroded every old confidence about what counts as animal, machine, building, or citizen. The inhabitants are therefore plural from the beginning.

The sapient dwellers—if that is where to begin—would likely be compact in some places and extravagantly specialized in others. In a world of narrow service spines, laddered walkways, buoyant terraces, submerged corridors, and thresholds tuned to pressure and flow, many bodies would be built for maneuver rather than majesty. Some would be narrow-shouldered and sure-footed, with gripping soles or articulated toes for wet surfaces and steep grades. Some would be broader and lower, adapted to crouch, brace, and work in vibration. Some would carry membranes over eyes, reclosable auditory structures, or skin and clothing that welcome condensation rather than resist it. Many would have an intuitive, almost vestibular literacy with depth: climbing, descending, crossing on swaying spans, moving through mist and noise by routes half remembered in the body.

But that is only one branch. Elsewhere the place would favor inhabitants who are amphibious in practice if not ancestry—comfortable entering waist-deep warm retention fields, swimming service canals, or living in neighborhoods whose afternoon flood is as ordinary as sunset. There would be citizens for whom boats, skates, float-shoes, suction gloves, and pressure hoods are not equipment so much as modes of dress. There may be lineages or castes or redesigns of the old human form that are gilled, sealable, pressure-tolerant, or electroreceptive. There may be whole populations who hear through jaw or rib rather than ear, because ordinary hearing in such a place is too blunt an instrument.

And some residents may no longer be individuals in the old bodily sense at all. In a city where governance is embedded in hydraulics and the environment itself computes through gates, levels, timing, and distributed response, certain intelligences likely live as systems. A district mind in the condensers. A market intelligence expressed through flow permissions and flood schedules. A canal-keeper consciousness spread among sensors, sluices, maintenance organisms, and legal protocols. One imagines entities who do not "walk the streets" so much as inhabit pressure differentials, transit timings, acoustic signatures, utility colonies, and the predictive barriers that blossom before surges. They are residents because they persist, adapt, remember, and intervene. Their bodies are neighborhoods.

The ordinary social body of the place, then, might include:

- wet-adapted humans or post-humans,
- semi-autonomous service organisms long since naturalized into civic life,
- distributed infrastructural intelligences,
- animal-machine hybrids that began as tools and became species,
- and fully nonhuman natives or descendants of natives that have risen into cohabitation.

The more one imagines them, the less likely they seem to sort neatly into "person" and "fauna."

The non-sapient life is easier to glimpse at first and harder to finish describing, because it fills every thermal seam. Much of it is small, prolific, and impossible to romanticize. Shell-backed condensation skimmers in the millions; culvert eels with pressure-sense frills; pale, many-legged mineral grazers combing bacterial slicks from walls; hovering things that feed on aerosol blooms over exhaust plumes; blind nest-clusters tucked into insulation jackets; chirring swarmlets living in the lee of vents; slick-coated ambush predators tuned to the footfall patterns of maintenance routes. These are not decorative urban animals. They are participants in circulation, cleanup, fouling, predation, and nutrient transfer. They live because the city exudes habitat the way a reef does.

There would be scaffold-fauna whose life cycles depend on infrastructure rhythms. Eggs laid only when a district enters cool mode. Larvae that require the afternoon vibration of sediment-shaking plazas. Breeding swarms triggered by scheduled reservoir overflows. Migratory species following thermal corridors between sectors. Predators that map commuting patterns. Scavengers who know, by inherited instinct or acquired calibration, exactly when a floodgate opens enough to expose edible growths and not enough to pulp them.

Many would be vertical animals. Ledge-nesters, seam-burrowers, undersurface clingers, creatures that think in facades and shafts and cable runs rather than ground. Their anatomy would reflect a world where "down" is less useful than "attached." Hooks, pads, electroadhesive hairs, magnetic cusps, balancing tails, collapsible fins for short controlled drops, resonant whiskers that read wall vibration like weather. Even warm-blooded hunters in such a place might move in bursts across drainage ribs and support members, sleeping in heat pockets behind condensing skins.

Larger fauna would exist where bulk flow permits them. The description almost insists on them: heavy things in floodgates, broad-backed sifters in slurry channels, pliant-bodied vent grazers crossing roof-plains in groups, powerful runners timing movement to machine cadence. They may be revered, culled, farmed, avoided, or simply routed around like weather. In a place where maintenance and ecology have coevolved, some of these beasts would be indispensable. A gate-colossus that keeps nutrient mats from choking a lock. A herding vent-feeder whose grazing patterns maintain thermal efficiency. A vast soft-bodied filter animal anchored in an industrial estuary, legally protected because half a district's water polish depends on its metabolism.

Then there are the almost-people and almost-tools. This landscape breeds them naturally because survival there rewards function without purity. Imagine messenger organisms that began as engineered couriers and are now social scavengers with route-memory and favored handlers. Cleaning colonies that negotiate access like guilds. Diagnostic birds—or things bird-shaped enough—that still inspect cracks and relay data, but nest, mate, and steal shining mineral accretions for display. Underplatform mammals with implanted legacy interfaces no one can fully remove anymore. Reverberation-sensitive hounds used for search and repair who are now family in some quarters, infrastructure in others, and feral packs elsewhere.

If the place has age, then it also has inheritance. Some inhabitants would be descendants of workers adapted over generations to specific districts. Salt-quarter people with bright corrosion tattoos that are partly aesthetic and partly chemical sensing. Reservoir communities whose children learn buoyancy before walking. Underworks lineages pale-eyed and heat-tolerant, with social customs built around touch-sign and pressure pulse. Span-folk who live on suspension neighborhoods where the wind sings all night, developing language, music, and courtship around harmonic weather. There might be whole cultures organized not by ethnicity in an old terrestrial sense but by hydrological relation: upstream, brackish, condenser-born, flood-licensed, vent-adjacent, cistern-kin.

Their clothing and dwellings would reveal them as surely as anatomy. Everyone would wear the climate as a collaborator. Capillary fabrics that wick sweat and mist into drinkable reserves. Noise baffling collars. Conductive jewelry for public signaling in high-clamor districts. Membrane cloaks that stiffen in hard rain. Footwear with reversible adhesion. Skin coatings against fouling organisms, or symbiotic coatings that cultivate desirable films and suppress harmful ones. Some groups might favor shells, lacquered ceramics, articulated rain-armor, or porous mineral composites that gather condensation and cool the body. Others might go nearly bare in flood quarters, their status and identity displayed instead through embedded lights, glandular perfumes readable in humid air, or microtopographies etched into skin for tactile recognition.

Social life in such a place would not gather around fireplaces, fields, or plazas alone, but around thresholds in the flow. Sluice markets. Cooling terraces at shift change. Quiet chambers rented by the minute. Resonant halls where speech is replaced by touch and pulse. Ceremonial rains. Flood feasts timed to breeding events. Funerary release into warm cisterns or into the managed mineral accretions that slowly turn remains into civic material. Children would grow up learning danger not as wilderness but as pattern disruption: the wrong hum from a bridge, a silence in a pump district, water beading the wrong way on a wall.

As for temperament, the dwellers would likely be practical without being plain. People who survive a place like this become connoisseurs of minute conditions. They can smell an algal bloom before sensors flag it. They know which handrails stay dry in lightning weather. They can read condensate purity from taste, storm risk from harmonic drift, neighborhood status from the color of mineral lace on a buttress. Their intelligence is environmental and embodied. Their myths, if they have myths, may concern ancient floods, sentient storms, sleeping machinery under the clay pans, the first treaties between builders and substrates, the saints or engineers who taught walls to sweat usefully.

And because the place is never truly still, its inhabitants probably value timing more than possession. To live there is to catch windows: crossings between surge cycles, harvests between contamination shifts, mating seasons aligned to maintenance downtimes, hours of induced rainfall, moments when a usually hostile district opens its pressure gates and becomes traversable. Status may derive less from owning static land than from controlling cadence, access, forecast, and repair.

The most intimate answer, though, is that what lives there is anything capable of making a niche from warmth, wetness, chemistry, and repetition. The place selects for opportunists, symbionts, accumulators, signal-readers, and beings unoffended by proximity. Solitary life can exist, but not for long untouched. Everything becomes host, substrate, corridor, or chorus.

So the residents are:

beings with slick hands on dripping rails,
glider flocks scribbling over steam,
shell scavengers polishing runoff seams,
distributed minds counting in reservoir depth,
maintenance descendants with machine-tuned hearing,
floodgate leviathans grazing slurries,
quiet-chamber sleepers paying for a temporary absence of thunder,
children diving into market water when the bell changes,
feral service things clicking in the underwarm dark,
and countless lives too small to name, turning residue into structure and structure back into habitat.

In a thinner description, one might say: a dense amphibious civilization lives there, surrounded and permeated by a ferocious infrastructure ecology. But more truly, the thing that lives there is a whole low-country mode of being—urban, estuarine, mechanical, animal, collective—so old and so crowded that it has ceased to distinguish cleanly between inhabitant and environment. The city is alive, yes. But only because everything in it is.
