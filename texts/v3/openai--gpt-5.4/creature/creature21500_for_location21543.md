What lives there is not a beast pacing the catwalks, nor a hidden people adapted into the vents, nor some single sovereign tree at the center. The inhabitant is the place’s own continued metabolism: a civilization of plants that has crossed so far into engineering, and an engineering substrate that has weathered so far into ecology, that together they amount to something like one dry, distributed organism.

If you insist on imagining it as a living thing rather than a living system, then it is a colossal sessile creature spread across terraces, shafts, gantries, roofs, ducts, and buried furnaces. Not one body, but one temperament. It lives by gradients. It eats exhaust, charge, dust, heat, vibration, trace minerals, old alloys, condensed rumor-water. It breathes through chimneys and valve forests. It circulates by sap-lines, belt runs, pressure pulses, conductive vines, and the slow opening of ancient thermal lungs. It has no face anywhere, yet there are places that feel facial: arrays of sensor-fronds angled into convection currents, black mirror leaves tasting for faint light, cilia-fine root fans laid over warm metal like skin testing fever.

At first you might call what lives there flora, because the tissues are rooted, because there are trunks and mats and fronds and seed-vats and bark-hard skins. But that word becomes too small almost immediately. These are plants only in the sense that they are stationary autotrophs or near enough; beyond that they have diversified into roles that in another world would have been taken by pumps, insulation, filters, muscles, capacitors, scaffold, even weather. Some are load-bearing organisms grown in sockets where columns once stood, faceted for strength, quietly thickening around old staples and braces until metal becomes only a memory inside wood. Some are chimney orchards whose crowns flare and furl to harvest heat differences from the stacks below. Some are cable-eels in all but locomotion: segmented creepers wrapped around lines, sipping current in infinitesimal quantities and answering with a ghostly glow. Some are dry succulents of ceramic flesh, storing not water but reactive compounds and thermal potential, patient as ammunition. Some are sheet-organisms stretched across frames, trembling with nonwatery circulation, each membrane a cross between leaf, condenser, sail, and listening drum.

The oldest inhabitants are probably not visible as individuals at all. They are the interlocked raft-mats, the district-spanning root congresses, the old overgrowths that have passed through so many cycles of scorching, sealing, cracking, and recolonization that no clean boundary remains between self and surroundings. A wall feeds a vine, the vine shades a conduit, the conduit preserves a thermal range, the range allows a felt of mineral roots, the roots trap dust, the dust becomes substrate for bark tile, the bark shelters charge-sensitive tendrils, those tendrils trigger old maintenance shears, the shears prune a crown, the crown drops carbon husks, the husks laminate into insulation for the wall. Which part is the organism? All of it, probably. Or rather: the organism is the loop.

It would be wrong, though, to make this sound harmonious. What lives there is not peaceful. It is simply nonanimal in its violence. The inhabitants compete by architecture and chemistry with a thoroughness that, in another register, would be called war. One species sleeves a hot shaft and steals enough frictional heat to weaken the machinery that feeds a rival. Another armors a gantry in kiln-bark and excludes every slower-growing root felt. A membrane colony sprawls over skylike openings and starves the absorptive vanes below. Poison resins are exhaled into enclosed slots. Mineral scavengers lace the surfaces around warm vents and leave the substrate too depleted for softer tissues. Shade is weapon, insulation is weapon, conductivity is weapon, timing is weapon. Even cooperation has a hard edge to it: the woven mats and stacked communities survive by interdependence, yes, but with the stern mutualism of things that would all die if they ceased to hold one another in shape.

And still, to describe only the botanic inhabitant misses half of what lives there. The dead industry is not dead enough to be scenery. The old systems persist with too much agency. Pumps throb. belts creep. shutters decide to open. turbines wake on inherited gradients. capacitors discharge into forgotten shears. vents continue exchanging the deep heat of buried structures with the choked atmosphere above. These machine remnants are no longer masters of the region, but neither are they merely carcasses. They are organs whose original animal has vanished. The vegetation has colonized them, depends on them, manipulates them—but is also manipulated in return. A buried piston sets the breeding rhythm of one seed caste. A thermal cycle determines when a whole district seals itself into hardness. A rare electrical surge trims canopies and thus shapes future crowns. So perhaps the true inhabitant is a hybrid not by design but by attrition: a plant-machine clade assembled over centuries out of reciprocal use.

If I were to make it singular, I would say an immense **dry intelligence** lives there—not conscious in a human or animal way, not plotting, not watching, but continuously solving. Every surface hosts an answer to some problem of heat, scarcity, support, shade, charge, exposure, storage, timing. The region thinks in redesigns. It notices by differential survival. It remembers in layers. Its memory is not stored in brains but in preserved conduits, inherited graft geometries, successful angles of frond, repeated placements of reservoirs near transformer heat, in the fact that one district has become domed because doming worked six hundred times and everything else burned away. The machine-garden does not know that it knows. But it has preferences written everywhere.

There may also be smaller presences within this larger one—not animals, since you have emptied the place of those, but local personalities of process. A stairwell permanently occupied by a pale root lace that opens and closes its pores in exact synchrony with a deep engine pulse, as if listening to a heart below. A grove of black thermal leaves that always edge themselves toward one particular venting seam, each frond scarred by old static and banded in conductive salts. A resin tower district that sings in cool periods, all its gas-release throats tuned by accident into a mournful chord. Seed foundries inside the bulbous domes where generation after generation of dry-adapted propagules are sorted by vibration and launched by pressure events into new cracks. They are not characters, exactly, but recurrent centers of behavior, like neighborhoods in a city or lobes in a brain.

If a human walker entered and asked, “What creature lives here?”, the most honest answer might be: **infrastructure after it learned to photosynthesize**. Or: **botany that inherited a factory and could not bear to let any machinery go unused**. Or even: **a reef built out of heat instead of water**. It has the same crowded mutuality, the same dependence on gradients, the same layer upon layer of specialists and opportunists and structural species, but translated into soot, resin, charge, and dark.

Yet none of those quite gets the emotional truth of it. The emotional truth is that what lives there is persistence itself after becoming biological. A tenacious, unhurried inhabitant that does not distinguish between growing, repairing, scavenging, and building. It occupies every ledge because occupation is life. It bridges every gap because gaps are intolerable losses of potential. It wraps hot metal because warmth is fortune. It seals windows because dimness can be tuned. It turns buildings into bark, rails into roots, vents into throats, ladders into trellises, signage into faint fossils under lacquer. It does not mourn the vanished makers because it is made of their leftovers and long ago passed beyond needing their permission.

So whoever lives there is immense, sedentary, composite, ancient, and still juvenile in one sense: still reaching. Every storm rearranges its posture. Every failure becomes another scaffold. Every cooled surface invites invasion. It is a resident that has outlasted the category of resident, because the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has dissolved. The plateau is not occupied by life. The plateau has become life, and that life is a black, dry, radiant, many-throated growth-machine that never stopped taking the world apart in order to keep itself going.

If you stood very still and tried to feel its presence, you would not feel watched. You would feel incorporated. As if the true thing living there were asking of every beam, duct, crack, and terrace the same patient question:

Can this, too, be made to carry?
