What lives there is not one thing but a citizenship of adaptations.

The first inhabitant, in a sense, is the place’s own continuing metabolism: a half-animal persistence distributed through pipes, heat, roots, tanks, vents, films of algae, and the habits of small creatures. It lives in the old machine the way tide lives in an estuary. But if you mean the beings one might meet there—what has truly made a home in that suspended engine-garden—then they are creatures of grip, condensation, signal, and ledge. Nothing broad-backed or pastoral. Nothing that needs a field. Everything that belongs there is good at using leftovers of intention.

The dominant lifeform might be a small simian, or something so convergent with one that the distinction hardly matters.

They would not be ground animals. The floor, where present, would be for crossing only in necessity. Their real territory would be the middle air of the place: vine-hung gantries, cable bundles, valve housings, railings warm with hidden steam, maintenance ladders, and the skeletal undersides of platforms. They would move in discontinuous routes only they understand, launching from bracket to root-mass to conduit with the certainty of swifts. Their hands would be exceptional—broad-palmed, dark-skinned, perhaps almost rubbery, with ridged pads adapted to wet metal and oxidized pipe. The fingers might be long enough to hook around cable and mesh at once. Nails not claws exactly, but hard and black, useful for scraping lichen, levering scale from mineral crusts, prying open seed cases, fishing larvae from drain lips.

They would be lean, heat-shedding creatures. Sparse fur over the shoulders and limbs, thinner still on the face, chest, and tail if they have one. The skin under the fur might be mottled in oil-dark and rust-red patches, a camouflage perfectly matched to old iron and leaf shadow. Their eyes would likely be large and laterally alert rather than soft and primate-gazing: built to read motion across layers of grating, steam, and interrupted light. Ears perhaps mobile and close to the skull, to avoid snagging and to help localize sound in that impossible acoustic environment.

But they would not rely on voice much. Anything evolved there would know that sound is unreliable. Calls would exist, yes—sharp visualizable barks, metal-click cries, brief piercing whistles timed to machinery lulls—but the true communication would be through touch, posture, scent-marking, and vibration. A palm drummed on a rail. A sequence of taps through a pipe. The presentation of throat-patches or underarm skin brightened by mineral pigments from the place itself. Grooming would be social, but also technical: removing scale dust, fungal growth, insect eggs, and conductive grit from one another’s fur and hands. They might maintain habitual routes by smell glands rubbed on warm junction boxes and favored ladder mouths, marking not territory in the mammalian frontier sense but continuity: this path still holds, this vent still warms, this basin still fruits.

Their diet would be inseparable from infrastructure. Not simply fruit and leaves, though there would be both. They would eat the figs and engineered pods grown in root troughs, pry snails from wet ceramic, strip insects from bulb halos, lap condensate where it gathers cleanest, raid nests, gnaw salt-mineral efflorescences for trace elements, and perhaps cultivate certain edible fungi in the felted insulation around warm pipes. It would not be surprising if they learned to open old service latches to reach hidden reservoirs of larvae or crustaceans breeding in sump overflow. Their young would learn the seasonal map of the place: when the hot orchids bloom and draw moths, when the cistern midges hatch, when a particular vent grows mats of nutritious algae, when carrion birds leave scraps on crane beams.

They would be visually unforgettable in motion. A family crossing the place would not read as animals traversing ruin, but as the quick dark syntax by which ruin continues thinking.

Yet they would not be alone, and perhaps not even central from the place’s own perspective.

There would also be gliders.

Not large, dramatic ones in the fantasy sense, but palm-sized to cat-sized membrane creatures that have made the vertical voids into their true landscape. In a place where one platform may face another across twenty meters of shaft, anything able to turn falling into travel has an immense advantage. These animals might sleep inside insulation jackets, behind gauge panels, or within the hollow ribs of trellised vent columns, then launch at dusk from warm housings to sail silently through bands of steam and amber lampglow. Their membranes could be dusky translucent, veined like old leaves, beading condensation before takeoff. They would feed on moths, soft fruits, and perhaps the sweet secretions of certain engineered flowers. They would also be pollinators by accident and design, carrying powder from terrace to terrace along routes no crawling creature could replicate.

If there are gliders, there are probably hunters of gliders: thin-bodied owls or owl-analogues that have learned to fly in machinery. Not open-sky hunters, but chamber predators. Broad wings would be useless; they would need rounded, instantly braking flight, with tails like steering fans and feather or skin surfaces resistant to steam damp. Their faces might be asymmetrical listening dishes tuned less to ordinary woodland rustle than to the tiny deviations in a constant industrial roar. An animal born there would hear prey the way a navigator hears current. These hunters would perch on dead switch arms and crane hooks, motionless except for their heads, reading vibration through their feet.

Bird life in general would be abundant but specialized. The swifts you mention feel inevitable: generations deep, nesting in exhaust mouths, drinking on the wing from rain-chains, stitching the open volumes together faster than thought. But there would also be rail-warmers, birds almost domesticated by thermodynamics. Small black-red perchers, soot-colored with furnace throats, selecting exact handrail segments at dawn because they know where internal pipes run beneath. Some might use strands of copper filament and peeled insulation in nests, weaving with an engineer’s eye because the available world taught them angles and tensile strength. Their songs, if they still sing, may be short piercing motifs designed to survive machinery by occupying frequency gaps.

The amphibians there would be extraordinary in proportion to how rarely they are seen.

Any frog that thrives in sumps and grates among metal resonance has become less a pond thing than a timing thing. Rust-colored, narrow-bodied, adhesive-toed, perhaps able to seal moisture into itself during dry intervals, it would emerge after pressure changes and rain shocks. Instead of a broad croak swallowed by machinery, its call might be a sharp pulsed note, almost electronic, repeated in mathematically spaced sequences that cut through structural noise. Tadpoles, if there are tadpoles, would develop in warm mineral water with bizarre chemistries, feeding on bacterial films in old condenser basins. Adults would know every hidden gutter. A person could live there for months and think there are only five, when in fact dozens occupy the unseen plumbing.

The invertebrates would be the true majority.

Not in the generic way that all ecosystems are mostly invertebrate, but in a way especially suited to this place’s scales of survival. Moss-backed beetles mining fungal felt from pipe lagging. Long-jointed spiders spanning instrument cabinets with catenaries of silk. Metallic flies breeding in nutrient films where plant runoff mixes with iron-rich condensate. Pale crustacean things in cistern darkness. Moths in impossible variety, each species associated with a microclimate: hot-chamber moths with heat-proof scales; fog-tray moths dusted green-white; great ash-colored dusk moths that pass through lamp halos like detached scraps of insulation. Bees would be there too, but perhaps not honeybees as such—rather thick solitary pollinators using bolt holes and ceramic tube banks as nurseries, sealing chambers with resin mixed from plant gum and oxidized dust.

There may be reptiles that are nearly indistinguishable from the hardware until they move. Lizards flattening themselves on transformer skins to draw heat, their scales patterned like pitted enamel and flaking paint. Their eggs tucked behind warm inspection plates. Their territorial displays performed with flashes of throat-color only visible when a shaft of sun cuts between tanks.

And then there are the fish, if fish is still the word after generations in dark basins.

Some old condenser tanks would hold stable enough water, fed by drip and overflow, to sustain blind or near-blind lineages. Pale bodies, whiskered mouths, slow movement, a trust in vibration rather than sight. They would feed on crustaceans, algae, drowned insects, whatever the machine and garden deliver. In a sealed world of tanks and channels, they might have become partitioned into tiny local populations: basin fish, trench fish, overflow fish, each subtly different because one valve failed decades ago and changed a temperature or mineral balance.

All of these creatures are plausible. But I think there is also room for a resident more singular than the rest.

Not a monster. A keeper species.

Something between bird, mammal, and ritual accident in the imagination of the place: a medium-sized, long-limbed creature that nests in old control booths and uses objects. Not as a human would, with generalized mastery, but with a narrow genius perfectly matched to the environment. It might collect bright washers, lengths of wire, dry seed pods, shed insulation fibers, wing cases, mica flakes, and arrange them at certain junctions or shrines of gauges. Perhaps courtship depends on curating resonant sites—choosing a panel or railing that produces a particular tone under wind or hand-tap. Perhaps they maintain clean condensate lips by scraping algae from them. Perhaps they open and close certain flap-valves not from engineering comprehension but from inherited behavior that incidentally preserves water access and thermal gradients for dozens of other species.

Such an animal would appear to outsiders as eerie, almost priestly, because its habits would seem purposeful at architectural scale. You would find polished patches on brass where it rubs its beak or snout. Small heaps of sorted snail shell near warm vents. Vines bitten back from one exact signal lamp year after year. Nest chambers lined not with random softness but with layered industrial relics chosen for heat retention and drainage. The whole place invites the existence of a creature that has become part janitor, part pollinator, part saboteur, part steward without knowing the names of any of those roles.

If there are people there—or something close enough to people to ask for separately—they would be few, quiet, and changed by the place more than it by them.

Not a populous settlement. One, three, perhaps seven at most. They would live in the sealed residence pods or reclaimed control rooms, in chambers insulated against noise with hanging roots, old rubber, packed fiber, and fabric darkened by steam. They would be ladder-thin, deft, attentive to valves and weather vanes and insect moods. Their clothes would be layered for microclimate rather than season: vent-warm wraps, drip capes, gloves with gripped palms, eye protection always close at hand. They would communicate in signs, shoulder movements, whistles pitched to carry in certain corridors, and tactile codes tapped through railings. They would know the whole complex as anatomy: where it sweats, where it strains, where it has gone numb.

Such people would not think of themselves as living in a ruin. They would think of themselves as residing inside a patient agreement. Their tasks might be minimal but exact: clearing one drain before storm season, resetting one baffle to keep a cistern oxygenated, harvesting fruit from a trellis before monkeys strip it bare, replacing one nest shelf after corrosion, taking warm water from one tank and never another. They would have a deep etiquette with the animals. One does not disturb the dawn rail birds. One leaves the amber-lit moth corridor alone during hatching week. One checks glove fingers for glider bites. One never startles the sump frogs because their emergence predicts pressure change. Their shrines, if they have them, would not distinguish sharply between saint, machine, and species.

But even without people, the place still has an inhabitant larger than any individual species: a composite organism made of recurring behaviors.

The monkeys prune routes.
The birds move seed.
The frogs read rain.
The insects convert bloom into brood.
The roots hold moisture where metal sweats.
The fish consume what falls through.
The gliders carry pollen through void.
The warm pipes sustain the moss.
The moss cools the housings.
The housings condense more water.

So what lives there?

A cliffworks menagerie of the made-natural:
grease-handed little primates,
heat-lizards,
sump frogs,
tank fish blind as old porcelain,
lamp moths,
copper-nesting birds,
membrane gliders,
fungus beetles,
and perhaps a few silent maintenance people with chlorophyll under their fingernails.

More deeply, what lives there is a style of life that can only exist where machinery has become weather and weather has learned the machine. A community of creatures that do not oppose industry or wilderness because they were born after the distinction ceased to matter.
