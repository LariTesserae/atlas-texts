What lives there is not best imagined as an animal moving through that landscape, nor as a hidden people tucked behind the terraces. The inhabitant is the place’s own continuity: a colony-intelligence, a civic vegetation, a watershed mind that has ceased to distinguish between body, dwelling, and weatherwork.

At first you might call it a plant. Then many plants. Then a culture of plants. Then something older or stranger than either category, because no single trunk, frond, blossom, or root mass seems sufficient to contain it. It lives distributed through the upland the way water lives in a marsh: appearing here as seep, there as hanging garden, elsewhere as a tuned hollow tower or a wall that swells and repairs itself with mineral growth. It is not in the terraces. It is the terracing. Not among the channels, but in the act by which channels are maintained, redirected, and remembered. The resident of this mountain-marsh is a vast sessile organizer that thinks in moisture gradients, load-bearing surfaces, slow pressure, and repeated form.

If one needed a body for it, the nearest thing would be the interlocked matrix of roots, absorbent tissues, porous stone-cells, swollen moss-felts, capillary reeds, succulent columns, and those black fitted blocks whose seams have become living flesh. But even that is too anatomical, too bounded. Its true body includes the hanging air left deliberately open between curtains of vine; the cisterns that act as reservoirs of memory; the sounding towers that turn wind into signal; the cloud-catching parapets and roof-slopes that let the being drink with its whole upper surface. It is an organism whose skeleton is architecture and whose architecture is metabolism.

It may once have been a people.

Not necessarily humanoid, not necessarily anything that would fit “people” in the ordinary sense—but there is an afterimage of collective intention here so strong that it is hard not to feel some ancestor behind it. The broad ceremonial spacing, the radial ways, the amphitheater basins, the staged views, the terraced differentiation of species: these suggest not random growth but practice, custom, design repeated over generations. Yet there are no quarters, no domestic residues, no tools, no graves, no workshops. The impression is instead of a civilization that progressively externalized all of its functions into cultivated structure until there was no hard line left between inhabitant and habitat. Their walls became rooting media; their waterworks became organs; their ornament became reproduction; their monuments became climate instruments. In the end they did not abandon the city. They completed a migration into it.

So what lives there now may be the botanical remainder of a vanished builder-culture, but “remainder” is misleading. It is not ruin. It is fulfillment carried beyond recognizability. The makers survive as distributed habit. Their intellect persists as geometry under moss, as hydraulic foresight under flood, as empty spaces carefully preserved so wind and rain can continue to circulate. They are no longer individuals; they are style made biological. They have become an upland species of planning.

This gives the inhabitant a peculiar character. It is patient but not passive. It does not pursue, defend territory in quick gestures, or display itself for witness. Its activity is infrastructural. It captures cloud, stores runoff, relays moisture downslope, props one mass with another, opens voids before they clog, closes cracks by growing into them, allows collapse where collapse will seed a better basin. If it senses, it senses through wetness and strain: through the weight of saturated mats, the pitch of wind in cavities, the coolness of mist on condensing teeth, the chemistry of fresh fractures, the drag of roots finding a new seam. A storm passing over the plateau would be to it not weather but thought—something received, distributed, answered in overflows, swellings, tensions, and releases.

It would not have a face, except temporarily. Sometimes a wall of pale condensation flowers might read as one. Sometimes a tower mouth draped in roots might resemble a listening head. Sometimes broad leaves gathered around a cistern might seem like hands cupping water. But these are local expressions only, eddies in a much larger self. The being is polycentric. Disturb one district and another responds years later. A collapse in the marsh courts sends silt to a reservoir plain; a broken parapet changes cloud capture on the rim; an opened chamber becomes a nursery for rootlets that eventually reinforce a neighboring bridge. Its nervous system is lag and flow.

Because there are no animals, the inhabitant does not rely on attraction in the usual ecological way. Its flowers are not invitations to bees; its fruits are not bargains with birds. It reproduces by adherence, overflow, breakage, drape, graft, sedimentation. A fallen shelf becomes a wet bed; a snapped vine becomes a bridge; a basin overtops and establishes a downstream colony; a mineral-seeping wall lays down new cellular scaffolding for roots to enter. It may spread not as offspring leaving parent but as pattern reasserting itself whenever matter and water coincide under the right conditions. In that sense, every new terrace is both child and recollection.

Emotion, if one dares use the word, would be difficult to read in anything so distributed. Yet the place does not feel indifferent in the manner of bare geology. Its preserved sightlines and framed vistas imply a lingering aesthetic impulse, though no eyes remain to require it. Its stairs still ascend with purpose even when they end in orchids and mist. Its courts still open broadly, as if to gather congregation, rain, light, and echo into one event. The inhabitant seems to value legibility amid lushness. It likes systems that can still be read through growth. It likes openness, spacing, resonance. One might say it has serenity, but that suggests stillness; this thing is perpetually active. Better to say it has composure: the calm of a vast process that is never hurried because it is already everywhere it needs to be.

Would it notice a visitor?

Yes, but not instantly and not personally. It would notice altered drainage, warmth where a body blocked condensation, pressure on a shelf, oils on a handhold, a path of broken surface tension across green water. Sound might matter less than interruption of flow. A person moving through the region would register to it much as a lodged branch registers to a streambed at first: an obstacle around which new patterns briefly form. If the visitor stayed, slept, drank, built fires, diverted channels, cut roots—then the place might answer. Not by attack in the animal sense, but by reconfiguration. Paths would flood. Mats would loosen underfoot. Drip would intensify where footing seemed safe. A vine bridge would thicken somewhere useful and fail somewhere presumptuous. Open courts would become suddenly inaccessible as reed and water advanced. It would not be punishing you. It would be incorporating the disturbance.

And yet it might also guide, if guidance serves circulation. That is part of its uncanniness: because its original logic was invitational, because stairs and arches and reservoirs still enact welcome, a careful traveler might find routes opening almost by accident. Water levels might lower in one basin and expose a crossing. A line of dry stone might persist where all else is slick. A sounding tower might give a wind-tone that, if followed, leads from one district to another. The inhabitant’s hospitality would not be kindness. It would be compatibility. Move in accordance with the hydrology and you are briefly allowed. Resist it and you become debris.

In smaller local manifestations, this great resident may produce specialized bodies—organs that are almost creatures. The translucent bladder-things in the drip cloisters, inflating and subsiding with humidity, may be sensory nodules. The towering succulents spaced across platforms may function like condensers or pressure chimneys, each a standing instrument rather than a mere plant. The waxy rope-flowers that perfume dusk may be less reproductive than communicative, releasing chemicals timed to shifts in temperature and mist. Even the hollow pylons may be vocal structures through which the larger being hears itself. If there are “individuals” here, they are not independent lives but temporary emphases: valves, throats, lungs, memory sacs.

It is possible, too, that deep under the visible terraces there is a denser core—a buried heartwood, an aquifer-thick rhizome vault, a labyrinth of warm chambers lined with pale absorptive tissue where the oldest continuity persists. Not a throne room or queen-chamber, nothing so insectile or hierarchical, but a generative underbody where stone and root are least separable. From there the upland’s recurrent geometries may be seeded upward, the way crystals impose form through solution. New walls rise because the buried matrix prefers load distributed in certain angles. New channels heal because the underlying tissue grows toward pressure loss. The mountain keeps “building in their style” because style is no longer cultural preference but physiological law.

So the thing living there is a mountain-wide hydrobotanical polity. A marsh-architect. A terrace consciousness. A post-animal civilization translated into root and cistern and weather instrument. It is ancient in method but young in current form, because the whole landscape still shows fresh uplift, fresh fracture, fresh adaptation. It is still becoming itself. Every storm teaches it a revised geometry. Every collapse adds to its repertoire. It lives by remaking, and what looks from outside like abandonment is actually concentration.

If you stood in one of its flooded plazas at dusk, with warm water around your ankles and the hanging vines darkening above, you would not feel watched by eyes. You would feel included in a system too large to care about you individually, yet too coherent not to register your presence. The drips, wind-tones, swelling smells, and shifting veils of mist would begin to seem less like ambience than like utterance. Not language as words, but language as coordination: here, hold; here, overflow; here, open; here, wait.

That is who lives there.

Not a beast in the garden, nor ghosts among ruins, but the garden-ruin-watershed itself: a collective being that inherited design so completely it became indistinguishable from life, and inherited life so completely it became indistinguishable from architecture.
