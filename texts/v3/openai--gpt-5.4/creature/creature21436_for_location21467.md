What lives here is not one thing but a temperament expressed in many bodies.

At first you might imagine hardy burrowers, miners with pruning hooks, blind cave fauna adapted to dust. Those are present, certainly. But the deeper truth of the place is that its inhabitants—human or otherwise—have been shaped less by undergroundness than by negotiated dryness. They are citizens of scarcity made intricate. Nothing in them is careless with moisture, energy, motion, or growth. Even the people, if there are people in the familiar sense, would seem a little kiln-cured by the city they keep: skin protected by waxed fabrics and fiber wraps, lips and eyes guarded from dust, habits precise and economical. They would carry water not casually but ceremonially, in sealed flasks or body-slung bladders with valves maintained as carefully as tools. They would know how to read the health of a district by scent, by the flex of a wall, by the tone of a ventilation trunk when struck with a knuckle. Their crafts would blur horticulture, civil engineering, and animal handling so completely that they might not consider them separate trades at all.

Such people would live as graftors, sifters, tenders, kiln-workers, tram technicians, pollen wardens, sanitation harvesters, scaffold masons of living timber, and surveyors of new chambers. Their city would have raised generations who think of roots as load-bearing members, of boulevards as vascular organs, of public lighting as a kind of weather management, of pruning as zoning. They would speak in the language of cure times, dust grades, fruiting cycles, seal integrity, and mineral amendments. Their elders would have the distinct authority of those who have watched neighborhoods slowly thicken, harden, sour, recover, and bloom again. Their children would grow up able to tell safe bark from irritant bark by touch, harmless duct-cleaners from cable-borers by the shape of their cast husks, and would likely play in ways that already imitate maintenance: tying mock support meshes, herding pellet-beasts, collecting dropped sheaths like treasure.

But if there are people here, they do not live alone. The city is crowded with adjunct lives, helper lives, tolerated lives, raiding lives, lives bred into usefulness and others forever slipping the terms of use.

The most characteristic animals would be dry grazers and civic scavengers: many-jointed, shell-plated, felt-mouthed things that move in coordinated little caravans through plazas and transit verges, cropping old fiber, digesting fungal crust, and returning it as prized fertilizer pellets. They would be so ordinary as to become part of the streetscape, like pigeons or street sweepers elsewhere, except more valued, more folded into systems. Children would know neighborhood herds individually. Sanitation guilds would follow their routes. Predators would watch them from cable shadows.

There would also be the long-limbed dust-pollinators you hinted at: elegant, almost absurd creatures, stilted and delicate-looking until they move with sudden exactness. Their bodies would carry electrostatic charge the way flowers carry color; they would step or bound from bloom-tower to bloom-tower in the upper garden wards, lifting yellow and silver particulate from one reproductive architecture to another. Perhaps they are not fully wild. Perhaps districts schedule around them, breed lineages for temperament and carrying efficiency, fit some with tiny marker tags or pigment bands. They would seem like a cross between antelope, crane, and instrument—too refined for brute labor, indispensable all the same.

Above, in the high vault thermals, the membrane-winged ceiling fliers would be among the place’s most visible wild citizens. Pale, root-fleshed, lantern-eyed, they belong to the upper air the way bats belong to twilight, but with less velvet and more parchment. Their colonies around lamps suggest a species that has adapted not merely to cavern roofs but to civic illumination, nesting in the lee of maintenance arrays, feeding on insects, spores, heat-loving drifters, perhaps even on the residue of industrial gas exchange. Their droppings become part nuisance, part propagation. So they are neither vermin nor sacred wildlife but a recurring civic fact: tolerated, cursed, harvested around, and woven into maintenance schedules.

Under decks and in slots, in the insulated dark where machinery sweats warmth into the cool, live the ambush predators of this world: lean, folded, patient things with barklike hides or matte chitin, evolved to spend almost nothing until opportunity forces commitment. They do not dominate the imagination of the city because predation here is too expensive to become theatrical. These creatures would be feared not because they are constant but because they are rare and decisive. A maintenance worker vanishes near an underused conveyor. A pet scavenger herd bunches and refuses a corridor. A cable nest shifts where no cable should move. The inhabitants would have a dry, practical folklore about such animals, full of caution and little romance.

And everywhere, more than anyone admits, there are the insects—or the near enough equivalents—that actually stitch this civilization together while threatening it from within. Duct cleaners with brush-feet. Pollen carriers with mineralized mandibles. Resin-borers. Switchbox nesters. Heat mites in insulation. Spore lice in fiber mattresses. Copper-chewers in relay housings. Tiny lacquer-backed predators released intentionally into cable galleries to control worse infestations. Their ecologies are probably more politically significant than large animals are. There would be ordinances about them. Guild rivalries over them. Entire careers founded on understanding which six-legged dust-colored nuisance can collapse a bloom ward or short a tram line, and which can safely be encouraged in the warm seams behind a digester.

What may live here beyond familiar categories is more interesting still: semi-domesticated growth-animals or mobile plant-analogs that trouble the distinction between fauna and infrastructure. In a city where architecture is alive, some inhabitants would not fit comfortably on either side of the line. There may be wall-creatures bred to patrol for rot by taste. Sniffing things used by survey teams to locate hidden mineral water traces, though water is vanishingly rare. Stubby, heat-seeking maintenance beasts that crawl ventilation ducts eating fluff, mold-analogs, and egg clusters. Slow load-bearing organisms harnessed in excavation fronts, more efficient than wheeled machines on certain yielding substrates. Little caged scavengers sold in markets might be exactly this sort of boundary life: not pets in the sentimental sense, but domestic micro-workers, chewers of waste, polishers of bone, alarm animals for bad air.

The city itself also seems to host forms of life that are not fully individuated. Colonial plants, growth-beds, root districts, membrane canopies, gas bladders, and structural trunks may be so responsive and so managed that they function almost like docile urban megafauna spread thin through architecture. A whole apartment block might, biologically speaking, still be one organism. A transit median might be a neighborhood-scale digestive exchange. A plaza arcade might enter stress, fertility, or dormancy as if it were an animal changing mood. Citizens would therefore live not only among organisms but inside them, attached to them, periodically inconvenienced by their decisions. Shopfronts disappear under fruiting events. Stair rails sprout sensory tendrils. Walls thicken. Passages seal. Whole districts become irritable in certain chemical seasons. What lives here, in that sense, includes the city’s own botanical body: not conscious in a human way, perhaps, but active, choosing, metabolic, impossible to reduce to mere material.

And then there are the people who are most native to such a negotiation: those frontier-maintenance crews at the edges. If any human type best embodies this undercity, it is them. Not conquerors, not simple settlers, but interpreters between fresh geology and opportunistic growth. They move with drills and grafting knives, yes, but also with nursery bundles and soil chemistry kits, with masks, lamps, survey chalk, acoustic taps, and perhaps little trained fauna that test air or locate stress fractures. They are followed by markets, by mold, by children eventually. They are the first diplomats of every new chamber. Their temperament would be cautious, improvisational, unsentimental about damage, reverent about stability. They know that every breach opens not just space but succession: dust, spores, scavengers, traders, pests, insulation creepers, tramlines, worship perhaps.

Because a place like this would generate belief as surely as it generates infrastructure. Its inhabitants would likely develop a civic spirituality—not necessarily supernatural, but deeply ritualized—around timing, restraint, and mutual dependence with the living fabric. There may be customs for first cuts into new growth, apologies offered before removing a structural bloom, observances for the fruiting of major district hosts, taboos around wasting viable dust or injuring sanitation herds. Even the irreligious would act with a kind of practiced respect. When your wall can scar, your street can seed, your roof can die, and your market square can become temporarily reproductive, etiquette expands to include metabolism.

So who lives there?

Dry-handed citizens in resin-sealed clothing. Children who know the smell of trace metals and ripe pith. Graft engineers and lamp farmers. Dust-herders and pellet collectors. Pollinator keepers. Pest-war tacticians. Tramside vendors selling conductive root and caged cleaners. Bark-skinned ambush hunters in the service alleys. Quiet herds clipping the civic substrate. Pale vault-fliers circling exhaust plumes. Mites in the switch housings. Resin beetles in the insulation. Exploratory tendrils nosing over a neglected handrail. Whole apartment towers that are also trees. Arcades that fruit. Medians that feed walls. Gardens that are load-bearing. Factories half-host, half-machine.

In short: what lives there is a civilization of cohabitation under pressure of thirst.

Not an underground city with plants in it, nor a cave ecosystem with people imposed upon it, but a dry urban biome whose residents—human, animal, colonial, structural, feral, cultivated—have all learned the same law: survive by sealing, sharing, timing, and making yourself useful to the whole tangled arrangement.
