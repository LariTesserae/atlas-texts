What lives there is a people who have become difficult to distinguish from their own maintenance practices.

Not literally plant-creatures, not dryads or moss-covered saints walking in procession under the leaves—nothing so singular or obligingly symbolic. They are a civic species, if anything: humans or near enough, made over by altitude, damp, long habit, and the constant requirement that life be negotiated with things that grow, shift, and occasionally disagree with geometry. They belong to the plateau in the same way espaliered trunks belong to walls: through training, pressure, adaptation, and time.

At first glance they might seem subdued. In a place so metabolically loud, its inhabitants are often visually practical. Layered clothing against the cool: oilskin capes, waxed fiber coats, aprons crosshatched with pockets, gloves with resin stains at the palms, boots soled for root and wet stone. Their garments tend toward moss greens, bark browns, iron blacks, fog grays, with civic flashes of color only where function demands it—yellow seam tape for pruning crews, copper-thread insignia for water councils, white mesh veils for pollination keepers, lacquer-red armbands for railway hands. Fabrics are woven from plant fiber, fungal leather, old sailcloth, industrial canvas, and fine membranes grown for specific trades. Most clothing looks repaired many times, and intelligently: patched not to hide wear but to map it.

Then you notice how finely tuned their bodies and attention have become.

These are people who walk by feel as much as by sight. Their balance is excellent, but not in the theatrical way of acrobats—rather in the quiet competence of those accustomed to surfaces that spring slightly underfoot, stairways that may reconsider their proportions, and bridges best navigated by listening. They tend to place a hand on walls, railings, trunks, door-frames as they move, as if taking the city's pulse or offering their own in return. Many have the slight forward inclination of habitual slope-walkers. Many have clear lungs or the scars of not having had them once. Hands matter here: callused, pruned by damp, nicked by thorns and wire, stained green in the cuticles, browned with grease around the knuckles, carrying the minute evidence of a life spent adjusting living systems.

Their faces would show weather before age. Cool air, filtered light, mist, and reflected green would leave many of them pale or bronze in a softened way, skin kept from cracking by damp but marked by the city's particulars: lash-lines of old bramble cuts, faint burns from steam valves, speckled freckling from altitude light, occasional lichenlike patterns of benign mold reaction among those who work in deep shaded wards. Hair is often worn tied close, braided flat, wrapped, netted, or shorn where machinery or climbing work makes loose strands dangerous. But adornment exists, and with more intimacy than ostentation: polished seed cases on cords, copper leaf pins denoting district, little grafted flower knots worn at the throat, memorial rings grown from laminated root and wire. Some cultivate tiny fragrant moss pads in hatbands or cuffs the way another culture might wear perfume.

What most distinguishes them is not appearance but temperament. They are patient in a muscular way.

They are used to systems that cannot be commanded outright. You do not shout a root back into alignment or argue a fog pocket into behaving. Their civic character has therefore grown around watchfulness, procedural memory, and a peculiar pluralism of explanation. A resident may tell you with perfect sincerity that a stair loops because of damp, because of unresolved growth stress, because the old foundations beneath dislike haste, and because everyone knows to pause on the third landing before continuing. These are not contradictions to them. They live in overlap. Their intelligence is infrastructural rather than philosophical: they know what keeps passage possible, what scent marks a trustworthy road, which bells to ignore, where time frays after rain, which hedges are in a receptive mood for cutting and which will thicken in retaliation.

They would likely be hard to impress with abstract wonder and very easy to impress with competent upkeep.

A stranger might marvel at a reservoir that contains more mist than volume. A local wants to know whether its membranes were resealed before the cold front, whether the measuring flowers still close in proper sequence, whether the west sluice has started singing in the wrong key again. This is a population with a deep respect for technicians, pruners, line-menders, fungus readers, root-surveyors, boiler hands, and anyone else capable of preventing minor anomalies from becoming municipal character. Heroism here is often a matter of catching a misalignment while it is still only inconvenient.

So the city is full of specialists.

You can imagine the municipal pruners first: broad-shouldered or wiry, moving in teams with harnesses, hooked knives, shears on poles, small saws with toothed chain assemblies, pigment chalk for marking dangerous growth. They know the appetite and resentment of every civic species. They talk to branches while cutting, not because they believe this changes the branch, necessarily, but because the rhythm of speech keeps the crew coordinated and because silence around a loaded limb feels disrespectful. They smell of sap, wet rope, machine oil, and crushed leaf.

There are grafting guilds, who are part surgeon, part architect, part diplomat. Their hands are extraordinarily delicate. They move through scaffolded facades inspecting seams where one species has been persuaded to bear another, checking for rot, mutiny, incompatibility, overachievement. A graft-master might be as prestigious in this city as an engineer or judge elsewhere, because walls, bridges, ventilation systems, even legal boundaries may depend on old botanical treaties holding. Their apprentices learn not only botany but lineage. This district's load-bearing rind came from a bitter slow-growing stock first introduced after the west slip. That flowering vent line cannot be crossed with the sweeter amber-bloom family without attracting a moth band that interferes with signal lamps. Such things are history here.

There are water council workers, who wear the cleanest boots and speak in measured tones because panic spreads fast in places dependent on stored wetness. They monitor cistern levels, mist nets, seep channels, condensation farms, capillary banks. Some are hydrologists in any recognizable sense. Some are almost priests of drip and pressure. They know where the mountain still sweats through old seams and where it only pretends to. They can tell by smell when a reservoir has become too reflective and needs roughening. They keep records in duplicate and triplicate because numbers may shift if left unattended near ancient root masses.

Root-cartographers may be the city's strangest professionals and perhaps its most emblematic. They map structures that are alive, moving, layered over older plans, and occasionally dimensionally noncompliant. Their charts are never merely visual. They use rubbings, scent codes, tension readings, acoustic profiles, and seasonal annotations. A root-map might include instructions like listen for hollow fifth note before turning, or avoid after noon in thaw weather, or lane presently intersects previous Tuesday. These are not jokes. Their work underwrites transport, mail, taxation, zoning, rescue, pilgrimage, and inheritance disputes.

There are mold inspectors, who in another city might be humble bureaucrats but here carry a certain stern dignity. Too much fungal sweetness in the wrong retaining wall can mean sickness, collapse, dream distortion, or all three. Pollination keepers move through orchard-manufactories with veils and tuned forks, managing insect bursts, hand-carrying pollen where local ecologies have become too curated to trust chance. Railway crews learn signal systems that include lamps, semaphore leaves, scent puffs, and resonance plates because visual distance on the plateau cannot always be trusted. Conservatory engineers route waste heat through nurseries while arguing with machinists over humidity quotas. Digest trench attendants feed the city's refuse to mouths made of petal and enzyme, then rake out usable loam by the glow of safety lamps.

Children raised there would be remarkably competent and slightly uncanny to outsiders. They learn early not to chase what appears to be a familiar person into fog without first calling by district-name and listening for the correct echo. They are taught textures the way others are taught alphabets: scored bark means civic route, smooth petrified root means freight priority, braided fiber edge means unstable shoulder, mint-resin post means you have crossed into a school ward. Games likely involve route memory, timing blooms, identifying useful drafts, daring one another to stand near a temporal snag and count how many bell-strikes fit into a minute that isn't behaving. Their nursery rhymes would contain practical warnings hidden in rhythm: pause at the third stair, never trust a bridge that sounds too certain, shadows before object mean wait.

The elderly would be especially important there, not simply out of sentiment but because prolonged habitation in a shifting city produces a kind of embodied archive. Someone who has lived eighty years on one terrace may remember where the avenue lay before the last re-centering, which wall used to be a tree, which grove answers poorly to metal, what the plaza shows in rain when no officials are around. Old age in such a city would confer spatial authority. A bent woman with a pruning hook and a bad cough might be worth more than a shelf of plans because she knows that if the east fog turns copper-colored, the lower funicular will arrive behind itself.

And there would also be those whom the city has altered more deeply.

Not monsters. Not a hidden caste of bark-skinned seers unless one insists on seeing every adaptation as myth. But in a place where labor, atmosphere, diet, and weirdness work on the body for generations, some residents might seem almost cultivar-like. There may be lineages with exceptional tolerance for spores, families whose sense of smell is uncannily precise and therefore prized in navigation and quality control, workers whose circadian rhythms entrain to the calibrated flowers used to stabilize timekeeping, people who can feel a pressure drop in living walls before a visible crack forms. The city would notice such traits and make use of them. It is that kind of place: practical, incorporative, not overly precious about where usefulness begins.

It may also produce a small population of the half-lost and half-attached: those who have spent too long in the oldest groves, surveyors who came back speaking in route-descriptions rather than addresses, pilgrims reluctant to leave places where sound dulls and moisture hangs in air like held thought. Every city with edges has such people. Here they might become respected eccentrics, cautionary examples, or valued intermediaries for districts where conventional orientation fails. They would live lightly, perhaps, in houses grown along the margins, keeping collections of impossible seeds, obsolete maps, and tools for measuring things that no longer hold still.

As for social life, one imagines a people less given to spectacle than to ritualized maintenance and seasonal adjustment. Festivals would likely coincide with irrigation turns, major prunings, successful reweavings of important facades, first flower-opening in the civic clocks, annual dormancy of overtaxed sectors, safe reopening of routes after growth-spasms. Music would suit the environment: bells, whistles, struck metal, reed organs perhaps, voices used antiphonally across open terraces where wind steals some frequencies and amplifies others. Public speech would be rich in qualifiers and precise local nouns. Their humor would be dry, timing-based, and fond of spatial absurdity. Bureaucracy would be oddly poetic because forms must account for things refusing to stay singular. Address line three: nearest stable scent. Address line four: corroborating texture. Address line five: if approached at dusk, use alternate stairs.

They are not, I think, a mystical people in the way outsiders flatter. They are a municipal people, and that is more interesting.

They are accustomed to dependency without illusion. They know that the city feeds them, houses them, employs them, and can also overgrow a valve line, soften a support, loop a road, hold a fog too long, produce a harvest that intoxicates the district, or remind everyone that the old mountain under all this horticultural governance remains older than policy. So they have developed the ethics of caretakers aboard a large, living machine that is also a landscape and also an inheritance and also perhaps not entirely obedient to shared reality. Their virtues are diligence, redundancy, craft memory, tolerance for ambiguity, and a hard-won affection for systems that need constant trimming.

If there is a ruling class, it is probably composed less of nobles than coordinators: councilors of water, growth, transit, manufacture, and anomaly management. Their authority would always be tested against practical outcomes. A failed official in such a place is one whose district buckles, molds, tangles, drifts, or loses track of where Tuesday went. Prestige would gather around those able to reconcile competing metabolisms: factory demands versus orchard recovery, route clarity versus habitat complexity, structural integrity versus heritage growth, measurable time versus lived time. Law itself might be botanical in temperament—pruning rather than prohibitive, grafting rather than replacing, coppicing problems so they return manageable instead of terminal.

And yet, for all this competence, they would not be entirely unromantic.

A city like that breeds attachment to particular corners: a stair that only works for the patient, a bridge that hums correctly in fog, a plaza whose rain-rings briefly reveal your grandparents' childhood street, a workshop wall that flowers warm orange only on heavy production days. People there would love places by maintaining them. Courtship might involve exchanging route secrets or tending a shared facade seam. Mourning might include planting a memorial graft into a public retaining hedge, so the dead become part of the city's holding action against erosion. Religion, where present, might concern thresholds, continuities, old groves beyond management, and the moral importance of making repairs before collapse demands sacrifice.

What lives there, then, is a culture of rooted engineers.

They are citizens of a plateau that has forgotten being a mountain and therefore remembers other things instead: seep, pressure, recurrence, slow knitting, the refusal of clean separations. They live wide rather than high, alert to wind and drift, making agreements every day with trunks, pumps, rail lines, molds, clocks, and the mild but persistent failure of space to behave consistently. They are not overwhelmed by the city's strangeness because they have built customs out of it. They carry scented cords, calibrated watches, pruning scars, district songs, layered maps in the mind. They accept that directions may need to be sung, that truth may require triangulation, that a wall can be alive and still count as public infrastructure.

Seen from afar, the city looks grown. Seen closely, so do its people.

Not in bark or blossom, but in rings of adaptation. In the patience with which they cut back what shelters them. In the way they leave room between things. In how they have learned that survival on an old high plateau is not a matter of imposing order once, but of returning, listening, adjusting, and returning again.
