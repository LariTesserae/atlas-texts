What lives there is not one thing but a way of being shaped by ledges, scarcity, damp, and long memory.

The people, if they are people, would be mountain-made in the same patient sense as the stone. Not dramatic highland romantics, not cliff-dwellers in a savage register, but citizens of adjustment. Their bodies and habits would belong to altitude: narrow-shouldered or broad-chested depending on lineage and labor, economical in movement, careful with breath, unastonished by stairs. They would know how to pause without seeming to rest. They would carry weather in their joints the way others carry time. Skin would tend toward the tones of low sun, lampglow, and reflected stone; hair and clothing would often hold a trace of mist, metal dust, fern scent, machine oil. Nothing about them would be wasteful. Even elegance would likely be compact.

They would be layered people, literally and socially. Coats waxed or lanolin-treated against fine wet. Felt collars. Fingerless work gloves under heavier mitts. Boots with soles made for slick stair and iron grating. Aprons with many pockets. Spectacles that cloud and are wiped and cloud again. People from the mistward districts might wear darker greens, grays, old blue, and mineral reds that do not show damp quickly; those from leeward terraces might favor lighter cloth, patched but sun-faded where there is actual sun to fade it. Metals on the body would not be decorative first; buckles, hooks, cord guides, water keys, vent gauges, foldout knives, cistern tokens. Then, after necessity, ornament would creep in by inheritance: lichen motifs cast into buttons, thin chains of blackened iron, polished stone beads, old workshop seals worn as pendants, tiny reliquaries or pressure-charms meant to steady the lungs in deep fog.

Their faces would be marked less by hardship than by attentiveness. This is a city that rewards noticing. Its inhabitants would be readers of minute conditions: a glance at a parapet and they know whether the night's condensation was worth collecting; a hand on a wall and they judge whether a buried pipe is running warm; one breath near a vent and they can tell if mineral vapor is ordinary, medicinal, intoxicating, or dangerous. They would listen well. Not because they are mystical by nature, but because the city is full of consequential subtleties. They would hear the difference between harmless settling and a retaining wall asking for inspection. They would know one tramline by its vibration through the railings and another by the timing of its brakes in fog.

This makes them seem reserved to strangers, but not cold. Their speech would probably be shaped by terrain: practical, elliptical, rich in local distinctions. Many words for kinds of wet, for grades of fog, for stone that may be trusted and stone that only looks trustworthy. A whole civic vocabulary around collection and loss: first-drip, wall-run, coilwater, false thaw, iron bloom, vent-sleep, cistern-turn, moss debt. They might speak softly not out of timidity but because sound carries oddly and because many generations have lived in close vertical proximity, separated by floors, shafts, ducts, and shared walls cut straight into mountain. Privacy there would be respected by habit, not abundance.

What do they do? They maintain continuities. A great number of the city's inhabitants would be caretakers in one form or another, though the title on paper may say engineer, gardener, machinist, clerk, inspector, mason, or beetle-breeder. The place survives through maintenance so persistent it becomes culture. Children would grow up watching adults clear channels with hooked blades, tap pipes to hear freeze coming, trim moss back from hinges while encouraging it over water-retaining stone, measure cistern depth by knotted line, and rotate shutters before a night of drifting lights. Every household would probably have at least one specialized inherited skill and one public obligation. You do not live there by simple residence. You live there by tuning.

So among them are fog-net menders with scarred fingertips and a superstition about knots tied after moonrise. Condensation auditors carrying folding rulers, chemical strips, and little brass mirrors to inspect hidden airflow. Bridge gardeners in waxed sleeves, tending suspended trays of herbs, sedges, and filter-root plants beneath the crossings where dripwater gathers. Vent tenders who know which deep exhalations can be directed to bathhouses, which must be diluted, which wake the pale cave ecologies that no ordinance quite governs. Foundation listeners, perhaps old enough to trust their knees against stone, moving from retaining wall to retaining wall with stethoscope-like rods, hearing minute shifts no instrument has fully replaced. Lichen clearers who scrape glass and solar panels with tools gentle enough not to scar them, yet who leave certain sanctioned colonies intact because they catch moisture and cool the frames. Salvage casters using ancestral molds to make machine housings no catalog elsewhere still remembers.

There would also be scholars, though perhaps they would not separate themselves cleanly from workers. In such a place, geology, plumbing, botany, and history are all one conversation. Archivists might keep not only charters and maps but wall samples, rust records, fungal blooms, decades of fog frequencies, hand-drawn diagrams of old pipes no longer officially in service but still quietly carrying half a district's life. Monastic traditions may survive not as remote piety but as disciplines of measurement, patience, and repair. Some of the old chambers tunneled into the rock could still house communities devoted to keeping the city's memory synchronized with its actual structure: who cut this stair, when that wall bulged, which vent first warmed in a dry year, where the old quarry cranes are buried under the newer gardens.

If there is governance, it would be tactile and local. Wardens of water. Stair stewards. Shared-wall arbiters. Moss-right assessors. People who inspect gutters with the seriousness other cities reserve for border defenses. Theft here would not only mean taking goods. It could mean diverting seepwater, neglecting a communal drain, overheating a private greenhouse during a lean season, letting invasive rootwork crack a cistern vault, opening shutters on the wrong night and drawing in those thin wandering lights that unsettle sleepers and spoil instrument readings. The citizens would be legalistic in oddly intimate ways. They would have ordinances regarding runoff, roof angles, vent sharing, fungus containment, and the etiquette of crossing someone else's condensation field.

As for temperament, I think they would be disciplined but not rigid. The city has taught them that survival depends on responsiveness more than force. They would not admire extravagance unless it proved useful. They would respect craftsmanship that ages well. They would trust what can be repaired. Their humor might be dry and local, built from deferred inconvenience: jokes about a stair that takes longer in fog, a lift that only sticks for outsiders, a family line famous for overbuilding gutters, a district so damp its dead need airing. Courtship there might involve practical intimacies: learning one another's routes, sharing weather timings, exchanging cuttings of shade-loving plants, being invited into someone's greenhouse loft or cistern gallery. Love would smell like tea steam, wool drying near pipes, and hands warmed around metal mugs.

They would also, almost certainly, be a little superstitious—though in a city so old and observant, superstition and empiricism would have grown together rather than apart. The moving lights in the fog: perhaps everyone claims skepticism and everyone still closes certain shutters. The mineral outcrops that gather mist unnaturally: officially charted, unofficially propitiated. Warm breaths from the mountain: sampled, measured, and still greeted with a phrase your grandmother taught you. Small offerings might be tucked into cracks near old pumps or shrines atop the thick-necked stone towers—coins, ribbon, iron filings, bitter herbs, bright glass beads dulled by weather. Not because anyone is naïve, but because life among old stone encourages courtesy toward processes larger than yourself.

And there are other residents who are not human at all, though they belong just as completely.

The city seems ideal for colonies of semi-domesticated mosses and lichens so intimately managed that they stand halfway between crop and neighbor. Certain wall lichens may be protected because they indicate mineral leakage before instruments do. Some moss strains might be bred for cistern filtration, insulation, or medicinal poultices. The people would know them individually: the blue-black parapet velvet that drinks night fog, the bitter pale thread that appears near copper leaks, the warm-vent cushion that harbors translucent eggs of some harmless moth. In a place this wet-in-pockets, plant life becomes diagnostic. Green is not scenery; it is information.

The salamanders in the reservoir gardens likely thrive with an almost ceremonial regard from the citizens. Children would be warned not to handle them with warm hands. The transparent-winged moths of the duct houses might be both nuisance and omen, their hatches marking a useful shift in heat distribution. Ravens would be near-citizens: thieves, heralds, inheritors of tower routes older than the cableways. Goats would remain insolent emissaries of the ungoverned ledges, impossible to fully exclude, occasionally appearing where no lawful creature should stand. Rodents in the retaining walls would be monitored with the same seriousness as cracks.

Below the everyday scale of notice, stranger organisms inhabit the place. The ice-white overnight filaments suggest a life adapted to condense itself out of very slight conditions—perhaps not exactly fungal, not exactly mineral, but a border form encouraged by metal, cold, and airborne trace salts. It may not "live" in the way lowland life does. It may precipitate, feed, dissolve, and recur according to pressure and alloy. Likewise the luminous films in underways: microbial sheets or chemotrophic veils feeding on seepwater and stone chemistry, tolerated because they reveal channel edges, controlled because too much of them means imbalance. Around the warm mineral vents there would be entire hidden food chains independent of daylight, pale and damp and a little prehistoric in demeanor: blind mites, red worms, fernlets with flesh-thick fronds, translucent beetles, maybe tiny shrimp-like things in sump basins no one publicizes.

And then there are the things the city has not decided how to classify.

The drifting lights that gather at open warmth feel less like ghosts than like a recurring atmospheric life-form, or a mineral intelligence too diffuse to imprison. If one lives there long enough, one probably stops asking whether they are alive in the usual sense. They are part of the civic weather. They come on certain nights, in certain pressures, around warmth gradients and open lamps. Children are frightened of them until they are taught respect; adolescents dare each other to stand among them; old workers note their arrival as one more indicator of deep conditions in the mountain. Some say they are harmless if ignored, others that they can lure sleepwalkers onto galleries, still others that they are attracted to fever and grief. The truth may be undramatic and still unsettling: they are curious, transient, and not wholly material.

There may even be larger mountain beings at the edge of the human map, though not in the simple form of monsters. In the fog-bent forests below the quarry lines, where terraces decay into old scree and low willow, something could move that belongs to an older ecological layer than the city—thick-furred browsers, cave-antlered ungulates, stone-colored omnivores, or simply remnant animals made legendary by rarity. But the mountain does not seem to want apex spectacle. Its strangeness is distributed, subtle, cumulative. Even its larger secrets would likely be glimpsed only as prints in soft mud, bark peeled too high for goats, a sound in lower mist like someone dragging wet brushwood uphill.

If you ask more deeply what truly lives there, though, the answer may be endurance itself, made local.

This is a habitat for beings who can share walls with geology, who understand that scarcity does not always mean deprivation and abundance does not always arrive as plenty. The dominant life-form of that city—human or otherwise—would be one that prospers by catching what passes lightly: water from cloud, heat from engines, light from fog, soil from cracks, usefulness from old structures, warning from small changes. Its virtues would be persistence, precision, thrift, mutual dependence, and a highly developed tolerance for incompleteness. Nothing there is ever finished; everything is kept.

So whoever lives there lives in layers of damp stone and reclaimed warmth, among moss law and iron memory, under a sky that seldom reveals itself all at once. They are not conquerors of the mountain. They are its long-term negotiators. They move through galleries and stairwells with lamps hooded against mist, greet one another by asking after wall-wetness and vent-breath, inherit tools older than surnames, and sleep behind shutters when the curious lights drift. They garden in drip and shadow. They repair what their grandparents repaired. They bury their dead where roots can find seepwater and names can be cut into stone that will not flake for centuries. They raise children who learn before anything else that every surface speaks.

And after enough time, they begin to resemble the city itself: weather-darkened, intricate, soft with moss in some places and iron-sure in others, difficult to read at a glance, wholly coherent to those who belong.
