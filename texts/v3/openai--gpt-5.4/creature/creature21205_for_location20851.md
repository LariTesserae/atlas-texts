What lives there is a people who have allowed themselves to be edited by water without surrendering being human.

Not fish-people in the simple sense, not a fantasy of fins and scales, and not merely ordinary landfolk holding their breath in a difficult environment. They are an upland littoral people of the drowned heights: amphibious by craft first, by habit second, and by body perhaps only slowly, across generations, in those subtle ways a place writes itself into muscle, lung, skin, reflex, superstition, and architecture until anatomy is no longer only flesh.

They would look, at first meeting, remarkably human in silhouette and uncannily not in motion.

Their bodies are built less for speed than for economy. The chest is important: broad-ribbed, not barrel-like exactly, but capacious, with a culture that prizes calm breathing the way mountain cultures might prize steady footing. The shoulders are mobile, the hands strong and articulate, fingers often slightly splayed from lifelong work in resistance rather than open air. The feet matter too—gripping, intelligent feet used on ladders, ropes, slick stone, woven platforms, and slow water ascents. The whole body would seem tuned for climbing through density. Not graceful in the sleek pelagic manner; rather, competent, deliberate, suspendable. They know how to inhabit buoyancy without being made foolish by it.

Their skin likely carries the environment’s finish. Not scales, but a constant sheen, a polished dampness. Salt-softened, resin-protected, sun-touched only in the upper districts. Scar patterns would be common: coral nicks, rope burns, pressure bruises, cuts from shell, white healed constellations from stinging growth. People who work around freshwater springs might have different skin textures from those who spend more time in saline gardens or smoky air-domes. The place would create occupations visible on the body. A cistern-keeper’s forearms. A cliff-farmer’s toes. A lift-builder’s back. A fire-tender’s smoke-rimmed eyes.

Hair would not be ornamental in the dry-land sense. It would be treated as drifting material, a thing to bind, weight, oil, braid, shell through, wrap in netting, or let loose only in certain contexts. Children might go mostly cloud-haired until old enough for working braids. Adults would wear practical arrangements that also signify district, kin, trade, or ritual obligations. In water, hair becomes aura; this culture would have made use of that. Status may be legible not through tailored cloth but through what remains stable while everything floats: knot styles, weighted ornaments, carved nose or ear pieces, shell rings, bead cords, bands of colored fiber, particular resins whose smell persists in air chambers.

Their eyes, in a story sense, are easy to overstate, but the place does imply a people deeply literate in dimness. Not necessarily biologically altered to some inhuman degree, but trained from infancy to read contrast, suspended movement, turbulence, silhouette, and the slight falseness of distance underwater. They would be excellent at noticing what has changed in a familiar volume: a rope more slack than yesterday, a spring plume muddied, a school absent, a wall hosting the wrong kind of white growth. Their seeing is not horizon-seeing. It is near-field, textural, volumetric. They read water the way foresters read canopy and desert peoples read wind.

More than anything, what lives there is a culture of practiced gentleness.

Underwater, force wastes itself. The inhabitants have learned to move without stirring silt, to handle objects without losing them, to pass one another in lanes of growth without damaging food or shelter. Their temperament—not individually, but civically—would appear patient, because impatience has poor mechanical outcomes. They pull, brace, anchor, wait, listen, test. Their city survives by thousands of small corrections, and so its people are probably attentive in a granular way that outsiders could mistake for caution or even slowness. In truth it is a very high competence: an ethics of maintenance made into personality.

Their speech would have split into environments. In water, language is likely tactile, gestural, percussive, close-range. Hands, wrists, shoulder angles, cord-tugs, taps on beams, clicks made with tongue or shell, pulses hummed into shared structures. Faces still matter, but mouths are less sovereign. Meaning travels through the whole body. In air pockets and dry chambers, spoken language returns sharpened, cherished, almost extravagant. Words there might become ceremonial, legal, intimate, historical. One can imagine that some things are only ever properly said in air: naming a child, settling a dispute, reciting lineages, invoking the dead, making promises around fire. Other things belong to water and become clumsy if spoken aloud: work songs, flirtation along guide ropes, market bargaining through touch signs, warnings passed by knock-pattern through scaffold posts.

So these people are likely bilingual not merely in vocabulary but in medium.

Their children would be astonishingly at home there. Childhood in such a place would produce a kind of three-dimensional familiarity rare in surface civilizations. They would know routes above, beside, and below; know which curtain roots hide shrimp, which beams creak before loosening, where warm freshwater folds into colder salt, where one may tuck oneself in a current shadow and dream. They would turn infrastructure into play. Counterweight lifts become contests. Ladder shafts become bravery tests. Marker chimes become games of stealth. They would be raised with a strong sense that verticality is social. To go upward is to reach weather, light, birds, and perhaps prestige or danger. To go downward is work, mystery, taboo, adulthood, or resourcefulness depending on district and season. A child there grows not toward “the world” but into layers.

The elderly would be especially important.

In a place whose survival depends on continuity of observation, memory is structural. The old know which cliff face only seems sound, which spring soured during the hot season thirty years ago, what shell-burn recipe produces plaster that does not crack, what hum in the stone columns means a storm above. Because some old people no longer swim strongly, and because dry chambers are reserved in part for them, age would gather around pockets of air, flame, and narrative authority. They become keepers of above-water speech, of long memory, of technical lore too subtle to encode entirely in rules. Perhaps many civic decisions are less “governed” than remembered correctly by the right elder at the right time.

And the place would produce specialists unlike those of land towns.

There are spring-keepers, certainly: people who understand freshwater behavior as both hydrology and blessing. Rope-law elders: those who know rights of passage, mooring precedence, weight tolerances, kin claims on terraces. Growth-readers who distinguish edible white fur from dangerous white fur, useful gelatin from ruinous gelatin, healthy algae bloom from bad water. Breath-wardens for the air-domes, counting fuel, occupants, and time. Fire-priests or simply fire-trained technicians, because under such conditions flame is never ordinary. Lift-tenders, seal-makers, resin workers, bladder stitchers, shell burners, nursery gardeners of floating mats, singers whose songs are meant less to be heard than felt through wood.

There may also be a class of people half practical, half shamanic who deal with the region’s rule-bending matter: those who map narcotic pockets, interpret moon-bubble cliffs, negotiate with humming stone fields, test for bad sweetness in trapped air, retrieve the dreamy if retrieval is still possible. Not sorcerers necessarily. More like hazard-ritualists, experts in phenomena too irregular to become mundane.

Their religion, if religion is the right word, probably does not center on domination of the sea or fear of monsters. It would arise from immersion, permeability, maintenance, and thresholds.

They would revere not a distant god of the deep but points of meeting: salt with fresh, air with water, growth with rot, stone with rope, surface weather translated downward. Springs would be holy because they are literal differences one can pass a hand through. Fire would be holy because it is impossible and communal. Knots may have ritual significance because they make intention durable in a dissolving world. Buoyancy itself might be morally resonant: to bear, to hold, to suspend, to keep from sinking. Their offerings would not remain crisp objects for long, so devotion may favor acts of tending over permanent monuments. Rewrapping a shrine. Clearing a lane. Repairing a wall after a storm in someone else’s district. Returning polished found things altered beautifully by water. Memory there is not dry preservation but repeated renewal.

As for whether they are biologically changed beyond culture: perhaps a little, if this people has been here long enough.

Not extravagant mutation, but drift in the direction of usefulness. Better mammalian dive reflex. Children instinctively calmer underwater. Slightly larger spleens, if one wanted realism. Ears trained or shaped to pressure variation. Noses broad and closable by habit if not by visible valve. A tolerance for humid fungus, for saline skin, for long immersion. Maybe their infants are born with a strong grasping reflex preserved and prized because everyone lives among ladders, nets, and moving water. Maybe their lungs are not larger so much as more disciplined. The most profound adaptation may be neurological: reduced panic in partial submersion, intimate proprioception in three dimensions, an almost impossible ease with not always touching the ground.

But one need not make them posthuman to make them belong. The city already has. Tools, customs, kinship, and architecture have done much of the evolutionary labor.

What else lives there? Not only the people, but their near-companions.

There would be cultivated creatures—not many, but carefully chosen. Small shellfish in suspended baskets. Cleaner shrimp coaxed to inhabit grooming niches. Tame or semi-tame grazers that browse filament gardens and are harvested sparingly. Perhaps air-breathing birds in the upper awash platforms, not domesticated exactly but entangled with human routines. Eels kept in stone runs to process scraps. Tiny crabs tolerated as scavengers until they become thieves. This is not a place of abundant husbandry, but of negotiated coexistence with useful life.

And there are the half-owned beings: district rays recognized by scar patterns, recurring enough to have names; watchful fish associated with certain springs; colonies of translucent growth considered almost civic because they filter water around a neighborhood’s beams; white recoiling organisms treated with wary etiquette. The locals would not draw our hard boundary between wildlife and infrastructure. If it lives on your wall for twenty years, cleans your water, stings your enemies, and glows when storms approach, is it wild?

Temperamentally, the people of this height-under-water might seem to outsiders both intimate and elusive. Intimate because their world requires mutual dependence, shared breath, visible labor, constant repair. Elusive because much of what they know is situational and unspoken, carried in gesture and timing rather than explicit doctrine. Ask them for a map and they may give you a sequence of temperatures, rope textures, and remembered sounds rather than a plan view. Ask who rules, and they may name six people responsible for six different kinds of failure. Ask where the city ends, and they may not understand the question until they rephrase it as where regular maintenance ceases.

They would likely have a strong localism. Each district has its own water character, its own scaffolding habits, its own spring taste, pests, stories, and soundings. Identity would be layered by depth, exposure, and attachment. A person is not only of a family but of a ledge, a mooring forest, an air dome, a current lane. Marriage or alliance may involve not just households but hydrological negotiation. To move districts is to learn a new version of balance.

Their aesthetics would favor weathered persistence over pristine form. Beauty lies in a well-weighted ladder, a clean knot, a shell curtain that parts correctly, a garden that survives two flood seasons, the green-brown luster of something often touched and often repaired. Ceremony might involve the rare assertion of color—dyes protected from wash, reflective scales, impossible firelight, clouds of pigment released in enclosed water—but daily beauty is matte, tactile, living, maintained.

And emotionally, what lives there is a people without the illusion of separation.

Dry-land cultures often imagine the house as a defended interior against the outside. Here the outside enters everything. So interiority becomes relative, negotiated, porous. This would shape character. Their sense of self may be less about boundaries and more about thresholds—what one admits, what one filters, what one ties fast, what one lets drift. They are likely difficult to surprise philosophically by change. They expect encroachment, growth, softening, failure, repair. They may grieve less in terms of lost perfection and more in terms of interrupted tending. A broken platform is sad because of work and memory, not because permanence has been violated; permanence was never offered.

If one had to condense them into an image, though the place resists single images: imagine a person pausing in dim jade water beside a woven railing furred with tiny life, one hand resting on a rope polished by generations, hair bound with shell weights, scars pale on the forearm, watching a freshwater plume braid through salt while overhead a floating garden darkens the light. They are not struggling. They are not conquering. They are attending. Around them hangs a whole civilization of ties, breaths, repairs, minor rituals, and practiced buoyancies.

That is what lives there:

a patient amphibious people of the drowned uplands, cliff-fastened and spring-minded, who have made a civic art of suspension;  
a society of knots, lungs, ladders, and gardens;  
humans rendered slightly strange by long courtesy with water;  
and around them, inseparably, the tame-feral ecology of a city becoming reef while still remembering fire.
