What lives there is not one thing but a manner of living that has spread across species, trades, materials, and weather until they resemble one another.

At first you might think the inhabitants would have to be severe—hard-faced industrial people in waterproof coats, creatures with thick pelts and iron habits, plants like wire, birds like scavenged metal. And some are. But that is only the outer register. The place does not really produce severity so much as adaptation without purity. Whatever lives there becomes composite. It grows a second use. It learns to tolerate damp in the bones and uncertainty underfoot. It develops the patience of moss, the opportunism of reeds, the quiet procedural intelligence of pumps.

The human population, if one begins there, would be a people of layered garments, practical hands, and highly local knowledge. Not frontier types, not rustic marsh-folk, not clean urbanites either, but residents of interstitial competence. They know which stair freezes first, which retaining wall bulges after three days of rain, which market lane stays warm from the bakery exhaust pipe, which underpass floods from below rather than above, which vapors are harmless and which mean evacuate the block for an hour. They would carry keyrings, sealable bags, work gloves tucked into belts, folding carts, insulated flasks, lengths of cord, old transit cards, multi-tools, herb bundles, replacement fuses, bait tins, spare socks. Their clothing would be an ecology in itself: oilskin over knitwear, reflective strips stitched onto old wool, rubber boots worn with office trousers, patched synthetic parkas shining faintly with condensation, market aprons over thermal layers, children in bright waterproofs moving like kingfishers through all the gray.

They are probably not romantics about their environment, though they might love it fiercely in practice. They complain about seepage, outages, root damage, gnats, rail delays, scavenger birds, thermal inversions, fungal spread, buckled paving, sink alarms, generator racket, district smells. Yet they would be deeply alert to small seasonal gifts and local efficiencies: the first edible shoots in a steam-warmed verge, a stand of mushrooms returning to a certain vent court, the annual arrival of translucent-eared grazers near the condenser meadows, the week when rust-colored reedbirds fill the loading yards, the way laundry dries faster beside the battery substation in east wind. Their affection would take the form of maintenance, salvage, rerouting, naming, warning, barter, and repeated use.

These people likely live in buildings that are never entirely one thing. A home might also be a repair room, a seedling nursery, a spool store, a laundry annex, a smoked-fish shed, a drone charging alcove, an office for shift logistics. Kitchens would smell faintly of metal and damp earth as well as tea and broth. Entryways would hold boots at various stages of drying, nets, reed-cutting knives, folded warning barriers, cages for delivery birds or trap animals, sacks of substrate, crates of machine parts beside jars of pickled stems. Windowsills would be crowded with practical life: fungicide sprays, rooting cuttings, condensate cups, radio batteries, small idols made from washers and antlers and glazed clay. To live there would mean accepting that the house is porous to district function. Heat, noise, spores, work schedules, animal visitations, steam plumes, and minor municipal interventions all pass through.

Children of such a place would be extraordinarily good at reading terrain. They would know how to cross on pallets, when ditch ice can bear weight, how to tell old concrete from fresh patch by color, how to distinguish edible dock from toxic runoff greens, which drones ignore them, where six-winged scavengers drop bright bits from vent rims, how to fish nails and coins and tools from flooded pits with magnets. Their games would involve routes, hazards, caches, trespass, elevated pipes, feral greenhouse interiors, the taxonomy of puddles. They would grow up with an unstartled relation to odd life-forms. An eel-bodied trench animal or a white-barked grove inside a transformer yard would seem to them less miraculous than to outsiders, because the whole place teaches that categories leak.

The older people would be especially formidable—not necessarily in body, but in calibration. They would remember where the old channels ran before they were filled, where the fill is thin, which tower block was once a machine laundry, which algae bloom foretells a warm winter, which apparently abandoned tram wires still carry charge in wet weather. They would gather herbs from drainage swales with absolute seriousness. They would know five names for the same district depending on whether one means the formal boundary, the old depot line, the flood map, the market route, or the animal corridor. They would have stories in which infrastructure and weather are characters with motives. Not myths exactly, but practical folklore: if the retaining wall sweats in diagonal bands, expect subsidence; if the vent orchids close at noon, there will be a pressure release by evening; if the scavengers circle low over sorting plant seven, some seal has failed.

The workers of the place would not form a single class of labor so much as a web of maintenance ecologies. There are machinists, growers, drain clearers, rail menders, culvert fishers, insulation crews, salvage graders, fungal scrapers, battery tenders, bird discouragers, vent gardeners, flood wardens, surveyors of slow ground movement, greenhouse medics, pipe laggers, feral-roof pruners, heat-leak prospectors, pest trappers, orchid smugglers, reed cutters, and those enigmatic municipal teams who appear overnight with jacks, lights, prefab barriers, and thermos flasks to correct some local distortion before dawn. Many of them probably cross disciplines because the place itself crossbreeds skill. A person who repairs loading-bay doors may also know marsh plants and can read steam color. A laundress may keep geese off the condenser lots. A substation guard may be the district expert on medicinal mosses. Knowledge here would not stay in its assigned container.

As for nonhuman animals, the place seems to prefer the alert, the social, the edge-dwelling, the moisture-loving, and the experimentally omnivorous.

Its common birds would be intelligent opportunists with weatherproof tempers: crows that understand shift changes better than visitors do, pigeons with iridescence dulled to peat colors and habits half feral, half domestic, gull-like transfer-station cruisers able to crack shellfish or polymer casings with equal professionalism. Reedbeds and loading docks would harbor tiny birds the color of rust, lichen, and wet bark, species that can vanish into industrial vegetation and reappear in clouds when a forklift startles them. There would be black waterfowl in every retention basin, their calls carrying oddly far in fog. Some birds would nest almost symbiotically with infrastructure—swallows under conveyor hoods, kestrel-like hunters on signal gantries, the six-winged scavengers in vent stacks, silent and unnervingly efficient, descending only when heat changes. Even ordinary pigeons, in such a place, would become specialists in thermal mapping and mineral taste.

The mammals would be compact, numerous, and hard to embarrass. Fox-sized culvert runners with slick fur and excellent teeth. Rat lineages so established in the underworks that whole maintenance schedules quietly account for them. Semi-aquatic swimmers in channels and condensate basins, sleek-backed and unbothered by rainbow films on the water. Deer-shaped browsers adapted to sidings, weed lots, and warm embankments—creatures that move through fog between stacks of ties and emergent willow with a strange municipal dignity. The soft-footed ungulates with translucent ears suggest the place can produce gentleness too, but a practical gentleness: light enough not to break the thawing crust over condenser meadows, shy but not fearful, feeding where warmth meets mineral seep. One imagines animals there are selected less for beauty than for the ability to navigate contradictory surfaces—grate, mud, ballast, tile, root mat, sheet ice, old insulation, standing water—without deciding any of them are impossible.

Reptiles and amphibians, if present, would be creatures of vents, sumps, and thermal pockets: pale frogs in pump-house moss, dark newts in settling ponds, heat-loving snakes no thicker than cables in laundries’ foundation cracks. Insects would be deliriously successful wherever machine warmth and wet substrate overlap. Moths dusting sodium light. Midges over tanks. Metallic beetles in greenhouse ruins. Thick-bodied pollinators in impossible winter blossom zones around steam leaks. There may be whole pollination systems that have shifted their timing to industrial exhalations rather than season alone.

Then there are the stranger beings, and the place’s greatest revelation is that it does not isolate them as marvels. It houses them by use-value and repetition. The eel-like trench inhabitants are accepted because they keep certain warm channels clear, or because they indicate contamination, or because they are edible if taken young, or simply because everyone has grown up stepping around them. The six-winged scavengers are pests, omens, sanitation aides, and neighborhood markers all at once. The translucent-eared grazers become part of dawn traffic logic. In another setting these would be cryptids or sacred fauna; here they are folded into municipal reality. That suggests the region produces not fantasy creatures in a romantic sense, but organisms that have evolved in lockstep with ducts, waste heat, altered mineral regimes, and chronic human disturbance. They belong because belonging here means ongoing adjustment.

Plants, too, feel less like background life than a populous nation. What lives there includes walls that have gone botanical, stairs occupied by liverwort republics, fenced lots held by self-seeded conifers, orchids clustered around vent mouths like small listening organs. Reeds become a kind of citizen. Birch saplings are almost a recurring weather pattern. Black mosses and sulfur lichens announce district character as plainly as architecture. Vines learn facades the way residents learn routes. One gets the sense that flora in this place are not merely opportunistic but behaviorally specific, as if each structure type invites its own vegetal guild: transformer birches, culvert cresses, rail-yard thistles, heat-plant orchids, laundry-vent mosses, depot willow scrub, signal-gantry shrubs. If the place has an indigenous intelligence, plants are one of its favorite instruments.

And perhaps that is the deepest answer: what lives there is a consortium. Not a harmonious one, not a single organism exactly, but something close to a negotiated metabolism. Human districts, animal corridors, fungal blooms, root systems, culverts, market rhythms, steam release schedules, flood maps, rail alignments, salvage economies, and forgotten buried waterways all participate in one another’s survival. The residents—human and otherwise—are creatures of adjacency. They know how to use waste heat. They know how to exploit delay. They flourish in regulatory ambiguity, in sheltered damp, in spaces maintenance cannot quite finish. They are not creatures of climax conditions or completed plans. They are citizens of the provisional.

If I were to name the characteristic inhabitant of that place—not by species but by type—it would be this:

A being with a talent for taking hold in an unfinished system.

It might wear gloves and carry a thermos. It might root in a crack under a pipe elbow. It might swim blind through a warm drain. It might nest in a fan housing, graze a condenser field, silver a retaining wall, sell herbs cut from runoff ditches, or spend the night quietly lifting a sagging street back into level. Whatever form it takes, it is moisture-tolerant, situationally intelligent, unoffended by mixed categories, and intimately allied with local process. It does not require purity of habitat. It prefers overlap. It thrives where one thing has become another without fully ceasing to be itself.

So the true inhabitants are makers of second landscapes: people who can live in draft form, animals that use infrastructure as terrain, plants that read machinery as climate, and stranger species grown from the long conversation between bog-water, heat, concrete, rust, and patience.

The place is full of settlers, but none of them are settlers in the usual sense. They have not conquered the original land. They are what happened when the original land kept arriving anyway, and everything else had to learn to live with its continued invention.
