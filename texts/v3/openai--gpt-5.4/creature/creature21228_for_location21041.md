What lives there is difficult to separate from what has been built there, and perhaps that is the first truth of it: the inhabitants are not merely residents of the city, but one more stable condition the city has learned to hold.

They are few. That is apparent before any clear sighting. A place with broad processional stairs, plazas measured for assemblies, and precincts layered across whole escarpments ought to reveal movement constantly if it is inhabited in any ordinary way. Instead one receives evidence in the smallest increments: a parapet edge polished recently rather than merely historically; a line of dust disturbed in a pattern too deliberate for settling stone; a gate crossed often enough that the air beyond it feels used. The city does not teem. It is maintained by presences so sparse that each one acquires the weight of office.

At first you might think the inhabitants are statues, caretakers carved in the same severe material as their surroundings. Many are seen this way initially: upright on rooflines, still within colonnades, paused at the rims of shafts or high courts, each figure so well matched to the architecture that motion is required before life can be admitted. But motion, when it comes, is not statue-like at all. It is exact. Economical. They do not fidget, stretch, pace, or display any of the little thermal restlessness familiar in warm-bodied creatures. They move as if each shift in posture has already been agreed upon by balance, threshold, and destination.

They are probably tall, though the city’s manipulation of scale makes certainty unwise. In open courts they seem long rather than bulky, attenuated by altitude and thin air. Their limbs have too many convincing angles. Not grotesquely many—nothing flamboyant here—but enough that a crouched figure can become, in standing, less a folding of joints than a rearrangement of priorities. Elbows may ride higher than expected; the hindmost joints of the legs may flex in ways that suggest climbing was never a specialization but a premise. The body plan appears built for steps, ledges, and vertical negotiation, for resting on narrow projections, for becoming narrow itself when passing slits, and for stillness in exposed places where warmth cannot be wasted.

Their surfaces are hard to read. In the city’s sidewise light, skin and garment and mineral accretion are almost impossible to distinguish. A watcher on a high wall may seem clothed in layered slate; then it turns and the layers move like integument. Another appears smooth and chalk-matte until the shoulder opens in tiny overlapping facets that catch the dimness like mica. Some may wear architecture the way hermits wear shell: collars, hoods, ridge-backed mantles, and plate-like wraps of worked stone, evaporite laminate, woven mineral membrane, or some substance grown in seams and then harvested. Others may simply have bodies that imitate such materials because in this place concealment and kinship with the built environment amount to the same adaptation.

Their coloration would be made of the local register: ash, bone-pale, iron, weathered violet, the faint yellow of old salts, the dark soot-fan black of those wall films, the translucent gray of crystal under dust. Brightness would be rare and official. If any vivid color exists among them, it is likely reserved for inner surfaces, ceremonial markings, or moments of signaling—something flashed and withdrawn, not displayed.

Their faces, when glimpsed, would be disconcerting less for monstrosity than for composure. One imagines broad ocular structures adapted to dim, uncertain light: eyes deep-set under mineral ridges, or many-layered lids, or reflective internal surfaces that silver briefly when the basin glow rises. Perhaps the face is not expressive in the mammalian way. Perhaps expression here lives in inclination, in the opening or closing of fine vents, in the orientation of faceted cheek-plates, in the degree to which the head aligns itself with a threshold rather than with a person. If they speak, the mouth may not be the primary instrument. The city is full of apertures and resonances; it would be fitting if their voices emerged through chest cavities, throat chambers, palatal slits, or external resonators built into ceremonial collars.

They are cold-tempered creatures, though not cold in spirit—cold in metabolism, in pace, in assumptions. Warmth is never abundant, so nothing about them would rely on wasteful intensity. Their lives are arranged around gradients rather than combustions. They would know where stone keeps a day’s meager heat, where denser gases spill at dawn, where a court remains reliably less mutable than a neighboring lane, where a wall can be leaned against without losing too much of oneself to it. If they are biological, they are miserly with moisture and lavish only with patience. Their tissues may be dry, dense, partly mineralized. Their blood, if they have blood, may be slow and dark and under no obligation to remain liquid in familiar ways. Their breath would be nearly invisible, perhaps more a periodic adjustment than a plume.

I do not think they are numerous enough to support a noisy society, and yet they do not feel solitary in the human sense. They are civic beings. This matters. Even one seen at a distance seems to be functioning not as an individual wandering through an environment but as a node participating in a layout. A figure standing motionless in a gate frame may be guarding, yes—but just as plausibly stabilizing passage, completing a necessary repetition, serving as the living member of an architectural equation. Another ascending a staircase with unbearable slowness may not be en route anywhere urgent; it may be keeping the stair itself in obedience. In such a place, where rows preserve reality better than singletons and thresholds train movement, an inhabitant’s mere placement could be infrastructural.

This may explain why they are so often found at edges: bridge mouths, plaza rims, colonnade ends, stair landings, the lips of shafts, the first and last steps of processional routes. They live where the city negotiates between one state and another. They are magistrates of transition, whether consciously or by evolved role. Perhaps their original ancestors were simply cliff-dwellers and dryland foragers. Over centuries in a city where geometry matters materially, posture became office, office became biology, and biology became municipal ritual. The species and the institution braided together.

Their dwellings, if one can call them that, are likely austere and partly public by our standards. Cells opening onto communal courts. Narrow rooms deep within walls where temperature and dimensional stability are best controlled. High niches with impossible views. Clusters of chambers around dry cistern-like hollows that never held water but collect some other necessity: cold, charge, dust of a certain grade, chemically active night air. They may sleep standing in alcoves, folded into buttresses, suspended in harness-like mineral lattices, or laid in shallow stone trays that preserve shape and warmth. Privacy would exist, but perhaps not around ownership of space. More likely it concerns orientation, sequence, and lawful access—who may cross which frame at which hour, who may inhabit a visible tower on the day it is present, who may descend the shaft when the glow in the basin has reached a given pallor.

They are almost certainly ritualized, but in the same way the city is: not lavishly theatrical, just deeply procedural. Their ceremonies may be indistinguishable from maintenance. A file of inhabitants carrying no tools may traverse a lane at metallic dusk, each pausing under an arch for a measure of silence, and thereby recalibrate some local reluctance of space. A court may have to remain empty except for one individual stationed at exact intervals across a season. Certain inscriptions cannot be read directly, so perhaps they are recited by touching them with the back of the hand, or by standing in designated spots while another speaks into the stone. Governance here may consist of placement, repetition, and witness. Law may literally keep the world from fraying.

As for intellect: they feel old-minded rather than primitive or advanced. They would possess a severe municipal intelligence, one developed under conditions where practical survival and metaphysical stability are the same discipline. They would know engineering without machines, jurisprudence without abstraction from matter, ecology without abundance. To them, emptiness would be a managed substance. Distance would be something to allocate. A wall would have legal as well as structural duties. They might keep records not in portable documents but in arrays of stones, in wear patterns, in licensed routes through shifting districts, in the continued absence of collapse where collapse would otherwise occur. Bureaucracy, in such a species, could become almost sacred: not pettiness, but the patient arrangement by which impossible terrain remains habitable.

If they have art, it is probably inseparable from exactness. Interval. Proportion. Repetition under changing light. The polishing of parapets. The shaping of a silent avenue so perspective hesitates in a particular way. Choral tones too low to carry far, designed instead to vibrate selected mineral bands. Temporary growths of crystalline lace cultivated on facades and allowed to shed. Processions performed not for spectators but for districts. They may make figurative objects rarely, perhaps because representation in a place where solitary forms change too quickly is dangerous. If they depict anything, it may be through arrays, never singular images: twelve similar forms to preserve one truth.

How do they reproduce, if indeed they do? The city suggests slowness, rarity, and distributed care. Perhaps young begin as soft, pale nodules in sheltered seams, tended in communal hollows until their bodies harden and articulate. Perhaps what seems like those chalky colonial growths are larval or companion phases of the same lifeform. Perhaps reproduction is not strictly biological at all but partly civic: an adult takes office at a threshold long enough that the threshold impresses a successor from mineral-organic matter prepared within the wall. In a place where architecture and life have entered mutual service, generation may involve both womb and quarry.

The smaller lives of the city—the wall membranes, pore-films, seam nodules, evening fliers, crack-folding basin walkers—may be related to them, domesticated by them, ignored by them, or ancestral to them. One can imagine a whole restrained phylogeny of dry, mineral-adjacent organisms, each exploiting trace chemistries, electrostatic gradients, and the city’s strange jurisdictions of space. The inhabitants at full scale would then be merely the most civic branch of a broader native tendency: life here survives by becoming geometric enough to cooperate with the place.

And there may be more than one kind of resident.

The roofline watchers and stair keepers could be only the visible caste: wardens, surveyors, functionaries. Deeper inside the high precincts there may be slower, larger beings almost never seen, those fitted not for ledges but for chambers larger within than without. In the honeycombed cliff districts perhaps there are colony intelligences, many-bodied administrations inhabiting whole facades. In the visible-every-third-day towers, another order of citizen may live on a schedule tied to local reality conditions, appearing only when the district coheres. Lower down, among more comprehensible house blocks, there may be humbler residents—gatherers of dust, tenders of films, counters of steps, auditors of erosion. The city feels stratified not by wealth in any obvious sense, but by degree of participation in the impossible.

Would they welcome a traveler? Perhaps “welcome” is not the right category. They would notice. Not immediately by pursuit, but by altered balances. Doors would begin to insist more strongly. Dust would choose lanes around the stranger. One might glimpse two or three figures occupying points ahead that make no sense tactically unless their purpose is to define a lawful route. The inhabitants might treat a newcomer first as a destabilizing object to be placed correctly. Hospitality could consist of leading one into a chamber whose dimensions agree with the body, offering not food or fire but a stable geometry in which to rest. Communication might begin with orientation: stand here, face there, cross only when the silver reaches the third pillar. Only later would anything like conversation emerge.

If one earned their trust, I suspect they would seem less ghostly and more administrative than expected. Not lurkers in a ruin, but clerks, judges, custodians, surveyors, choristers of maintenance. Their great achievement would not be survival alone, but continuity. They have persisted in a place where dry cold, scarcity, and ontological drift should have erased any ordinary civilization. They endured by becoming legible to the mountain’s altered rules, then writing those rules back into streets and bodies and offices until city and citizen formed a single long-lived compact.

So what lives there?

A thin, patient people of stone-adjacent flesh and ritualized motion. Cliff-adapted, cold-economical, sparse as punctuation. Their bodies are part mineral, or dressed to appear so; their society is half municipality, half stabilizing rite. They inhabit thresholds more than rooms, responsibilities more than possessions. They are the keepers of empty plazas, counters of steps that do not agree, maintainers of arches that look onto elsewhere, civil servants of a landscape in which reality has gone old and local.

And alongside them live lesser, stranger companions: wall-films that breathe by pores, seam colonies that harden and soften with the cold, silent membranes that harvest dusk, gliding things that erase light, basin walkers that fold into cracks, and perhaps whole hidden orders of citizen-creatures distributed through the impossible districts on schedules the city itself enforces.

Most of all, though, what lives there is a form of inhabited order. The residents are whatever kind of beings can survive by treating law, architecture, erosion, and metaphysics as one continuous household task. They are not exactly the descendants of the builders, because in a place like this descendants and builders and institutions eventually become the same category.

They live there the way frost lives in a pattern or judgment lives in a code:
quietly,
repeatedly,
with authority,
and for a very long time.
