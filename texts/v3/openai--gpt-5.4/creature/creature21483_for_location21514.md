What lives there is a people of maintenance.

Not merely workers, though they are that. Not merely survivors of an inhospitable altitude, though survival is built into every habit they keep. The city seems likely to produce a population whose deepest cultural trait is not ambition or piety or conquest, but attentiveness to systems that do not forgive neglect. They would be practical in the way coastal people are practical about tides, or desert people about shade and distance. Here the thing one learns early is flow: what rises, what backs up, what condenses, what bursts, what can be trusted by sound, by smell, by warmth under the palm.

They are probably not visually flamboyant at first glance. At a distance, one might read them as dark figures moving in measured ways across terraces and catwalks, their clothes chosen less for display than for durability against damp, rust, and mineral abrasion. Fabrics would be heavy enough to resist the chill of wet mornings but breathable in the warm air: waxed canvas, oiled fibers, softened leather, tightly woven cloth gone shiny at cuffs and knees from use. Colors would sit comfortably inside the city’s palette—charcoal, umber, iron blue, faded ocher, green gone nearly black—because anything brighter would soon be subdued by mist, dust, and salts. Yet up close there might be unexpected precision: good fastenings, carefully repaired hems, family-specific stitching, polished closures, enamel tags, a bright cord indicating district or trade, small ornaments made from ceramic, copper, glass slag, old gauge faces, shaped mineral crusts. The city likely teaches that beauty should survive moisture.

Their bodies would show the altitude and the labor, but not all in the same way. Some are long-limbed and sure-footed from stairs, ladders, inclines, and bridgeways; some broad-shouldered from turning valves, lifting grates, hauling hose and cable; some stooped a little from years in pipe galleries and maintenance tunnels. Hands matter here. So do knees, balance, hearing, lungs. Children grow into caution not because they are timid but because the environment trains perception early. A child in this city might know before adolescence how to tell clean condensation from dangerous runoff by smell, which rails are safe to touch in fog, how to walk wet stone without haste, how to hear when a pump is working too hard. Old people, if they live long, become repositories of microclimates and exceptions. They know which stair will glaze first after rain, which retaining wall sings before a slip, which district’s chain-spout runs warm in winter because of some buried rerouting done forty years ago and never entered into a map.

It feels like a city where social status would attach not only to wealth but to competence, reliability, and stewardship. The most respected person on a street may be neither merchant nor official, but the one who can keep a cistern from fouling, arbitrate a water-sharing dispute, coax another season from a failing pressure regulator, or remember the hidden shutoff behind three generations of masonry. Expertise here is intimate and distributed. Many people know a little hydraulics because ignorance would be dangerous, but beyond that there are castes or guilds or simply longstanding lineages of very specific skill: pumpwrights, seal-keepers, lime-scalers, riveters, drain-clearers, valve listeners, ceramic channel setters, fog-bell wardens, thermal masons, rail-brace inspectors, pond skimmers, filter tenders. Some occupations sound almost sacred because the city itself blurs the line between utility and ritual. To clean a catchment basin before the rains may be infrastructure, but if everyone depends on that basin, the work acquires ceremony whether anyone intends it to or not.

So perhaps the people are not uniformly solemn, but they are likely ceremonious in practical ways. Their calendar would follow weather pulses, reservoir levels, maintenance windows, migratory mists, pressure seasons, the flowering of leaks. They might mark the first hard rain not with poetic abstraction but by opening selected overflow stairs and hanging warning cloths in the upper wards. They might celebrate the annual emptying of an old treatment pond, or the safe restart of a deep pump after refurbishment, or the brief period when a certain quarry-lake turns mirror-bright and children are allowed its edge under supervision. Their festivals may be loud enough to meet the city on its own terms: bells, whistles, drums felt through paving, choirs in cistern chambers, lanterns reflected in wet stone. But even revelry would likely route itself around function. Nothing blocks an emergency spillway for long. Nothing decorative is allowed to interfere with drainage. To live here is to understand that negligence can kill neighbors you know by name.

They may be a people with a dry sense of humor, because environments that demand vigilance often produce jokes as pressure valves. Their idioms probably come from seepage and load. A stubborn person is calcified. A secret is under pressure. Someone unreliable has a cracked sump. To say a person is generous may be to say they overflow uphill. Children tease each other with mock safety warnings. Lovers meet under vents because everyone else avoids the noise. Elders tell stories about impossible repairs performed in weather no one now believes. There are ghost tales too, but likely of a municipal sort: voices in disused mains, lamp-glows in condemned galleries, the splash that follows no body, the old inspector who still rings the bell in blind mist. Such stories would be held in the same mind as practical caution. In a place like this, folklore is just hazard memory after enough generations.

Their architecture has probably entered their posture. People duck instinctively under low braces, test steps with the ball of the foot, stand clear of joints and seams, favor walls that stay warm, gather in overhang markets where runoff can be heard and judged. They are comfortable with verticality in a non-dramatic way. Heights are not scenic first; they are routes, responsibilities, weather indicators. The city has too many levels for anyone to remain abstract about where they are. People navigate by pressure zone, watershed, retaining line, old district, pipe color, wall temperature, the taste of air. Ask directions and you may not get left and right so much as “above the warm laundries,” “below the cracked red stairs,” “past the green-marked wall where the stone sweats,” “keep the hum on your good side.”

The inhabitants themselves may smell faintly of their city no matter how clean they are: damp cloth dried near warm masonry, iron, lime soap, machine oil, smoke from hot food eaten under rain shelters, the green note of algae caught in boot seams, metallic dust. Their homes are likely immaculate in the ways that matter and forgiving in the ways that do not. Thresholds are scrubbed. Storage is elevated. Water vessels are labeled or known by shape. Everything has a place away from seepage. Walls may bloom with harmless mineral tracing that no one bothers to remove because it would only return. Roofs are used if they can be used: as gardens, walkways, drying fields, repair surfaces, gathering spaces in evening warmth. The domestic and infrastructural probably interpenetrate constantly. A family kitchen might share a wall with a service shaft whose warmth helps bread rise. A courtyard cistern might be the center of neighborhood politics as much as a utility.

As for temperament: patient, but not passive. Cautious, but not fearful. Curious in a diagnostic rather than romantic mode. They likely distrust grand simplifications because no part of their city stays simple for long. Everything reroutes. Everything has a hidden history. A person formed here may listen before speaking, not from meekness but from habit: first hear the load in the room, the pressure in the line, the thing behind the thing. They may be poor at spectacle and excellent at endurance. Outsiders might mistake them for slow because they do not hurry theatrically, but they would probably react with startling efficiency when something ruptures. A burst main, a steam warning, a fresh slip on a stair—then you would see the city’s true speed, which is communal and exact.

There are probably people among them who belong less to the daylight terraces than to the undercity of service ways and cistern galleries: inspectors, scavengers, unofficial repairers, children gone feral only by local standards, scholars of abandoned infrastructure, saints of the warm dark. This city feels as though it would produce specialists in neglected spaces. Individuals who know which hollow factory frame now houses migrant families in dry months; which flooded tunnel has a breathable ledge above the valve house; which old mineral chamber is used for clandestine meetings because the acoustics blur identity; which “disused” channel still carries enough clean trickle to sustain roof gardens no one has permits for. Such people would form the city’s unofficial circulation, as necessary in their way as the formal systems.

And because the city has had so long to become itself, its inhabitants have likely absorbed some of its minor strangeness without becoming mystical about it. They know which glowing canal film to avoid and which can be skimmed for dye. They know the stones that ring, the walls that fur over overnight, the rooms where condensation behaves oddly, the harmless crust and the dangerous crust. A child might keep an iridescent utensil as treasure; an elder might scrape the same sheen off a kettle with annoyance. The miraculous and the municipal are not fully separate categories here. There may well be shrine practices around springs, vents, lion-spouts, old pump housings, pressure bells, and certain impossible leaks that never dry. But even reverence probably includes maintenance: wiping scale from a saint’s drain, repainting warning marks at a sacred outflow, leaving offerings in jars that also happen to measure drip rate.

If I try to imagine one representative inhabitant, the image refuses to stay singular. It becomes several.

A woman in late middle age carrying a ring of keys and pressure tags, who can identify six neighborhoods by the taste of condensation on the air and is greeted with equal respect by market vendors and pipe crews.

A young tram mechanic with mineral-bright scars on his forearms, who wears three generations of repaired workcoat and tends succulents on a roof fed by a leak everyone pretends not to notice.

Children racing along the warm parapets after rain, forbidden from the lower grates, chanting some game-rhyme about sirens and steam while an elder shouts corrections that are half safety lecture and half affection.

A reed-thin cistern cleaner emerging from a hatch in a halo of vapor, face slick, eyes irritated, carrying up a small enamel cup because he found it in the silt and recognized the district mark.

An old couple in a house built over three retaining eras, arguing gently about whether the chain-spout has changed note since last winter.

A bell warden in upper fog, invisible except for the moving circle of sound.

And perhaps, more metaphorically, what lives there is also the city’s appetite for continuity. It breeds people who do not expect clean breaks. They inherit patched systems and become patchers themselves. They are born into a landscape already modified beyond easy origin, and so their identity is unlikely to rely on purity. They would be syncretic by necessity: old rites attached to newer pumps, ancestral names spoken in neighborhoods rebuilt four times, recipes adapted to mineral water, laws layered over customs layered over emergency exceptions that became tradition. They understand that a thing can be damaged, ugly, essential, and beloved at once.

If there are animals particularly associated with them, they may be the kinds that tolerate edges: scavenging birds overhead, lizards on hot housings, patient marsh waders in subsidence pools. But the true companion species of the place might be humbler and stranger: mosses around vents, iron-loving films in drains, heat-borne algal skins, invisible colonies changing the color of stone. The people would notice these as indicators more than as scenery. Life, in this city, announces condition. A certain orange bloom means this pipe is failing. A certain green on masonry means the hidden channel still runs. A certain smell in the laundries means the thermal mix is off. The inhabitants become readers of these texts.

So: who lives there?

A highland civic species, almost.

Humans shaped by wet stone and machinery into something disciplined, communal, and technically superstitious in the best sense: respectful of systems they partly understand and partly mythologize, because both responses keep you alive. They live with noise until it becomes weather. They make homes in load-bearing gaps. They inherit caution without surrendering warmth. They know how to bronze in sunset and blacken in work, how to joke under sirens, how to grow herbs above cisterns, how to listen to walls, how to trust a repaired thing more than a new one if the repair was done by the right hands.

They are the kind of people who would call a city beautiful not because it is untouched, but because it continues.
