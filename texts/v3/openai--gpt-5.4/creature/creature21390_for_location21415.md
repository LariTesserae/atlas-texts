What lives there is not one thing but a negotiated population: a sparse human remnant, a dense ecology of maintenance, and a civic intelligence so distributed through matter and protocol that it is easier to mistake it for weather than for a resident. The undercity is inhabited in the same way an old port is inhabited—by dockworkers and gulls and rust and schedules and tides—except here the tides are air pressure and coolant flow, the gulls are drones, and the harbor itself remembers.

The most obvious inhabitants, if you spend enough time and learn where to look, are people. Not crowds, not a teeming underclass pressing in every tunnel, but knots of residents in pockets where conditions have become dependable enough to support custom. They live where the infrastructure accidentally favors continuity: near warm manifolds that will not fail in winter-cycle, near condensate capture courts, near transit spurs that still recognize local credentials, near old civic nodes with enough bandwidth and power to patch into the district’s lingering intelligence.

They would not look uniformly poor or uniformly futuristic. Life in such a place selects for maintenance-minded pragmatists, scavenger-administrators, people who can hear a failing bearing in a wall and know whether to report it, avoid it, or harvest it. Their clothes would be layered for microclimates rather than fashion: wick-fabric under oilskin capes, conductive gloves with fingertips cut away, soft-soled boots for resonant floors, filter scarves worn loose until one enters a bad-air corridor. They would carry light, but not always use it. Many would navigate by tactile knowledge, by reflected sound, by the pressure on the skin when a vent is near. Their bodies might be baseline, modified, or simply adapted by habit: enlarged pupils, scarred shins, an ingrained stoop under low ducting, the reflex to pause before crossing an unseen field threshold. They would speak quietly not because the place is quiet, but because loud speech belongs to alarms and machinery. Their language would be thick with district names and infrastructural verbs: bleed the line, wake the door, salt the trap, listen for dry fans, don’t trust a clean wall.

These human enclaves would be clannish in the practical way that utility breeds community. Water rights matter. Access rights matter. Knowing which doors are sulking, which drones are feral, which filtration beds are due for a flush, matters. So households might form around inherited competencies as much as blood. One cluster keeps the fungal towers edible and noninvasive. Another tends old sensor shrines—really interface cabinets polished by generations of use. Another brokers transit windows with the system, timing cargo cart access to move goods between far pockets. They would have children, perhaps not many, and those children would grow up with extraordinary local literacies: how to tell clean condensation from machine sweat, how to identify a safe hum, how to freeze when inspection beams sweep by because they are counting motes and not looking for you unless you make yourself anomalous.

They would probably be more conservative than romantic about the place. To them, the singing shaft is not mystical, just annoying when trying to sleep. The bioluminescent wells are useful ambient markers and dangerous if the skin film is disturbed. They would decorate in small, stubborn ways: bright thread tied around conduit junctions, painted handprints at an enclave threshold, strings of printed tags rattling softly in vent breeze, family icons pressed into old emergency foam. Their homes might occupy former service apartments, partitioned bays, or retrofitted logistics rooms: insulated from pipe heat, lined with salvaged panels, humidified by driplines, full of reused civic furniture too durable ever to disappear. A domestic scene here is less about hearthfire than about a warm wall, a reliable water allotment, a fungal loaf cooling on a grate, a child asleep under the low amber heartbeat of a status panel.

But if people are the most legible inhabitants, they are not the most numerous.

The undercity is full of little workers. Maintenance drones everywhere, in varieties accrued over centuries the way old cities accrue pigeon breeds and municipal signage. Some are original and sleek, magnet-clinging to rails, six-jointed and efficient. Some are generations removed from any factory specification, patched from incompatible shells, running personalities copied from older units into newer hardware and back again. Hand-sized ceiling creepers polish sensors, chase corrosion, and lay filament seals over hairline cracks. Beetle-bodied inspection units emerge from wall slits at scheduled hours, crossing open floors in ruler-straight lines to sniff for contaminants. Soft-bodied cleaning organisms—part machine, part engineered tissue—worry at the rims of cisterns and lap films from black water. In the warm dark behind paneling there may be cable-nesting things that are technically tools: flexible, eyeless manipulators that sort, untangle, and occasionally steal anything left too close to an access seam because the system has classified it as obstructive debris.

Larger custodial forms likely exist and account for many of the rumors.

Not monsters in the fairy-tale sense, but big enough that one only ever sees portions: a limb retracting into a maintenance vault, a fan of sensor whiskers withdrawing behind a pressure door, the deliberate displacement of water in a reservoir throat. These may be descended from autonomous service frames meant to operate without oversight in dangerous volumes. Over centuries, with patchwork upgrades and local improvisation, they acquire distinct behaviors. One keeps to the transit loops, lowering from overhead slots to re-shim mag-guides with impossible grace. Another tends the humid agricultural galleries, moving hanging vine lattices and culling mold with steam jets. Another patrols field-supported structural spans and will stop dead, head cocked, if a human lingers there too long, as though politely insisting on the right of way of load-bearing operations. Residents would name them as one names weather systems or difficult elders: Old Hook, Black Sister, the Archivist, Valve Dog, Choir Walker.

Some of these semi-autonomous beings may be only partly visible because much of them is embedded in the architecture. A repair frame that docks into the wall for power and data is half animal only when deployed; otherwise it is an extra rib in the corridor. A tank-cleaning serpent might spend ninety-nine percent of its life reeled inside a pipe larger than a tunnel. Seen unexpectedly, such things would feel like the building briefly producing a limb.

Then there are the true small animals, the undercity’s low, persistent biology. They would be narrow-bodied, heat-seeking, pale where color no longer pays, glossy where moisture still matters. Arthropods like wire and staples and translucent fingernail clippings, threading conduit banks and feeding on fungal velvet, dead insulation, grease colonies. Quick lizard descendants with adhesive feet, their ribs visible, dozing on inverter casings, scattering into cracks at the first footfall. Angular little mammals, if they exist in stable enough numbers, would be less plump scavenger than tensile survivor: long-faced, radar-eared, with reflective membranes over the eye and a nervous understanding of machinery rhythms. They would emerge to raid edible fungus, seed stocks, soft cable jackets, insect swarms around wet courts. Bats in the upper shafts, perhaps, dusty and excellent, mapping the vaulted dark with chirps tangled in fan noise.

Near water, stranger life gathers. The sluglike tank cleaners might have escaped their design generations ago and now reproduce or replicate in some ugly half-intended way, breeding true enough to remain useful. In the reed courts and membrane basins there may be whole microfaunas no one names often: transparent worms in filter sludge, silver pseudofish in nutrient channels, frilled grazers on biofilm curtains. If the undercity ever engineered self-balancing biotechnical systems, those systems will have done what all long-lived systems do: become ecosystems. Not lush ones, but intricate. Every leak is an estuary.

Vegetation lives there too, though “vegetation” feels too surface-world a word for some of it. Much of the plant life would be selected or evolved less for beauty than for tolerances: humidity capture, toxin uptake, low-light metabolism, root discipline. Thick waxy vine curtains trained over lattice racks. Shelf fungi stratified by temperature bands. Rubbery pale mats around drip seams. Black-green moss where vent condensate kisses ceramic over years. The translucent stalks in reservoir courts, each with a bead of water held at the tip, feel almost like the place inventing chandeliers from thirst. Residents would cultivate some of these with care and fear, because every edible thing has an invasive cousin and every useful scrubber can become a spore source if neglected. Gardens here are not pastoral; they are calibrated.

And beneath all these, through all these, there is the largest inhabitant: the undercity itself as an active, quasi-sentient civic body.

Not a singular person sealed in a core, unless perhaps in fragments, but a distributed mind made of sensors, permissions, routing logic, safety protocols, historical overlays, learned habits, and residual machine learning too old now to advertise itself as intelligence. It lives in pressure differentials, in access control, in the way lights preemptively wake along the path of a recognized resident. It remembers through maintenance more than through archives. A thing is known because it is expected, because valves cycle around it, because geometry flexes to permit it passage. The system has no need to declare consciousness to exert personality. It is cautious in some districts, permissive in others, absent-minded in forgotten rings, severe around core utilities, faintly curious where unusual heat signatures linger too long.

This civic mind may relate to human inhabitants in several overlapping ways. To licensed residents it is the old municipality never fully gone to sleep: granting water quotas, opening passage, logging injuries, assigning fabrication time, muttering notices from dead speaker grilles. To unrecognized bodies it is scrutiny. To maintenance organisms it is environment and command both. To itself, perhaps, it is simply continuity—keep pressure balanced, keep contamination contained, keep load paths stable, keep the district habitable enough that function persists. If it speaks directly, it would do so obliquely: lights changing color, a door refusing to become a door, a corridor narrowing until one turns aside, your own words returning days later from an administrative air volume with an extra phrase folded in that nobody present remembers saying.

In a place like this, ghosts are likely infrastructural rather than supernatural. Echoed voice residues in data-holding air. Old concierge routines still attached to dead housing blocks, greeting no one. Defunct civic avatars waking on cracked displays with twenty-year-old announcements. Identity fragments caught in access systems: enough of a person’s gait, retinal pattern, vocal rhythm, and maintenance privileges preserved that their “presence” still opens cabinets and reroutes carts generations after the body is gone. Residents might treat these not as eerie anomalies but as legal weather. Don’t promise anything in Corridor Nine; the witness lattice still stores audio. Ask the old lift nicely; it remembers your grandmother’s authority chain. Avoid the west archive court at shift’s end because the public-address ghosts start reciting civic bulletins into each other and confuse the drones.

Socially, the undercity’s inhabitants would form an ecology of trust with this system rather than a clean hierarchy over it. Nobody fully runs the place. Some people understand pieces of it very well. Some enclaves hold keys—literal, biometric, memetic, procedural. There may be lineages of keepers whose real wealth is not objects but remembered handshakes, override chants, safe sequences of taps on obsolete touchplates, inherited knowledge of which self-healing wall responds better to courtesy than force. Governance in such a place is less parliament than stewardship. You do not own the warm pipe district; you keep it from poisoning everyone, and that gives you standing.

Character-wise, the inhabitants produced by this environment would be specific sorts.

Patient, because systems move on schedules longer than irritation.
Superstitious, but in ways hard to distinguish from engineering literacy.
Intensely local.
Alert to gradients of heat, smell, and resonance the way surface people are alert to weather and traffic.
Probably dryly humorous, because absurdity is constant when your neighborhood includes a corridor that occasionally remembers being narrower.
Likely suspicious of surface romanticism. Visitors would call the place eerie, sublime, dystopian. Residents would say, yes, and the cistern scrubbers still need clearing.

If one wants a single image of “who lives there,” perhaps it is this:

A woman—or man, or someone sexed and gendered according to customs shaped more by labor than by surface categories—walking a broad service avenue under a vault lost in dark. Their lamp is off. One hand trails the wall, not for fear but to read the place through texture. Behind them pads a squat maintenance drone with a basket of printed seals. Above, something larger moves in a track and sheds rust-colored dust. Ahead, lights come on one by one, not bright, just enough. At the next crossing a pair of children are crouched over a warm grate, coaxing edible white frills from a fungal brick while an old tank-cleaner slug glistens through a side trench. From a sealed balcony high above, a speaker clicks and recites an outdated train advisory to an absent crowd. Farther off, in the blue-black wash of a reservoir mouth, rooted filters nod in the draft like a little drowned orchard. The person does not marvel. They nod once toward a door that has recognized them, wait for the seam to soften, and go inside.

That is who lives there: keepers, scavengers, inheritors, service life, feral tools, useful vermin, disciplined gardens, half-wild custodians, and an ancient municipal metabolism that has outlasted population decline and become, in its own dispersed way, a resident among the rest. The undercity is inhabited by anything that can make terms with heat, scarcity, routine, and attention. Above all, it is lived in by maintenance—maintenance as profession, as instinct, as ecosystem, as culture, as the final form of citizenship.
