What lives there is something that has learned, over a very long span, to stop distinguishing sharply between organism, dwelling, and craft.

At first you expect the inhabitants to be austere—thin survivors, brittle ascetics adapted to scarcity. But the basin argues against that. Nothing in it is merely surviving. The growth is too various, the built forms too elaborated, the systems too tuned. So whatever lives there is likely not a desperate remnant but a patient specialist: a people, or a people-plant continuum, shaped by dryness so completely that abundance and restraint have become the same habit.

They are almost certainly quiet-bodied beings.

Not silent, but built for an acoustic world where every motion carries. In those narrow resonant streets, a heavy, clattering species would make the city unlivable. I imagine inhabitants who move with practiced economy, whose footsteps are soft on mineral dust, who close membranes instead of slamming panels, who speak in low tones because architecture amplifies them anyway. Their hearing may be excellent, less from romance than necessity: in a place without animal noise, changes in wind, vent pulse, seed-rattle, and structural creak would be information. They would know the basin by ear. A blocked condensate funnel, a cracked jar lining, a plant-grove entering stress, a shift in deep gas release—these could all announce themselves acoustically before they become visible.

Physically they need not be large. In fact, the city suggests medium or small stature: ladder-scale access, hand-built masonry, dense use of shaded understories and sunken courts, a world navigated close to surfaces rather than by broad monumental space. Their bodies may conserve rather than dissipate. Tight skin, perhaps waxed or dusted; eyes adapted to amber dimness rather than full glare; nostrils or breathing folds able to close against powdery winds. I would not picture sweaty mammalian heat-shedders. Sweat would be an absurd expense here. Better some other strategy: insulating garments, phases of activity, cooled burrows, broad vascular membranes that only expose at certain hours, or a blood chemistry that tolerates high internal temperatures before retreating to shade.

Their relationship to water would be intimate to the point of reverence, but not in the familiar fountain-and-cup sense. They would think in traces, not pools. They would handle condensate as one handles a rare pigment or medicine. A few drops would be infrastructural, ceremonial, and biological at once. Their containers would be absorbent, metered, capillary, never wasteful. They might not even drink in the way wetter peoples do. They may take moisture from gels, from plant tissue, from mineral membranes pressed to the mouth or skin, from slow inhaled vapors concentrated overnight. “Thirst” for them could be less an emergency pang than a set of maintenance rituals embedded in architecture.

Because the vegetation is so integrated with the city, I suspect the inhabitants are gardeners in the deepest possible sense—not ornamental cultivators but co-designers of a dry biome. They prune for airflow, braid living conduits across streets, harvest husks for matting and plaster fiber, train columnar groves as shade towers, and seed walls with creeping tessellations that stabilize masonry. The city would not be built first and then overgrown later. It would be laid out with growth in mind from foundation upward. Certain lanes would exist because a vent-garden needs a pressure corridor. Certain houses would be wrapped in mature frond-thatch generations older than the families inside. Certain public courts would be planted as thermal machines, not parks.

This suggests a people comfortable with slowness.

Not static—there is too much adjustment in the place for that—but accustomed to cycles longer than a human day and perhaps longer than a human lifetime. They would inherit systems already old: a wall whose best shade depends on a grove trained by grandparents; a membrane array that only reaches full efficiency after years of mineral accretion; a vent orchard that must not be overcut because it responds on decade scales. Their craftsmanship would therefore be conservative in the best sense: reluctant to destroy functioning age, eager to patch, layer, repurpose, and read weathering as instruction. They would not worship the new. In a basin where little vanishes, novelty would be less valuable than compatibility.

So their tools are simple because their intelligence is distributed into procedure.

Give such people basket, cord, blade, kiln, wedge, scaffold, and time, and they can make a city precisely because they do not ask one object to do all the work. They build by sequence, by repeated small acts, by understanding how wind, heat, plant growth, salt deposition, and human maintenance will continue the construction after the builders stop touching it. A wall is not finished when laid; it is finished when weather and trained growth have interpreted it into its mature state. An inhabitant of this basin would think that way instinctively. They would build for becoming.

What do they look like in dress and custom? Probably layered, filtered, wrapped. Clothing here is climate apparatus. Dry woven plant fiber, mineral-dusted cloth, segmented veils against reflective spores, sandals that insulate from hot paving, perhaps rigid or semi-rigid shoulder capes that cast personal shade. Faces may be partially covered in the open, not for modesty alone but to reclaim exhaled moisture and exclude caustic dust. Hands would be important tools—protected but dexterous. Belts and harnesses would carry hooks, ties, little cutting implements, seal-paste, folded membranes, perhaps packets of absorbent salts.

They may appear sparse at first because the city itself is sparse. But that emptiness may be deliberate spacing. In such an acoustic and thermal environment, crowding is a tax. So inhabitants are distributed in pulses through the basin: a worker high on a collection frame at dawn, two figures crossing a court with bundles of husk fiber, a cluster in a deep workshop at heat-peak, voices drifting from shaded interiors while the streets remain mostly empty. A stranger might think the city abandoned simply because its people have learned not to occupy exposed space unnecessarily.

Their daily life would be synchronized with openings and closings. Before the basin warms, they move through roofscapes and condensate rigs, collecting the reluctant bounty of the cool dim hours. As heat rises, they retreat inward to rooms with thick walls and planted screens. Midday is for tasks that can be done with little motion: sorting seeds or crystal scales, repairing woven membranes, grinding mineral additives, teaching children the sounds of vents and the names of growth forms. Later, as temperatures fall, there is another expansion outward: pruning, hauling, tuning chimes and pressure shutters, tending the gas-gardens, trading across courts, cooking if cooking is needed, perhaps communal work in the courtyards while the whole basin audibly changes state around them.

And because there are no animals, their imagination would develop in a peculiar direction.

They would not inherit the usual vocabulary of fluttering, stalking, hunting, herding, nesting birds, burrowing pests. Much of what other cultures learn from animal behavior they would instead learn from plants, minerals, and airflow. Patience would not be “like a spider waiting” but like a sealed frill waiting for dusk. Suddenness would be a popping pod, not a striking snake. Ancestry might be pictured as layers of husk and scaffold, not bloodlines of beasts. Their myths, if they have myths, would be botanical, aeolian, and geologic. Spirits might inhabit vents, pressure changes, old root-mats, salt faces, resonant cavities in walls. A child there might grow up recognizing a hundred plant postures and a hundred qualities of wind before ever conceiving of a creature that runs.

That absence of animals also changes diet and ethics. If they eat, and surely they do, then much of their food likely comes from plant tissues, stored compounds, mineral-assisted ferments, perhaps fungal or bacterial analogues hidden in jars and membranes. But even “eating plants” here would not look like plucking ripe fruit from abundance. It would be precise harvesting of thickened tissues, starchy piths, seed-scales, resinous storage organs, condensate-rich nodules, and slow-processed extracts from otherwise inedible leathery matter. Cookery would be extraction chemistry. Kitchens would be dry laboratories of heat, pressure, grinding, soaking in precious micro-quantities, and patient separation.

As for temperament, the basin seems to favor people who are attentive rather than expansive. They would be hard to surprise because they live amid constant small signals. They would likely seem formal to outsiders—not because they are cold, but because in a world ruled by limited resources and cumulative maintenance, manners are practical. You do not slam a door-mat, waste a shaded lane, tread carelessly through a vent garden, cut a mature conduit without reason, or talk loudly in a sleeping court. Courtesy would arise from ecology. Cleanliness, too, would be dry and particulate: brushing, sealing, dusting, sorting, not washing.

Yet they need not be severe. There is too much beauty in the basin to support a merely grim culture. Beauty here comes from refinement under constraint: ceramic chimes calibrated to crosswinds, patterned mats woven from old frond fiber, salt-polished thresholds, trained vines hung in mathematically graceful catenaries, mirror-flashes aimed into gloom, the careful patchwork of repaired plaster. Their art would probably be sonic, textural, and relational. Not lush pigments squandered on giant murals, perhaps, but subtleties of filtered light, tuned resonance, shadow geometry, seed-rattle arrangements, braided living trellises, mineral inlays that brighten only at a certain angle. They would make elegance out of necessity until necessity itself felt ceremonial.

It is also possible that “who lives there” is not entirely separable into plants on one side and people on the other.

The city hints at mutualism so deep that the inhabitants may physiologically depend on cultivated growth beyond ordinary agriculture. Perhaps some of the dry gardens regulate air quality for enclosed quarters. Perhaps the people sleep in rooms lined with specific membrane leaves that release breathable compounds or trap toxic ones. Perhaps their wastes feed mineral-reactive root mats; perhaps harvested plant salts are required for their metabolism; perhaps spores or volatile oils affect cognition, ritual, memory, or healing. An outsider might see gardens and architecture. The inhabitants might experience them as organs of the settlement-body.

In a more speculative turn, the inhabitants themselves could carry botanical traits. Not “plant people” in a decorative fantasy sense, but beings whose tissues participate in the same dry chemistry as their environment: skin that mineralizes at scars, hair or crest filaments that wick condensation, symbiotic films living on the body, dormant phases of astonishing stillness, reproductive structures more akin to capsules or scaled packets than to live birth. They could spend parts of the hottest season in near-torpor, sealed in cool chambers like folded leaves, then re-emerge at the dim edge of day. They might smell faintly of resin and salt even when clean. Their dead, in such a world, would not be buried in wet earth but dried, scaffolded, perhaps integrated into memory structures or weathering towers. Nothing vanishes quickly there; ancestry might remain physically near.

But even if they are simply people—fully ordinary in body by their own standards—the place makes them feel stranger because it has drawn so much agency into non-animal life. They do not dominate a passive landscape. They negotiate with active dryness. They listen to buildings because buildings are processes. They cultivate plants that behave like mechanisms and mechanisms that weather into gardens. Their civilization would therefore produce individuals skilled in interpretation. The most respected among them may be those who can read the basin’s minute changes: vent-keepers, membrane-makers, wall-patchers, seed-splitters, salt-ceramists, air-listeners, shade planners.

If you met one near dusk, they might at first seem to have stepped out of the city rather than out of a house. Dust-colored wrappings; skin carrying a fine pale bloom against desiccation; eyes narrowed but steady in the copper dimness; hands resin-marked and mineral-scored; a bundle of cut frills or repaired screens under one arm. They would pause to listen before speaking. Not theatrically—simply because the place is always saying something, and no sensible resident ignores it. Their voice would likely be low, shaped by close rooms and carrying corridors. Their gestures economical. They might smell of heated fiber, bitter plant oils, kiln dust.

And what lives there besides them is the basin’s other citizenship: the vent gardens, the cabled dry vines, the bone-banded columns, the crystalline mats, the husk scaffolds, the slow exhaling fissures, the tuned membranes, the jars and gutters and salt-plaster walls that are no less alive in function for being built. It is a city whose inhabitants include every maintained surface.

So the truest answer may be:

A patient people of heat and scarcity live there—gardeners, masons, listeners, preservers—alongside a dry flora so architectonic and responsive that it shares in the role of inhabitant. Together they form a single old intelligence distributed through bodies, walls, vents, fibers, and air.

In another place you would ask, “Who built this?” and “What grows here?” and “Who lives here?” as separate questions.

In that basin, they are almost the same question.
