What lives there is a people who learned very early that dominance is a wasteful idea.

Not because they are meek, exactly, nor frail, nor spiritually abstracted into some saintly harmony with their surroundings. They are practical to the marrow. But they come from a place where water does not forgive error, where stone outlasts intention, where every wall becomes eventually a ledge for something else, where noise means company rather than alarm, and where survival belongs less to the strongest individual than to whatever can enter an old equilibrium without breaking it. So the beings who inhabit this basin would be shaped above all by accommodation: to dimness, to dryness, to crowding by other species, to the long memory of masonry, to a city that is never finished and never truly ruined.

They are, I think, human or near enough that one first reads them as human from a distance. The architecture wants hands, shoulders, carried burdens, gathered families, crouching work. The streets suggest foot traffic and herd movement more than claws alone; the thresholds are for bodies that stoop slightly under loads; the repaired plaster, the trained trellises, the sorted middens all imply a mind that orders by repetition, not spectacle. But if they are human, they are the basin’s version of humanity, pressed over many generations into forms that make immediate sense here and perhaps slight strangeness elsewhere.

They would not be tall. Height is expensive in a low world. Long limbs catch on passageways and waste heat and force the body into the upper, brighter, drier strata of air. These people would be compact through the trunk, deep-chested for voices and lungs that work well in dust and echoing cold, with strong hips and thighs from ramped streets, terraced labor, and constant carrying. Their gait would be economical, balanced for slopes and uneven paving polished by centuries of use. They would move with the confidence of those who know the exact width of every turning and the habitual paths of animals through every court. There would be very little theatrical motion in them. No flinging gestures. No careless speed. In a crowded, resonant city, abruptness is both inefficient and rude.

Their skin, whatever its inherited range, would wear the basin visibly. Even the darkest complexions would carry a veil of ash-pale dust by noon; even the fairest would tend toward mineral tones rather than bloom. They might protect themselves less from sunlight, which is brief and slanting, than from desiccation. Oils, lime-cut unguents, resin salves: not cosmetics in the luxurious sense, but daily maintenance against cracking air. Lips, knuckles, heels, the delicate skin around the eyes—everything in such a place asks to be sealed. The people might smell faintly of dry herbs and mineral grease, the same way the walls smell faintly of old plaster and cool stone.

Their eyes would be noticeable. Not in the fantasy sense of glowing in darkness, but in the simpler way of any population long adapted to reading low contrast, reflected light, and movement in shadow. Heavy lids, perhaps; dark irises more common than pale; lashes dust-coated; a habit of stillness while looking. They would squint less than surface folk do, and blink more deliberately. In the roofed districts they might read whole social situations from small tonal shifts on a face that an outsider would miss. One imagines them pausing at thresholds, letting interiors reveal themselves rather than forcing entry with brightness or noise. Their visual culture would prize matte finishes, pale edges, contrast by texture rather than saturated color.

Their hearing would be as culturally important as sight, perhaps more. A people raised in a city that is never quiet would learn not to desire silence but to parse the total noise into significance. The morning wing-rush, the terrace herds beginning to move, the hollow crack of seed-pods, the whistle in a narrowed passage, the sudden cessation of one colony’s chatter, the scavenger argument at a midden wall, the different hoof-sounds of animals at ease and animals startled—these would all be legible. Their speech might therefore develop not toward loudness, despite the surrounding uproar, but toward shapes of sound that survive it: low-register vowels that carry through stone, sharp consonants for near instruction, coded whistles or clicks for passageways, drummed knocks on walls and lintels. Public life may be less a matter of shouting over the basin than of sending the right sound into it.

I suspect they are not conversational by surface standards. Not because they are cold, but because unnecessary speech has low value where the world is already making so much of it. They would save voice for coordination, ritual, humor, warning, recounting, bargaining. They would be very good listeners, though perhaps in a way outsiders find unnerving: listening not only to a speaker, but around them, through them, to what else the basin is saying at the same time. Their pauses would be full rather than empty. Children would learn early how to identify places where echoes lie and where words travel cleanly. Privacy, in such a city, might depend less on enclosure than on acoustical skill.

Clothing would continue the architecture by other means. Layered wraps, draped shawls, hooded dust-cloths, sleeveless work tunics under heavier outer folds, garments that can be loosened in open cuts of light and tightened in cold passages. Pale cloth would be common, not as pristine white but as the whole family of limestone, ash, faded reed, old bone, green-gray, salt, smoke. Dark fabrics too, especially for lower layers, but likely matte and dust-masking. Nothing glossy. Nothing trailing far enough to drag in salts or dung or seed husks. The people would carry cordage constantly: slings, nets, loops for baskets, ties for bundles of browse, repair-thread wound around wrists. Their dress would include pouches because storage is survival in a place of tiny economies. A passerby might seem to wear half a workshop on their person: knife, scraper, twine, seed packet, wax plug, resin pellet, little brush for dusting vents or lamp niches if lamps exist at all.

And they would ornament themselves, but with the same logic as everything else here—through materials that answer the place. Bone polished pale by age. Shell-like mineral inclusions from the plaster quarries. Seeds hardened and lacquered with resin. Feathers from common city birds, not rare trophies but familiar textures. Braids wrapped with thin strips of whitened leather. Scarification perhaps, if skin is seen enough indoors and in courtyards, though the dry climate would favor clean-healed marks. Hair might be kept bound more often than loose because of dust and labor, with styles signaling district, kin-group, trade, or role in ways subtle to outsiders and obvious to locals.

Their relationship to animals would be intimate without sentimentality. In a place where “wild” and “domestic” blur through every thoroughfare, the inhabitants would think less in those categories than in terms of tolerated, invited, useful, troublesome, sacred, seasonal, and kin-familiar. They would know which nesting species must not be disturbed because their guano feeds terrace soils, which scavengers can clean a yard without threatening infants, which burrowers undermine retaining walls if not diverted, which herd mothers favor which sleeping hollows, which reptiles eat the grain insects and therefore are left in peace along storage chambers. They would carry the city in their heads as a map of interdependent occupations.

That probably makes them seem patient and ecologically wise in a tidy modern sense. They are not tidy. No living city is. They would be full of grudges against particular species, district disputes over noise, endless arguments about droppings, crop theft, bitten children, collapsing trellises, territorial birds in market season, old rights of passage for herds through newly enclosed gardens, and whether some scavenger colony has become too bold around the dead. Their intelligence would be civic and negotiated, not idealized. They endure the abundance because abundance is wealth, labor, warning system, fertilizer, meat, pest control, omen, nuisance, and chorus all at once.

What they eat would shape them too. Dryland foods make particular bodies and habits. Seeds ground and steamed or baked on hot stone; leathery fruits from orchards coaxed in cool terraces; resinous leaves; eggs gathered from managed colonies; milk or curd from herd-beasts if those are kept close enough; insects in quantity, perhaps toasted, pounded, or made into meal; flesh as occasional commonness rather than exceptional luxury, depending on season and status. Their meals would not be wet in the lush way of river cultures. They would value chew, salt, oil, stored sweetness, souring, and the savor of things concentrated by dryness. They would know a hundred ways to make food seem less austere through texture.

Because the place changes by substitution rather than event, the people would think historically in a manner unlike chronicle cultures. They might not organize memory around reigns and battles so much as around alterations in use: when the north market became the swallow quarter, when the old west vault opened and made a new garden possible, when the white salts took the mural hall, when a herd-line shifted after a roof collapse, when a burial court became a seed store, when a certain retaining wall started its long lean. Their stories would be topographic and maintenance-bound. Ancestors would be remembered not only as blood, but as repairers of thresholds, trainers of groves, founders of roost agreements, discoverers of cool chambers, makers of plaster recipes that keep one more shade of light in a room.

This means their spirituality, if we call it that, would also cling to use and duration. They would not worship “nature” and “city” separately because the basin does not permit the distinction. Sacredness might gather around continuities: a species that has nested under one arch longer than anyone can remember; a court where every generation has left its dead for excarnation before bone collection; a root-tree whose shade is measurably colder than others; a seam of white mineral whose plaster makes rooms visibly brighter; a ventilation shaft said to carry the breath of the deep stone. Rituals could involve feeding, sweeping, sealing, opening, guiding herds through old paths, leaving ledges intentionally rough for nesters, sounding certain calls at dawn or dusk to synchronize movement. Maintenance would not be secular and ritual occasional; maintenance would be ritual’s most credible form.

They would probably bury little in the ordinary sense. The dryness argues otherwise. Bodies in such a climate do not vanish quickly, and a practical people in a crowded ecology would have developed intricate customs for the dead. Exposure in protected bone courts; careful stripping after natural desiccation; reuse of long bones in architecture or markers, perhaps with no horror attached; family ossuaries set into cool walls; memorial niches plastered over and reopened by generations. The dead would remain present materially, as everything else does. In such a city, one lives among old repairs and old bones with equal familiarity.

Socially, I imagine them organized by quarter and task more than by abstraction of state, though some larger civic coordination must exist if causeways, commons, herd movement, and storage systems are to persist. Their politics would be conservative in the literal sense: heavily concerned with keeping balances from tipping. Who clears the market trench after seed season. Which terrace receives the first browse rights in a lean year. Whether a new roof should be rebuilt or left open because the new shrub growth is proving useful. How much noise from one colony is tolerable before public sleep is impaired. How far scavengers may be trusted with waste before carrion birds become too numerous. These are not minor matters here; they are government.

Their leaders, if visible at all, would not look like conquerors. They would look like reliable custodians, interpreters of inherited arrangements, people whose authority comes from remembering precedent and managing compromise between humans, herds, birds, burrowers, gardens, salts, and stone. The most prestigious knowledge might belong not to warriors but to those who understand ventilation, plaster, seed storage, breeding cycles, migratory timing within the basin, structural creep, and the exact manners by which one species can be displaced without accidentally inviting three worse ones.

And yet they are not merely caretakers in a static museum-city. They would be humorous, desirous, gossipy, competitive, superstitious, flirtatious, and stubborn as any long-rooted urban people are. One can imagine children racing in ringed courts until a flock lifts in outrage overhead; adolescents climbing prohibited cornices to harvest eggs or impress each other; old women or old men sitting in door-recesses sorting edible insects from grit while adjudicating neighborhood memory; lovers finding the few acoustic shadows where words can be private; merchants in pale wraps trading dried leaves, plaster powders, tough fruits, polished bone, woven reed mats, salt cakes, and hatchling stock for domestic colonies. Their civilization would feel old enough to have accumulated jokes about the basin itself.

As for their faces, the place suggests faces marked more by endurance than by drama. Dust in the creases, wind-abraded skin, perhaps broad noses or at least noses valued for warming and filtering cool dry air, mouths shaped by habitual reserve and quick practical humor, brows often shaded by wrappings. They would age visibly but not flamboyantly: leathering rather than sagging, silvering hair catching the dim light, hands becoming rootlike. Elders would matter because patience is the chief local literacy. To know what a wall is doing over thirty years, or which species can be trusted after a roof-fall, or where dawn condensation darkens longest in a bad season—this is power.

If they are not strictly human, then the departures would still follow the basin rather than fantasy. Slightly larger eyes, perhaps. Ear structures tuned to direction in echoing spaces. Nictitating membranes or thick lashes against dust. Callused soles if much of the city is walked unshod indoors and sandaled outdoors. Skin less given to sweating heavily, more to conserving moisture. But I do not feel tusks or chitin or luminous membranes here. The place is too grounded in old civic animality for that. Its inhabitants should feel evolved by attrition, not designed for spectacle.

So what lives there?

A low-voiced, dust-pale, deep-chested people of terraces and thresholds. Keepers of a city that is half masonry and half habitat. Herb-scented, salt-fingered, wrapped in chalk and reed colors. They move aside for herds because the streets were always meant for herds. They leave niches for birds because the birds are part of morning. They judge wealth in stores, shade, trained growth, intact plaster, useful noise, surviving young, and obligations reliably met. They are surrounded by creatures and so do not imagine themselves alone at the center of things. They inhabit rather than possess.

And because the basin is so old, they themselves may seem almost like one more long adaptation of it: not the masters of an undercity, but one enduring species among many in a dry, resonant hollow where architecture has become climate and civic life has become ecology.
