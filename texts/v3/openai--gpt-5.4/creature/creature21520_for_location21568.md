What lives there is not a singular beast, nor even a population in the ordinary sense, but a civic organism broken into many quiet bodies.

At the most visible level, the inhabitants are those pale plated creatures glimpsed at thresholds and vents: low, careful, mineral-armored beings that seem assembled from the same vocabulary as the town itself. They are not dramatic animals. They do not stride, hunt, display, or proclaim their presence. They edge outward. They brace. They test the air with trailing fronds and place each felted foot with a surveyor’s precision. Their bodies seem designed less for speed than for correct contact: with stone, with dust, with membranes, with one another. They look half arthropod, half collapsed instrument, and half quarried artifact—too many halves, because they resist neat biological reading. The eye wants to find a face and cannot. It wants to decide whether the plates are shell, skin, or portable masonry and gets no satisfying answer.

They are probably nearest to what one would call citizens, but even that word misleads. Any one of them appears incomplete. Alone, one seems like a maintenance caste, a vent-tender, a scavenger of salts, a carrier of membranes, a sorter of husks. Seen in groups, arranged around an opening or distributed along a wall at exact intervals, they begin to read less as individuals than as temporary organs of a larger process. A cluster at a cold vent may be feeding, but just as plausibly sensing chemical changes, regulating flow, exchanging stored dusts, or taking instructions encoded in temperature and trace compounds. They gather where gradients are sharpest. They are connoisseurs of threshold conditions.

Their anatomy reflects the basin’s laws. Fluid would be a liability here, so they seem built to keep little of it near the surface. Their overlapping pale plates may be secreted mineral, grown in laminae from salts drawn out of the subsoil or harvested from nightly condensate. Fresh individuals might be slightly translucent at the joints, later whitening into the chalky finish of age. When they molt, they do not leave behind dramatic skins but neat partition-sheets, mica-thin cups, nested scale fragments, all of it useful. Almost nothing in the town is merely shed; it is sorted, stacked, re-employed. The regular drifts of translucent material under overhangs may be years of bodily surplus turned back into architecture.

Their senses are probably distributed. In a place where sound is sparse and sight is diffuse, they would not privilege a forward-facing head with paired eyes. Instead they may read the world through contact arrays along the flanks, thermal pits under the plates, fronded chemoreceptors tuned to dust composition, static, vapor traces, even stress lines traveling through stone under distant movement. One can imagine them pausing for long periods because to pause is not inactivity but perception at full resolution. A human standing still listens to silence; these creatures standing still may be listening to pressure drift, to membrane tension three walls away, to salts crystallizing in a chamber below.

They almost certainly communicate, but not by call. The settlement itself suggests the medium of their language: dust laid in deliberate trails, micro-scratches at doorway lips, warm or cooled patches on specific stones, timed opening of vents, the replacement of one membrane thickness with another. A phrase might be a pattern of granules redistributed across a sill. A warning might be a subtle change in chimney output. Kinship could be registered in matching mineral signatures on their plates, district affiliation in the particular horn-fiber laminate they carry, reproductive status in the chemistry of what they exhale at the vents. The town, in this sense, is not where they live. It is what they are saying all the time.

And there are likely several forms among them, perhaps not fully separate species but castes, ages, or condition-states.

Some would be the visible vent attenders: broad-plated, patient, tolerant of exposure, built to sit in open courts and regulate exchanges with the underground chambers. Others would be the narrow-bodied seam-runners, able to fold themselves through slit entrances and the finger-wide interstices beneath lifting flanges. These might be the ones responsible for opening and closing membranes, resurfacing paths, and carrying sorted dust to where it is needed. Deeper below, there may be softer-bodied forms almost never seen outside: larval or worker phases protected from desiccation, pale as raw fungus, doing the granular labor of shifting insulation beds, tending eggs or spores or some less familiar analogue. Perhaps the armored surface creatures are not the whole animal at all but one life stage of a being that spends most of its existence subterranean and communal, hardening only when it must enter the abrasive public air.

The reproductive life of the place may be inseparable from the architecture. The roofless courts could be brood-temperature regulators. The low towers may vent not only stale chemistry but reproductive dust. Those black lace fans on shaded walls may be symbiotic nurseries where microscopic young settle and harden. The regular pellets and nodules might be incubatory capsules moved from depth to surface and back again according to the basin’s minute thermal rhythms. New inhabitants may not be born in nests but precipitated at interfaces: in the seam between a warm cavity and night-cooled stone, under a membrane trembling with the right differential, inside worked pockets lined with old scales and filament mat. To such beings, construction and reproduction would be difficult to disentangle. To make a chamber would be to make a future body possible.

Yet the plated creatures are only the most legible inhabitants. More fundamental are the colonies that never emerge clearly enough to claim the eye.

Within the white stone itself there may live mineral-secreting consortia: films of microbial or quasi-lichenous life that digest trace chemistry from the basin dust and exude binding matrices, gradually turning loose sediment into coherent slab. These would be the masons beneath the masons, patient enough that a wall and a reef are the same kind of event. In dampless darkness below the streets, sheets of fibrous tissue may creep through cracks, drying into the horn-like sealants found between fitted blocks. What looks like mortarless construction might in fact be a disciplined biological adhesion, grown and then desiccated to hardness. The smoke-colored vent membranes almost insist on makers that are neither animal nor plant in familiar terms: organisms that can spin selective films, responsive to temperature and chemistry, then surrender those films to civic use.

The filament mats in the seams are inhabitants too, not background flora. Their jointed opalescent threads suggest a life specialized for the basin’s smallest gifts: molecular condensation, static deposition, airborne salts. They may be harvesters at the edge of viability, lifting and collapsing to comb the night air for traces of usable matter. When dust from a vent settles on them and they bend toward it, one sees not plant behavior as we know it but participation in a nutritional network. They could be public infrastructure as much as vegetation—district-level filters, catchments, perhaps even information surfaces that register chemical traffic from below. The black wall-lace, opening and folding over long cycles, may be another layer of this distributed metabolism: shaded specialists that darken just enough to exploit thermal contrast, or cryptic reproductive bodies that release spores only into the basin’s rare correct stillness.

Even the stalk-plants with bone-colored paddles feel less like independent flora than like negotiated installations. They stand as if permitted because they are useful. Their hooked surfaces collect dust, scales, fibers: raw material concentrated out of the air and made available for animals or colonies that visit them. Their slow rotation toward thermal gradients hints at a role in climate management, perhaps shading vents at specific hours, perhaps directing minute convection currents down into inhabited seams. In another world they would be scrub. Here they are towers in the municipal sense.

So what lives there, in the broadest answer, is a layered society of dry-adapted, mineral-bodied life forms whose distinction from their city is mostly a matter of scale and temporary mobility.

The town is their shellwork, lungwork, broodwork, archive, and speech.

Its apparent emptiness comes from our bias toward creatures that carry their lives around with them visibly. Here life has externalized itself into fitted slabs, regulated voids, sorted middens, kept-clear courts, and breathing seams. The inhabitants do not merely occupy structures; they deposit and inherit them the way a snail inherits shell chemistry, the way coral inherits a reef edge, the way termites inherit a mound’s airflow logic. Each generation may add only a few plates, a repaired membrane, a redirected vent, a new undercell beneath an old platform. Over time that becomes urbanism.

Psychologically—if one can risk the word—they feel vigilant, procedural, collective without being uniform. I do not imagine them as soulful solitaries wandering a ruin. I imagine them as participants in an exact etiquette of scarcity. Every emergence is timed; every path preserves a crust that must not be ruptured; every open area remains open because many small decisions keep it so. They may possess individuality in matters we would struggle to notice: plate pattern, preferred vent chemistry, district loyalty, sensitivity to certain thermal bands. But identity here would likely be relational and positional. A being is where it tends, what seam it knows, what membrane thickness it can judge by touch, what chamber lineage produced its plates.

If one stayed long enough, one might also discover predators or parasites, though probably in similarly restrained forms. A slight quickness under a debris drift. A thinner, glossier variant of the plated citizens that steals membranes or brood nodules. A dust-mite ecology almost invisible to the eye, feeding on the filament mats and in turn fed upon by the wall-lace. But even these would not alter the town’s overall impression, because the dominant life here is maintenance. Not conquest, not bloom: maintenance under severe conditions.

So the simplest true description is this:

A people of pale, many-braced, stone-shelled creatures lives there, along with seam-growing mats, black folding crusts, dust-catching stalks, and hidden mineral colonies beneath the streets. But more truly still, what lives there is a collective metabolism that has learned to take the form of a town. The inhabitants are the visible nodes of it—caretakers, secretors, sensors, menders, brood-bearers—while the walls, vents, membranes, and courts are its slower tissues.

In another climate, such life might have become forest or hive or reef.

In that basin, under that gray-violet sky, it became masonry.
