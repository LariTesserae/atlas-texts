What lives there is not a beast in the ordinary sense, nor a people merely adapted to a strange habitat. The first and deepest inhabitant is the place’s own appetite for becoming: a civilization of cold-growing life, and within it, perhaps, a human or near-human culture that has learned to live as a minor organ inside a vegetal continent.

If one asks more narrowly—what kind of creature would belong to such a realm?—then it seems to me that whatever lives there would have to share three loyalties with the land itself:

to light,
to structure,
and to patience under cold.

So the inhabitants begin, in imagination, as readers before they become makers. They would be weather-readers, draft-readers, growth-readers, collapse-readers. Not conquerors of the underworld, because this is not a place one conquers. It shifts too widely, too recently, too impersonally. The ones who live there endure by interpretation.

I do not think they would be large in the heroic fantasy sense. Against that scale, largeness is a liability. Better to be compact, sure-footed, layered against chill, capable of climbing, hanging, bracing, waiting. They might be human, or close enough that we would name them so at first glance, but the place would have edited them.

They would likely be tall only in the way reeds are tall: narrow, tensile, economical. Or perhaps not tall at all—perhaps slightly short, broad-chested, with excellent lungs and a low center of gravity for traversing root bridges in crosswinds. Their hands matter more than their faces. Long-fingered, strong in the grip, equally competent with stone knife, fiber cordage, and living vine. The thumb thickened, the fingers scarred with fine cuts from crystal-edged leaves and mineral bark. Soles tough, toes splayed or highly articulate from a culture that climbs as much as it walks. Knees and shoulders built for uneven load.

Their skin would tell stories of light that is abundant but never kind. Not sun-browned in the usual sense. More likely pale to olive to ash-gold, but weathered by cold brightness, wind-burnished, the exposed parts often reddened or silvered with salves. Eyes narrowed by glare rather than darkness. This is important: cave-dwellers are often imagined as creatures of blackness, but here the brightness would shape the face. Deep-set eyes, perhaps with a habitual squint; pale lashes crusted some mornings with frost; lids adapted to reflected mineral light. Maybe many wear veils, slitted hoods, or transparent membranes from treated plant skin to soften the white blaze from above.

Their hearing would be very good, but trained more than supernaturally acute. In a quiet world of exact sounds, survival belongs to those who can tell seedfall from pebblefall, branch-settling from fault-sigh, benign downdraft from shaft-opening gale. Children would learn those distinctions the way coastal children learn tides.

The people—if people they are—would be dressed less like hunters and more like itinerant gardeners crossing bad weather. Layered woven fibers, thick capes, wrapped leggings, hooded shoulder mantles beaded with condensation runoff. Their garments would not seek elegance of drape but cleverness of thermal and moisture management. Inner layers soft with brushed root-floss. Outer layers waxed, scaled, or tightly plaited to turn drip and spore dust. Cloaks broad enough to gather fallen pollen or line a sleeping hollow. Mitts that can peel back at the fingers for knotwork. Harnesses worn almost daily, because much of life involves tying oneself to something alive and load-bearing.

Color in their clothing might be mostly restrained—bone white, gray-green, fungal tan, black-root brown—except for marks of navigation and kin. In a world where mist, glare, and distance continually erase edges, vivid color becomes practical before it becomes decorative. So one can imagine cuffs braided with crimson bracts, ladders marked in blue spore dye, bright trail-tabs on children, signal panels of dyed fiber flashed from watch platforms. Beauty here would emerge from legibility.

Their settlements, as you suggest, would be small. That suggests a people suspicious of overcentralization not for ideology but for geological common sense. A great city invites a great death. Better a mesh of little anchorages: roost-villages in rootworks, basin hamlets by seep lines, temporary harvesting camps beneath pollen cliffs, shrines and weather towers on stable ledges. They would know many routes and few capitals. Their maps would be seasonal arguments rather than fixed diagrams. A path is not simply “there”; it is safe in hard frost, unsafe in thaw-drip, closed in spore season, reopened if the north downdraft weakens.

Such people would be archivists of transience. Their memory would be one of their principal technologies.

Not writing first, perhaps—though they may write—but memory carried socially: long recitations of where water forms after a west crack opens, which hanging gardens are old enough to trust, which pale sedge fields hide voids beneath rootfelt, where the blind pool-creatures gather thick enough to net, how many drip cycles a cistern wall has endured, what sound precedes the shearing of bark-ice curtains. Knowledge in such a world would not separate neatly into botany, meteorology, engineering, religion. It would all be one craft: staying in relationship with a living instability.

And because the flora dominate so absolutely, I suspect the inhabitants would not draw a hard line between wild growth and built structure. They would prune, graft, weight, redirect, sing to, score, and train the plants that support them. Architecture would often mean persuading a buttress-tree to make a wall, encouraging a vine curtain to thicken as a windbreak, trimming a root bridge rather than replacing it, feeding mineral slurry to a structural lichen to seal a crack. Their masons would be horticultural. Their gardeners would be engineers. Their elders would know which species may be invited close to a dwelling and which always eventually break foundations or lure too much frost.

This tends to produce a temperament. The people of such a place would probably appear reserved, but not from lack of feeling. Rather from living among scales that punish melodrama. If collapse is always possible, one learns to husband alarm. Their speech might be measured, with a large vocabulary for nuance in air and water. They may seem ceremonious because precision itself becomes a courtesy. One does not shout “storm” for every draft. One does not say “water” when one means “condensation likely by second bell under north lip.” To outsiders this could look austere. To them it would simply be honesty.

Yet there would also be tenderness, because this world rewards care. Seedlings at new light wells, cistern mosses, grafted ladders, salvaged cuttings from a fallen grove—these are intimate things. I can imagine households keeping not pets exactly, but favored growths: a warmth-heap culture, a resin bulb vine, a reliable drip fern near the sleeping place, a little shelf of medicinal frost lichen. Children would be assigned seedlings to watch over. Mourning might involve planting in newly opened scree. Family lines might be associated not with heraldic animals but with inherited cultivars, known routes, or old agreements with particular groves.

As for religion—if that word applies—it would likely center not on depth or darkness, but on openings, failures, and continuities. The sacred would not be “the cave” in a generic sense, but the moment stone yields light without immediately killing what receives it. A light well would be both blessing and threat. Collapse would have an ambivalent holiness. Certain fresh fractures might be taboo until tested through seasons. The oldest shrines might stand not at the safest places but at memorable negotiations between ruin and growth: where a chasm was bridged by roots; where a fog basin first became arable; where a deadly shaft later turned into the valley’s chief source of winter brightness.

I also think the inhabitants would be excellent with verticality. Rope, ladder, hook, sling, basket, pulley made of bone and hardwood, nets of astonishing reliability. Their children would grow up clipped to guide-lines. Their dead might be raised into root tangles or sealed in mineral banks depending on local custom. The distinction between road and wall would be less absolute than ours. There would be public routes that descend as much as they extend, and neighborhoods known not by adjacency on a flat plane but by shared anchor systems in the same giant growth.

Perhaps they herd something small and hardy—pale browsers, blind burrowers, slow woolly things that can digest the fibrous cold flora better than humans can. But even if they do, I doubt animal husbandry is their defining relationship. The realm belongs too decisively to plants. So these people would be foragers, cultivators, pruners, resin-tappers, spore-collectors, frost-watchers, salvage workers after collapses. They harvest not from a field opposed to wilderness, but from a continuum of managed wildness.

Their cuisine would reflect scarcity of heat and abundance of structure. Cold-preserved foods, fermented roots, sugar gels from amber bulbs, dried leafmeal, mineral salts, smoked goods only where venting allows, steeped infusions from bitter white herbs, fungal cakes grown in insulated chambers, occasional rich broths on communal fire days. Warm food would be an event rather than a default. Sweetness would be precious and luminous.

If they are physically distinct from surface humans over long adaptation, perhaps they have slightly enlarged rib cages, extraordinary balance, a strong tolerance for chill and low-level mineral exposure. Perhaps their hair is worn long and braided not for ornament alone but as insulation under hoods and as a place to tuck tools, tags, or scenting herbs. Perhaps they scent themselves lightly with resins that deter white insects or mask the sugars of harvested bulbs. Perhaps their teeth bear wear from mineral grit in food and air. Their bones might become dense from climbing and load-bearing.

But there may also be nonhuman neighbors, because a realm so large and sparse often suggests a few specialized creatures rather than many general ones. If so, they would feel less like a bustling fauna and more like occasional signatures in the margins:

small frost-grazers moving from grove to grove, pale-backed and narrow-snouted, clipping the softest growth;
slow membrane-winged herbivores drifting between high root shelves, part bat, part glider, feeding on spore veils and tender canopy shoots;
white, many-legged seep insects clustering so thick on mineral walls that they resemble mold until they move;
and in the black pools, translucent feeding cords and jawless mouths like folds in the water itself.

Above all there might be one emblematic animal, not because the ecosystem needs a mascot but because human imagination does: a tall, delicate browser built for open underchambers, something like an antelope crossed with a crane and a moth, pale-coated, long-eared, stepping over rootfelt on splayed hooves that distribute weight over hidden hollows. It would appear at tremendous distances, briefly silvered in a shaft of mineral light, and then be gone among the tree-pillars. The people might track it, perhaps even reverence it, because it knows the safe crossings before anyone else.

Still, even these creatures would not feel like the true sovereigns of the place.

The true sovereign is the vast composite life of the flora itself.

So another answer to your question is: what lives there is a forest that has crossed the line into infrastructure and almost into thought. Not literally a single mind, perhaps, but something distributed enough to blur metaphor. Root networks storing water, old growths steering wind, fused mats determining where animals and people may pass, reproductive seasons arriving as weather events—this is life acting at territorial scale. A person dwelling there might feel less like a resident of “nature” than like a tolerated cell in a continental body.

That would shape the culture profoundly. Humility, yes, but not submissiveness. More like negotiated belonging. They prune what threatens them, burn carefully where burning is possible, sever dangerous overgrowths, harvest sap and fiber and spores. Yet they likely do so with ritual caution because every intervention enters systems too large to fully predict. Cut the wrong curtain vine and a whole fog pattern changes. Open the wrong shaft and a beloved basin freezes. They would know that survival is not domination but calibration.

If one wished to render them in a single image, one could imagine this:

A line of travelers crossing a suspended root causeway over a white-lit void. They are hooded against the cold brightness, roped together, carrying baskets, bone hooks, pruning knives, and folded drip-cloths. Their garments shine here and there with frost. They pause not because they are afraid, though they are, but because one of them has noticed a change in the angle of hanging leaves ahead. A new draft. Somewhere beyond sight, stone has opened. They stand in silence, reading what the plants are already saying, deciding whether this is a danger, an opportunity, or both.

That feels like the right inhabitant.

Not troglodytes.
Not subterranean elves.
Not rugged pioneers in a green cave.

Rather a sparse, resilient culture of cold-bright horticultural wayfarers, living among monumental plant forms in a world still opening around them; people who build lightly, remember deeply, climb well, waste little, and treat every shaft of new light as both prophecy and test.
