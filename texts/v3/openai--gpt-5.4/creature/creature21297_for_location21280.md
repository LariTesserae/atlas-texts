What lives there is human, but not in the dryland sense first implied by the word. Not a people standing against that country, not settlers persisting despite it, not remnants waiting for some imagined return of fields and roads. Whatever lives in that drowned upland has been shaped so long by saturation, dimness, drift, and acoustic distance that it belongs to the flood as completely as the reed-beds and root-mats do. If you saw one at first in mist or rain, you might think less of a fisherman or marsh-dweller than of a careful adjustment the landscape has made toward self-awareness.

They are slight without seeming fragile, and broad in the hands, feet, and chest where this country demands breadth. Not heavy people, because weight is costly on floating things and in soft ground, but dense with tendon and practice. They learn balance before speed. Their legs are strong from bracing in unstable craft and on hidden causeways; their shoulders are built by poles, hauling root-cables, lifting wet things that are always heavier than they look. The cold has not made them thick so much as deliberate. They waste little motion. Even a child of the flood would move with the instinctive caution of someone who assumes every surface may shift, every edge may sink, every silence may conceal current.

Their skin tends toward the tones of a light-starved country: not necessarily pale, but rain-muted, wind-marked, often darkened in patches by tannins, peat stain, resin, and the permanent weathering of wet work. Their hands are usually the darkest part of them, fingers lined with ingrained black from soaked wood, mineral silt, and sealing pitches. Their faces are hard to read at a distance because they are so often hooded, half-veiled by dripping hair or woven rain-shields, and because expression in such a place economizes itself. A raised chin toward a hum-post, a pause in paddling, two fingers laid briefly against a tether-line to feel its vibration—these may carry more meaning than a louder people would put into a whole exchange.

Their eyes are not supernatural, but they would seem strange to outsiders because they are trained less for brightness and detail than for interval, stain, motion, and implication. They read the surface as others read a path. They are attentive to gradients of dark: the almost-black that means depth, the iron-black that means cold upwelling, the tea-brown blooming from a disturbed shelf, the pale drag under the surface where a causeway top is near enough to risk. They watch bubbles as if watching script. They know when ripples are made by wind, by submerged stone, by gas release, by a rope under strain, by a rooted mat beginning to shear loose. In fog they look less than they listen. Their heads turn often not toward sight but toward resonance. It would not be wrong to say that hearing, for them, has taken on some of the social and geographic work vision does elsewhere.

They dress in layers that never pretend to become fully dry. Clothing is made to shed, wick, cling, and continue functioning while soaked. Oiled reedcloth, tightly felted plant fiber, woven sedge, moss-padded wraps, resin-treated capes, leggings bound at the calf against leechless but grasping weed—though there are no animals to bite, there is endless wetness to infiltrate. Their garments are dark, not from any love of severity but because everything in that world ends by becoming rain-colored: brown-black, slate-green, peat-red, lichen-pale at seams and edges. They wear buoyancy without calling it armor: bladder-fiber vests, braided root belts, floating collars for children crossing rough reaches. Their cloaks often trail threads of green because no one there remains uncolonized by the place for long. A traveler returning from several days among outer basins might come home carrying on their hem the spores of three marshes, a silver algae sheen on one sleeve, and a fringe of pale bacterial velvet on a pole tip.

Their hair, when uncovered, is usually worn in ways that acknowledge water rather than resist it—close-braided, corded, looped high, or weighted so it will not blind them in wind. The old and those who spend much time near mineral springs may have hair with a faint discoloration to it, coppered or greened by water chemistry. Their teeth may be darkened by peat infusions and resin chewing. Their nails are often cut blunt, practical, sometimes stained with ink made from bog tannin and fungal black. Many of them bear shallow scar patterns not from combat with beasts—there are none—but from work: cuts from slate edges, rope burns from root-cables, punctures from splintered wet wood, old cold-cracks across the knuckles, smooth pale marks where fungal irritation once took hold and was burned or salted away.

But physical appearance is the least of what the place has made of them.

What truly lives there is a culture of calibration. They are people of measured pressure, tuned material, remembered routes, and disciplined attention. They do not divide the world as sharply into natural and built, because in that country a woven raft becomes a garden, a tree becomes an anchorage, a marker post becomes an instrument, and a house if neglected enters the plant economy almost at once. So they think in states of relation: moored, drifted, grounded, breathing, taking water, sounding true, gone soft, carrying weight, turning, loosening. A person might be described with the same practical metaphors as a platform or channel: reliable in crosswind, poor under sudden strain, deep-rooted, prone to drift in grief, humming wrong before storms.

They likely do not speak loudly unless weather forces it. Their language would be full of submerged distinctions impossible to translate cleanly. Not just “water” but water over stone, water over peat, trapped water, fed water, dead water, listening water, climbing water, roof-water, root-water. Not just “cold” but kinds of cold classified by how they move through the body: finger-cold, ankle-cold, chest-cold from fog, bone-cold from standing still on saturated timber, spring-cold that climbs through a pole into the palm. Their verbs would be rich where dryland languages often thin out: to tether against a hidden current, to test depth by sound, to read drift caught over a shelf, to make a thing temporarily dry, to train a living root into structure, to lower oneself through black water into known architecture without stirring silt.

They would build memory collectively because the land itself keeps changing while retaining pattern. No map fixed on bark or board could remain fully true for long. So the people who live there are likely great reciters of route conditions, weather stories, and anchored facts. Their lore is not heroic in the usual sense; it is topological, seasonal, and acoustic. The old are valued not because they preserve ancient glory but because they remember where a drowned ridge has begun to shed stone after thirty quiet years, which marker posts still sing in a useful interval after lightning split them, which floating gardens belong to whom by drift habit, where a ruin’s upper lintel lies close enough to strike a boat keel in low rain months. Their epics, if they have epics, may be chains of successful crossings, winters survived on failing platforms, migrations of entire households from one mooring-field to another, or the first training of certain trees into living bridgework.

Because animals are absent, they would be spared a whole category of fear and companionship that shapes most human societies. That absence would alter them deeply. They would have no inherited language of hunting skill, no shepherding, no herd-metaphors, no folklore of predators at the edge of camp, no quickening reflex to bird alarm or insect whine. Silence to them means something else. So does loneliness. In many human cultures, nonhuman creatures occupy the near background of consciousness; here that background is empty, and the emptiness sharpens intention. As your description suggests, geometry becomes conspicuous. A straight line means someone. A regular knot means someone. A trimmed branch, a paired stake, a rhythm tapped on hollow cane: someone. I think the people of this place would therefore be acutely literate in traces of will. They may be reserved not because they lack feeling, but because in such a world every deliberate sign carries unusual force.

This could make them a ceremonious people, though perhaps in muted ways. Their rituals would not need spectacle. They would rely on placement, repetition, sounding, and maintenance. Imagine mourning conducted by adjusting the moorings of a house that belonged to the dead, letting it drift one final measured distance before reanchoring it as part of another cluster. Imagine a coming-of-age not marked by blooding a hunt, but by crossing alone between three marker lines in heavy fog and returning by ear. Imagine courtship in the exchange of worked cords, in invitations to stand together on a tether-tower during a storm, in trust granted to another person’s reading of hidden ground. Imagine prayer as the tuning of posts, the cleaning of algae from resonant grooves, the lowering of votive lights into drowned halls where root curtains sway over old stone.

I do not think they would worship the flood as a god, exactly. It is too ordinary and too total for that. More likely they treat it as an encompassing condition with which one negotiates respectfully but unsentimentally, as mountain peoples may treat altitude or desert peoples dryness. Their sacredness, if present, would gather around thresholds where intention meets impersonal process: springs that preserve food and memory, certain ruins that remained sound through generations, ancient groves of layered half-drowned trees that support communities of rafts, or the humming channels whose tones shift before storms. They may have sanctuaries not because the landscape lacks grandeur, but because in a world so materially continuous one needs crafted distinctions in order to hold reverence. A platform above wave height, kept dry at great effort, could be more sacred than any vast view.

The people themselves may be somewhat amphibious in habit without being biologically transformed. They would spend much of life wet to the thigh, the waist, the chest. Children would learn breath control young. The elderly would know how to descend by touch into certain clear cold pockets to retrieve sunk goods. Some occupations might require near-constant immersion: tending underwater gardens of translucent fronds, cutting old rooted platforms free before storm season, exploring drowned halls for workable stone or fungal harvests, resetting anchor weights in crevices. Such people might wear weighted belts, eye veils of polished resin, nose-clips of carved wood, and line harnesses to guide them back in murk. Their bodies would bear the adaptation of repeated cold-water submergence: calm pulse, scarred sinuses, an almost uncanny tolerance for numbness.

And yet they need not all be solemn. A place can be harsh without draining wit from those within it. I suspect their humor would be dry in exactly inverse proportion to their world’s wetness. Deadpan, acoustic, situational. Jokes about drifting away while speaking, about houses that choose their own neighbors, about someone so inattentive they could lose a ladder to still water. Children would delight in false sounds made through marker grooves, in sneaking up along causeways under fog, in racing small woven floats through competing current tongues. There might be games of listening: identifying which post hums, which basin-note is sounding, whether a hidden shelf lies to left or right from the way rain stipples a slick. Their songs would not soar; they would carry low, repetitive, and far, meant to ride mist rather than conquer it. Perhaps they favor drones and call-and-response over melody because the country itself answers in that fashion.

As for how they dwell emotionally in a world with almost no animals, that is harder and stranger. Humans often pour attachment into living presences around them. Here, many of those instincts would have flowed elsewhere. Into plants, certainly—but not sentimentally, not as pets or mascots. More like kin categories based on usefulness, age, temperament under weather, and structural reliability. A particular layered tree-grove might be spoken of as stubborn, generous, treacherous in thaw, old enough to remember a different current path. A floating garden could have status almost like a household elder if it has carried generations and returned each year. Ruins too might gather personality. Not haunted, perhaps, but addressed. One does not easily spend a life among drowned halls and resonant sinkholes without granting some moral texture to enduring forms.

So who lives there?

People whose children learn routes before horizons.
People who can smell a spring in rain.
People who keep a coal alive through weeks of weather as if carrying a lineage in a bowl.
People who know five ways to make a platform hold and ten ways it can fail.
People who hear danger in a changed pitch before they see it.
People for whom dryness is not default but achievement.
People who leave little because little left unattended remains where it was.
People whose tools are half carpentry, half botany.
People who read buoyancy and rot the way others read tracks.
People to whom a straight row of stones in dark water is as eloquent as a face.

If you want a more singular image, perhaps the figure that best embodies the place is not a chief, fisher, or hermit, but a channel-keeper: a person in a low black sled-boat, moving at dawn through mist between nearly submerged rises, listening as carved posts hum one by one out of the gray. Their hood is slick with rain. Their pole descends and finds old stone exactly where memory said it would. Around them stand drowned groves with pale lichen-glow below the surface. Behind them, on a drifting platform half garden and half dwelling, a thread of smoke struggles under a rain hood. Ahead, somewhere invisible, a basin throat booms under pressure. The keeper pauses not because they are lost, but because they are reading the whole flooded country at once—the drift of leaves, the tilt of reedheads, the mineral stain widening from a cold vent, the warning note hidden in the weathered wood.

That is what lives there: not merely survivors, but readers of a submerged grammar. Human beings made precise by inundation, austere without emptiness, intimate with water’s patience, and so fully accustomed to a world without beasts that they themselves have become the only large moving intention in it. In such a place, that would make them seem both lonely and immense.
