What lives here is not one creature but a citizenship of creatures, with one species at the center only because it has taken responsibility for the rest.

The primary inhabitants are people shaped by maintenance. Not miners, not hermits, not the romantic poor of ruins, but keepers of continuities. They are the sort of people whose intelligence is distributed through the fingers, nose, shoulder, ear, and memory before it ever becomes abstraction. They live by noticing gradients: this wall is sweating more than usual; that draft has reversed; those birds are unsettled; this trough sounds hollow; the fungal shelves need thinning; the lower pens are too warm; a valve is beginning to fail because the pump’s complaint has changed key. Their world has taught them that survival is not won once but renewed hourly through small corrections.

They would be compact in habit whether or not they are compact in body. Underground life in a place so crowded makes every movement economical. They duck without thinking, turn sideways through partitions, climb ladders carrying impossible things, speak across levels, balance on narrow ledges, feel with their feet for familiar dips in steps. Their clothing is layered and practical, probably many-pocketed in effect if not in tailoring: aprons, wraps, sleeveless vests, tied sleeves, head cloths against dust, belts hung with hooks, twine, little knives, plugs, tags, scraps of leather, sealing wax, bone needles, chalk, feed scoops, lamp tools. Nothing about them is empty of use. Even ornament would likely be mnemonic—knots that signify a lineage of tasks, bead patterns denoting water rights, little charms that double as counters, polished shell pieces that catch shaft-light in the dim.

Their skin and hair would carry the place the way stone carries seep. A faint glaze of oil, soot, dust, lime, herb smoke, and animal musk would never quite wash away. Hands would be dark in the lines and shiny across the palms from wet rope, polished railings, clay jars, and feed sacks. They would smell, not unpleasantly, of occupancy: cooked grain, damp plaster, feather dust, wool grease, mushroom beds, old wood warmed by breath. Their voices would be tuned to acoustics—to when one must call sharply down a shaft, murmur in a sleeping alcove, click to move a lizard-hunting animal from underfoot, whistle pigeons inward, hush stock, or produce the deliberate courtesy-sound before entering shadow.

They are probably organized less by broad profession than by braided domains of stewardship. Water-keepers first, perhaps, or seep-masters as your description suggests: those who know the hidden arguments of walls, drains, settling floors, salt bloom, cistern moods, the places where a repair can be made and the places where one must instead accommodate. They would be conservative without being rigid, because in such a place reckless improvement is a form of vandalism. Then animal hands of many kinds: bird-keepers of the lofts, pen-runners of the lower chambers, fish-tank tenders, beetle women or boys who know the scrap bins intimately, lizard fosterers, rabbit or burrow-stock breeders, goat handlers with scarred shins and perfect balance on ramps. There would be fungus gardeners whose work looks humble and is in fact central; vent-tenders who understand thermal lanes like shepherds understand weather; compost sorters whose labor keeps filth from becoming death; pot patchers, reed weavers, plaster menders, wick makers, shell-stringers, feed mixers, bone scrapers, lamp counters, birth attendants, slaughterers, quiet old people who do little visible work and yet remember where an abandoned conduit still runs behind a wall no one has opened in three generations.

Children live here too, but not as decorative symbols of innocence. They are the species of human most quickly adapted to this environment, moving through it with unnerving fluency. They know shortcuts through crawl ducts, can tell one household’s pigeons from another, collect dropped feathers, steal warmth by sleeping near fermentation walls, get scolded for teasing the rope-climbers or reaching into snail bins, and learn early that darkness must be greeted and water channels respected. Their games would mimic management: sorting pebbles like eggs, making tiny pen systems from broken tiles, racing nutshells down legal runoff lines, daring each other to identify chambers by smell alone. By adolescence they would already possess a frightening amount of environmental literacy. One hears such a child say, without drama, “Don’t sleep there tonight, the wall is close,” or “Open the upper reed-screen, the moth room is breathing wrong.”

The elderly would not be marginal unless infirmity prevents climbing. In a place built from precedent, age is infrastructure. Old people would remember old leaks, old collapses, old breeding lines, old taboos whose reasons everyone else has forgotten. They might be physically narrowed by the architecture, bent like the corridors themselves, but socially they would be load-bearing. Their speech might contain vanished names for chambers now repurposed; they would still call a mushroom room by the name of the family that once kept doves there, or remember that a shrine niche was originally a drain inspection recess. They would know what not to disturb.

As for the nonhuman residents: they are numerous enough that the human community cannot be described apart from them. This is a multispecies settlement in the plainest, least sentimental sense.

There are birds everywhere, but not as wild ornament. Pigeons or pigeon-descended loft birds occupy the upper grids and shaft-edges, half managed and half self-willed, their feathers greasing the light. They are messengers of pressure change, meat, eggs, scavengers, status markers, weather vanes with hearts. Fowl bred to dimness scratch in enclosed runs and mutter under shelves; they may be pale-combed, broad-footed, less startled than surface chickens, adapted to close smells and human proximity. Swifts or bats in the hotter galleries occupy a tolerated margin between domestic and feral, welcome for insect control, resented for droppings, impossible to fully govern. Their overhead presence keeps the ceilings alive.

There are climbing mammals, small and intelligent enough to become personalities. Perhaps rope-running omnivores the size of stoats or civets or dwarf raccoons if the culture had such stock once and pared them down over generations; perhaps simply domesticated mustelids or tenacious little monkey-like creatures is too exotic for the tone, so better to imagine practical lineages: ferret-kin, hardy rat-catchers, basket thieves, creatures with excellent feet and shameless morals. Every dense built ecosystem seems to invite one such species—a tolerated delinquent that converts vermin into affection and annoyance.

Lower down are the penned herbivores and omnivores adapted to architecture. Goats, but shortened by breeding and custom into agile, platform-wise versions of themselves, animals that know ramps and ledges the way ordinary goats know hillsides. Rabbits or rabbit-analogs, perhaps larger-headed, calmer in dimness, selected not for cuteness but for feed conversion and tractability. Snails in bins, hugely important because they turn damp scraps into protein and calcium. Fish in tiled tanks, some surface species gone cave-pale around the gills from low light, others sturdy mud-colored things content in managed water. Eel-like cleaners in certain channels, not pets but colleagues. Frogs or toadlets near drain gardens if deliberately maintained for insects. Blind lizards in the walls, perhaps never fully owned, merely encouraged. Cats may exist, but if so they are probably lean, mannered, highly restricted creatures, because too much feline independence in such a food-dense environment would be war. More likely there are specialized ratting animals with stronger bonds to handlers.

And then the insects—absolutely essential, more numerous than any vertebrate, though people might speak of them by function rather than category. Hive-clickers in woven chambers. Glossy scrap beetles. Meal or feed larvae. Pollinator moths where herbs and creepers need them. Silk moths in hanging trays. Tiny midden workers in compost pits. Lice and mites as the constant enemy. The residents would know each by sound, sheen, smell, and usefulness. A healthy colony’s ticking at the wall might reassure them the way rainfall reassures farmers. In such a place, the line between livestock and process is thin.

One begins to feel that the true thing living here is not any individual organism at all, but an inherited metabolism. The district itself behaves like a body and its inhabitants are both organs and symbionts. Water enters, is slowed, clarified, rationed, breathed back out. Waste becomes feed, feed becomes dung, dung becomes fungus, fungus becomes supper. Heat migrates. Air is inhaled through shafts and exhaled through vents. Animals occupy trophic levels assigned as much by architecture as by nature. Human law intervenes not to suppress ecology but to keep it legible. The community is less a population inside a structure than the sentient part of a domesticated ecosystem.

So what kind of people would emerge from that? Likely patient, but not soft. Practical, but not spiritually empty. A little suspicious of open sky, perhaps, or at least unromantic about it. Surface people might strike them as wasteful, weather-exposed, careless with gradients, oddly comfortable with distance and emptiness. The underground folk would understand nearness better than horizon. Their metaphors would come from seepage, grafting, hatching, circulation, stored heat, pressure, and rot arrested at the right moment. They might think of kinship in terms of shared channels rather than blood alone—who draws from whose cistern, who inherited whose vents, who keeps the upper birds that enrich the lower beds that feed the mushroom shelves that supply the kitchen of another household.

Their religion, if present, would probably not worship wilderness but balance, continuity, thresholds. Shrines blackened by smoke suggest deities or ancestors of apertures, reservoirs, lucky repairs, successful births, spared collapses, clean drains, walls that held. Reverence here would attach to ordinary miracles: plain daylight in a shaft, a cistern surviving drought, fungus taking properly, a difficult kidding, a crack stabilizing after a tremor, the first condensation of a changing season. The sacred would be embedded in upkeep. To neglect a hinge might be a minor sin; to foul a water line, a moral obscenity.

Physically, over generations, they may or may not have changed much in any dramatic sense. One should resist making them pale troglodytes unless there is reason. They probably spend some time near shafts, on steps, on the surface at intervals. Better to imagine subtler adaptations: broad pupils, sure low-light vision, careful hearing, excellent balance in cluttered spaces, lungs accustomed to varied humidity, strong forearms, supple ankles, a culturally acquired rather than biologically fixed ease with enclosure. Their sense of direction would be topological, not compass-based. Ask them where something is and they might answer: “Past the warm pump, above the snail room, below the red vent, where the old wall leans.”

Emotionally they might carry a curious combination of intimacy and reserve. In a porous environment no one is ever entirely alone; therefore privacy is made from etiquette rather than architecture. People would be skilled at not noticing certain sounds from behind curtains, at announcing themselves before arriving, at understanding when silence means grief, anger, prayer, labor, or sleep. Because every household depends on the conduct of others—water use, waste sorting, breeding discipline, vent timing—the culture would prize reliability over display. Competence would be beautiful here. A person who can hear a bad pump leather from three chambers away, calm a panicked loft, and patch a sweating seam before dawn would be admired more than any swaggering hero.

And yet individuality would not vanish. In such a packed world, personalities become strongly textural. One keeper is known by the herb smoke on her clothes, another by the shell charms on his lamp hook, another by his dangerous fondness for taming half-wild drain creatures, another by the songs she uses to pace wheel-pumping. Some families prefer brighter alcoves, some breed darker pigeons, some are famous for clean troughs, some for mushroom broths, some for impossible repairs made with reed, glue, and patience. The district would be dense with reputations.

If you insist on giving the place a singular inhabitant—a representative life-form that expresses it best—I would say what lives there is a burrow-city people who have become ecologists without ever using the word. They are husbanders of edge conditions. They share their home with an armature of domesticated and tolerated fauna so extensive that “household” and “habitat” are the same thing. They do not merely endure the underground; they have taught built matter to behave like landscape, and in return the landscape has taught them to think like caretakers of a slow, crowded, breathing machine.

In the end, perhaps the most characteristic resident is someone very ordinary: a woman or man standing barefoot on worn tile in the cistern court at first light, one hand on a damp pump handle, listening before beginning the day. Listening to birds in the lofts, to water under slats, to beetles in their woven dark, to a goat stamping overhead, to the draft in the upper vent, to whether the district sounds healthy. Around that person, thousands of lesser lives stir in their appointed niches. Above, a plain circle of morning. Below, everything made, everything inhabited.

That is who lives there: a people of repairs, and all the creatures they have made room for.
