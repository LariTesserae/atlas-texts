What lives there is not one creature but a citizenship of adaptations.

If you try at first to name the place by a flagship animal, you get it wrong. There will be birds—of course there are birds, broad-winged scavengers riding thermals off the escarpments and hopping with ugly authority along parapets. There will be rats, if only because any human-built density invites some quick, bright, grasping intelligence into its margins. There will be things in the drains, things under the grates, things in the warm dark behind machinery. But the city resists being reduced to a mascot. Its true inhabitant is abundance shaped by wet mineral architecture.

Still, certain kinds of life emerge as especially native to it.

The smallest visible citizens are the film-eaters and grazers: slick-bodied, low-profile animals that move over damp walls and gutter tiles, rasping at mineral precipitates, bacterial sheens, soft chemical colonies, and the rich ambiguities that gather wherever warmth and moisture meet. They are probably not elegant. Flattened things, perhaps, with gripping mouths or combing denticles; pale where they live in shade, smoke-colored where they spend mornings on exposed stone. Their trails are not green bites in moss but clean swaths through gray jelly, sulfur bloom, and velvety exhaust-fuzz. They make the walls look licked.

Then come the runoff creatures, the blind channel-dwellers mentioned in passing but likely essential to the city’s metabolism. They feel old in the way cave animals feel old: not ancestral exactly, but overcommitted to a narrow truth. Sight has become unnecessary in the shallow ceramic rills, culverts, settling basins, and under-street streams. Instead they read current, vibration, chemistry. They nose through silt, consume rot, larvae, sloughed skin, offal washed from markets, and whatever else the city sheds into water before pumps or filters reclaim it. Their bodies might be soft and white as split root, or armored in translucent plates, or ribbed like hose. They are reluctant to retreat because the channels are theirs in the same way roads are ours: continuous pathways through the whole organism of the city.

On drier but still humid ground live the shell-backed files in the alleys: many-limbed scavengers with an industrial relationship to debris. Not insectile in the neat terrestrial sense, but convergent with all things that prosper by fitting themselves into cracks and letting abundance come to them in fragments. Their clicking against grit suggests hard segmented carapaces, perhaps layered to shed drips and dust; perhaps they fold over soft underbodies and can wedge into gaps under retaining walls by the dozens. They would be city janitors in the rudest possible sense, reducing carcasses, spoiled feed, dropped offal, shed nesting material, and one another. The place probably measures neglect by how many of them arrive.

Higher up, where warmth strikes concrete early, there are the sun-clustering huddles: thousands of small warm-brown or rust-colored animals pressed shoulder to shoulder on parapets, terraces, and broad utility housings. They might be furred, or scaled so finely they read as fur at distance. Their social body matters more than the individual. They pile for heat, erupt in coordinated waves when shadow passes, stream into cracks with liquid unanimity, and return to the same thermal surfaces day after day until stone is polished by the grease of generations. They are not picturesque. They are infrastructure users. The city makes warmth available; they occupy it.

The roofs and towers belong to another caste: gliders, circlers, and drop-feeders. In a place with no trees, height itself becomes canopy, cliff, and rookery. So the scavenging fliers would be built less like forest birds than like kites, vultures, gulls, and bats reimagined for a plateau of masonry and updraft. Broad wings for economical circling. Excellent scenting or visual detection of disturbance. Feet good enough for pipe runs, crane arms, statue heads, cable gantries. Harsh voices optimized for throwing across courtyards and down shafts. They feed on the inevitable dead, yes, but also on afterbirths from breeding colonies, market waste, stunned runoff creatures lifted from channels, and the weak among the rooftop huddles. They are not symbols of doom. They are sanitation with feathers or membrane.

Inside the city’s warmer organs live the nest-clusterers: duct breeders, beam hiders, loft clouders. These are the species that turn every suspended ceiling, cable tray, fan housing, abandoned hopper, and insulated pipe chase into maternity architecture. They probably come in several scales. Tiny tremulous things by the thousand, making dry papery nursery combs in places where no plant fiber exists, using hair, skin flakes, scavenged cloth, mineral fluff, bone dust, and secretions instead. Mid-sized opportunists that carry whole social lives inside the false interiors of buildings, emerging at shifts’ end to feed among the human spill. And larger, more territorial tenants whose ownership of a vent stack or water tower is respected because eviction would cost more than accommodation.

There are also burrowers in the embankments and spoil hills, and these may be the most visibly successful mammals if mammals exist there in anything like familiar form. A city without roots offers soil to little, but old fill, cinder slopes, slag heaps, weather-softened quarry edges, and the crumbly shoulders of roads all invite excavation. So you get colonies of robust diggers with mineral-stained coats and strong forelimbs, making whole apartment systems under switchyards and retaining terraces. Some are prey species, nervous and fecund, flushing in ripples from the shadow of a circler overhead. Others are omnivorous engineers whose tunnels alter drainage enough to concern municipal workers. Their mounds would not be loam but sorted gravel, ash, bone fragments, tile chips.

And among all this, inevitably, are the latch-learners.

These are the animals most people in the city would tell stories about: the ones with enough memory, dexterity, and social transmission to exploit human regularity. Not fully domesticated, not companions, not quite vermin. They know shift sirens, warehouse schedules, how long a loading door stays open before the second bell, which market drains carry edible wash after noon, which school roofs are warmest, which cistern-keepers are careless, which districts set out intentional feeding trays to draw them away from more vulnerable stores. They test hooks, undo simple catches, wait under the right windows at the right times, and teach their young where not to stand when freight lifts rise. Depending on body plan, they might be monkey-like, raccoon-like, corvid-like, mustelid, cephalopodic, or something with no Earth analogue at all. What matters is that they have crossed into culture. The city is in their minds as a map of opportunities.

There may even be large, almost invisible herbivore-equivalents—not plant eaters, since there are no plants, but bulk consumers of low-grade urban productivity. Thick-bodied wall-raspers, sump-strainers, filter-feeders in quarry ponds, heat-loving grazers that move at night over miles of retaining wall harvesting mineral-biological films too sparse to interest smaller specialists. One imagines them only occasionally because they keep to service margins and dim infrastructures, but the city’s trophic budget suggests something substantial must turn diffuse productivity into body mass. Perhaps whole municipal legends concern hearing them under bridge decks or finding broad wet feeding patches high on inaccessible facades by morning.

If the city has beloved creatures, they are probably not the clean or beautiful ones. They are the dependable ones: species whose habits can be predicted, whose breeding seasons can be negotiated, whose droppings signal weather shifts, whose sudden absence from a district warns of contamination, pump failure, gas leak, or structural trouble. People there would read animal distributions the way other cities read trees in blossom or migratory birds. “The white channel eels are crowding the upper grates.” “The tower-kites are circling over cistern nine.” “The red huddlers have abandoned the east-facing barracks wall.” Such observations would be practical knowledge, almost civic literacy.

And the humans?

Humans do live there, certainly, but they do not feel solitary in the way modern urban humans often do. They live as one articulate species within a broader crush of animal tenancy. Their houses are sealed, skirted, ridged, warmed, netted, drained, and still never entirely theirs. They are keepers of thresholds more than owners of interiors. Their habits have been shaped by the fact that anything unwatched becomes habitat. They store food overhead, wash surfaces not for polish but to reset scent trails, build cabinets that hang from ceilings, sleep with the ambient scrabble and murmur of neighboring species as an accepted civic weather. Children grow up identifying calls from echo alone. Sanitation workers, pump operators, freight crews, roof tenders, and animal-control guilds probably hold disproportionate status because they mediate not between civilization and wilderness but between one mode of occupation and another.

These people would be practical, damp-aware, and almost certainly unsentimental about life while also deeply entangled with it. They do not imagine a cleaner city beyond all fauna; that would be like imagining a drier sea. Instead they classify, divert, tolerate, harvest, appease, vaccinate, cull, trap, relocate, and occasionally revere. There are likely legal species and illegal species, protected nesting times, licensed scavenger harvests, municipal feed dumps to draw dangerous concentrations away from schools and pumping works, old taboos about sealing certain culverts because pressure below must vent somewhere and something always has first claim.

Some creatures are eaten. Some are milked, rendered, shorn, harvested for shell, hide, glue, enzyme, oil, or the peculiar bacterial cultures that grow best in their guts. Some are semi-domestic in the same messy way pigeons, street dogs, temple monkeys, or dock cats are semi-domestic: supported, cursed, indispensable. The city may maintain working species specifically because plants do not mediate waste streams there. Animal metabolism does what composting under leaves would do elsewhere. A slaughter district might feed a scavenger district; a tannery’s runoff might sustain blind canal feeders downstream; rooftop huddlers might be trapped seasonally for meat and hide. Nothing remains merely nuisance when pressure is old enough to become economy.

If there is a singular “who” that lives there in the poetic sense, it is a kind of reef-minded life. Life that thinks in ledges, thermal gradients, damp seams, echoing cavities, nutrient drips, protected overhangs, reliable schedules, and inherited routes through hard substrate. Life that does not need soil to feel at home. Life that treats architecture exactly as coral, shell, or volcanic rock would be treated in another world: as a surface to settle, tunnel, rasp, haunt, breed in, and pass over in innumerable generations.

The inhabitants, then, are colony-formers, scavengers, seep-drinkers, film-grazers, burrowers, latch-thieves, circling watchers, sump-things, loft-breeders, and the people who have become one more enduring species among them.

What lives there is whatever can turn wet stone into habitat.

And in that city, almost everything can.
