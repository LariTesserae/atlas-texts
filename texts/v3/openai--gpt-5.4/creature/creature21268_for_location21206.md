What lives there is not one thing but a people and a mode of living so entangled with stone, frost, seepage, and spatial uncertainty that they can hardly be described apart from the plateau itself. They are the kind of inhabitants who arise only where the world is difficult in a consistent way—where hardship is not episodic disaster but a permanent set of terms, and where the impossible has been present long enough to acquire etiquette.

At a distance they might be mistaken for another aspect of the architecture. They are sparse in the landscape, appearing only after patience: a figure crossing a high causeway with a burden pole across the shoulders; two or three silhouettes moving along a seam of green below a cliff; someone standing motionless beside a fog wall, as if listening to it. Their settlements are too large and too dispersed for bustle, so their life is perceived in traces before it is seen directly: smoke held low under a thermal overhang, shutters opening in a carved wall at dawn, wet laundry of woven reed-fiber stiffening in the cold, a sequence of stone lids turned back from cistern mouths, a line of newly cut moss set to dry on racks.

They are probably human, or near enough that the distinction matters less than the adaptation. But they do not feel like lowland humans transposed uphill. They seem selected by this place’s grammar—tall perhaps not in the heroic sense but in the elongated, layered way of people who wear climate around themselves; broad through the torso from breath-work in thin air; deliberate in gait, conserving motion on vast uneven routes. Their hands would be noticeable: cracked, strong, mineral-stained, deft with knotwork, sluice mechanisms, stone fitting, fungal cultivation, frost-sealed latches. Their faces would likely have the high-country economy of expression one sees where weather punishes unnecessary exposure: eyes narrowed against glare and wind, skin darkened or blanched according to local custom and heredity, cheekbones and brow often wrapped in strips of woven fiber or felt. Not because they are severe as personalities, but because the land has trained everyone into a practical reserve.

Their clothing would carry the whole ecology on it. Since there are no animals, “hide-like” things must be made from plants, fungus, woven bark, pulped fiber, and felted vegetal down. They wear layers with different functions: an inner softness to preserve heat, dense woven middle garments that hold shape when damp, and outer shells waxed with plant resins or fungal oils against sleet and freezing fog. Cloaks are probably common, but not romantic flowing ones—short, weighty, asymmetrical things that can be pinned over the windward shoulder and rolled into bedding. Hoods matter. Mitts can be tied back to free the fingers for intricate work. Boots are thick-soled, stitched from bark-fiber composites, wrapped at the ankle against meltwater, and marked with local treads for gravel, ice-rime, or uncertain ground. Nothing in their dress is decorative first and useful second; but because everything is handled with care, utility accumulates beauty anyway. Braids indicate basin or route allegiance. Chalk bands on staffs are both measures and memory aids. Pigments on hems may warn of a district’s conditions. Fastenings are carved with motifs that are perhaps family signs, perhaps hydrological maps, perhaps both.

They are not conquerors of the plateau. They are readers of it.

Their greatest skill is practical ontology: knowing what kind of real a thing is, today, in this weather, from this direction. They know which stair arrives where in thaw versus deep cold. They know that one cistern can be trusted by sound and another only by touch on the rim. They know that a wall humming with runoff behind it means a chamber beyond has shifted into condensation season. Their children are probably taught not geometry but sequence, not abstract orientation but experiential order: after the split lintel, before the white lichen face, never cross open paving when frost points uphill, always count seven drains before turning toward the vent-towers. In another culture such knowledge might be called superstition by outsiders. Here it is engineering, law, inheritance, and common sense all at once.

So the people who live there are keepers of recurrence. They maintain the arrangements that allow stability to persist where the world has become topologically temperamental. They replace warning disks before they fail. They repaint pigment lines after snowmelt. They clear channels not because flood is dramatic, but because one unattended overflow this year becomes structural betrayal six years later. They trim root-mats away from load-bearing joints and encourage moss over unstable scree. They seed impossible corners where steady condensation can be harvested by leaves. They brace towers whose upper stories only sometimes belong to the same season. They do not try to cure the weirdness. They garden it, shepherd it, negotiate leases with it.

This would shape temperament. Such people are unlikely to be impulsive in the lowland sense. But neither are they timid. They accept oddity with a calm that elsewhere would look uncanny. If a plaza repeats itself after an hour’s walk, one notes the weathering direction and chooses accordingly. If a room is larger within, one checks the moisture on the threshold and the echo underfoot before bringing in grain. If a field edge migrates by one impossible angle every spring, one alters the terrace wall. Their composure comes from long familiarity with a reality that is not broken enough to inspire panic, only variable enough to demand humility.

Because their world contains no animals, their emotional life may also be tuned differently. There are no herds to manage, no domestic companions, no hunting traditions, no constant triangulation with other moving species. This does not make them less alive; it may make them more attentive to vegetal and mineral tempos than most human societies have ever had reason to be. They notice leaf tension, spore release, thaw depth, stone resonance, the hour at which ice withdraws from north lintels, the scent of fungal bloom in shaded culverts. They read a valley by pollen drift the way sailors read currents. They listen to rootwater in walls. They may speak of groves almost socially—not as persons exactly, but as persistent local agencies with habits, thirsts, and moods. In the absence of beasts, weather itself has inherited some of the dramatic roles animals occupy elsewhere. A moving fogbank is not background. It is an event with approach, behavior, consequence.

I imagine they are quiet speakers. Not because of solemnity, though there may be much ritual solemnity, but because the landscape rewards close hearing and because sound carries strangely in cold open air. Their settlements would be full of low conversation, tapping codes, throat-clearing signals, stone-on-stone percussion used to test spaces, and perhaps songs built less around melody than around interval and echo. If there is music, it likely cooperates with architecture: flutes played into vent shafts, suspended lithophones, reeds tuned by condensation weight, humming galleries that turn a single sustained note into a map of hidden chambers. Some buildings may only be safely entered after sounding them. Thus acoustics becomes not art separated from life, but another way of touching the world to learn if it is answering properly.

Their social form is probably distributed, like their cities. Large populations may exist, but not in the dense visual sense. Communities are basin-based, fracture-valley-based, shelf-based: each one self-knowledgeable in water, fog, and route conditions, linked to others by roads and signal systems over immense intervals. Authority in such a place would likely belong less to warriors or merchants than to those who can preserve continuity: route-keepers, cistern wardens, terrace stewards, stonewrights, weather readers, fungi cultivators, memory-keepers of districts whose rules have changed. A respected elder might be someone who can tell, from mineral taste alone, whether a spring has begun feeding from the old western channels again. A civic office might consist of walking the warning lines after each freeze and listening at designated walls. There may be councils organized not by clan alone but by function of place: the people of vents, the people of overhang gardens, the keepers of thaw stairs, the watch of mirrored rooms.

Their religion, if religion is the word, would almost certainly emerge from maintenance rather than transcendence. In a landscape where impossible chambers still need cleaning and folded distances still require railings, the sacred would not stand apart from utility. Ritual could be the repeated correct action that keeps the negotiable world negotiable. Replacing the disk chimes, sounding the basin before crossing, waiting for the third melt, closing shutters before freezing rain: these are practical acts, but repeated over centuries they become liturgy. Their myths may not describe gods creating the plateau so much as old covenants with processes—Cold, Seepage, Settlement, Fold, Return. Water would be central, not as abundance but as intelligence: hidden, delayed, reappearing where law thins. They might regard springs as statements made by the world, cisterns as acts of listening, and roots as witnesses to agreements older than architecture.

Their agriculture tells even more about them. To farm in that place is to think like a leak, a frost lens, a lichen patch, a wall. They cultivate not broad easy fields but opportunities. Terrace lips, roofbeds, mist chambers, fracture seams, thermal margins, sinkhole rims, the overspray of sluices, the edges of internally weathering cisterns. They likely grow cold grains in sheltered bands, tubers in dark heat-trapping soils, waxy broadleaf marsh crops in insulated basins, medicinal fungi in old excavations, reed-fibers and bast plants for weaving, and orchard forms adapted to wall warmth and reflected light. Some crops may be ancient domesticates gone feral enough to survive neglect yet still subtly guided by pruning and seed selection. Their gardens would often seem half-wild to an outsider, because they are working with systems too large and strange for strict rows. The desired condition is not control but persistent yield.

What makes them most themselves, though, is that they have learned to build a civilization under conditions that would tempt most peoples into either mythic fear or technical arrogance, and they have chosen neither. They have accepted scale without demanding mastery over it. They have accepted impossibility without surrendering to mystification. Their roads are real roads, their walls real walls, their maps practical maps, but all are made with the understanding that place is locally negotiable and that endurance comes from revision. They live well not because they have solved the plateau, but because they continue to answer it.

There may also be another sense in which “who lives there” is not exhausted by the visible inhabitants.

The plateau itself lives there, in the older broader way landscapes sometimes seem to when human adaptation has become so exact that the distinction between resident and setting begins to blur. Moisture lives there as a slow hidden citizen, moving under shelves, entering cisterns, raising moss on old joints, teaching routes and redrawings. Frost lives there as a legislator, revising thresholds each dawn. Vegetation lives there with unusual agency, because in the absence of animals it occupies movement, succession, storage, and even warning. A creeper crossing a court over twenty years is almost a kind of migration. A reed-bed turning the wind visible is almost a public voice. A lichen face recording the seep line on a wall is almost an archive. If the people have become anything unusual over centuries, it is because they have lived among these non-animal presences long enough to treat them not metaphorically but socially: to account for them, defer to them, design with them.

So if you encountered one of the plateau’s inhabitants on a road between escarpments, what would strike you first would not be strangeness in their body so much as confidence in relation. They would walk as someone walks who knows exactly how much of the ground can be believed. They would pause before speaking, perhaps listening for some nearly inaudible condition in the stone. Their tools would look handmade and old in principle, but perfected through generations. Their face would contain weather and patience. If they offered guidance, it would not sound like directions elsewhere sound. It would be something like: keep to the right after the wall begins to sweat; do not trust the second shadow in the long court; if fog forms before noon, wait by the vent tower until you hear water on the left; the green stair is safe unless the pollen is moving downhill.

And to them, this would be ordinary hospitality.

In short: what lives there is a dispersed high-cold civilization of stonewrights, water-listeners, terrace keepers, and route-memorizers—people made intimate by vastness, practical under ontological strain, clothed in plant-work, sustained by hidden hydrology, and culturally shaped by a world in which the laws of place are weathered rather than absolute. They are neither ruined nor primitive nor mystical in the simplistic sense. They are mature inhabitants of an old plateau that has become stable around its anomalies, and they have become, in turn, a species of calm negotiator between architecture, frost, vegetation, and the folded edges of reality.
