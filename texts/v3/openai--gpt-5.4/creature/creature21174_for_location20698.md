What lives there is not one thing, though the city keeps trying to make its inhabitants countable.

At first you might say: workers live there. Foundry crews, surveyors, tram operators, condensate collectors, line inspectors, slaughterers, archivists, mechanics, nurses, topologists with tired eyes, roof-herders, cable riggers, bell-tuners, animal wardens, map-printers, boiler tenders, children who can judge fog by taste. And this is true. The human life of the place would be enough, in another city, to define it. The people who belong to such a place would be compact in posture and patient in temperament, not because they are uniformly stoic but because impatience is useless on slopes, in thin air, under mutable geometry. They would have strong calves, careful hands, layered clothing polished by damp, pockets full of clips and cord and hooks and folded notices. They would speak in routes rather than distances. They would trust routines but not layouts. They would know which walls seep warm water, which stairwell echoes wrong in winter, which dogs are better than instruments, which bells mean closure, crossing, drift, herd, shift, revision.

But if you stop there, you miss the more interesting answer.

What lives there is an entire culture of things that survive by accepting the unfinished.

Not merely people adapted to a difficult mountain, but minds adapted to a world that has not fully chosen consistency. A resident of that city is, above all, someone who can live without demanding that every edge stay where it was yesterday. That temperament would shape the body as much as labor does. These would be people less startled than most by ambiguity, but not romantic about it. They would not worship uncertainty; they would maintain through it. They would treat reality the way harbor cities treat tides or desert towns treat wells: as a variable to be monitored, respected, and worked with. Their deepest civic virtue would not be bravery. It would be upkeep.

So whoever lives there is practical to the point of ritual.

Children would be raised on little procedures the way others are raised on prayers. Touch the rail before the turn. Listen for the second echo. Mark the doorframe in chalk if you are staying long. Check the window count. Tie off in upper fog. Offer the cliff-herds space if they lower their heads. Do not look into pooled water too long before work. If the old women on the stair say the block has shifted, believe them before you believe a printed notice. If the carrion birds go quiet all at once, take another route home.

This does not make them fearful. It makes them exact.

I think the people there would have a reputation elsewhere for being dour, but in place they would be full of a dry, situational humor—the humor of those who live amid chronic inconvenience grand enough to become cosmology. Jokes about revised addresses. Complaints that a cousin moved away without moving. Casual remarks that the north wall is in one of its moods. A whole category of flirtation based on walking someone home by the route least likely to alter. Courtship gifts of good cord, brass clips, annotated maps, polished sticks for driving off grazers, cuttings from particularly resilient balcony vines. Love, there, would contain a lot of maintenance: adjusting a brace, reheating soup after a transit delay caused by nocturnal wings over the power lines, waiting together while a herd passes through the laundry district.

They would probably be a beautiful people, though not in a delicate way. Mountain beauty, industrial beauty, damp-weather beauty: broad-shouldered, thick-wristed, wind-burnished, soot-marked, hair forever trying to hold mist, skin used to cold metal and warm pipework. Faces lined early by squinting into luminous gloom. Eyes trained to pick out minute discrepancies in contour, sheen, or sound. They would move with the balance of those raised on stairs and suspended walkways. Even their fatigue would be specific: not the collapse of overwork alone, but the constant low expenditure of orientation. To live there is to navigate not just elevation, but negotiation with the possible.

Yet even this is only the human layer.

Because the city is also inhabited by creatures that have learned the same lesson in other forms.

The cliff-herds live there, certainly, and in a way they may be more native than the stone itself. They move over surfaces not fully stabilized as if born from the principle of provisional footing. Their split hooves are not just equipment for climbing; they are instruments of reading. Each step tests what has set and what still remembers motion. They browse the margins where civic planning and vegetal opportunism overlap, and by doing so they participate in maintenance too, however unwillingly: trimming embankments, scattering seed, loosening gravel that warns of fresh heave. Their routes are old enough to interrupt traffic and authoritative enough to be obeyed. If one wanted to be mythic about it, one could say the city borrows from them its knowledge of how to proceed across near-vertical uncertainty.

The scavenger birds live there as a second intelligence, aerial, municipal, priestly, vulgar. They know heat, death, leakage, and updraft. They occupy the interfaces between refuse and omen, sanitation and sign. In another place they would be only birds. Here, because so much of life depends on reading patterns in what should be ignored, they acquire advisory force. Their circling over a slaughter yard is practical. Their refusal to roost in a familiar vent may not be. The line between behavior and warning is thin in such a city, and everyone learns to live on that line without quite admitting it.

The blind amphibians in the reservoirs are a purer form of local adaptation: pale custodians of hidden systems, creatures bred or introduced for function and then absorbed into a more complicated belonging. They thrive in the collected hesitation of the city—in seep, runoff, condensate, pipe-dark, tank-dark, the cold breathing undersides of infrastructure. They are almost symbolic in their suitability. Of course a city that drinks from sweat and fog would raise its own white keepers in cisterns.

The rust-colored dock scavengers, the warm-catwalk ribbon things, the rooftop grazers, the mesh-rattling flocks: all of them live there by exploiting the exact spaces where machine and weather fail to segregate. They are not intrusions into the built environment. They are consequences of it. The city makes niches the way a cliff makes ledges. Heat leak, cable run, grain spill, structural shade, mineral damp, sheltered draft—these are its ecological vocabulary. What lives there is whatever can read that vocabulary fluently.

And then there is the harder answer.

Something else lives there too. Not metaphorically, not necessarily as a monster, but as an ongoing agency distributed through the unfinished matter of the place.

The description keeps hinting at it from several angles: the city is built on a mountain still becoming mountain; geometry is provisional; reflections are disobedient; distance and adjacency are negotiable; fresh stone seems less fully committed to existence than older landscapes are. Taken together, these suggest that the place is inhabited by a kind of settling—not just geologic, but ontological. As if the local bedrock has not entirely passed from possibility into fact. As if the mountain is alive in the weak but consequential sense that yeast is alive, weather is alive, a bureaucracy is alive: not as a single consciousness with plans, but as a persistent process that reacts, adjusts, thickens, and learns repetition.

So perhaps what lives there is a young law.

Not youth in years, but youth in certainty. A formative principle still discovering what “street,” “wall,” “distance,” “inside,” and “later” are supposed to mean when burdened with freight, rain channels, apartments, rail timetables, and hoof traffic. This would explain why the city feels collaborative rather than antagonistic. The place is not haunted by a finished alien will. It is being co-authored by habits still hardening. The mountain has not rejected human order; it is studying it. Every retaining wall, every corrected map, every repinned brace, every repeated commute is a lesson impressed into half-set reality: this is where the corridor goes, this is how far apart buildings should remain, this is what downhill means, this is the acceptable number of landings in a stair.

In that sense, the oldest and strangest resident is the city’s own process of becoming.

And the people who live there know this, even if they would never phrase it so abstractly while scraping moss from a cistern lip or waiting for a funicular. Their customs amount to pedagogy directed at the world. Maintenance is instruction. Repetition is persuasion. Surveying is a conversation. Bells, color bands, acoustic markers, line reels, revised charts—these are not only safeguards for human confusion; they are ways of teaching unstable space to remember itself.

That may be why the place is not panicked. One does not panic at a child, however immense, merely for being unfinished. One watches it closely. One keeps dangerous objects out of reach. One reinforces the stairs. One accepts that growth will alter the house. One corrects, guides, endures. The mountain-city is a nursery for form, and its residents are, knowingly or not, its caretakers.

If I had to describe the characteristic inhabitant in a single image, I would resist making it a person standing heroically against a vast landscape. That is the wrong genre. The true figure is smaller and more specific: someone in a heavy coat at dawn, pausing on a damp landing with a lunch tin in one hand and a line reel clipped at the hip, waiting for a herd to pass while checking whether the opposite building still owns all the windows it had yesterday. Not afraid. Not amazed. Simply attentive. Behind them a wall sweats. Above them something winged shifts in the fog. Under their boots the stair thinks quietly about whether to remain itself. And the person waits, counts, listens, and goes on.

So who lives there?

People who have made peace with provisional reality.
Animals that can read infrastructure as habitat.
Municipal systems so elaborate they verge on instinct.
And beneath them all, within the damp black stone and its slowly correcting errors, a half-formed local physics learning endurance from those who refuse to leave.

It is a city inhabited by labor, by scavengers, by herds, by moss, by condensation, by caution, by wit, by strong legs and revised maps.

And by the unfinishedness itself, which is perhaps the oldest citizen and the newest.
