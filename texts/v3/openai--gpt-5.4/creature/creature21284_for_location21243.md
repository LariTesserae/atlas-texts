What lives there is not one thing but a citizenship of appetites.

At first it seems the place belongs to the small, the sessile, the innumerable—to all the creatures that can turn waiting into a way of life. The nearest equivalent to householders are the fixed-bodied builders: tube-worms in crowded chimneys, mussels mortared into draperies and spans, sponges full of internal corridors, colonial animals that make a wall by each becoming one room of it. They are the masons, landlords, tenement blocks, insulation, public works. They do not merely occupy the chamber; they give it its legibility. A rib of limestone becomes a colonnade because enough soft mouths agree to cling between its uprights. A seep becomes a district because filterers, grazers, brooders, and scavengers all discover that a little warmth and chemistry can be divided many ways. These lives are patient, adhesive, and almost architectural in temperament. They thicken edges. They seal drafts. They turn accident into address.

Among them live the minute citizens of constant labor: shrimp with glassy limbs, amphipods, copepods, larvae, worms fine as threads, tiny snails crossing one another with bureaucratic persistence. If the larger structures are buildings, these are the traffic, errands, and maintenance. They queue at warm cracks, strip films from stone, pick through detritus, harvest what settles, become what drifts, vanish into a thousand apertures and emerge elsewhere carrying energy in speck-sized installments. Many of them are nearly transparent, as though the city prefers its workers not to block the little light that exists. They flicker rather than travel. They appear in densities more than in individuals, and the chamber seems to think with them: every shift in current instantly translated into a rearrangement of tiny bodies.

There are also the watchful soft-bodied ones, creatures made for thresholds. Anemones inhabit windows and mouths of flow, each one a guarded opening fringed with tenderness and hazard. Feathered filter-fans bloom and close by the hundreds, giving whole neighborhoods the rhythm of breathing. Flatworms slide over parapets like spilled color. Tunicates sit in communal clusters, each body a door with two purposes: taking in and giving back. Brittle stars thread themselves through balconies of shell and sponge so that only their arms show, a sign language of hunger protruding from the dark. Sea slugs, if they are here, would look less like visitors than like thoughts the walls are having—brief, ornate, and entirely committed to contact chemistry.

The city’s middle stories belong to opportunists and specialists in flow. Small fish hold themselves at intersections where currents divide and recombine, not resting exactly, not moving exactly, but reading the water as if it were both road and weather report. They are the clerks of the crossroads, the flocking minds that know which alley is feeding today and which has gone dead. Some species would be mirror-sided and quick, turning an entire lane bright for an instant before becoming indistinguishable from dusk again. Others would be blunt-headed, lantern-eyed, hovering nose-first into the stream as if contemplating a difficult text. Cleaner fish and cleaner shrimp likely keep stations at certain arches and chimney-bases where larger residents present gills, mouths, and skin under a local armistice. Those precincts would have all the feeling of shrines or clinics: places where danger is briefly bracketed by mutual need.

Then there are the creatures that make the chamber feel governed rather than merely crowded.

White crabs in sponge-shadow, for one: deliberate, pale as chips of old shell, each occupying a recess with the self-importance of a magistrate. They are not fast but they are certain. They know the geometry under every overhang, which passages can be defended by one body-width, which dead fish is worth contest, which clutch of eggs belongs to a neighbor too dangerous to offend. Their claws are not just weapons but zoning tools. With them live squat lobsters or something like them—ornamented little custodians of heat and crevice, keeping to the mineral-rich places where the stone sweats useful chemistry.

The eels are something more complicated. They are among the nearest things this city has to police, thieves, ghosts, and old families all at once. In such a chamber they would not present as dramatic attackers but as continuities of motion: a head appearing in one window while the tail still remembers another district; a body passing through the city’s grammar rather than interrupting it. They know conduits no broader than a wrist, vertical shafts hidden behind shellwork, the private plumbing behind the visible facades. When they hunt, the effect is administrative. A school parts. Shrimp erase themselves. Some lane becomes unusable for a while. The eel does not conquer territory; it audits it. Yet it belongs completely, because its body is exactly suited to architecture that is all seam and loophole.

Larger fish also pass through, but not all are residents in the same sense. Some are commuters from the wider drowned dark beyond the slit entrances—visitors from the inland sea who come to feed at the margins, to be cleaned, to spawn in safer cul-de-sacs, or to raid nurseries when the current favors them. Their arrival would be felt long before fully seen: a tightening among the smaller schools, feeding fans furling in sequence, the whole chamber developing a subtle lean in one direction. A predator here is not necessarily monstrous. It may be simply a body large enough to have social consequences. One pike-shaped shadow gliding between mineral spires can change the occupancy of twenty doorways.

In the lower gutters and colder pockets live the blind or near-blind things, the ones less interested in light than in gradients: soft scavengers, pallid worms, isopods armored like dropped machinery, skinless-looking fish with sensory faces, cusk-like lurkers that prefer ceilings to floors. These are the undertakers and cellarers. They inherit what the brighter districts lose. Because decomposition is slow but inescapably communal here, such creatures are indispensable. They read death not as event but as lease transfer. A body caught in a narrow lane becomes, in order, meal, scaffold, mineral trap, neighborhood extension. The creatures of the lower hollows are those patient enough to stay for every phase.

And under, within, and over all of them are the bacterial nations, which may be the truest natives of the chamber. They silver the seams, bruise them violet, frost warm seeps with color so fine it only appears when the light catches sideways. Some oxidize the minerals exhaled from the tiny chimneys, making food from chemistry where no green thing could be asked to prosper. Some coat roots descending from above, translating foreign organic matter into local economy. Some are the first settlers on any fresh crust, the velvet foundation upon which larger claims will later be staked. If the visible animals are the city’s crowds, these films are its deep law: deciding where a surface becomes edible, where a crack becomes attractive, where a vent becomes worth overbuilding. Whole neighborhoods may effectively belong to microbes, with larger residents merely grazing or farming the conditions they create.

The roots themselves host another category of life—hangers, clingers, grazers of imported worlds. Pale rootlets descending from the marsh or drowned forest above would carry fungal threads, bacterial fuzz, perhaps tiny worms and snails adapted to that softer substrate. Along them may move root-shrimp, midge larvae if any survive submergence, or minute crustaceans treating each hanging strand as a suspended road. The roots are like foreign embassies from the world above: slim, fleshy, not fully assimilated, bearing rare sugars and occasional debris from a very different kingdom of life. Anything able to browse them or shelter among them occupies a border culture within the chamber.

As for who most embodies the place—who feels less like one species than like the chamber imagining an inhabitant for itself—it may be a fish, but not a grand one.

Perhaps a small, elongated fish the color of mineral dusk, with a blunt translucent face and fins adapted not for open-water speed but for exact hovering in braided current. It would know every branch of the chamber, every hour of tide-breath, every warm leak and cold spill. It would sleep in a habitual arch among familiar neighbors, present itself at cleaner stations without fear, steal larvae when it could, retreat upward when dense cold pooled in the lower bowl, and return after. It would lay eggs not in exposed nests but on the undersides of shell terraces or in the swaying lee of hanging roots, where the current combed them clean. It would not build, exactly, but its repeated preferences would help determine what gets built around it. A thousand generations of such fish choosing the same ledges, the same pause-points, the same spawning alcoves would slowly make those choices structural. The city would remember them in shape.

Or perhaps the emblematic resident is not fish at all but a colonial tube-worm tower crowned with feeding plumes and riddled with tenants. In that case the true inhabitant is a composite life: the worm that secreted the tube, the bacteria that feed it, the shrimp sheltering among the tubes, the snails grazing the film on their outer walls, the eggs pasted in crevices between them, the crab under the base, the eel using the whole chimney as cover, the dead predecessors cemented into the lower rings. One address, many species, one metabolism braided from many hungers. That feels faithful to the chamber too. Nothing there lives alone except briefly, and even solitude usually turns out to be scaffolding.

So whoever lives there is communal in the strictest biological sense. Not harmonious, not utopian, but densely contingent. They are creatures that understand passage, seep, ledge, plume, and pause. They are animals of occupancy rather than conquest. Most are small enough to fit the city’s scale and numerous enough to make vacancy impossible. Many are translucent, pallid, reflective, or faintly self-luminous, as if shaped by a world where light is rare and every signal must be modest. They are cold-bodied and exact. They spend little energy wastefully. They breed into current. They eat what comes. They become each other’s walls.

And if one asks for a singular answer—for the one who lives there—the truest response may be: succession lives there. Accretion lives there. The resident is the ongoing conversion of crack into colony, colony into structure, structure into ruin, ruin into substrate, substrate into city again. All the chamber’s inhabitants are expressions of that single civic species: life that does not need tools to urbanize a room of stone, only time, chemistry, and the refusal to leave an opening empty.
