What lives here is hard to separate from where it lives, because the oldest resident of the plateau is not any single species but a habit of life: a civilization-scale ecology that long ago stopped drawing firm lines between plant, animal, machine, weatherwork, and citizen.

If you insist on beginning with a central inhabitant, then the true denizen of this place is the city-vegetation itself: the rooted, plated, tensile, electrochemical biota that has occupied nearly every exposed surface and turned desiccation into an organizing principle. It is not “flora” in any simple sense. It behaves at times like lichen, at times like coral, at times like circuitry, architecture, gut, memory, public works, and law. Some of it lies flat in ceramic fronds and black metabolic skins; some rises in translucent vascular columns full of stored charge; some stretches as bridge-tissue between mesas; some braids itself into retaining walls and habitation shells; some hangs in the sky as collector organisms vast enough to alter the acoustics of weather. There are individual growths, certainly, but the impression is of an upland superorganism partitioned into districts, utilities, gardens, markets, archives, and wild margins. It drinks almost nothing. It eats atmosphere, light, stone, cold, electricity, dust, chemistry, and time.

It is old in the way reefs are old, or forests, or languages. Not ancient because one body has persisted unchanged, but because generation after generation has thickened the same intentions. The oldest towers lean into vanished winds. Civic channels have changed purpose without losing their form. Sleeves of newer tissue reinforce tissues that once reinforced earlier ones. A wall may contain six historical periods of growth and still be alive all the way through. A plaza may also be a processor. A balcony may also be a pollination organ. A doorway may close and, over a century, become a nursery knot for some sanctioned or unsanctioned species. The resident intelligence of the place—whether distributed computation, inherited patterning, symbiotic governance, or something no longer divisible into those categories—is conservative in the deepest ecological sense: it wastes nothing, forgets selectively, and repurposes almost everything.

Within and upon that living infrastructure live innumerable animals, and they have the look of creatures shaped by scarcity so successfully that they give an impression not of deprivation but of exact fit. They are lean, articulate, quick-folding, and wary of unnecessary mass. Many seem assembled from useful angles: hook feet for gripping bark-hard membranes, scraping mouths for mineral crusts, sails for traversing gulfs of dry air, insulated bellies for warm conductive perches, electroreceptive whiskers or combs to read field-lines in fogged dimness. Their abundance is distributed rather than crowded. You notice them by recurrence: a sudden skitter into a crack, a glider’s snapping landing, a ring of grazers abrading a nutrient skin, a perched hunter dropping like released hardware, soft traffic beneath the plazas where burrowers harvest castoff sacs and aerate the dead root foam.

Many of the animals are half-citizens in practice whether anyone uses that word for them or not. The city depends on guilds. Pollinators carry not just gametes but coded materials, district signals, perhaps legal markers. Burrowers maintain underlayers even when they are stealing. Scavengers clear storm-fall. Herds crop excess plating and keep surfaces metabolically active. Courier beasts, with living saddles and throat pouches, move messages, embryos, tools, cultivated spores—whatever can survive a warm, guarded trip through thin air. Some neighborhoods are grown to attract them, feeding them with nectaries, heat vents, perching combs, or seasonal pheromone blooms. Others repel them with bitterness, subsonic disorientation, static fuzz, or legal exclusion written directly into tissue chemistry. The result is an urban ecology in which wildlife is not outside the system. It is labor, kinship, nuisance, infrastructure, and weather-sense all at once.

The aerial fauna are perhaps the most characteristic, because the sky here is not background but resource field, road network, hunting ground, and electrical text. Things with conductive wing rods or skin sails drift ridge to ridge, harvesting blooms charged by daylight and dust. Crackling listeners navigate by the city’s electromagnetic architecture. Some creatures seem to fly by reading tension gradients in the air around the collectors. Others trail pollen storms and spore highways the way pelagic animals follow currents. There are likely predators that hunt not by sight but by the sudden absence of electrical chatter, by the shape a frightened field makes when lesser creatures freeze. There are likely carrion specialists evolved for the aftermath of ion storms, combing the base of harvest towers for stunned sky-life and carbonized insects. In such a place even a scavenger must understand weather as a form of harvest schedule.

Below the obvious traffic is another residency: the microscopic and symbiotic populations without which nothing above would hold. Mineral-cracking bacteria or alien analogues inhabit root chambers and porous stone, digesting what geology offers too slowly. Condensate collectors host films of specialist life adapted to live in nearly imaginary water budgets. Smart lichens monitor stress in old structural tissues. Warm black mats run low humming metabolisms on stored charge and buried gradients. Pressure bladders and chemical reservoirs are guarded because they are not merely plant organs; they are public utilities, dowries, emergency stores, and attractive theft targets. If the city has memory in any literal sense, part of that memory surely lives in these small residents: in stress patterns retained in tissue, in symbiont populations tuned to former climates, in deep biochemical archives that tell a district when to open, harden, shed, bloom, retract, or call the storm inward.

And then there are the people.

One hesitates, because to describe them too quickly as “human” or “humanoid” would flatten the evidence of the place. Whatever the people are, they are a species—or a culture complex—habituated to intimacy with living systems so complete that architecture itself appears domesticated biology and public administration resembles horticulture crossed with electrical engineering and ritual law. Their bodies are implied by the city: pathways scaled for repeated foot traffic, entrances at practical reach, gathering spaces tuned for voices, market districts designed around exchange by hand, claw, tool, or mouth. They are social enough to build plazas, courts, quarantine gardens, and outlooks. They are technical enough to cultivate weather-harvesting towers, distance-folding pollen routes, legal frequencies in substrate hum, and materials grown for storm prediction. They are patient enough to think in maintenance generations. They do not seem to have conquered this ecology so much as entered into a binding contract with it many ages ago and never stopped renegotiating the terms.

I imagine them as sparely built compared to lowland peoples, with coverings and equipment designed for abrasive air, static, cold, and mineral dust. Their clothing would likely layer insulating felt, fungal leather, woven conductive fibers, and living accessories that monitor charge or chemistry. They may wear scents as permits, district affiliations, or temporary root-access rights. Their tools are probably less “metal objects” than graft-compatible devices: tuning forks for substrate hum, pruning arcs, coded seed capsules, membrane patches, portable field dampers, call instruments for courier fauna. Their households might inherit cultivar-lines as other societies inherit land deeds. Their politics might involve stewardship obligations to animal corridors, water-analog reservoirs of captured condensate, or rights to the output of a tower’s seasonal discharge. A good engineer here may also be a grafter, mediator, veterinarian, and liturgist of storms.

Their temperaments, if one can infer such things from urban form, are likely shaped by exact accounting and layered reciprocity. In a place where every reserve is precious and every surface alive, wastefulness would feel obscene. So would simple domination, because pull too hard on one category—nutrition, traffic, reproduction, defense—and the whole woven system answers back. These residents probably think in terms of thresholds, permissions, timings, and coexistence with active infrastructure. They would have a subtle understanding of neighborhood personalities: which plazas digest quickly, which bridge-veins dislike heavy synchronized traffic, which old district remembers a prior climate and blooms early, which tower becomes erratic before an ion event, which scavenger flocks are safe to feed and which indicate disease in the underlayers. Their children would grow up not merely learning street names but learning what moods a street can have.

They may not all belong to one species. The place feels hospitable to assemblages: lineages modified for different labor ecologies, symbiotic castes, co-citizen animal intelligences, or visiting peoples adapted to lower and wetter lands who experience this plateau as half metropolis, half sacred machine. There may be districts where the residents are rooted for part of the year, or where dormancy is a legal and social practice, or where some inhabitants offload memory into civic tissues during the storm season and retrieve it later. Given the negotiation with local physics, it would not be surprising if personhood here extends to long-lived towers, district minds, seed banks, or weather organs whose decisions materially affect communal life. One can easily imagine lawsuits or liturgies involving a bridge that changed its tension profile and thereby redirected trade, or a storm-calling crown accused of favoring one mesa’s harvest over another’s.

What definitely lives here is attentiveness.

The city and its creatures are all readers of faint things: trace volatiles, minute charge differences, shadow shifts, migratory pressure changes, the approach of auroral weather, the chemistry of ownership, the acoustic signatures of safe footing or structural stress. Nothing can afford dullness. Even the stationary life is sensorily extravagant. Frond edges taste the air. Vanes orient to gradients almost too small to name. Blossoms carry charge as signal and reward. Tower crowns track realities “approximate” to local physics. Animals listen with skin. People probably listen with instruments and cultivated reflexes until their own habits approach those of the place. In such an environment intelligence is not merely in brains. It is in membranes, roots, traffic patterns, legal customs, storm shutters, migratory traditions, and the timing of markets to pollen drift.

If one wanted a single image of the inhabitants, perhaps it would be this: not a lone figure, but a dusk crossing on one of the living spans. Beneath your feet the bridge pulses with load-sense light. Along the rail, cilia test the wind. Overhead, gliders angle home in crackling skeins. In the pores of the bridge skin, mites and microflora tend films of harvested dust. Below, a herd moves through luminous plating while hidden burrowers revise the understructure. Ahead, a cluster of citizens in layered conductive cloaks pause to let a courier animal pass, its throat pouch glowing faintly with coded seedglass. Farther off, a tower leans into a dead wind from ten millennia ago and opens its crown to a storm not yet visible. Everything on that bridge is alive, and almost everything is also infrastructure.

So who lives there?

An old dry people, perhaps several.
Lean beasts that graze ceramics and read electricity.
Gliders, scrapers, burrowers, perch-hunters, scavengers of lightning.
Symbionts in stone.
Memories in roots.
Markets in blossom.
Laws in scent and subsonic resonance.
Architecture that can heal.
Gardens that compute.
Weather organs that vote.
A city that is also an ecosystem, and an ecosystem that long ago learned citizenship.

Above all, what lives there is a mastery of constraint so complete it feels, from outside, almost like serenity. Not because the place is gentle. Because everything in it has had time to become exactly equal to the difficulty of being there.
