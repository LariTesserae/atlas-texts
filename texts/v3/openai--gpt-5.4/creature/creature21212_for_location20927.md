What lives there is not one thing but a manner of living made flesh over very great time.

If there are people in that country—and there are, or have been so continuously that absence is only a phase of being overlooked—they are creatures of exact placement. Not conquerors of the plateau, not even shepherds in the ordinary sense, but readers of it: an old high folk whose chief adaptation is not strength or speed but calibration. They live the way the local animals live, by knowing where reality is committed and where it is merely habitual.

At first one imagines them as gaunt ascetics, all bone and weathering, because the land encourages that mistake. But they are not starved-looking. They are finished-looking, in the way the cliffs are finished: spare, settled, with nothing extraneous left to carry. Their bodies would tend toward wiry compactness rather than height, because height is costly where air is thin and warmth does not linger. Wide chests, perhaps, though not massively so; rather, ribcages that seem to open high toward the collarbones, as if built to take in the least available atmosphere without hurry. Their limbs would be dry and corded. Hands broad in the palm, with strong finger joints, made for gripping stone, sorting crystals, and feeling slight differences in surface. Feet probably remarkable—splayed, articulate, accustomed to ledges and inclines, as much tools as supports.

Their skin would carry the country visibly. Not simply dark or pale, but mineralized by living there: tones of ash, umber, salt-beige, iron-brown, skin that in youth may have been softer in color but over years acquires a matte finish from cold, altitude, dust, and reflected light. The lips crack and heal thin. The eyes are the most striking thing—not because they are mystical, but because anyone who survives there must become a virtuoso of distance. They would have the fixed, unblinking patience of animals that watch ledges for hours. Iris colors might skew pale by adaptation or custom—gray, amber, washed green, black so deep it reads as polished stone. Many would narrow their gaze not from suspicion but from habit: light, clarity, and the need to notice very small motions far away.

Their hair would be treated as fiber before ornament. Kept braided, bound close, woven with shed filament from cliff-herds, with reed wire, with strips of softened hide, with cords crusted in salt. In old age it would not merely gray but chalk. Some might shave portions of the scalp to reduce vermin and to feel cold currents more clearly; others might let it mat into segmented ropes that resemble the rootlets of the bone-trees. Body hair perhaps little valued except where it can be braided into cord or tucked into offerings. Nothing wasted, but also nothing fetishized. Utility, then continuity, then beauty arising from both.

Their clothing would not flutter. This matters. In a country where the atmosphere leans but seldom gusts, where animals attend to silhouette and interruption, garments that flap would seem childish or alarming. So they would dress in layers that hug and articulate: wrapped tunics of felted herd-filament, sleeveless overjackets of thin cured hide stiffened with salts, leggings cross-bound at calf and thigh, shoulder capes composed of many narrow strips that can settle independently over the body without billowing. Outer surfaces likely dust-colored, rock-colored, lichen-silver; inner linings unexpectedly rich where only the wearer sees them—sulfate yellow, oxide red, a green like tarnished copper. Cold is managed by trapping stillness, not by bulk. Ritual dress might involve shell-domes sewn in sparse patterns, or polished mineral plates that catch the preserved light peculiar to certain basins.

They would smell faintly of stone dust, bitter resin, old wool, and the clean ammoniac edge of nesting places. Smoke would be rare on them if fire is rare in practice. More likely the scents of heated rock, dried lichen fuel, crushed medicinal crusts, the wax of bone-tree fans, and perhaps the strange metallic emptiness one picks up from spending too long near thin places in the world.

But what most defines them is tempo.

They would move with the local law: nothing quickly unless necessity is overwhelming. Not from laziness, not from ceremony for its own sake, but because haste in such a place is both wasteful and epistemologically dangerous. A person who moves too fast fails to notice which shadow is attached, which path extends farther than it should, which ledge is already occupied in more than one layer of time. Their gait would be economical and strangely even, as if each step had been pre-approved by the ground. They pause often, but the pauses are part of locomotion, not interruptions of it. To outsiders this might read as uncanny composure. In truth it is perception taking its due.

Their speech, if overheard, would likely be sparse and low. Not because they are solemn, though they may be, but because a loud voice would seem disproportionate to the acoustic scale of the plateau. Language there would be built for precision of placement: many terms for shelves, seams, crusts, exposures, inherited tracks, kinds of cold, classes of shadow, degrees of firmness in seemingly identical stone. Also for temporal adjacency. They would need grammar capable of saying not only “was” and “is,” but “persists beside,” “returns in place,” “never fully departed,” “entered the old pattern,” “visible from before.” Their myths, if they have myths, would not separate geology from ancestry. A ridge and a lineage might be spoken of with nearly the same verbs.

Silence among them would not indicate estrangement. It would be a medium of joint attention, just as the cliff-herds communicate by freezing together. One can imagine small groups standing on a lookout, all facing outward, and then one person inclining the head by a degree so slight you almost miss it, after which the others alter course. Instruction may often consist of correction so minimal it scarcely seems communicative. Children would be taught routes bodily: heel here, hand here, wait through this brightness, do not cast a shadow over that lip, never answer an echo from that cut, if the pebbles take too long then step backward without turning.

Their settlements would be less villages than recurring permissions to remain. A few improved hollows, low walls, storage crevices, wind-lean shelters, egg niches, lookout tripods, all distributed according to fixed affordances in the land. Population density might be greater than it appears because dwelling does not always require visual congregation. Families could occupy the same escarpment at different ledges, or the same ledge at different temporal depths, meeting only in certain hours or seasons when local reality aligns more generously. This would make them hard to count and harder to eradicate. It would also shape kinship strangely: descent traced not only through blood but through path-inheritance, shelf-rights, and successful repetition of exact motions in exact places.

They would herd the six-legged grazers, perhaps, but not by enclosing them broadly. More by stewarding lichen fields, guarding passes, culling with restraint, learning the synchronized stillness that turns a thousand bodies into topography. The shell-backed baskers might be harvested only after particular rotations of light. Burrower spoil heaps could be sifted for edible crystals, pigment salts, trace nutrients. Glider membranes, found rather than hunted when possible, would become tough translucent material for weather screens, veils, instrument skins. The local ecology is crowded enough that one does not need agriculture in the lowland sense; one needs literacy in densities.

And yet even this does not fully answer the question, because the truest inhabitant of that country may be something more distributed than a people.

What lives there, above all, is accumulation without replacement.

The land is full of creatures, yes, but also of versions. Occupancies stack. A nesting shelf is not simply home to a colony now; it is home to the persistent adjacency of all colonies that have learned that shelf. A footpath is inhabited by every traversal that fit it well enough to remain. The dry-stone circles are occupied not merely by current bodies but by the continuance of posture—everyone who ever sat there in cold light, sorting, waiting, watching, has left not a haunting exactly but a retained fit in space. So the people of the plateau, if you meet them, may seem denser than their numbers justify. One person may cast the social weight of many generations because the place does not surrender prior presences promptly.

This might affect their psychology in profound ways. They would have less use than we do for novelty, self-invention, dramatic conversion. Identity in such a realm would be cumulative and positional. You become yourself by entering the old path correctly enough that it accepts your addition. Ancestors are not elsewhere; they are local conditions. Memory is not wholly interior; it resides in routes, thresholds, notches, recurrent vistas, preserved tracks. Grief would therefore be peculiar. The dead do not leave cleanly. They may stop answering, but their path-pressure remains. A sleeping shelf retains the shape of a body for years. A watch post remains subtly occupied by the expectation of a watcher. To mourn, perhaps, is to learn the new intervals at which an absence can be encountered.

Their religion, if the word applies, would not center on gods of command. Too much in that land is indifferent to command. Instead they might venerate adherence, fit, enduring arrangement, the moral beauty of correct placement. Offerings in the niches—pebbles, feather-fans, mineral hair, eggs, lamps—suggest not propitiation so much as acknowledgment: this belongs here now; let it join the maintained pattern. Sacred places would be those where the world’s inconsistency is oldest and most legible, where time compacts visibly, where tracks of many eras coexist, where shadows remember former suns. Priests, if any, would be route-keepers and interval-counters, those who can tell when a certain saddle is safe to cross, when a cave is larger than itself, when a child has become stable enough to inherit a dangerous path.

Their art would be subtle almost to invisibility. Arrangements of shell on ledges that align with preserved dawn-light. Knotted cords encoding routes through spatial anomalies. Stone balancing too exact to be natural but too modest to announce itself. Carved notch sequences that must be read by touch while facing away from them. Songs, perhaps, but songs nearly monotonal, made of recurrence and tiny deviations, more like paced respiration than performance. In a crowded-silent ecosystem, beauty would not strive to dominate attention; it would reward prolonged attunement.

There may be other residents too, less humanly reassuring.

The plateau seems apt for an organism—or quasi-organism—that is not bounded by a body at all, but by adherence to certain physical conditions: cold, dryness, mineral bloom, temporal compaction, repeated occupation. Something like a colony-being woven from the habits of animals, the routes of people, and the memory-retention of stone. Not sentient in the conversational sense, but capable of preference. It would “want” fit and continuation. It would discourage violent alteration not by attack but by making interventions fail to take hold. Wells collapse into sterile seams. Roads wander out of themselves. Large fires go mean and localized. Fortifications feel unnecessary and are never finished. Visitors become cautious, then reverent, then either adapted or gone. In this sense the land itself is a resident, not merely a setting: an old equilibrium that has become behavior.

If one tried to picture the quintessential inhabitant, then, perhaps it would be an elder standing on a high stone ladder-cut at dawn: wrapped in filament cloth, one hand on a horn-and-bone tripod polished by generations, breath barely visible in the cold, face lined not by worry but by long squinting at distance, while all around and below the plateau is furred with life. Shell-domes turning to light. Herds gone still in unison. Gliders crossing impossible air. Burrow fields maintained grain by grain. Tracks of the living lying among tracks that never finished becoming past. The elder does not rule this. Does not even fully understand it. But belongs to it with the hard-won legitimacy of something that has found the one position it can keep for a very long time.

And if no one stands there at that moment, the place is still inhabited in the same way.

Because what lives there is persistence itself, diversified into animals, people, customs, mineral growths, paths, and retained moments; a whole elevated biosphere of exactness. The people are simply the form of it that can look back at you and know that you are misplaced.
