What lives there is hard to separate from where it lives.

At first you might expect the inhabitants to be austere things: narrow, dusty, reduced by the lack of water to a few mean strategies and a permanent caution. But the place does not produce meagerness. It produces elaboration under restraint. So whatever lives there is not gaunt in the simple sense. It is armored, resonant, pressurized, sheathed, partitioned, economical—and often surprisingly grand. Life there tends to come in emphatic designs, each one shaped by wind, minerals, dryness, exposure, and the fact that anything available will quickly be processed by something else.

The most characteristic residents are probably not the animals first, but the vast plant-colonies that blur the line between organism, habitat, and terrain. They are the real standing population of the uplands, the slow majority. A single "plant" may be a plateau-spanning pelt of interlocked fibers; a grove of towers sharing one buried web; a basin-wide braid of succulent cables; a field of upright blades that all open, hiss, and silver in one motion when the pressure shifts. They live by not wasting shape. Their surfaces are waxed, furred, faceted, powder-coated, ribbed, folded into fins and cowlings and heat-radiating vanes. They are dry-bodied but not empty: packed with gels, oils, starches, gas bladders, volatile chemistries, mineral slurries, and sealed wet microchambers hidden from the air.

Many of them are less green than one would expect. There are blue-matte cushion forms, iron-red stalks, pale ash columns, black heat-harvesting rosettes, silver leaf-fans, copper-blooming pressure plants, white-frosted crusts. Photosynthesis here seems only one trade among many. The flora also condense, comb, distill, crack, ferment, trap dust, store charge, precipitate minerals, and vent metabolic exhausts for neighbors to use. Some stand like chimneys, drawing air through porous tissues and plating their lips with powdery crust. Some spread rootfelt over talc-fine dust and hold entire slopes in place. Some grow dead slowly—remaining upright for decades or centuries, becoming architecture for the living. The old ones are often hollow, and that hollowness is not decay but opportunity: echo chamber, nest vault, brood tube, thermal refuge, ambush blind, fermentation shaft.

If there is a single recurring body plan among the large vegetation, it is this: a narrow commitment below and an extravagant apparatus above. Deep or broad hidden structures, then at the surface a resonant crown, a fan, a wheel, a segmented pipe, a battery of blades, some device to meet light and moving air at once. Height is used carefully. Tall things are seldom solid. They are chambered, ribbed, flexible, tensioned like masts. They survive by yielding. When they die, they remain as porous monuments, and other lives move in.

Among these great dry producers live the eaters, and they are numerous.

The herbivores are likely beautiful in a severe way. Not soft-muzzled grazers, not wet-nosed browsers, but lacquered and articulated creatures with bodies sealed against loss. One can imagine mid-sized six-limbed walkers with reflective shell-plates and narrow feeding mouths designed to shave waxy tissue, split rind, rasp mineral-rich bloom crusts, or insert a proboscis into a pressure sac without rupturing too much precious solvent. Their joints would close neatly. Their eyes might be hooded or faceted against glare. Their feet would spread weight over dust, crust, and root-web rather than sinking into soil that is barely soil at all.

There would be hoppers as well, because open spacing favors intermittent bursts of movement. Not necessarily insect-like, though some may be; more generally, creatures whose locomotion turns exposure into an advantage. A body held clear of the hot ground, a springing launch between feeding stations, a mirrored or chalky dorsal surface to throw back noon light. Some might travel in loose, shifting commons rather than coherent herds: many individuals working the same plant-field, each keeping just enough distance to avoid drawing predators or exhausting one patch too completely. They would sound like machinery while feeding—cracking rinds, clicking mandibles, valve-breathing, shell edges tapping stems.

Burrowers would be everywhere because the world above is bright, loud, and dangerous, while just beneath the rootfelt there is thermal stability and concentrated food. These might be compact, wedge-headed miners with ceramic incisors or rotating tooth-plates for gnawing into pith bulbs, tapping stored sugars, fats, or stranger compounds. Their tunnels would not just be shelter but plumbing within the living mantle: routes between root clusters, dead trunk vaults, cold dust pockets, carcass traps, fungal or microbial symbiont chambers. Some of them may almost farm decay, though decay here is slow and dry; perhaps they cultivate breakdown in sealed humid guts of dead tissue, carrying inoculants from one chamber to another.

And then the climbers and gliders: creatures shaped by the vertical organ-forests and scaffolded dead thickets. The air is useful here, if not forgiving. So there would be membrane-bearers, skin-winged or frond-ribbed animals that launch from crowns and ride pressure gradients from trunk to trunk. Not airy, delicate gliders of humid forests, but taut, dust-proof things with wing surfaces that can furl tight against desiccation and static. They might feed on pollen-smoke, on insects or analogues in the upper crowns, on the gases and exudates of bladder plants, or on the small nesting creatures hidden in acoustic fronds. Some may be active only in the coldest dawn and dusk, when the air layers are predictable and the predators below less heat-driven.

Smaller life would be astonishingly dense. The place almost requires it. Every surface, seam, vent, and cast-off strip offers a niche. There would be swarms that look like smoke around the crowns at noon, charge-sensitive pollinators or scavengers or spore-carriers, each with bodies insulated against static and limbs adapted to cling to vibrating surfaces. There would be leaf-edge skitters living on the undersides of blade fans; dust-sifters moving in the talc drifts between giant plants; tiny armored nectar thieves robbing bloom pylons; translucent or mineral-backed eggs cemented into shaded grooves. Much of this smaller fauna would vanish at a glance because stillness and resemblance are so rewarded. The dead and the living probably imitate each other constantly.

Predators in such a place would seldom dominate by raw speed alone. The world is too noisy and visually broken for simple pursuit in the open, though some pursuit specialists certainly exist: lean, tendon-rich runners with long stride cycles built for dry heat exchange, able to keep contact through vibration and scent traces in dust. But many hunters would key into the environment’s existing confusion. Some would mimic brittle dead wood, letting prey land, shelter, or browse against them. Some would be blind or half-blind ambushers tuned to percussion through root mats, striking when a feeding pattern transmits the right rhythm. Some would haunt the shade wedges cast by mineralized trunks, advancing only within those moving bars of darkness. One can imagine a tall stalking form, almost all leg and neck, with matte heat-dulling skin and jaws made less for blood than for puncturing sealed tissues and withdrawing concentrated internal stores before competitors arrive.

The aerial predators and scavengers would be among the most conspicuous animals. Thermals are reliable, visibility immense, carcasses valuable. So above the plateaus there are likely broad-winged circlers with very little wasted anatomy: dark on top, pale beneath, using every rising current. They might not flap much except in the violence of storms. Their heads could be sheathed and wedge-like, suited to breaking dry hide or reaching into desiccating carcasses before the rendering flora claim them. Some may descend not singly but as abrupt committees, folding from invisible altitude into a feeding event in seconds. Others may specialize in storm aftermath, gathering stunned gliders, charred small fauna, and dislodged nestlings after electrical weather.

The night population would differ sharply from the day’s, not because the place becomes gentle but because it becomes thermally strategic. As temperatures drop, animals that spent the daylight sealed, buried, or motionless would emerge to feed while conserving water. Ground colonies might open vents and begin communal calling. Shrilling, rattling, drumming, throat-comb humming—these sounds would map territories and mating networks across the basins. Many nocturnal creatures may navigate more by vibration and temperature than by sight, because moonlight in such clear air can be stark but deceptive. Some may gather around warm venting plants, feeding on condensed chemicals or on the insects and scavengers drawn there. Others would patrol the frost line, harvesting the brief vulnerability of chilled prey.

It seems likely, too, that some of the most successful residents are not fully one thing or another but layered partnerships. This is a place that rewards internalization: keeping useful chemistry enclosed, outsourcing risky steps to symbionts, carrying whole little economies inside a sealed body. Plants may house solvent-using microbes in nodules. Browsers may depend on gut consortia that can break waxes, resins, mineral-bound nutrients. Burrowers may transport spores that open only after gut passage plus electrical shock. There may be animals whose apparent "fur" is actually dry microbial felt used for condensation harvesting at dawn, or plants whose pale bloom dust includes dormant hitchhikers waiting for a host. Few inhabitants here would live as isolated self-sufficiencies. The climate is too exacting; alliances would be common, though often hidden.

There is probably intelligence there too, though not necessarily in a form that first looks familiar.

A place this loud and spatially open might favor minds that think in timing, rhythm, and infrastructure rather than in furtive secrecy. If there are highly behaviorally complex animals, they may organize around routes between shade, mineral licks, nesting towers, safe overhangs, and seasonal bloom fronts. Their lives might be deeply architectural without ever producing durable artifacts beyond what all animals produce by repetition. Communal burrow cities in rootfelt. Suspended nursery baskets woven into the lee of organ-crowns. Resonant signaling grounds where chosen stems are maintained for optimal sound. Harvest circuits memorized across kilometers of deceptively empty upland. Such creatures would understand pressure changes, storm smell, electrical tension, and plant phenology with an intimacy that would make them seem half meteorologist, half herdsman of stationary forests.

If social, they may communicate as much by percussion and resonant use of surroundings as by voice. Why waste the booming cane, the drum-root, the clattering blade field? A knock pattern could travel farther than a cry and be harder for some predators to localize. Courtship might involve tuning a stand of acoustic fronds, or selecting and cleaning resonant hollows in dead trunks. Territoriality might be expressed through maintained pathways and the chemical overprinting of feeding stations rather than through visible border lines. Their young would have to learn not merely what to eat or fear, but how the whole sounding machine works: which hisses are weather, which are swarms, which are a predator’s false note.

But even without positing anything sapient, the thing that truly lives there is a style of life: dry abundance, old and unsentimental, extravagant only where extravagance pays. The residents are likely durable rather than tough in a blunt way. They do not endure by resisting the world head-on; they endure by partitioning, sealing, timing, storing, booming, flaring, burrowing, going dormant, launching offspring into storms, reusing carcasses, inheriting dead structure, and making the air itself part of their metabolism.

They would look, very often, as if shaped by engineering diagrams drawn under conditions of ecological war. Fluted ribs. Heat fins. Dust screens. Valve mouths. Shock seams. Interlocking plates. Collapse points that fail safely in storms. Surfaces that turn silver when stressed. Seeds shaped like kites, screws, razors, rattles. Eggs lacquered against evaporation and glued into shadow slots. Larvae or nymphs that spend most of life sealed inside pith or dead wood, emerging only for a brief noisy adult phase synchronized with pressure season. Adults that may live long because growth is expensive, or very briefly because the niche is ephemeral and explosive.

And because the biome is so old, many inhabitants would carry antiquity in their forms. Not primitive, but deeply revised over long time. Strategies polished until they seem inevitable. Plant lineages that have become topography. Animal lineages that have become tools for reading and exploiting that topography. Organisms so fitted to this ventilated high world that removed from it they would make little sense.

So: what lives there?

Dry forests that behave like organs.
Mats and cables that are half ground, half plant.
Chimney groves and blade fields and black frost-harvesters.
Shell-backed browsers, springing rind-chewers, pith miners, membrane gliders.
Percussion speakers, dust sifters, crown swarms.
Shade hunters, deadwood mimics, thermal circlers, storm scavengers.
Hidden symbioses in sealed tissues.
Communities organized by wind, mineral wealth, and the brief economics of cold.

Above all, things that do not expect mercy from their world and therefore have become extraordinarily good at using it.

The uplands are inhabited by life that sounds manufactured, looks armored, feeds like an industry, breeds on weather, and has been learning for ages how to thrive where water almost never shows its face.
