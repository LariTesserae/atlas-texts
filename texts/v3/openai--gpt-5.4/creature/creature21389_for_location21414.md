What lives there is, first of all, something patient.

Not a single sovereign beast announced by the landscape like a crest on a shield, and not merely “a people” in the plain sense either. A place made by training growth over ages suggests inhabitants who think in graft-times, in wet seasons, in inheritance measured not by objects kept but by shapes maintained. Whatever lives there has learned to inhabit duration the way other beings inhabit shelters. It is likely communal without being crowded, technically sophisticated without hardness, and intimate with both biology and anomaly to such a degree that the distinction between craft, agriculture, religion, and local physics has largely dissolved.

The clearest answer is: people live there—but people so long adapted to this living architecture that they may seem, to an outsider, almost like one more cultivated species within it.

They would be distributed rather than concentrated: ridge communities, bridge-keepers, canopy herders, mist-gardeners, root-hall dwellers, pollen-watchers, tenders of water bulbs, singers of brace-groves, mortuary grafters, path-memorizers. Their settlements would never sprawl in the way stone cities do. Instead each inhabited cluster would cling to a usable knot in the giant living fabric: a trunk-well with platforms wrapped around it, a broad bridge swollen into a hamlet, a sheltered basin beneath an overleaf where cooking smoke can be swallowed by bromeliads and fungus vents. Between such pockets would lie hours or days of green distance. So even if the total population were large, daily life might still feel solitary, spacious, acoustically thin.

Physically, they would likely bear the mark of verticality and damp. Lean, sure-footed, broad-handed perhaps, with the balance of climbers and the unhurried precision of people for whom a slip is more consequential than a delay. Bare feet would make sense in many districts, or else soft wrappings that can grip wet bark and root. Calves and toes strong from ladders, stem-stairs, swaying causeways. Skin and hair would be cared for against constant moisture: resins, oils, antifungal smokes, leaf powders, clipped nails, elaborate habits of drying where true dryness can be found. Their clothing would not fight the climate with thickness but negotiate with it—layered barkcloth, woven bast fiber, leaf-treated capes, netted harnesses, belts with hooks of bone or hardwood for securing oneself while working over open air. Ornaments would likely be light, organic, and meaningful: shell-signs, seed cases, polished wood, fungus dyes, old bits of rare metal worn not as display of wealth so much as continuity.

They might seem quiet not because they are solemn, but because the landscape rewards economy of sound. Speech could be soft and close-range, with long-distance communication done by sparse calls, tapping codes on resonant rootwork, plume-smokes, hanging ribbons, pollen releases, tuned rain-harps, or songs directed through specific groves that carry pattern better than volume. A child raised there might learn early that shouting is crude and often useless; one note placed correctly into a ravine may travel farther than a whole argument.

Their minds, or at least their habits of thought, would be infrastructural. To live in a house that is also a trunk, that must be pruned to remain a house, changes one’s sense of ownership. They would not think of building as the making of inert shelter and then moving on. A wall is a relation. A bridge is a promise renewed seasonally. A stair is mortal. A room can thicken, root, bloom, soften, split, host beetles, need to be fed ash or drained of water. Home maintenance would be closer to medicine and gardening than carpentry. Their language might contain dozens of distinctions for healthy tension, cooperative rot, overbinding, thirst hidden in glossy leaves, dangerous exuberance, good shade, fog-worth, song-responsive tissue, and structural moods perceptible only after years of observation.

And because the place itself behaves strangely, these inhabitants would almost certainly be practical metaphysicians. Outsiders often divide a thing into “natural phenomenon” and “belief.” People who survive among bridges that lengthen according to company or mists that alter distance would have little use for such a split. They would know, in one seamless body of tradition, that certain crossings are safest at dawn, that one should never bring worked iron into a particular root-knot, that grief spoils grafts in the western wet months, that the third note in a pruning chant calms the parasitic bloom enough for cutting. They may not care whether this is botany, acoustics, taboo, courtesy, or magic; all those words are probably too blunt. It is simply the correct way to behave toward a living world with preferences.

I suspect they would not imagine themselves as rulers of the range. More likely they see themselves as continuers, negotiators, perhaps descendants of older shapers whose works have become ancestral environment. There may be stories that the mountains were once more stony, or more wild, or more impossible still; but daily life would not be organized around origins so much as around keeping the old bargains intact. Whole lineages might be responsible for particular forms: one clan or house entrusted with cloud combs on the uplands, another with the funerary wall-groves, another with routes through districts where space folds badly, another with the breeding and guidance of those great gliders if they can be handled at all.

Because the dead are set into living walls and become support, ancestry there would be literalized. The inhabitants do not merely remember their forebears; they climb on them, sleep against them, harvest shade from them. A grandmother may persist as the central graft in a house-rebuilt-many-times. A revered engineer-singer might endure as a buttress root no one cuts without ceremony. That produces a society with an unusual continuity: not conservative exactly, because everything must keep changing to remain alive, but deeply recursive. Innovation would likely appear as a new training pattern in an old grove, a new song grafted into a known maintenance cycle, an improved way of persuading impossible weather to become useful.

They may keep animals, though “keeping” here probably means relationship rather than enclosure. The long-limbed browsers on the causeways sound almost domestication-adjacent: not livestock in fenced fields, but semi-wild fellow users of the architecture, tolerated or guided because they prune beneficially and spread desirable seeds. Certain gliders might be watched, fed, revered, or tracked for membrane molts. Small pollinators could be lured near nurseries with scent-bowls and fungus salts. Pale mulch-scavengers might be cultivated under storehouses as cleaners. In such a world husbandry would blur with ecological editing.

Children there would grow up astonishingly competent in three dimensions. They would learn knot codes before formal counting, perhaps, and branch etiquette before abstract law. They would be taught not just plant names but plant biographies: this bridge was started in your mother’s mother’s season of smoke; this tower is younger than its skin; this root hall listens badly during heavy condensation; this basin leaks lies in late heat. Games would involve echoing across gulfs, catching drift seeds, reading air currents from pollen, racing on safe overcanopies, daring one another at the edges of physics but within memorized tolerances. Adolescence might be marked by a first solo crossing into a neighboring ridge-settlement, or by the successful tending of one’s own graftling brace.

Their art would be inseparable from maintenance. Decoration, in a dry stone culture, can be added onto surfaces. Here the surface is alive and changing. So beauty would be enacted through timing, pairing, bloom choreography, selective scarification, colored fungus cultures, water courses tuned to make certain sounds, leaf arrays that catch moon-pollen, carved shell tags whose shadow matters more than their shape, and songs that leave visible effects in opening flowers or tightening tissue. A feast might include not only food but the coordinated blossoming of an entire hanging wall. Memorials might be patterns of regrowth. Histories could be walked rather than read.

As for temperament, I imagine neither saintly serenity nor primitive mysticism, but a people made courteous by risk and complexity. In a place where hasty damage can take decades to repair, impatience is expensive. Anger may be treated as a dangerous weather event: not morally forbidden, but managed away from delicate infrastructure. There would still be conflict, jealousy, prestige, rivalry over routes and water rights and ancestral obligations. Yet even politics would be botanical. A faction might not fortify a border with walls; it might redirect condensation, deny access to training stock, alter a bridge-song, marry into a lineage that keeps the safe maps of a reality-warped basin.

Then there are the other inhabitants—not makers, but fellow lives molded by the same old regime.

The great gliders feel emblematic: creatures of the gulfs, broad and nearly silent, patterned to vanish against rot and leaf-shadow. They may nest in folded supercanopies, hanging in colonies like dead leaves until dusk warms the air enough for launch. To the human inhabitants they could be weather signs, messengers, prey too dangerous to hunt often, or sacred commuters between separated green masses. Their bones, if light and strong, might become prized tools.

The browsers of the upper causeways sound almost domesticated by etiquette rather than leash. Long-faced, patient, perhaps with prehensile lips or tongues suited to stripping epiphytes from load-bearing branchwork without injuring it. One can imagine herders accompanying them not to control every step, but to steer them toward overgrowth that needs reduction and away from juvenile braces. Bells would be too loud for this world; instead there might be throat-clicks, leaf whistles, scent marks, or dangling tassels of crushed herbs the animals follow.

Predators in flower-masses suggest ambush hunters so still they are mistaken for part of the architecture: fur or skin patterned like petal-rot, eyes adapted to green dimness, limbs able to cling beneath overhangs. They need not be numerous to shape behavior. Their presence would encourage overhead awareness, ritual tapping of blind leaf folds, perhaps children’s rhymes about maroon flowers opening with teeth.

Underlevels likely host a whole pale economy of decomposition: many-legged gleaners, soft-bodied burrowers in humus banks, translucent amphibious things in hidden cisterns, nest-makers in dry cavities between root bundles. Some may be harmless recyclers, others venomous, some edible, some indispensable. A people living there would know which scavengers keep mold balance, which indicate bad sap rise, which emerge before a slide.

And in the strangest districts, there may be organisms that are less species than behaviors of the place. Seedfish riding warm updrafts. Medusoid spores drifting through canyon interiors. Hanging plants that intercept falling leaves from impossible distances. Seedlings that briefly root in the coats of animals passing through enchanted mist. These are not just curiosities; they are reminders that life there has evolved inside a world where local laws are negotiable. Its creatures may exploit bent gravity, delayed time, shelfed fog, or pathways through folded distance as casually as ordinary birds exploit thermals.

It is possible, too, that something older than the present human culture remains embedded in the range—whether as remnant intelligence, distributed memory in root networks, or the cumulative agency of the cultivated forest itself. The description invites that suspicion. A continent-scale practice continued long enough can begin to look like a mind. Not a mind in the sense of a single speaking entity, but a responsiveness spread through groves, water bulbs, brace systems, bloom timings, impossible corridors, and inherited songs. The current inhabitants may converse with it without naming it as separate. To them, perhaps, the mountain listens because listening is one of the mountain’s jobs.

So who lives there?

A sparse, old, deft people of climbers, grafters, singers, herders, and keepers of living infrastructure. Communities nested in trunk and mist, whose architecture is agriculture and whose religion is maintenance. Quiet animals scaled to vast vertical emptiness: gliders, browsers, ambush things, mulch-pale scavengers, drifting pollinators and spores. The dead, still structurally present. Ancestors rooted into walls. And beneath all of them, around all of them, perhaps through all of them, a cultivated vegetative intelligence so ancient and distributed that it no longer stands apart from landscape.

What lives there is a civilization that never stopped gardening and a forest that learned, over generations, to meet it halfway.
