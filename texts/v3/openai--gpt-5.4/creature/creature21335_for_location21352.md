What lives there is not one thing but a manner of living: a people and all their companion species made compact by altitude, made precise by thirst, made old not in the sense of feebleness but in the sense of having had time to fit.

At first you might think the inhabitants would be severe, because the place is. But severity is only their outer grammar. What actually lives in that basin is busyness, argument, inheritance, technique, appetite, and a kind of unsentimental tenderness. Nothing there can afford waste, least of all emotion spent for display, so care has learned to disguise itself as habit. A water jar sealed correctly is affection. A wall patched before the rains that may not come is devotion. Saving seed from the strongest terrace plants is prayer without announcement. The people who belong to such a place would likely seem lean rather than large, weather-marked early, darkened by dust and sun except where cloth has protected the skin, with the particular economy of movement that comes from climbing every day since childhood. They would have strong feet and hands, good balance, lungs trained by height, and an instinctive awareness of surfaces: what stone is loose, which stair is polished slick by hooves, where the shade will be an hour from now.

They would not move like mountaineers conquering a landscape. They would move like residents of a vertical town: turning sideways through lanes without looking, stepping over sleeping dogs and feed baskets in one fluid motion, calling up two terraces while still descending a stair, carrying impossible loads with the composure of people to whom difficulty has become background. Children would be especially uncanny in this regard—quick as swallows, goat-sure on parapets, appearing and vanishing through gaps adults barely register. Elders, too, would belong visibly to the place. In a young settlement age can look accidental; here it would look architectural. Old people would know where every family’s wall once stood, which cistern has the sweetest taste in late cold season, what kind of dust means a slope is preparing to move, which years the cloudbanks rose high enough to wet the eastern nets twice in one week. Their memory would function as part of the infrastructure.

I do not think the people there would be grand in bearing, but they would be exact. They would have many small distinctions outsiders miss. Not simply “dry” and “wet,” but dry enough to thresh, dry enough to store, dry enough to travel, dangerous dry, false wet, young frost, sting-fog, roof-sweat, root-damp. Their language—if one imagines one—would likely be rich in terms for age, use, repair, slope, vessel condition, animal health, and kinds of wind. They would read the world through maintenance. A cracked plaster lip, a slack net, mold in a grain jar, unusual silence in the fowl cages, resin bleeding too freely from a courtyard tree: all these would be legible events.

Because the basin is crowded, the people in it would be crowded inwardly too. That is, they would develop personalities tolerant of nearness. Privacy would be real but narrow, something made of etiquette and timing rather than distance. Everyone would know one another’s animals, coughs, roof repairs, marriages, losses, and quarrels. Not from gossip alone but because sound carries and labor is visible. In such a place, character matters because temperament has structural consequences. A careless person wastes water. A lazy person weakens a retaining wall. A vain person might overplant the household plot. A generous person lends shade cloth, breeding stock, seed, labor at cistern-cleaning time. Virtue would be measured in practical forms. Patience would rank high. So would steadiness, memory, restraint, and the ability to mend what one did not make.

Their clothing would likely echo the settlement: layered, repaired, fitted rather than flowing freely. Wrapped wool or coarse plant-fiber cloth dulled by dust, leather panels at knees and palms, cords and toggles for securing tools, veils or scarves against grit, broad hats or woven visors for the brief hard angle of noon, heavier mantles by dawn. Ornament would not vanish in hardship; it would become concentrated. Beads of bone, shell inherited from lower lands, hammered metal too precious to use broadly, dyed threads worked into cuffs, tiny bells on animals and perhaps children, carved pins, amulets holding a seed, a tooth, a flake of old stone. Beauty would appear in edges, in repeated motifs, in things touched often. Spirals for cloud and return. Horn forms. Ladder forms. Terraced zigzags. Painted marks over lintels to guard stores and births.

As for their houses: one can almost say the houses live there too, because they are not separate from the people or mountain. But if there is a characteristic inhabitant, it may be a household rather than an individual. A household of five or twelve or twenty, spread across stacked rooms and roof spaces and adjoining pens, with kin not entirely separable from neighbors. Grandparents in the warmer inner room. New grain in the sealed dome. Kids or lambs under a lean-to on cold nights. Drying herbs overhead. Infants tied close to someone pounding grain. Adolescents trusted with ladders, birds, and errands to outer plots. The boundary between domestic and agricultural life would be thin; the boundary between human and animal thinner than urban minds expect but not sentimental. Goats are milk, fiber, manure, blood, alarm bells, path-finders, currency, and trouble. Fowl are eggs, feathers, noise, omens, scavengers, and timekeepers. Dogs are guardians of stores and walls. Cats or catlike hunters are tolerated at the granary edge and cursed in the loft. Everyone lives by everyone else’s appetite.

The animals themselves would be a major portion of the answer. Not sleek pastoral herds spread across open slopes, but a dense, agile, urbanized livestock adapted to ledges. The goatlike creatures especially feel central: narrow-faced, sure-footed, rib-deep, with hard glossy coats in dun, black, rust, and dirty cream; horns swept back or twisted; bells small and dry-voiced; eyes bright with perpetual practical insolence. They know the lanes, know which family is lax with latches, know where kitchen peelings appear, know how to climb onto what ought not be climbed upon. Children would grow up among them and old women would scold them by name. The settlement would be partly designed around containing, diverting, and forgiving these animals.

There are birds too, in two categories: the kept and the unkept. The kept ones are harsh little fowl in woven cages and dusty coops, selected less for beauty than toughness, but still becoming beautiful through familiarity—the way a useful creature always does if one lives beside it long enough. The unkept ones are the true aerial residents: swallows or swifts stitching the alleys, pigeons or cliff doves under ledges, seed thieves in the gardens, the one recurrent carrion bird high above whose silhouette everybody knows. A child may be named for its patience or circling. A shrine might use one of its feathers. People in a place like that would read raptor movement as weather, as omen, as practical information, as all three without contradiction.

The wild things at dusk matter because they prove the basin is not merely occupied but ecologically finished. Rodents with membrane-glides or soft cliff-adapted leaps. Pale fox-things on outer shelves. Snakes warming in retaining walls. Owls taking the evening lanes as if they too own rights of passage. Lizards in every crack where warmth holds. Insects loud enough to become weather. None of these would be considered mysterious in the romantic sense; they would instead be familiar presences folded into proverb, warning, and recipe. The people know which burrows undermine foundations, which owl calls mean rodents are thick this year, which beetles arrive before the rare hard rain. Their children imitate these animals in play. Their artisans carve them. Their healers use their fats, feathers, skins, or habits as lessons.

If I had to narrow further and imagine the sort of person who most embodies the place, I do not first see a chief or priest. I see a middle-aged woman carrying three different responsibilities at once: a coil of rope over one shoulder, a kid tucked under one arm because its leg has gone bad, a ceramic jar balanced against her hip, pausing halfway up a stair to shout advice across the lane about a mildew scare in someone’s grain pit while also noticing, without appearing to, that condensation has formed thinly on the north net and should be collected before the sun reaches it. Or I see an old man on a watch platform not really watching for enemies but for the shape of cloud under the western rim, tapping a dry seed pod against the rail as if in thought, knowing from the smell of the air whether to tell the lower terraces to cover their mulch. Or a gang of children with dust-whitened ankles and resin-sticky hands moving in a pack with two dogs and six bells worth of goats, every one of them louder than necessary because in such a place noise is both play and location.

The spiritual life of the inhabitants would likely center on transitions so slight outsiders would miss them. Not thunder gods and cataracts, because those are not the daily grammar here. Instead: rites for the first bead collected on a net after the long dead season; for sealing a cistern; for opening a grain room; for moving herds between inner and outer grazing; for rebuilding a wall after slippage; for naming a child under a certain phase of cold dawn when the clouds sit lower than the houses. Ancestors would matter because continuity is practical. If you survive in a place by accumulated method, the dead remain technicians. They are consulted not abstractly but through precedent. The oldest shelter, darkest with smoke, may not hold idols at all so much as worn objects and marks recording remembered thresholds: years of hard scarcity, miraculous fog harvests, roof collapses survived, births that coincided with rain, slopes that took houses and then gave them back as quarry.

These people may be wary of largeness. Not hostile to strangers, perhaps, but skeptical of anything that ignores limit. Big promises, broad gestures, crops that drink too much, architecture that stands free of support, speech that shines brighter than it works—none of that would feel trustworthy. Their humor might be dry to the point of abrasion, because in a hard place mockery is one of the safer luxuries. Their affection might emerge as instruction, teasing, shared labor, food portions adjusted quietly in another’s favor. They would probably produce excellent craftsmen in modest materials: net-makers, sealers of jars, planners of terraces, workers of horn and hide, patient carpenters who know how to use bent wood because straight timber is a fantasy, women and men who can read the firing color of clay in thin smoky kilns, people who can sharpen a blade until even poor metal serves nobly.

And perhaps there are those who mediate between the basin and what lies beyond it: traders or seasonal walkers descending to lower markets with salt, resin, medicinal leaves, wool, carved goods, dried cheese, and stories; returning with metal, dyes, rare grain, shells, maybe news of floods in countries they can scarcely imagine. Such people would be liminal figures, carrying the smell of other climates on their clothes. Yet even they would remain marked by the high place. They would walk downhill as if it were temporary and uphill as if returning to grammar.

So what lives there?

A cliff-village people, yes, but more exactly a dry-height people of repairs and thresholds. Small dark trees live there, and lichen, and bitter herbs. Red grain lives there because it has been convinced, generation by generation, to accept meager soil. Short-horned goats live there and bells, and dust-colored dogs, and swifts, and owls, and fox-thin dusk hunters. Smoke lives there under eaves. Water lives there only when persuaded. Ancestors live there as method. Children live there as noise and future balance. Age lives there in polished beams and hand-worn stone. Nothing is abundant except adaptation.

If you met one of them away from the basin, you might know them by what they notice first. Not the horizon. Not the grandeur. They would notice where water is escaping, where a wall will fail, where shade gathers, which plant remains green without softness, how sound moves, whether the animals are calm, what the air is keeping back. They belong to a place where survival has become culture and culture has become architecture. They live close together because life itself has only a few workable ledges. And after long enough, that closeness turns into a civilization small in footprint, immense in detail.
