What lives there is not a populous species in the ordinary sense. The place does not feel built around multiplication, chatter, appetite, or the warmth of bodies in number. If something inhabits it, it is either very few, or very distributed, or so adapted to stone-and-water routine that it scarcely announces itself as life at all.

At first one imagines custodians.

Not citizens: custodians, keepers, listeners to slow systems. Thin people perhaps, though even “people” may be too quick a conclusion. Figures made for altitude and damp chill, with the spare economy of creatures that climb more than they run and handle cold water daily. They would have long hands, not delicate but practiced—hands for lifting fitted lids, feeling seepage in mortar, testing the weight of a gate by its swelling hinge, reading moisture by touch the way others read weather by sky. Their bodies would be wrapped rather than adorned: layered pale cloth or felted fiber treated against wet, capes or shoulder mantles that darken in mist, leggings bound tight for stairwork, sandals or soft-soled shoes that grip slick stone. Hoods would be common. Bare heads would steam faintly in morning cold.

They would move with a local caution that is not fear but literacy. Never a full-footed stride where algae ought to be but somehow is not; never a careless lean on a parapet whose damp has loosened the render beneath. They would know by sound which court sits over a full cistern and which over an emptier chamber; by smell which runnel carries fresh condensation and which old standing water; by the tone of a ceramic knock whether a delayed gallery is flowing true. Their labor would be dispersed into tiny acts: clearing mouths, resetting plugs, skimming films, scraping mineral bloom from lips of channels, rotating shutters to favor night cooling, lowering cups into dark reservoirs, listening at bulged walls for yesterday’s rain.

And yet when one tries to populate the terraces with too many such keepers, the place resists. It remains too quiet. So perhaps they are a small order—monastic in rhythm if not religion. A few dozen over a territory built for hundreds, or built over centuries by successive hands and now maintained by remnants. Their quarters would be hard to distinguish from utility blocks: sealed dry rooms above the wet works, sleeping cells with thick walls and no windows, ladders withdrawn after dusk, little hearths vented into towers already made for draft. They would speak softly because the architecture already carries enough information in drip, echo, and gate-slap. They might even prefer not to speak near certain basins, so as not to spoil the hearing.

But there is another possibility, and the place invites it more the longer one looks: that the true inhabitant is not the keeper but the water made almost-organism by so much guidance.

Not alive in any single basin, not sentient in the sentimental way, but a distributed being of recurrence and memory. Here, water is not scenery. It occupies every function that windblown grass, street traffic, flocks, and gardens might occupy elsewhere. It makes the local soundscape, the local softness, the local changefulness, the local sheen. It records every fault and every intention. A blocked culvert alters its note. A fresh clay plug changes its seep. A night colder than the last one doubles the morning condensation. The whole region is a body whose circulatory intelligence is purely physical: head, gravity, phase change, retention, overflow, delay. If one wanted to imagine what “lives” there in the broadest sense, it would be this patient moving network, never still except in parts, never visible all at once, always becoming elsewhere what it has just ceased to be here.

The builders may have understood this and built not for themselves exactly, but for cohabitation with that larger inhabitant. The little shrine-like housings around valves begin to read less as superstition than etiquette. One does not worship the water; one attends it, courts it, avoids insulting it with waste or sudden force. The culture inferred from the place seems capable of reverence without mythologizing personality. Its devotion may be procedural: to keep the flow clean, the catchments open, the storage layered, the losses useful. In that sense the inhabitants would be priests only accidentally. Their liturgy would be maintenance.

Still, something stranger can be felt in those cistern interiors where translucent films tremble and opaline skins stretch across corners. The place hints at forms of life that are neither beast nor moss nor fungus in familiar categories, because the world as described has denied green life so absolutely that its alternatives have had room to develop. What lives there may include membrane colonies bred—or evolved—to work with the architecture. Pale veils that line settling chambers and capture fine mineral from water. Soft translucent sheets stretched over grilles, drinking condensation from air and thickening by almost invisible increments. Black mirror skins in deep basins that reconfigure metallic patterns across their surfaces as if exchanging information too slow for ordinary attention. These would be the nearest equivalent to gardens: tended, scraped back, reseeded perhaps from fragments carried in ceramic trays. They would not beautify the place in a floral sense; they would make it more itself—pearled, filmed, breathing faint chemistry into the damp.

If animal life exists at all, it would be reduced, hidden, and highly specialized. Not birds crossing the sky or mammals in alleys; the place has no invitation for that. Perhaps only blind water-thread creatures in covered channels, feeding on mineral membranes. Pale shelled things no larger than fingernails attached under sluice lips. Soft transparent worms in cistern overflows, more like animated capillaries than fauna. Beetle-analogues with ceramic-hard cases, dwelling in dry tower interiors and feeding on the ghostly films. One hesitates even to call these animals, because they would be so shaped by wet masonry and scarcity that they might seem like moving deposits, little mobile functions within the greater hydraulic body.

And if there are larger dwellers, they would have to belong to the same aesthetic of restraint.

One can imagine tall, damp-pelted pack creatures bred to carry tile, clay, and stone up the ramps—sure-footed, silent except for the click of split hooves on wet paving. But the place feels too acoustically spare for herds. More likely any larger companions are rare and stabled in upper dry courts: narrow-bodied, deep-chested beasts tolerant of thin air, led singly, their tack made of mineral-stiffened fiber and pale leather. Or perhaps there are none, and every load is borne by sled, lever, and many patient hands. The architecture suggests trust in gravity over muscle; perhaps it has long since minimized dependence on other life.

The most compelling inhabitants, then, are a triad.

First: the human or near-human maintenance caste—few, quiet, precise, living among towers and cistern mouths like monks in an aqueduct.

Second: the semi-living films and precipitate organisms that occupy the wettest margins, making a non-green ecology of skins, sheens, veils, and black reflective mats.

Third: the system itself, whose movement is so constant, distributed, and responsive that it behaves like a resident presence. Not metaphorically only. Truly: the place is inhabited by circulation.

If one were to meet one of the keepers, they might at first seem almost part of the masonry. Lime-pale garments, damp-dark hems, mineral stains at the cuffs, a face weathered not by sunburnt openness but by reflected glare and cold moisture. The eyes would narrow habitually against brightness off water. The voice, when used, would be low and unhurried, as if loudness were an extravagance meaningful only in places where sound must cross foliage or crowds. They might wear cords weighted with little ceramic pieces that serve as keys, measures, plugs, and tuning tools. They might know every district by taste as much as by map: this tower gives sweeter dew, that court collects harsh lime, the northern slabs harvest mist best in late cold season. To outsiders they would seem severe; among themselves perhaps they would have a dry humor based on leaks, delays, and the personalities of difficult gates.

Their beliefs, if any, would likely be inseparable from upkeep. To neglect a basin would not merely be impractical; it would be indecent. To waste clean water on display would verge on sacrilege. To understand a slope and answer it correctly with stone would be an ethical act. Their dead, if they bury or enshrine them, might be remembered in those comb-fields of upright slabs that harvest condensation: memorials that continue to gather water, allowing the departed to remain useful. That would suit the whole place—grief converted into catchment.

Perhaps children are rare there, but if they exist they would learn by ear before by diagram. Blindfolded, they would be taught to identify overflow, blockage, depth, and freshness from sound alone. Their games would involve channels scratched in dustless courtyards, little gatelets and timed releases. They would grow up without green as a category of comfort. Their idea of abundance would be a full cistern, a cool wall sweating at dawn, a dish-shaped plaza holding yesterday’s sky.

Or perhaps there are no children because the builders are gone, and what remains are the last functionaries plus their engineered membranes and obedient flows. In that reading the place is post-populous, not dead but thinned. The remaining inhabitants would be those able to endure repetition without society: guardians, hermits, technicians elevated into ritual by isolation. They would ring no bells except the accidental bell-notes of dripping towers. Their calendar would be cloud seasons and mineral accretion rates. They would count time in repairs that held and repairs that failed.

The place might even support something more uncanny: individuals who have become physiologically entangled with the hydraulic environment over generations. Skin unusually cool, perhaps; lungs tolerant of perpetually damp air; senses sharpened for echo and runoff; a metabolism that welcomes mineral-heavy water others would find harsh. Hair kept cropped or covered to avoid perpetual wet. Fingernails worn smooth from stone. A people subtly selected by architecture. Not amphibious, not fantastical—just made reciprocal with their environment to an unusual degree. In them the distinction between inhabitant and instrument begins to blur. They are not masters of the system. They are one of its moving parts.

That, finally, is who lives there: beings who do not stand opposed to the masonry but complete it.

They are sparse rather than numerous, exact rather than expressive, patient rather than forceful. They live in thresholds between bright terrace and cold chamber, between condensation and storage, between procedure and devotion. Around and beneath them live lesser wet ecologies—films, skins, mineral-feeding analogues, tiny pale things in grates and basins. Through all of them lives water itself, the truest resident, endlessly descending, waiting, reappearing, remembering the shape of every surface it has been given.

In another place one would ask what people built this. Here the better question is what kind of life could remain among such constructions and not try to turn them into something softer, greener, louder, more obviously inhabited.

The answer is: a life that has accepted chill, altitude, and repetition; a life that finds beauty in the clean lip of an overflow; a life that hears a hidden cistern the way others hear a bird in a tree; a life content to dwell where nothing blooms except salt, lime, and the brief brilliance of light on wet stone.
