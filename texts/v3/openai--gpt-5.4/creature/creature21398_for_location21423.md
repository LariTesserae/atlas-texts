What lives there is a people of adjustment.

Not nomads, not exactly settlers in the usual mountain sense, and not priests of wildness either, though from a distance one might mistake them for any of those. They are inhabitants of the provisional. Their gift is not mastery over the place, but continuity within it. They live as though staying is a craft.

They would be few. Fewer than the town seems designed to hold. Enough that every face is known, every absence noticed, every voice locatable by ear when it crosses the damp air. The settlement’s emptiness would not feel to them like failure. It would feel like allowance: room for the mountain’s revisions, room for water to choose, room for walls to be opened and remade, room for children to become workers and workers to become elders without the place hardening too quickly around them.

At first glance they might seem austere. Their buildings are plain, their streets practical, their tools repeatedly repaired rather than replaced. But the longer one watched them, the less austerity would seem the right word. They are attentive people. Their labor has taught them to care for minute deviations. They notice a new sweat-line on a wall, a draft where none moved last week, a cistern note changed by a finger’s depth of settling, the way moss has advanced across the north side of a stair. Their knowledge is granular and bodily. They put their palms on stone to feel chill and damp. They sight along lintels. They listen to drains. They smell earth where paving has lifted. Their conversations are likely full of practical observations that, elsewhere, would sound like omens.

And yet they would not be anxious. Or rather: their calm would include anxiety already metabolized into custom. They do not expect fixity, so they are not perpetually shocked by change. A wall shifting half a thumb’s breadth in a season is not catastrophe; it is information. A spring appearing under a storehouse is not miracle or curse; it is a new fact that must be fitted into the next day’s work. To live here is to relinquish the fantasy of completion. The people who remain are the ones who can bear that—more than bear it, perhaps, who are nourished by it.

They are builders, certainly, but builders in ongoing correspondence with matter. Masons, carpenters, drain-cutters, terrace-keepers, cistern tenders, roofers, reed harvesters, mortar mixers, tool sharpeners, haulers, weather-watchers. The settlement suggests no aristocratic concentration, no ornament-hungry court, no great military garrison. If there is authority, it likely belongs less to rank than to proven judgment: the elder who can tell from a hairline sheen where seep will emerge next thaw; the carpenter who knows how much movement a gallery can forgive before the pegs must be reset; the woman who remembers where the first paving line ran before the stair canted south; the bell-keeper who can hear fog thickening before it is visible.

They may have some ritual life, but it would be inseparable from maintenance. Not because they are unimaginative, but because here the sacred would likely reveal itself through recurrence and care. The first clearing of a blocked channel after winter. The resetting of a threshold before the rains deepen. The sounding of bells when cloud closes the lower wall. The annual measuring of the basin’s known seams. Perhaps children are taught to place a finger in old moss-marks to feel where damp once stood and compare it with the present. Perhaps names are given not only to buildings, but to recurring water paths, to bulges in paving, to cliff-seams, to the several kinds of mist that arrive from different quarters. Their oral memory would be infrastructural. Their legends might sound, to outsiders, like repair manuals with souls in them.

I think they would dress for saturation rather than spectacle. Layered wool, felt, waxed or oiled cloaks if they can manage them, wrapped leggings, soft boots resoled often, sleeves tied back for wet work. Clothing in stone-colors, bark-colors, moss-colors, not from ideology but because those dyes are close at hand and wear kindly in damp. Yet there would be touches of brightness where moisture makes color worth having: red binding cords, copper pins gone green, blue stitching at cuffs, polished bone toggles, silver rings darkened by weather. Hair often braided or tied up under scarves and hoods, because the air catches water in it. Skin chapped at knuckles, nails rimmed with earth or lime, shoulders strong from lifting and bracing and carrying.

Their hands would be the most eloquent part of them.

Children raised there would learn balance early: on wet stone, on half-finished stairs, on the shifting etiquette between what is path and what is runoff. They would know not to trust an old route simply because it was dry yesterday. They would be warned away from quarry lips and taught the sounds of secure timber versus soaked timber, settled wall versus working wall. Their games would involve channels and dams, little flotillas of bark in runnels, races along covered walks, daring each other to leap the narrow wet cuts where seepwater gathers. They would grow up with an exact but unromantic intimacy with materials. Mud, moss, pegs, wedges, splinters, reed fluff, cold bell-metal, the grain of freshly split slate—all these would enter identity before abstraction did.

The old people there would be invaluable, not because they preserve some static tradition, but because they remember previous configurations. In a town where the ground itself edits the record, memory is structural reinforcement. An elder may be the only surviving witness to where a wall stood before it was moved, or where a spring used to rise, or which terrace was once considered safe for grain and then slowly soured with damp. Their recollection would not be nostalgic in the ordinary sense. They have seen too many revisions for that. Instead it would be comparative, layered, quietly astonishing: here the paving was lower; there the birches had not yet taken root; this cistern once rang deeper; fog used to catch first on the east ridge before the cliff lifted that shoulder.

Because the settlement feels partially unfilled, one might imagine melancholy in its inhabitants. I think there would be some, yes—but not the theatrical melancholy of ruins. More a long acquaintance with incompletion. Some families may have come expecting growth that never fully arrived. Some terraces may have been prepared for houses never built. Some sheds may hold tools for trades no longer practiced at full strength. In such a place, hope and restraint would live side by side. They likely continue to build not because they are certain of flourishing, but because building is how one remains in dialogue with possibility.

The place might also attract a particular sort of person from elsewhere: those who are out of tune with places that demand certainty. A younger mason from a valley city, weary of straight walls and final plans, might come and stay because here his skill matters after the blueprint ends. A widow with a knowledge of herbs might find that the basin’s damp margins produce useful growths unavailable below. Someone thought overly patient elsewhere might be considered perfectly tempered here. A bell-maker, a surveyor, a keeper of records, a healer attentive to lungs and joints and molds—such people would fit. It is not a place for conquerors. It is a place for listeners, revisers, and those who can love a thing that never quite repeats itself.

As for temperament, they would likely be sparing but not cold. When population is small and distances inside the basin are amplified by mist and weather, every encounter acquires shape. Greeting matters. Calling across a square matters. Borrowing a tool matters because one knows exactly which hand last honed it. Their humor, I suspect, would be dry, local, and precise. They would joke about stairs choosing to become streams, about doors migrating in the night, about the mountain taking back poor workmanship by slow degrees. They might tease newcomers for trusting the apparent level of anything. They would probably distrust grand declarations while valuing competence almost tenderly.

There may be animals kept among them, but only a few and those chosen for resilience: perhaps short-coated goats on the drier margins, a handful of chickens under covered runs, dogs narrow enough to thread the lanes and clever enough not to tumble into every marshy hollow. But even these would feel secondary. The primary nonhuman life of the place remains vegetal and hydrological. In a sense, what most fully lives there is not fauna but process.

And perhaps that is the truest answer: what lives there is a form of companionship between humans and an unfinished mountain.

Not harmony, exactly. Harmony suggests resolution. This is closer to a practiced mutual tolerance deepened into respect. The people do not ask the mountain to stop arriving; the mountain does not particularly care whether they approve. But over years, then generations, the two develop a legible relationship. Stone rises or shifts; hands answer. Water emerges; channels welcome or redirect it. Roots probe a terrace; gardeners decide whether to cut, train, or build around them. The settlement learns the mountain’s syntax while never becoming fluent enough to presume on it.

If there is a spiritual disposition here, it would be one of alert humility. Not fear of catastrophe—though they are surely capable of fear—but reverence for ongoingness. They would understand deeply that the world is not made once and then occupied. It keeps occurring. Their town is not a finished object placed in scenery; it is a temporary clarity maintained within larger making. To live there is to participate in that making with tools small enough to fit in two hands.

So whoever lives there are people of wet thresholds and measured bells, of moss-memory and reset plumb-lines. People who can hear when a beam has taken too much damp. People who leave parts of buildings unfinished on purpose because wisdom, here, includes making room for what has not happened yet. People whose patience is not passive but muscular. People with an intimacy with beginnings usually lost once a landscape matures.

They live a little like gardeners in stone, and a little like archivists of water.

And if one wanted to speak less of the people than of the settlement’s presiding presence—its true resident spirit, if such language is allowed—it would be something almost impossible to separate into a single figure: the inhabitant is becoming itself. The town is inhabited by emergence. By seep finding route. By fog taking inventory. By roots reading mortar. By fresh rock learning weather. By human intention returning each day to negotiate, not defeat.

In that basin, life does not declare itself with abundance or dominion.

It persists by attending.
