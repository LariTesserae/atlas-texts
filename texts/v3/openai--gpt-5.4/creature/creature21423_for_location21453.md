What lives there is something like a people, but not a people in the easy sense.

At first one wants to say: hermits, cliff-gardeners, listeners. A thin human culture adapted to altitude, dryness, and the ceaseless pressure of sound. And that is true, as far as truth here tolerates edges. There are indeed inhabitants who lace paths between resonant spires, who cultivate the silver-black comb plants and pale translucent fans, who know which ledge can be trusted in morning light and which seam must be crossed only while humming into a bone tube. They build low, thick shelters in acoustic hollows. They wear layered dust-cloth and mineral felt. They mark safe traverses with tuned cairns and hanging rattles. They keep records not only of seasons and births, but of pitches, delays, standing waves, shadow-behaviors, and those rare hours when a certain wall brightens from within and reveals a doorway that is usually still stone.

But after a little longer with the place, “people” is too clean a word. The inhabitants have been revised by living there. Or perhaps the country only permits those already susceptible to revision.

They are often called, by whatever neighboring imagination once tried to name them, the Keepers of Accord, or the Fault Choir, or the Dry Gardeners. Their own names for themselves are probably contextual rather than fixed: those-who-cross-on-tone, those-who-tend-the-facing-fronds, those-beneath-this-ridge-in-this-agreement. In a land where form is local, identity is probably local too. A person may be one name from the western terrace and another from the basin floor. A lineage may be traced not by blood alone but by resonance: who can still hear the old third note in the southern columns, who inherited the breathing count for the seam above the white flats, who remembers which unfinished stair accepts descent only if you do not think of it as a stair.

Physically they would look gaunt to outsiders, but not weak. Dry-country bodies: rope-muscled, narrow, spare with stored fat because the place rewards efficiency more than abundance. Their skin would tend toward ash-brown, mineral-paled umber, cold-dark bronze under dust, though always altered by exposure—salt blooming at cuffs and collars, faint violet scarring where reactive plants brushed them, the fine whitening of lips and knuckles in the constant desiccation. Their hair, when worn long, is likely braided close or wrapped, not for ornament first but because loose hair catches dust, static, and the incessant tremor of sound. Many may shave sections of the head to better seat padded hoods, ear-caps, resonant plates, or listening frames.

Their ears matter. Not romantically—practically, almost sacrally. Infants are tested not only for health but for how they respond to certain tones carried through stone. Some hear better through their feet than through their outer ears; some can distinguish the mountain’s ordinary roar from the dangerous slippage-note that means a path is disagreeing with itself. These children are apprenticed early. In old age, hearing loss would not simply be disability but transformation; elders whose ears fail might become readers of vibration through palms, teeth, reed rods suspended in dust, bowls of sorted crystals that jump according to hidden waves. To live there is to distribute listening across the whole body.

So they are clothed accordingly. Layered wrappings of woven fiber from the stiff translucent plants. Felted skirts made of dust-trapping filaments. Hard shoulder mantles of thin mineral plates sewn onto cloth so the bones can take the constant tremor without tiring. Hoods lined with the mossy sound-dampening growth you mentioned, if that soft substance can truly be called moss. Mouth veils not only against dust but against involuntary breath-rhythm near dangerous seams. Gloves with open fingertips for reading stone. Sandals or split-soled climbing shoes tuned to the terrain: one sole for grip, the other for sensation. Their ceremonial dress is probably more resonant, not more decorative—pendants of drilled salt-ceramic, belts strung with tone-stones, ribs of polished frond shell that buzz faintly as they walk.

And they do walk carefully. Even the local gait would be distinctive. Outsiders stride; these people place themselves. They are always aligning—shoulder to cliff, breath to interval, step to the phase of a returning echo. From a distance, a file of them on a shelf path might resemble a procession. Up close, it is simply competent motion in a country where competence looks like ritual because the world demands so much attention. Their habits blur utility and ceremony until there is no point separating them. If a traveler taps every seventh standing stone before crossing a basin, is that prayer, map-reading, or structural verification? In such a place all three may be the same act viewed from different distances.

They are not numerous. The land could not support crowding, and perhaps crowding would be metaphysically dangerous there anyway. Their settlements are little constellations of persistence: five dwellings tucked into a fault-shadow, a hanging nursery garden under an overhang, a listening house with a roof weighted in crystal crust, a buried store chamber full of sealed jars of powder, cuttings, and resonant shards. A large settlement might still feel nearly empty. Voices are used sparingly—not because they are solemn by nature, but because ordinary speech must compete with a mountain that is always already speaking through your ribs. They likely communicate by touch, by practiced glance, by tapping code into ladder rails or floor slabs, by hanging ribbons that show invisible pulses, by pipe and bell and comb-plant choruses set to answer one another across distance.

It would be tempting to imagine them as monks of instability, but they are probably more horticultural than monastic. They tend the plants because the plants are not background life: they are insulation, chemistry, food perhaps in narrow forms, medicine, binding fiber, measuring instruments, warning systems, archives, and companions in a country without animals. A grove can be a pantry, weather station, shrine, and family inheritance at once. There are gardeners who specialize in root-filament mats that stabilize powder slopes. Others cultivate bladder-plants whose inflation patterns indicate pressure changes in the rock. Others breed cup-flowers for perfume-phases used in seam crossing or mourning. A master grower might be less a farmer than a conductor of very slow, very dry symbioses.

Because there are no beasts, the people are the only mobile animal life, and that fact would shape them deeply. The land has no scavengers to take its dead, no herds to follow, no birds to read, no insects to pollinate by proxy. So the inhabitants must manually assist many processes that elsewhere are surrendered to ecosystems. Flowering stands are dusted by hand with sifted mineral pollen. Seeds are carried in padded tubes and inserted into cracks chosen for chemistry and pitch. Rot is rare; transformation is curated. The dead may not be buried in any simple way, for the ground itself is unstable in meaning. They may be given to desiccating gardens where special fronds draw salts and trace compounds from the body. They may be seated in wall niches near resonant chambers so that over years the bones become tuned relics, consulted not for supernatural speech exactly, but because the dead continue to participate in the mountain’s conversation. Ancestors there would not rest silently. They would persist as part of the acoustic architecture.

That may be the heart of the inhabitants: they are not trying to conquer reality’s fracture. They are learning household management within it.

Their social roles would therefore revolve around kinds of attention. Path-readers. Seam-crossing guides. Echo-keepers. Garden binders. Dust masons. Pipe tuners. Bright-stone watchers who note the emergence of new internal glow and decide whether a fresh crack is promising, sacred, or fatal. There would be children tasked with listening at dawn to the changed count of a stair. Old men or women who sit beside black slabs etched in repeated grooves, running fingers along them to compare current vibration to inherited pattern. Teams who maintain bridges that may only connect on certain days. Mask-makers, because face and hearing must be negotiated together. Scribes perhaps, though their writing might be less lexical than diagrammatic: wave traces, contour overlays, knot records, tone ladders, maps that only resolve when sung.

They almost certainly hold that the mountain is not merely noisy but articulate. Not personified in a simple godlike sense—more intimate and more maddening. The land is mid-sentence. To live there is to dwell inside an utterance too large to hear whole. Their cosmology would arise from this. Creation may not be remembered as a past event but understood as continuing lithic speech. New stone glows because it is still being pronounced. Seams are grammar failures or overlaps. Echoes arriving before causes are not paradoxes but reminders that sequence is provincial. The world does not fully agree with itself, and agreement must be locally maintained by observance, by crossing rightly, by tending the plants that anchor one version of a slope to another.

So perhaps they sing—but not beautifully, by ordinary standards. More likely they drone, count, sustain, and interfere. They produce notes meant to cancel dangerous harmonics, to reveal hollow chambers, to settle panic in the body, to tell whether a cliff is currently continuous. Choir here is engineering. Prayer here is calibration. A wedding might involve the harmonizing of two households’ route-patterns and garden tones. A dispute could be settled by whose account of the seam survives the next brightening. Grief would have its pitch. Exile too: to lose the local agreements that make your home traversable would be a severe punishment, perhaps worse than death.

And yet they are not all severity. Sparse cultures are often imagined as joyless by outsiders who cannot read their abundance. But any people who survive there must have humor, tenderness, and ordinary appetite. Children would play with resonant dust, drawing patterns that jump under hidden vibrations. Lovers would meet in the rare nulls where one can hear breath over mountain. Families would have favorite ledges, inherited sitting stones, recipes for succulent cores pickled in mineral brine or fermented in sealed gas jars, stories about the year the eastern butte insisted on seven sides for a whole season. They might decorate interior walls with pale plant membranes stretched translucent over lamps, making whole rooms glow amber and blue like sheltered organs. They would celebrate the opening of a long-watched flower, the safe return of a crossing party, the discovery that a once-hostile seam now admits passage on the third bell. In a world of immense intervals, small continuities become feastworthy.

There may also be others among them—not a separate species exactly, but persons altered more drastically by proximity to the unfinished parts of the land.

Some have spent too long near thin seams, or worked too many years in cliff chambers where the humming blurs the eyes. Their outlines feel delayed. They seem to occupy their bodies with slight lag, as if arriving through themselves a fraction late. Their shadows may not quite match their posture. They are not considered monsters; they are a known occupational and spiritual category, half-feared and half-revered. Such people make exceptional seam guides, bright-stone readers, or custodians of ancient unfinished structures because they themselves have become somewhat local in form. They cannot travel easily to more stable countries. Those countries would reject them, or they would reject the overcommitted singularity of those places. Here, however, they belong as specialists in partial agreement.

And there may be recluses higher still, above the ordinary garden terraces, dwelling among the organ-pipe extrusions and violet-lit fractures. These are the nearest thing the place has to oracles. Not because they foresee the future in a mystical haze, but because they maintain long acquaintance with ongoing emergence. They know which needle peak is leaning toward materialization, which suspended arch is likely to complete a missing side within the decade, which monolith contour map will briefly become accurate when the low light turns copper-gray. They descend rarely. When they do, people listen not because they are holy in the abstract, but because they are weathered instruments of the country itself.

As for what they eat: perhaps little that an outsider would recognize as a full cuisine. Thick tissues from the copper-blue fans, sliced and cured. Nutrient nodules grown where reactive gases seep. Powder cakes from ground seeds and mineral-binding starches. Ferments coaxed from root filaments in sealed jars warm-wrapped against the cold night. Salts in endless variation. Bitter infusions from black cup-plants. Rare sweet condensates collected from flowering membranes. Their bodies would be accustomed to scarcity of water in the free state; they derive moisture from tissues, from chemical release, from dewless extractions and patient condensers hung in acoustic shadows where trace vapor settles. Offering someone drink in that country might mean handing them not a cup, but a warmed succulent bulb split open with a dull greened knife.

If they keep animals at all, they do not do so visibly; but your landscape insists there are none. So companionship must partly migrate into objects and plants. Tools are personal to a degree outsiders might mistake for superstition. A tuning rod inherited from a grandmother is not merely a stick but a trusted relation, calibrated through years of crossings. A household’s grove has personality because it has memory: it opened in the famine year, it sang before the cliff failure, it rooted into the seam where the eldest died. The people living there likely do not divide sharply between animate and inanimate. How could they, when stone revises itself, plants listen, and echoes can precede action?

If there is one figure in whom the whole country gathers itself, it is perhaps this: a person on a narrow ledge at dusk, wrapped in layered silver-black fiber, one hand on the wall, one hand holding a rod of pale resonant stone. Their face is half-hidden by a dust veil and ear hood. Around them rise dry gardens of translucent fronds trembling with the subterranean roar. Beneath them hangs a gulf whose depth comes in incompatible answers. Behind them, in a low shelter roofed with shale plates, others wait around jars of powder and dim amber light. Ahead lies a seam crossing that only exists if taken in proper count. The person does not look brave in the theatrical sense. They look practiced. Attentive. Intimate with danger but not enamored of it. They wait for the returning tone, hear in it that the slope is presently one thing and not another, and step.

That is what lives there: not conquerors, not exiles exactly, not mere survivors, but a civilization of practiced alignment. Dry-eared horticulturalists of the unfinished. Acoustic masons. Keepers of local reality. People who have learned to inhabit a country before it finishes becoming itself, and who in doing so have become slightly unfinished too—not broken, but open, conditional, precise. Human enough to build ladders and grieve their dead. Changed enough to understand that a mountain may be less a mass than a negotiation, and that life, in such a place, is the art of joining the chorus without ever assuming you lead it.
