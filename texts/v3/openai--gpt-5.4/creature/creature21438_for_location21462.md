What lives there is not one thing but a way of being alive in layers.

At first you might imagine a people defined by engineering alone: practical, sure-footed, preoccupied with weight, drainage, tensile strength, crop yield, pump capacity. And certainly they are those things. Anyone born into that mountain of terraces would learn early how water moves, how heat pools, which stair stays slick longest after rain, which parapet monkeys favor, which freight incline can be crossed safely when loaded and which must be given the whole width. Their intelligence would be infrastructural in the bodily sense. Children would grow up with calves and balance like climbers and porters, with an instinctive reading of routes that outsiders would mistake for genius. They would know not just addresses but elevations, exposures, catchments, the hours when one bridge is in blazing light and another cool enough to carry seedlings. To live there is to think in section as much as in plan.

But they would not be austere people, despite all that utility. A place so saturated with managed abundance would make residents who are intimate with maintenance without worshipping sterility. Their idea of order would include moss where moss belongs, vines where shade can bear them, birds nesting in grilles designed for precisely that negotiation between openness and enclosure. They would be a population that prunes rather than eradicates, redirects rather than clears, repairs rather than demolishes. Not because they are sentimental, though some surely are, but because the place itself teaches that coexistence is cheaper, wiser, and more beautiful than perpetual exclusion. A pipe can curve around a trunk. A market can put nets over the guavas and still leave the upper branches to parrots. A monkey-proof latch can be invented without declaring war on monkeys. This is a civilization of accommodations.

So the people who live there are likely patient with slow solutions. They inherit systems older than themselves and expect to be inherited by systems not yet complete. Their ambitions would therefore take a long form. A family might plant shade for grandchildren on a landing above a machine court. A cooperative might lobby for ten years to rebuild a stair not because it is unusable now but because everyone agrees a better geometry would ease traffic for the next century. Even private improvements would have public consequences in such density: a new cistern changes humidity for three adjacent gardens; an added awning throws welcome shade onto a lower alley; an illegal enclosure blocks a breeze half a district relies on. Residents would become political in the practical, daily sense. They cannot afford to imagine themselves separate. Gravity and plumbing forbid individualism from becoming fantasy.

Physically, they would probably be compactly strong rather than uniformly robust—people shaped by stairs, loads, crouching repairs, climbing into service spaces, standing long hours in bright heat, carrying baskets, tools, lengths of pipe, children, crates of seedlings. Skin darkened or burnished by the high hard light, or carefully shielded from it depending on labor and class; shoulders used to straps; hands callused in very specific places. Their clothing would be adapted to damp mornings and hot middays, to vertical transit, to brushing against wet leaves and whitewashed walls. Many pockets, ties, slings, head-wraps, aprons, sandals or soft gripping shoes, hats with brims, garments that can survive mildew, rust dust, chlorophyll stains. And color, likely, because the whole place permits and perhaps encourages it. In a landscape already full of painted tanks, flowering trellises, laundry flags, and ceremonial walls, drabness would not be the only sensible answer to industry.

They would be listeners as much as climbers. In that acoustically tiered world, hearing becomes a civic sense. People would recognize neighborhoods by the flavor of their noise: this basin with its water-hammer cough and doves; that upper shelf with school bells, greenhouse hiss, and monkey crashes at fruiting time; lower works with chain knock, forklift horns, and foundry exhale. A resident might know from bed at dawn whether the pumps have started on schedule, whether last night’s rain filled the cistern enough to change the overflow note in the drains, whether the market below has opened by the scrape of shutters and the first bargaining calls reflected up the shaft. They would likely speak across distance well—using whistles, names, short phrases shaped to travel, maybe colored flags or shutters too where sound is unreliable. Their speech might be full of altitude words, water words, maintenance words, inherited distinctions between kinds of wetness, kinds of rust, kinds of useful shade.

And because the mountain is all built surface, their metaphors would come from joined things. Not roots but anchors; not horizons but ledges; not buried secrets but sealed galleries, blind cisterns, old conduit runs, inaccessible service voids where history still hums. To “know where someone stands” might literally mean knowing their level, district, retaining line, or trade shelf. To “rise” would not be abstract. To “carry” could mean economic support, emotional burden, or the visible labor of hauling food and water between terraces. Their sense of belonging would be topographic and infrastructural at once. You would not simply be from the city; you would be from a specific band of light and runoff, a stair web, a catchment culture.

There are surely many kinds of people there, because such a place supports specialization. Water-keepers who understand every cistern, valve genealogy, and fog net. Gardeners who are half horticulturist, half mason, reading root pressure and wall stress together. Lift operators, rail mechanics, glaziers, monkey-proofers, drain clearers, plasterers, tile workers, shrine custodians, orchid smugglers or orchid saints, market women with impeccable route timing, rooftop poultry breeders, bath attendants, structural inspectors who can tell by smell when damp has entered a retaining seam, conservatory managers breeding peppers against new fungal blights, children who know ten forbidden shortcuts through ventilator corridors, elders who remember which warehouse wall used to be an exterior face before the next shelf was built beyond it. A place this revised over time breeds professions that elsewhere would not exist, or would be hobbies rather than necessities.

Religiously or ceremonially, I suspect they would honor continuity more than purity. Their sacred places would not need isolation from utility. A shrine in a retaining wall beside a freight ramp would not feel diminished by proximity to grease and produce crates; it would feel properly embedded in the real traffic of life. Offerings might be flowers from hydroponic beds, clean water, polished machine parts, ribbons tied to guardrails, painted cistern lids, prayers for pump reliability and safe footing and healthy children and trees whose roots must not crack the wall they bless. A people living in such intimate dependence on systems would develop reverence not only for abstract gods but for thresholds, repairs, successful harvests, the first frogs calling after dry heat, the annual flowering of some vine over the old viaduct, the day a long-promised stair reopens. Their festivals might travel vertically, processions climbing levels, lanterns mirrored in reservoirs, music answered from balcony to balcony.

They would also be a people with a distinct ethics toward animals. The goats are workers and scavengers; the cats, tolerated patrol; the doves and chickens, household economies; the frogs, beloved indicators of damp health; the monkeys, infuriating neighbors; the birds in the towers, almost co-citizens. Not romanticized, but accounted for. Children would grow up learning which creatures may be fed, which merely watched, which chased from the melons, which left entirely alone during nesting. Pest control would exist, of course—grain must be protected, disease prevented—but complete extermination would feel like a failure of design. Better latches, better mesh, alternate fruiting zones, decoy plantings, drains that do not breed the wrong insects, lights tuned not to call every moth in the district. Living there means understanding that habitats proliferate wherever structure and moisture permit; therefore design is always ecological whether intended or not.

The social life of such a place would be unusually visible. There are too many overlooks, bridges, opposing facades, shared courts, laundry lines, roof walks, and stair pauses for anonymity to be easy. Privacy would exist, but as an interior achievement rather than a default condition. Everyone would know who waters at dawn, who sings in the baths, whose grandchildren race the aqueduct walk, which machinist keeps feeding papaya to the parrots, whose trellis always fruits early, who patched the cracked landing with ugly but sound concrete, who has a feud with the monkeys and who secretly admires them. News would rise and fall with people and sound. Gossip might travel faster uphill in the evening than any official bulletin. Yet that same visibility would also create resilience: many eyes on every child, every fire, every broken rail, every sudden illness on a hot stair.

Class and status, if present—and they almost certainly are—would map strangely onto altitude, access, breeze, noise, and labor. The highest levels are not automatically richest if they are harder to reach and more vulnerable to water scarcity; lower shelves are not automatically poorest if they control transport and trade. Prestige might belong to shaded central terraces with reliable cistern rights, or to old districts known for structural sound and fruiting walls, or to certain greenhouse bands where air and light are most forgiving. Wealth might express itself not in isolation but in control over maintenance, over water security, over the right to enclosed cool spaces, over quieter routes, over ownership of productive roofs and vine-glazed courtyards. The elite, if they exist as a distinct set, would still have to negotiate with gravity and runoff. Even luxury there cannot pretend to stand outside infrastructure.

And there are probably people who rarely descend and people who rarely climb. Whole local identities would form around habitual height. Lower-terrace foundry families might joke that upper gardeners are half monkey already. High conservatory districts might call the industrial shelves smoke lungs and mean it affectionately. Middle bands with schools, markets, and transit nodes would think of themselves as the true center, as middle bands everywhere do. Yet all of them depend on one another too visibly to sustain myths of self-sufficiency for long. When a lift goes down or a cistern fouls or a retaining drain clogs, dependence becomes immediate and collective. In that sense the true resident of the mountain is cooperation under pressure—sometimes grudging, sometimes festive, sometimes bureaucratic, but constant.

If you ask not just what people live there, but what sort of being the place itself has become, then something larger emerges. The manufactured mountain is inhabited by a civic organism: part city, part watershed, part greenhouse, part machine. Its residents are like cells in a metabolism of lifting, catching, storing, ripening, venting, patching, feeding, and carrying. Yet that comparison should not flatten them into function. They remain vivid individuals—mechanics with basil on their windowsills, grandmothers who keep frogs in cistern gardens as omens of good water, teenagers leaping rails, priests with grease on their cuffs, engineers arguing with vine growers, bathhouse attendants who know every district by the smell of its people after rain. What lives there is humanity after a long apprenticeship to constructed terrain: no less human for being technical, no less practical for being sensuous.

And alongside them live all the secondary citizens the place has made room for: parrots in transmission towers flashing green against galvanized steel; lizards on warm boxes; cats in service alleys; rooftop goats cropping what would otherwise become blockage; bees among citrus walls; frogs singing from overflow beds; monkeys with criminal intelligence; orchids tied above machine shops because someone could not bear the rail to remain bare. These lives are not decorative. They are part of the local truth. The residents do not live despite them but among them, in negotiated proximity.

So whoever lives there is a terrace people, a cistern people, a brightness-and-condensation people. They are builders who no longer distinguish sharply between structure and habitat. They accept that anything enduring enough will gather moss, memory, and neighbors. They are suspicious of unused surfaces. They understand that every wall creates a microclimate and every repair enters history. They are at home in an environment where diesel and jasmine share the same air, where a turbine’s continuity is as reassuring as birdsong, where food climbs trellises above loading docks, where children know both the names of flowers and the sound of a healthy pump.

In another place such people might be called industrial, or horticultural, or mountain-dwelling, or tropical, or communal. None of those quite suffices. They live in a made terrain so old and so fully inhabited that the proper term may simply be native. Not natives to untouched land, but natives to a human mountain that has had long enough to become a climate, an ecosystem, and a home.
