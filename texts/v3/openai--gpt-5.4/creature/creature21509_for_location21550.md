What lives there is not a single species so much as a practiced citizenship of things: a people, their herds, their cultivated growths, their tame anomalies, and the place itself, which has crossed some threshold from environment into inhabitant.

If I begin with the people, they seem less like masters of this basin than its officiants. Dry-gardeners. Breath-keepers. Clay-handed horticulturalists of the invisible. They would be compact, patient folk, sun-browned where visible but usually filmed with a fine residue of lime, pollen-metal, ash, and aromatic dust. Their skin might carry the place’s palette—warm umbers, olive undertones, chalk at the elbows, silvering at the brows from vapor work. They would dress for heat and contact rather than display: wrapped reed-fiber cloth, soft leather aprons, sleeveless tunics stiffened here and there with patches, veils or masks over mouth and nose when tending strong vents, many-pocketed belts holding pruning blades, resin plugs, twine, tuning forks, chalk, pellets of feed, little mirrored discs.

Their hands would be the most expressive part of them. Broad-nailed, scarred by kiln heat and mineral bites, permanently remembering pressure points: how to seat one ceramic tube inside another without cracking the seal, how to pinch a vine before it starts imitating the wrong shape, how to tap a jar and hear whether it has begun to develop an inner bark. They would move with the confidence of those who live among conditions outsiders would call supernatural but they would call fussy. They do not gasp when a corridor is longer than yesterday; they click their tongues, adjust the shade ratios, and send a child around the stable route with a bell. They know that if a beast has acquired an extra counterpart overnight, one must not separate them too early or both may lose appetite. They know which spoken names settle an agitated pen and which words should never be repeated in seed rooms.

Their culture would be built on repetition elevated to covenant. Songs are maps. Recipes are legal codes. Pruning patterns are acts of theology. They likely do not write history as a line of rulers and wars but as chains of successful continuities: the year the sour-vapor vessels stopped splitting after the addition of bone ash; the generation that taught the north trellises to stop remembering absent ladders; the old handler who discovered that herd names must always contain one unvoiced consonant, otherwise the animals become too eager to fulfill them.

Children there would grow up with uncanny common sense. They would play games that double as calibration: counting steps through mutable corridors, tossing dust into cup-flowers to watch which way the metallic grains align, learning to tell ordinary shadows from persistent ones. Their first lessons would not be “this is impossible” but “this is touchy.” They would be taught never to tie loops in certain creepers, never to praise a fruiting column with tool-words unless tool-blossoms are desired, never to look over the perimeter ladders after dusk. They would learn animal care alongside spatial etiquette, because in such a place the two are related. To startle a herd in a narrow court might not only cause trampling; it might teach the court panic.

The elders would be repositories not merely of memory but of reality-maintenance. Their authority comes from having lived long enough in one pattern that the pattern trusts them back. An old woman who has breathed the dawn fumes of a given court for forty years might be able to calm a cracking wall simply by sitting beside it and reciting the names of the repairs in order. An old herder may carry a forked staff polished by generations of grip, not magical in itself, but so repeatedly associated with sorting, counting, and return that strayed animals align with its gestures. Their superstition and engineering would be inseparable because both have proved useful.

As for the animals most intimately associated with these people: they are dry domestics, creatures selected not for lush flesh or milk but for compatibility with tile, vapor, mineral diet, and close architecture.

The six-legged grazers with parchment folds feel foundational: small herd animals, nervous but tractable, with ribby torsos and delicate spoon-mouths made for scraping bloom and film from porous stone. Their hides would be dry-soft, layered, perhaps translucent at the ears. They are tagged because counting matters in a place where counting can slip. Their quiet neck-clicking is as much a practical sound as a reassurance—this many present, this many moving, this many real. Their dung, already half processed into useful dry pellets, makes them treasured cohabitants rather than dirty necessities.

Then there are the larger browser-beasts, almost architectural in temperament. Tall enough to disappear beneath hanging gardens, broad-sided, plaster-pale, with tassels at the ears and lids heavy against resin-rich air. They browse slowly on suspended mats and dry vines, and they know how not to blunder into support posts. One imagines them as creatures bred for stillness, because stillness is a survival trait in crowded places where the difference between trellis and animal should remain negotiable. They may have split upper lips for handling powdery fronds, and long, inward-curving toes or padded hooves that spread weight gently on ceramic floors.

The enclosed herd-creatures in the courts are humbler and more communal: squat, heat-tolerant, broad-backed, perhaps with a faint mineral sheen to their coats from the vapors they breathe. They eat chopped stalk, crystal-fungus mats, dried gels, and the refuse of pruning. They do not low loudly; they huff, clack teeth, press flank to flank in strips of shade. Their molts become stuffing, cordage, insulation. Their sheds become bowls. Even their parasites, if any, are probably managed as a secondary resource.

The membrane-winged aviary things are companions to the upper air of the settlement. Not songbirds, not exactly bats, but drift-creatures evolved or coaxed to make use of still, partitioned warmth. Seed-husk faces suggest pecking at dust cups, probing sponge-fruit seams, perhaps carrying pollen-metal between flower arrays. In roost they are black punctuation marks under the lattices. In motion they are scraps of dark cloth briefly remembering life. I suspect they are adored, mildly feared, and never quite considered fully domestic. Their timing may be used as a clock: when they descend, one vent cycle is beginning; when they fold, another is done.

The ribbon-lizards along the pipes represent the place’s quickness. Pale, synchronized, heat-loving, they are like nerves made visible. Perhaps they are useful in pest control, or perhaps they are simply accepted because every dry ecology acquires things that love its seams. Their eerie coordination would make them seem like one distributed organism, and the inhabitants likely read omens in their collective starts.

The beetles carrying curls of plant matter are almost certainly semi-tended. In a place where vents must be modulated and tiny apertures matter, any insect that plugs, trims, reinforces, or redirects airflow becomes collaborator. These beetles might be attracted, bred, or bribed to maintain certain microclimates. Their labor looks accidental until one realizes someone has arranged for it.

And then there are the plants, though “plants” here means a whole parliament of lifeforms persuaded into botanical roles.

The tube-plants are nearest to trees in status: structural, atmospheric, infrastructural. Their segmented stalks drinking mineral fog and vaporized oils suggest a flora whose vascular logic has been rewritten around breath instead of water. They are not decorative. They are standing organs of exchange, living chimneys, green distillation columns. When they thicken into groves, they probably alter local acoustics and chemistry enough to function as neighborhoods.

The felted wall-growths—lichen-analogs in coppers and blue-greens—are the settlement’s pelt. They soften surfaces, hold coolth, trap useful condensates, and visually prove that even plaster can become habitat. Their spread over walls likely marks places where the basin exhales well, where solvent coolness rises and the place’s substitute-for-damp gathers. Children might nap against them. Elders might read their color shifts as diagnostics.

The wire-dry vines are perhaps the most characteristic life here because they are both crop and hazard. Tendonlike, articulate, eager to remember forms, they are the species in which the basin’s metaphysics becomes easiest to see. Trained properly, they make shade, netting, cordage, feed, fuel, and living architecture. Trained carelessly, they begin to imitate absence, trace vanished structures, complete loops that should remain incomplete, and bloom into topological nuisances. They are what happens when horticulture becomes negotiation with habit itself.

The sponge-fruited shrubs with gas-gel bladders are storage organisms as much as living things. Their fruits are jars grown from tissue, and one senses the people have selected them for docility, capacity, and predictable sighing collapse. Whole courtyards may depend on them the way an ordinary village might depend on cisterns.

The cup-flowers full of metallic dust are less like flowers seeking pollinators than little ceremonial exchange stations between mineral and animal life. Whatever visits them returns altered, dusted, faintly magnetic, made part of a subtle circulation of charge and trace metal. They make the ecology shimmer with hidden transactions.

Most intriguing are the species that do not stay themselves. Seeds listening to conversation. Vines remembering trellises. Blossoms imitating tools. These are not aberrations tacked onto an otherwise stable flora; they are the local expression of heredity under social pressure. In that basin, lineage is porous to use. Repetition is a breeding force. The inhabitants surely classify plants not only by shape and flavor but by temper, susceptibility to suggestion, loyalty to previous forms, and rhetorical danger.

This suggests that what truly lives there, at the deepest level, is habit made flesh.

Not metaphorically. Literally enough to matter.

The basin is inhabited by custom as an ecological agent. Walk a route long enough and the route lives. Name a beast often enough and the name begins growing hooves. Use a vessel faithfully and it acquires tastes. The central ventilating mast-shrine, the pendular clay wheels, the feeding courts where many species converge—these are not just tools for management but anchors for continuity. They are the big, slow, visible habits around which all the smaller habits can accrete safely.

So if asked “who lives there?” the most honest answer is: a domestic civilization in alliance with responsive matter.

Its members include:
- breath-gardeners who farm fumes, shapes, and routines;
- dry herd animals bred for tiles, dust feed, and close quarters;
- hovering membrane-things that inhabit the rafters of the air;
- innumerable service-creatures along surfaces, vents, pipes, and trellises;
- plants that eat vapor, store gas, imitate memory, and take conversation as weather;
- vessels, walls, paths, and pens that are not alive in the ordinary sense but have crossed into preference.

The place itself has become a sort of collective organism whose cells remain visible as jars, mats, beasts, posts, and people. It is warm because it is metabolizing. It is quiet because its life is mostly maintenance. It is lush because abundance here is not a matter of rainfall but of recursive care.

If one wanted a single image after all, perhaps the inhabitant is this: a community of careful beings who have taught a dry basin to behave like a garden by treating breath as water, repetition as gravity, and tenderness as a construction technique. Over generations, the garden learned them back.
