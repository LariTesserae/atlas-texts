What lives there is not one sovereign creature stepping neatly out of the scenery. The first answer is that the place itself lives there. The terraces, hanging forests, root-vaults, bladder canopies, reed colonnades, moss lacquer, coral-wood ridges—all of it is inhabitant as much as habitat. In a country where ground is grown, walls are accumulated tissue, and avenues are recurring current-patterns held open by the habits of generations, “who lives here?” has to include the mountain-city organism made of innumerable lineages slowly agreeing to become landscape.

But within that immense collective body, certain citizens begin to emerge.

The most characteristic large inhabitants are creatures that understand structure. They are not wanderers of open ocean so much as users of halls, ledges, vaults, stairways, buttresses, and suspended groves. One imagines six-finned grazers with deep chests and broad, flexible mouths, moving in patient bands over the mat-plains and terraced shelves. Their bodies would be built less for speed than for control in dense three-dimensional terrain: slow powerful tails, steering fins spread like leaves, skin dark and velvety except where pale abrasion marks show along the snout from feeding on bark-coral and moss felt. Their eyes would be moderate, useful at close range, but not relied upon more than vibration and memory. They know routes. They browse the same ramps, shelter beneath the same arches, rear their young in the same folded groves where the current is slowed by bladder-vines. They are like antelope, dugong, and forest ungulate translated into a drowned civic world. The plants expect them. Whole frond-fields likely fruit and spore in response to their clipping.

Near them, and often beneath them, live the terrace walkers: elongated armored crustaceans, many-jointed and deliberate, with shovel-limbs in front and softer tufted feeding limbs beneath the body. Not giant in the monstrous sense, but large enough to make a sound when they pass in file over hollow growthstone. Their shells are not clean; nothing here is clean. They carry gardens on their backs—films of moss, small fan lichens, tiny filter anemones, the occasional parasitic worm—and so move through the city as walking fragments of wall. They enter cracks in fallen root-beams, patrol detritus plazas, harvest rot from old seams, prune fungal blooms, stir up clouds of edible silt for attendant fish. They are janitors, masons, and livestock all at once, though of course they do not call themselves any of that. Their migrations may be among the oldest recurring motions in the region, older than many individual groves.

In the open halls between ridges the midwater citizens gather: translucent schooling fish, knife-thin or leaf-broad depending on level, whose collective turning provides some of the only sudden brightness in the abyss-garden. These are creatures of avenue and square. They treat clear corridors between trunk-colonies the way swifts treat streets or pigeons treat plazas. Their bodies catch the milk-blue residue of ambient life and flash it back in sheets. They move not randomly but according to inherited civic geometry—down one current-lane, around a hollow terrace, through a cathedral-vault of reedbone, out across a basin where the sound becomes huge. Predators shape them, yes, but so does architecture. A school here is partly a social animal and partly a temporary rendering of the city’s flow-lines.

Predators are present, but they are not the defining intelligence of the place. The likely ambush hunters would be creatures of resemblance: root-knot lurkers, bark-skinned eels with frilled camouflage, flattened ray-things whose dorsal surfaces host films of living moss and thus disappear against terraced mats. They do not dominate the emotional tone because the landscape itself is too old and too broad to be dramatized by violence. Even the great hunters belong to equilibrium. They erupt from overgrowth and are immediately reabsorbed into it. Many probably have slow metabolisms, long fasting cycles, and territories measured not by exclusive ownership but by resonant familiarity—certain chambers, ledges, and arches that carry their scent, electrical trace, or low drumming call.

Then there are the infrastructural giants: the huge filter-beasts whose passage is heard before it is seen. These feel essential. In a place filled with reproductive dust, shed tissue, drifting larvae, suspended organics, and endless particulate bloom, some immense lineage would have evolved to move through the valleys like living harvesters. Perhaps they are long-bodied, rib-sided animals with broad sieve-fans opening from the jaws, or perhaps more vertical, like moving gatehouses trailing curtains of baleen thread. They would travel prescribed circuits through the drowned passes and broad basins, mouths humming as current is drawn through them. Smaller fish shelter around or within the wake of their feeding apparatus. Parasites graze their skin. Their calls travel for miles because they are not merely talking to mates or rivals; they are sounding the geometry of the city, reading echo from root-vault and terrace face, feeling where the old avenues remain open. Such creatures would be among the few beings here large enough to seem almost civic in themselves, moving landmarks in a landscape of living architecture.

Something eel-like is inevitable in so many catacombs. The pale eels mentioned by the place feel central—not as apex figures, but as signatures of continuity. They thread the root labyrinths, the honeycombed flanges, the old load-bearing cavities inside trunkstone where softness persists in the dark. They are probably blind or nearly so, their heads sensory with pores and delicate whisker-fins, their bodies capable of reversing through impossibly narrow vegetal channels. They feed on eggs, worms, carrion, small crustaceans, perhaps on the nutrient-rich films coating the oldest internal walls. They know the city from inside. If the schooling fish map the public avenues, the eels map the plumbing. To imagine this realm fully, one has to imagine hidden pale bodies moving through the walls the way thoughts move through a sleeping brain.

The sessile inhabitants are perhaps even more important than the mobile ones. Every beam, flange, and overhang would carry colonies of anchored siphon-creatures, fan-mouthed strainers, bead-cluster egg masses, clattering shellworms, and hair-fine net casters. Some may resemble pale candelabra of translucent flesh rooted in cracks of coral-wood; others may appear as dark felted blotches until current reveals a forest of feeding threads extending invisibly for meters. These are the city’s curtains, signage, weather vanes, and dust collectors. They make movement visible by reacting to it. They are why occupancy produces light: not because the water is bright, but because every lived surface is lined with organisms that glimmer, pulse, retract, or rattle when touched by passing bodies.

There are likely birds translated into water—not literally feathered things, but heron-shaped logics. Tall, stalking hunters among the mat-plains; long-necked gleaners of egg colonies from folded leaves; poised spear-faced creatures that stand balanced on broad fin-limbs while probing crevices with exquisite patience. This environment invites such forms because it contains edges, shallows of relative depth, nursery pockets, and layered vertical cover. A place of terraces and hanging gardens wants specialists that work seams and margins.

If one asks after intelligence in the narrower, more individuated sense—something like a people—the place does not immediately yield a tidy answer, and that feels right. A world this old may have little use for tool-using conquerors. Yet it would be surprising if no highly aware inhabitant had evolved from so richly structured an environment. If there is such a being, it is probably not a builder in the terrestrial sense, because the building is already accomplished by life itself. Instead it would be a reader, pruner, herder, listener, and keeper of routes.

Perhaps they are soft-bodied but heavily mantled creatures, not unlike a convergence of cuttlefish, newt, and browsing mammal. Medium to large, neutrally buoyant but capable of clinging with many clever gripping tendrils to bark-coral walls and suspended vine masses. Their skins would be able to take on the tarnished silver, peat-black, lichen-blue, or bone-pale of local surfaces, not for theatrical disguise alone but as ordinary etiquette within a visually dim world where texture is identity. They would have broad, forward-set sensory crowns tuned less to light than to current-change, vibration, pressure echo, and the chemical signatures of old places. Their intelligence would be architectural and temporal. They would know which groves are spawning, which root-vaults are unstable, which current-lanes carry migrating shoals, which ancient terraces host aggressive nesting colonies this long cycle. They might not construct walls, but they could weave gaps shut with living cuttings, clear choked avenues, transplant bladder-vines to rebalance suspended canopies, or tap resonant husks to communicate across distance in patterns the whole city helps transmit.

Such beings would feel less like masters of the abyss-garden than like civic participants in an order older than themselves. Their homes, if homes they have, would not stand apart from the environment as houses. They might prefer cavities within colossal root-flanges lined with cultivated soft moss; chambers under buoyed leaf-rafts where faint seedlight filters through; old collapsed lattices edited into habitable honeycombs. They might herd the six-finned grazers away from nursery groves, follow the filter-beasts for harvest opportunities, collect eggs from certain communal colonies, and tend bioluminescent skin-moss as memory marks or route signs. Their art, if any, would likely be arrangement of growth rather than manufacture: tuning resonant chambers, encouraging silver-moss on selected columns, orchestrating delayed pulse-lines through seed-pod avenues so an entire hanging orchard can carry a signal.

Or intelligence might be even more distributed than that. The true “resident mind” of this place may be consortium rather than individual: fish migrations that maintain lanes, grazers that prune mats, terrace walkers that recycle collapse, root-colonies that thicken where stress persists, pulse-pods that answer disturbance, filter giants that turn bloom into dung, eels that clean the internal cavities, scavengers that keep the plazas open. No central planner, yet a stable civic order. In that reading, what lives here is an old republic of lineages, each species a trade, each growth form a guild, each current-lane a public work maintained by use.

Even the smallest life would reflect the city’s character. There would be clicking seam-crustaceans packed into every crevice, shell-backed mites grazing luminous skin films, transparent larvae drifting like punctuation through the vaults, colonies of geometric eggs set on the undersides of giant leaves in repeating arrays so regular they look ceremonial. There would be tiny fish that live only inside the breathing chambers of giant reed columns, worms that tunnel dead trunkstone and release nutrient plumes into surrounding mats, and coin-sized medusae that bloom in the plazas when detritus gathers thickly enough. Nothing wasted, nothing solitary for long.

What does not live there, notably, are creatures of haste, of drought memory, of bright-sky panic. Nothing built for sprinting across bare ground. Nothing needing fire, heat, or direct sun. Nothing dependent on seasonal reset or dramatic bloom-collapse. The residents are all, in some sense, conservatives of pattern. They are adapted not to catastrophe but to continuity.

So whoever lives there is old in behavior even when young in body. They move by habit through a world that long ago completed the transition from growth to geography. They are drummers in reed towers, browsers on moss terraces, net-casters from fallen beams, silver-skinned keepers in root cathedrals, pale eels in the walls, filter leviathans in the avenues, schools of living mirror-glass in the plazas of open water. They are gardeners that do not call it gardening, citizens that do not know the word city, and pieces of architecture that happen to swim.

Above all, they belong to a place where organism and infrastructure have become the same category. Whatever lives there lives not on the mountain, or in the forest, or among the ruins, but as part of the ongoing fact that forest, mountain, reef, and city are one submerged body—and have been for so long that its inhabitants have inherited composure from the landscape itself.
