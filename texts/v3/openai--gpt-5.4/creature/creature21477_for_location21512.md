What lives there is not one kind of people so much as a people shaped into many adjacent forms by altitude, scarcity, and stone.

At first glance you might say: mountain folk. Hard-handed, close-mouthed, durable. And that would not be wrong, but it would be too blunt. The place produces a population with distinctions as fine as fracture lines in rock. Spring-keepers are not the same as quarry families. Upper-herd clans are not the same as terrace merchants. Those who live nearest the watch platforms develop one alertness; those raised among pens and lanes develop another. Even within one household there may be the cistern-minder’s patience, the mason’s shoulder-set gait, the runner’s thin-chested endurance, the dog-handler’s sideways attention always partly on the animal world.

Still, some common shape emerges.

They are people made compact by cold and economy. Not necessarily short, though many are; rather, they seem gathered inward, built to conserve. Faces weather early. Skin is darkened not by heat but by wind, glare, and years of reflected light from stone and snow. Around the eyes there are fine contractions from squinting into brightness. Lips chap. Hands broaden. Knuckles split and heal and split again. Even the beautiful among them are beautiful in a durable register: strong nose bridges, high cheeks, thick hair braided or wrapped away from weather, shoulders trained by carrying. Children here are lean and quick-footed; old people, if they survive to old age, become astonishingly economical in movement, as if every gesture has been filed down to necessity.

Their clothes continue the architecture of the city. They do not wear garments; they inhabit layers. Wool if they have it, felt if they can make it, hides turned inward in the deep cold, woven underlayers close to the skin, outer wraps stiffened by frost some mornings before body heat loosens them. Nothing is purely decorative, but decoration appears all the same wherever utility permits it: braided edge-work in dyed threads, bone toggles carved into birds or horns, stamped leather cuffs, bits of hammered metal sewn at the throat, mineral pigments worked into hems and collars. A person’s district may be known by the cut of a hood, the color striping of a sleeve binding, the shape of a boot toe, the knot used for fastening water skins or dog leashes.

They smell faintly of smoke almost always. Smoke, wool grease, cold air, tannin, animal hair, stone dust, and whatever their work adds: sour milk, iron, blood, wet clay, juniper resin, stale snow, crushed herbs. A child from the spring quarter might smell green by local standards, meaning moss and seep water. A stonecutter’s daughter smells of grit. A runner carries the dry clean smell of distance and old sweat frozen and thawed. Lovers know one another by these gradations before touch.

Their speech would likely sound spare to outsiders, not because they are unfeeling but because life in thin air and shared labor favors precision. Words are used like pegs driven into ground: enough to hold. Yet this terseness exists beside a deep appetite for naming. They would have many terms for kinds of snow not in the folkloric sense but in the practical one: roof-sagging snow, channel-blocking snow, hoof-cutting crust, false thaw, drift snow worth harvesting, rotten spring snow above a void. They would distinguish stone by fracture, water by source, wind by route, animal cries by implication. They are probably not verbally lavish in greeting, but they are exact in warning. “Not that path after sun.” “Draw from the third cistern first.” “Those birds are low today.” Whole philosophies live inside such sentences.

Who lives there, then, are rememberers.

Not mystics in the easy sense, though they may have rites and springs and tower offerings. More deeply, they are a people whose survival depends on distributed memory. Grandmothers know which wall failed two generations ago and why. Boys sent to collect dung learn which gully echoes before a slide. Girls carrying water are taught not just how to lift but how to read the sheen on cistern ice. Shepherds know not only where grass is but where dogs panic before humans notice anything wrong. Masons know which quarry face sings before it cracks. Bird-keepers, if there are such specialists, read migrations as calendars. The city is full of knowledge that is ordinary only because everyone cannot live without it.

This makes them conservative in one sense and improvisational in another. They do not waste effort disputing proven practices. If a roof pitch works, it is repeated. If a shrine marks a safe spring, no one is eager to test whether reverence may be omitted. But because the mountain is unfinished, conditions shift. A flow changes. A ledge breaks. A herd route moves. So the people are also habitual adapters, quick to alter a pen wall, reroute a channel, dismantle one stair and cut another. Their tradition is not devotion to fixed forms. It is devotion to retained attention.

The city likely breeds a strong ethic of mutual visibility. Not intimacy exactly; there are too many people packed too close for romance about communal life. There will be rivalries between quarters, old grudges over water shares, disputes about grazing rights, accusations of theft, contempt for traders from lower districts, mockery of the shrine people by practical households and secret reliance on shrine people in years of poor melt. But in such a place privacy can never be the primary social unit. Everyone’s fuel shortage becomes the lane’s concern because frozen neighbors become everyone’s burden. Everyone knows which household lost animals, which roof needs relaying, whose son has not come down from the upper tracks, whose daughter can read weather off raven behavior, who has clean buckets, who hoards, who lends. Reputation is infrastructure.

The people who live there are therefore hard to fool in some ways and easy to wound in others. They are not impressed by boastfulness detached from competence. Anyone can claim boldness; fewer can notch stone true, deliver a lamb in sleet, set a broken wrist, cross the basin before dark, keep water unspoiled, train a dog not to break a herd. Skill is visible here, and pretension must survive repeated contact with necessity. Yet because life is interlocked, insult and shame also travel quickly. A failed promise may remain attached to a family for years. A generous act may do the same. Honor is likely practical before it is abstract: to arrive when called, to return tools, to leave agreed water untouched, to keep watch when your turn comes, to speak plainly about risk.

As for temperament, imagine neither cheerful stoicism nor grim severity alone. There would be humor, but dry and accurate, used like flint. Mountain humor often is. Children would grow up adept at mimicry—of goats, traders, ravens, the pompous, the unlucky. In hard climates laughter tends not to vanish but to become sudden, cutting, and communal, breaking out in workshops and pens, around butchering tables, on long climbs where complaint has become too obvious to bother disguising. There are probably songs too, though not lush ones: work songs with strong feet in them, narrow melodic ranges built to carry in open air, laments that sound almost like counting, ceremonial chants at springs or midwinter towers, herders’ calls that hover between language and animal sound.

And there are the nonhuman residents, who are not backdrop but fellow claimants.

Dogs, certainly—not pets in the soft sense but companions with legal and emotional presence. Some lines bred for driving, some for guard work, some rangier and half-feral at the edges, attached to districts more than owners. The people of this city would know dogs as extensions of household will and warning systems with fur. Children learn early how to read ears, hackles, fatigue.

Ravens live there too, in the civic imagination as much as in the towers. No one who survives long in such a place thinks of ravens as merely birds. They are weather readers, thieves, scavengers, unofficial witnesses, and opportunists sharp enough to seem political. If winter offerings are left for them, that is less worship than negotiated coexistence.

Goatlike herd beasts, wool animals, antlered stock in some quarters—these are wealth, food, clothing, fuel source, transport, dowry, inheritance, and argument. To live there is to live among animals not sentimentally but intimately. One knows the sound of a ewe in distress, the gait of an injured pack beast, the smell of rot in stored fodder, the patience required to move reluctant bodies through a narrow lane in freezing dawn.

The wild things remain close enough to matter: foxes at the edges, rock rabbits in walls, nesting birds under bridges, cliff colonies above all human claims. This proximity likely shapes local character. People brought up there would not imagine a sharp division between civilized and wild; they would imagine gradients of habituation, zones of negotiation, species with overlapping uses for ledges, water, scraps, and shelter. A person from this city looks at an empty slope and automatically inventories hidden life.

There may also be a certain kind of authority living there: not kings in broad courts, perhaps, but wardens, cistern stewards, quarter elders, path-judges, herd arbiters, shrine custodians, signal masters. Power in such a city would accumulate around choke points—water, routes, storehouses, labor coordination, weather interpretation, memory of precedent. The most respected figures might be those who stand at the intersection of practical and ceremonial knowledge: the woman who knows exactly when to open the melt channels and also leads the first-thaw observance; the old tower keeper who reads smoke signals and raven patterns with equal seriousness; the mason-priest who blesses foundations because he has seen enough collapses to know blessing and good footing are cousins.

If the city has an emblematic inhabitant, it might be someone hard to categorize elsewhere: a middle-aged woman with a runner’s lungs gone slightly thick, a mason’s forearms, a herder’s eye for weather, two dead dogs remembered by name, three living grandchildren underfoot, authority in one district but not beyond it, superstitions she denies while practicing them exactly, and a mind full of stored contingencies. Or a boy not yet grown whose daily walk to the upper cistern teaches him more geography, law, zoology, and mortality than a library might elsewhere. Or an old man who no longer climbs but sits where the lane widens, repairing harness and identifying every passerby by tread.

What lives there, finally, is a culture of fittedness.

These are people who do not imagine themselves separate from conditions. They do not romanticize hardship because hardship is too repetitive to be romantic. Nor do they dream primarily of domination; the mountain is too large, too unfinished, too obviously prior. Instead they cultivate exact correspondences between need and place, season and act, wall and wind, beast and route, custom and consequence. They are not lush people because the land is not lush. Their festivals, when they have them, are probably intense precisely because abundance is brief: thaw days, safe returns, first water flowing through a cleaned channel, a successful trade caravan, the birth of healthy animals after a bad winter. Joy there would be sudden and muscular.

So whoever lives in that city is flinty, layered, watchful, unsentimental, deeply skilled, not unkind but careful with kindness because everything given is drawn from a margin. They are builders of ledges and keepers of fractions: half-hours of sun, inches of thaw, one good seep, one stable route, one remembered rule that saves ten lives when stone shifts.

And because of this, they would seem to an outsider almost as if the mountain had made a second mineral out of itself—something warmer and moving, something that speaks, carries water, ties knots, buries its dead in stubborn ground, teaches its children where not to step, and each winter learns again how to remain.
