What lives there is a people that seem, at first, inseparable from their animals and their mud.

Not because they are primitive in any empty sense, and not because they are swallowed by the landscape, but because they have learned to exist in a place where the usual boundary lines—between street and stable, house and embankment, water and wall, labor and maintenance, settlement and wetland—never became clean. They are a broad, low-country civilization of handlers, builders, tenders, dredgers, burners, plasterers, drovers, fishers, and keepers of thresholds. They live in intimacy with persistence. Their genius is not conquest of the land but continuous arrangement.

They are probably physically shaped by the place in ways an outsider notices immediately. Stockier than tall, perhaps, not by blood alone but by habit: bodies made for carrying loads over slick causeways, for squatting to work clay, for levering baskets, dredges, poles, and nets up out of water. Feet wide, callused, and sure. Legs strong from ramps and embankments. Hands thick-palmed, with the fine dexterity that comes from tying wet cordage, setting bricks, cutting hide, sorting shellfish, checking kiln temperatures by color and breath rather than instrument. Skin almost always carrying some sheen—of humidity, of clay dust gone tacky, of animal grease, of smoke. Hair usually bound close or covered, not ceremonially but practically, because loose hair in this air is one more absorbent thing to manage.

They would smell, to anyone not born among them, astonishingly alive: damp wool or hide, mineral water, smoke, sour milk, fish slime, clean clay, dung, wet rope, fermented grain, skin. Not filth exactly. More like an environment that cannot be rinsed off because one is part of it. Cleanliness here would not mean freshness in the airy sense. It would mean correct separation: drink water from this jar, wash tools in that channel, dump blood downhill, keep sleeping mats high, replaster before the seep reaches the grain bins, lime the pen after slaughter, smoke what cannot dry.

Their clothing would emerge from scarcity and dampness both. Little flowing fabric, nothing too absorbent if it can be helped. Wrapped skirts, short tunics, aprons of treated hide, reed-cloth mantles for mist rather than rain, belts carrying hooks, cord, knives, pegs, pouches of salt or ash. Sandals where the ground allows, bare feet where they grip better, wooden clogs perhaps in some wards, though anything rigid risks being lost to mud. Those who work kilns or slaughter courts or salt pans might be recognizable at a glance by their accreted trades: white salt along hems, soot in creases, red clay permanently embedded in nails, fish scales flashing on forearms, a sweet-sour animal musk in the cattle districts, tannin stains among hide-workers, chalk bloom on the cistern tenders.

And they are never far from sound. Their speech, in such a place, would likely have adapted to constant acoustic competition. Not delicate, perhaps. Rhythmic. Projected from the diaphragm. Rich in repeated calls, whistles, hand-signals, shoulder-taps, stock phrases robust enough to survive frogs, bells, bellowing, and kiln roar. Markets would not be scenes of elegant bargaining so much as patterned vocal combat. Herding speech would blur into chant. Work crews would move on count and chorus. Religious language, too, might favor droning continuity over sudden revelation—low recitations that can run under other noise like a second current.

If one asks what sort of beings “live there,” the answer is not only humans. The true residents are assemblages: households composed of people, dogs, fowl, penned grazers, tethered monkeys, cistern fish, wall lizards, grain beetles forever at bay, fungi in the damp corners, bats in the lofts, tolerated birds in the drains. The people do not inhabit the city alone; they curate populations. Their skill is not sterilization but managed coexistence. Every family likely knows which scavengers are welcome, which vermin merely inevitable, which insects indicate rot, which frogs mean a cistern is healthy, which sheen on water means danger, which snail clusters can be eaten, which molds are harmless, which color of wall-sweat foretells collapse.

So the inhabitants are practical naturalists, though they may not think of themselves that way. In another land one might become a philosopher by gazing at stars or forests. Here one becomes a philosopher by watching seepage, spoil, breeding cycles, fermentation, kiln draft, silt load, hoof spread, the rate at which damp climbs a wall. Their lore would be empirical long before it is abstract. They would know mud by taste, by the sound it makes under a pole, by how it shrinks when fired, by whether it takes a thumbprint or sheds it. They would know animals not sentimentally but intimately enough that individuality cannot be denied. The old draft beast that only kneels for one song. The bull-marsh grazer that drowns itself if startled in a narrow pool. The dog line that can smell bad cistern water. The bats whose evening exit is more reliable than any sundial under that lid of cloud.

Their social structure likely resembles the city’s growth pattern: additive, layered, thickening rather than replacing. Houses accrete around courtyards; kin groups accrete around storage, shrines, and animal rights; wards accrete around kilns, pens, pans, pits, and channels. Authority may therefore belong less to distant monarchic spectacle than to those who can keep continuance going—those who control drainage, brick firing, herd passage, cistern purity, causeway repair, clay allotment, breeding records, slaughter timing, fish weirs. Priests may exist, certainly, but in this place priests would be hard to separate from maintenance experts. The sacred would attach to thresholds of transformation: raw to fired, foul to usable, flood to storage, carcass to meat to hide to glue to bone tool, pit to pond to embankment to wall.

Their gods, if gods they have, are probably not sky gods in any primary sense. The sky is too remote, too occluded, too undramatic. The powers here would be underfoot and at hand: seep, pressure, curing, accretion, digestion, gestation, rot, glaze, breath, enclosed heat, standing water that nourishes and poisons according to vessel and rite. They may worship mouths and jars, kilns and culverts, wombs and cisterns, horned patience, the keeper-beasts of marsh edges, the mineral bloom that appears overnight on shrine figurines. Their offerings would make tactile sense: milk, blood, first fish, salt, fired miniatures, knotted reeds, pellets of clay pressed with thumbmarks, the clean scapula of a slaughtered beast, beads of shell or carved marl. Their rituals would not seek transcendence away from material life; they would ask material life to remain in balance.

Children there would grow astonishingly competent very young. A child in such a city must learn routes before letters, footing before theory. Which plank tilts. Which wall ledge is safe. Which pools are for washing tools, which for ducks, which for drinking after settling and boiling. How to avoid the tusked dray animals’ blind side. How to catch fish from overflow channels with both hands. How to tell, from a dog’s bark, whether a stranger, scavenger, or loose beast has entered the lane. They would be half feral only in the healthy sense that children in dense working ecologies often are: omnipresent, mud-slick, observant, useful, darting under adult labor with baskets, messages, and stolen snacks of roasted snail or dried eel.

Old people, if the city allows many to grow old, would become repositories of continuity because continuity is the highest currency here. Someone remembers where a causeway once failed. Someone remembers the fungus bloom that preceded the grain spoilage of thirty years ago. Someone can look at a new wall and predict where damp will climb first. In a place with little seasonality, memory does not anchor itself in winters and harvests but in repairs, animal lineages, kiln collapses, unusual births, channel reroutings, the year the bats failed to emerge, the period of brackishness in the eastern cisterns, the rebuilding after the embankment slumped. History is infrastructural.

Temperamentally, the people who thrive here are likely patient, unsqueamish, communal, and conservative in the literal sense of conserving what works. Not uncreative. Very creative. But their creativity expresses itself as modifications to routine rather than love of rupture. A new basket weave that dries faster. A better plaster mix. A pen layout that reduces trampling. A channel lip that traps fish while spilling silt. Innovation exists, but it must prove itself under damp, under use, under repetition. Fashion cannot stray far from maintenance because maintenance always wins in the end.

This does not mean they are humorless. On the contrary: places of constant inconvenience tend to breed robust comedy. Their jokes would be bodily, work-based, animal-based, merciless toward pretension. Anyone trying to stay ceremonially pure in a city where frogs leap into shrine basins and dung beetles interrupt processions would need either flexible theology or a very good sense of humor. Festival, when it happens, would likely be loud even by local standards: painted horns, drums deep enough to ride beneath the animal chorus, processions over causeways, communal slaughter, great kettles, clay masks, races of sledges through mud, night fires reflected in every standing pool, mineral glazes and fat smoke turning everyone copper and blue.

As for their appearance as a collective presence: they would make the city seem even denser than it is. Not individuals spaced decorously through architecture, but streams of bodies brushing walls, balancing loads on shoulders and heads, leading rope-harnessed beasts, carrying wet clay in slings, paddling low canal skiffs between embankments, kneeling to scrub vessels with ash, lifting reed shutters to let the dim light in, hanging strips of fish, skimming scum, patching plaster, collecting eggs, hauling dredge baskets, plucking bats from netted vents perhaps, or collecting guano from beneath them. Everywhere occupied, because occupation is how this city remains a city and not wet ground with ambitions.

If there is one creature to imagine as the emblematic inhabitant, though, it is not any single animal nor even the human alone. It is the household cluster at dawn: a woman or man unlatching a swollen clay-plastered door; warm fog and breath mingling; dogs slipping out; a child squatting to scoop yesterday’s ash; a tethered marsh bird craning from a trough; frogs still pulsing in the ditch; a sleeping mat rolled high onto a shelf; fish flickering in the channel below the threshold; somewhere overhead the bats just returning; somewhere nearby a draft beast beginning its deep complaint; and the person in the doorway already reaching to feel the wall, to judge by touch how the damp has moved in the night.

That is what lives there.

A civilization whose primary relationship is not with open land, forest, mountain, or sea, but with wet matter made usable by attention. People of embankments and brick skin, of animal nearness, of unfinished ground accepted as permanent condition. They are not elegant in the way old dry cultures become elegant. They are durable, crowded, sensory, exact, and deeply literate in continuance.

They live there, and so do all the mouths they feed, fear, breed, cull, tolerate, and depend on.

The place is inhabited by caretakers of a young earth who already behave like its oldest species.
