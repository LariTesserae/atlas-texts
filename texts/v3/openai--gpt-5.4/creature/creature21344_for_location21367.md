What lives there is not one creature but a principle with a thousand bodies.

At first you might think the inhabitant of such a place must be something singular and grand: a leviathan in the central canal, an old blind sovereign in the Calcium Quarter, some jawed god moving once a season through the Mussel Wards and making the towers shudder. And certainly there are great things there, old enough to be mistaken for geography and large enough to alter neighborhoods by passing. But they are visitors in the deepest sense, even if they are born there and die there. The true resident is more distributed than that. It lives in curtains, films, swarms, draperies, accretions, pulses. It inhabits by encrusting. It remembers by settling again in the same current line, in the same pocket of warmth, in the same undercut lip where generations before found enough flow and enough shelter to turn chance into structure.

If you insisted on meeting “who lives there,” you would have to be introduced district by district, body plan by body plan, hunger by hunger, until gradually a personhood would emerge from repetition.

In the Silt Courts, the resident seems shy, half-submerged in its own thinking. It is all mouths hidden in the mud. Soft-bodied burrowers pump the black sediment through themselves and return it changed. Worms thread the peat-dark layers into ventilated seams. Clams lie under the skin of the basin like buried machinery, opening and closing in the dark. Small watchful fish hover inches above the bottom, silver only when they turn; otherwise they are a change in grain, a hesitation in the water. Rays visit like script being written and erased. Long-nosed grazers root and sift. Eels use the Courts not as citizens use a square but as secrets use a face: appearing where there seemed to be only texture, vanishing into any line that promises concealment. Here the living presence is digestive, judicial, patient. It takes what falls. It sorts. It redistributes. It decides, by chemistry and disturbance, what may remain open and what will be claimed.

In the Mussel Wards, the inhabitant becomes architectural. It is black-shelled, layered, communal, severe. Mussels stack on mussels until the first generation can no longer be found except as depth. Tube worms lace the seams. Small crabs, porcelain-pale or rust-dark, patrol the interstices like tireless maintenance workers. Fan-feeders unfurl whenever current freshens and instantly retract when a hunter’s pressure wave brushes the avenue. Predatory snails glide over the lower courses with the grim patience of tax collectors. Star-shaped flesh grips wherever there is purchase. If one could speak of temperament, this district’s resident is acquisitive and composed. It makes walls by refusing to let go. It trusts flow. It feeds together. It survives insult by sheer multiplicity. Break one chimney and the fragments become foundations for six smaller ones downcurrent.

In the Root Halls, the same inhabitant learns to be nursery, ambush, and rumor. There are creatures here that truly belong to shade rather than dark: juvenile fish in glittering, nervous clouds; translucent fry striped with bars that mimic root-shadows; shrimp that vanish when still and tremble into existence when they move. Reptilian residents linger at the threshold between water and air, their patience of a different order from the colonies below—more calculating, more individual, but no less ancient. Serpent-necked hunters, or the suggestion of them, may hang among the trailing rootlets. Broad-mouthed fish wait under the mats where falling things collect. Snails graze the films from bark gone soft with decades underwater. Every hanging root is both street and pantry; every pocket of dim green is temporary wealth. The resident here wears more eyes. It is quicker to panic, quicker to breed, quicker to be eaten. It is where the city is youngest each season, and where death from above enters most often as a sudden vertical fact.

In the Calcium Quarter, the resident becomes austere and inward. Pale things prosper there: white films, glassy shrimp, blind catfish with sensory pores like script on their heads, cave eels that know every chamber by turbulence alone. Colonial sheets drape the arches like old fabric. Tiny crustaceans click in the hollows. There are anemone-like beings colorless as milk, opening in the dark where mineral seep and current meet. There are crusted ledges where nothing obvious grows until a disturbance sends a million pin-light responses rippling away into the chamber beyond. This quarter’s inhabitant feels less like an animal and more like a consensus of tolerances—who can bear sour water, who can exploit the seep, who can spawn in resonant shelter, who can navigate a city when sight has become an extravagance. It is a more listening form of life. It knows stone.

And in the Flood Roofs, when the rains lay a new world on top of the old one, the resident becomes opportunistic to the point of delirium. Eggs hatch there that could not hatch below. Surface-breathing things patrol under the lens. Insects blown or dropped from far-off land become sudden festivals of feeding. Transparent drifters spread themselves through the freshened layer like gauze traps. Lower-city fish rise warily into this weather-made district and either gorge, stagger, or die depending on what the flood has written into the water. The inhabitant here is improvisational. It treats meteorology as real estate.

Yet even these are only neighborhoods of a larger being.

What truly lives there is a civilization of interfaces: whatever can exploit an edge, a gradient, a seam between one water and another, one hardness and another, one speed of current and another. It consists of filterers and sifters, encrusters and borers, grazers and scavengers, lurkers and flash-swimmers, egg-guarders, mucus-spinners, shell-thickeners, brood-hiders, carcass-openers. It is not united by kinship but by adjacency. The mussel tower and the crab army and the hidden eel and the shrimp veil and the fungal film and the fish school are all organs of the place’s way of being inhabited.

If you wanted the nearest thing to a face, perhaps it would be found in the mid-sized creatures that move through many districts and bind them together. Not the tiny fixed colonies, and not the rare immense passersby, but the old practical citizens of murk: the thick-bodied fish with scarred flanks and barbels rubbed pale from probing stone; the broad freshwater rays that know every feeding flat; the air-gulping hunters that rise under root-shade and sink again like dropped weapons; the shelled turtles mounded in algae and worms, carrying quarter-sized ecologies on their backs; the long black eels that can be thread, rope, resident, thief, and priest all at once. These are the beings in which the city’s distributed intelligence seems to gather. They know routes. They know seasons of turbidity, where the cleaner stations operate, which arches hold eggs, which culverts trap carrion after storms, which upper lanes become lethal when the fresh lens lies too thick. They are not rulers, but they are practiced readers of law.

And still there may be larger singulars.

There may indeed be an old one.

In a place with so much repetition, one ancient survivor would not stand out by ornament but by interruption. It might be a giant soft-shelled turtle with a head like drowned wood and a carapace so overgrown that juvenile fish graze on it as if on a moving plaza. It might be a river-dolphin-like hunter, scarred pale around the snout, navigating by pressure and electric whisper, entering the Root Halls only in certain weather and vanishing for months into deeper channels no smaller thing maps whole. It might be an immense gar armored like a relic weapon, hanging motionless below the leaf mats until all around it has forgotten the concept of suddenness. Or a trunk-thick lungfish or eel, ugly with longevity, that aestivated in some earlier geography and now moves through the warm black avenues with the authority of a flood remembered by muscle.

Such a creature would feel less like the king of the city than like one of its recurrent myths made flesh: the mobile catastrophe, the accidental urban planner, the one whose passage opens light-shafts and feeding frenzies, whose shed parasites seed colonies elsewhere, whose waste nourishes whole minor districts, whose body is so old and so revisited by smaller lives that it has become a temporary neighborhood whenever it slows. The city would not depend on it, but stories within the city—if stories can be made of startle responses, inherited routes, instinctive avoidances—would gather around it.

If, however, you ask not what animals are there but what sort of being this place would be if its inhabitants could be gathered into one character, then it is something like this:

It is a warm, low, many-chambered intelligence that distrusts emptiness. It abhors a vacancy and immediately puts lips around it, roots into it, hangs from it, fills it with eggs, stations a cleaner there, sends scavengers to patrol it, drapes it with feeding threads, or turns it into an ambush. It is ancient but not monumental in the human sense. It does not preserve; it reuses. It has no center. It thinks in currents, in salinity lines, in recurring appetites. It is tender toward the young only where conditions permit tenderness. It is lavish with offspring and indifferent with individuals. It can glow, but mostly chooses not to. It prefers touch, chemistry, vibration. It notices the fall of a feather from above, the leak of minerals from below, the death of a fish two alleys away, the pressure signature of a broad predator entering a lane. It is busy everywhere and hurried nowhere.

Its body is made of layered tenancy. A drowned trunk becomes sponge-scaffold becomes worm-city becomes nursery shade becomes rot becomes silt-bar. A shell bed hardens into bluff, undercuts into refuge, fills with broods, collapses in storm, and is grazed flat into the beginning of another district. What lives there, finally, is this process become habitat and then become character. Not ruin reclaimed, because nothing was ever truly abandoned. Not wilderness, because every surface has a history of occupation. Not city, except insofar as cities are places where repeated hungers make structure and structure makes further life possible.

So whoever lives there is:

the colony that is also a wall,
the wall that is also a pantry,
the pantry that is also a nursery,
the nursery that is also an ambush,
the ambush that is also a road,
the road that is also a wound healing into architecture.

And moving through all of it, reading all of it, occasionally remaking it without intent, are the old citizens of the murk: eel, ray, turtle, armored fish, blind catfish, crocodilian watcher, shrimp cloud, mussel nation, worm republic, root-district fry, pale seep-feeders, shell-buttress builders, carrion juries.

The drowned city is inhabited by multitudes so entangled that, from close enough, they stop being a crowd and become a single resident with innumerable mouths.
