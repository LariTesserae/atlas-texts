The land lies low beneath a permanent ceiling of vapor. There are no hills in the usual sense, only broad swellings of dark ground, shallow basins, and long shelves of newly exposed stone rising no higher than the crowns of the surrounding vegetation. The horizon is visible in fragments: a dim green distance glimpsed through curtains of leaves, steam, wings, and suspended rain.

Heat fills everything. It does not arrive with sunrise or gather toward afternoon; it is simply the condition of the world, deep and constant. The air has the temperature of breath held in the lungs. Moisture condenses on bark, scales, seed husks, and the smooth faces of rocks. Drops fatten without falling for a long time, then descend heavily into the foliage. There is frequent water but little open water. It runs beneath mats of roots, occupies hoofprints and hollows, beads on waxy leaves, and seeps from low ledges as if the rock itself were sweating.

The sky is pale but gives little light. Dense cloud and denser crowns divide it into green-gray panels. Underneath, there is no true shadow because there is no direct sun—only a dim, warm illumination spread evenly through the vapor. Pale fungi and the undersides of leaves provide the brightest surfaces. The landscape appears saturated yet subdued: bottle green, black mud, bruised violet, rust-red stone, cream-colored roots, and occasional blooms of an almost luminous yellow.

The ground seems recently made.

Flat plates of claystone emerge between root masses with their edges still sharp. Pebbles lie loosely packed in beds that have not yet become soil. Along shallow channels, soft mineral crusts bulge upward in smooth folds, uncut by gullies. Water has begun to find paths but has not had time—or perhaps is not permitted—to deepen them. There are no old ravines, rounded boulders, weathered cliffs, or broad deltas. Every exposed surface has the raw quality of material just pressed into place: dark basalt without lichen scars, red silt without layers, pale marl splitting in clean damp seams.

And yet the vegetation is mature beyond measure.

Great low trees spread outward rather than upward, their limbs running horizontally through the humid air and rooting wherever they touch the ground. Their trunks are crowded together, wrapped in cords of vine and padded with mosses, bromeliads, liverworts, and dense shelves of fungi. Fan leaves overlap like roof scales, though they shelter no empty space beneath them. Reeds thrust up through the gaps. Ferns fill the gaps among the reeds. Smaller ferns fill the gaps among those ferns. Even the pools are occupied by floating rosettes, submerged ribbons, and fine green threads that tremble under the surface.

Nothing stands alone. A bare patch of stone, if one can be found, is crossed by roots, shaded by leaves, wet with condensation, and occupied by resting insects. The density is less like a forest than a living fabric stretched over unfinished earth.

The animal life is overwhelming.

At first it seems to be only noise. A massed metallic chirring fills the air, rising and falling without ever stopping. Deeper calls pulse from the wet ground: resonant croaks, booming throat notes, soft repetitive knocks made by creatures striking hollow roots. Wings produce a continuous papery rush overhead. Leaves click as small bodies jump between them. Water plips from ledges and from the backs of animals moving through the undergrowth.

Then individual forms begin to separate from the abundance.

Long-legged waders pace across root mats, placing wide feet carefully among beetles and small frogs. Their plumage is dark and velvety, absorbing what little light reaches them, while their bare faces shine blue and red through the vapor. Palm-sized moths cling in layers to tree boles, opening and closing wings patterned like wet mineral deposits. Schools of tiny fish travel through water only a few fingers deep, following hidden channels beneath fallen leaves. Where the channels vanish underground, the fish vanish with them, reappearing elsewhere in silver bursts.

There are lizards on nearly every exposed branch. Some are flat-bodied and leaf-colored; others carry short sails that gather condensation, channeling droplets toward their mouths. Snakes are common but difficult to distinguish from vines until a vine blinks or releases its grip and slides away. Heavy amphibians sit half buried in black mud, their backs planted with harmless algae. Clouds of gnats hang above them, and small bright birds ride on their shoulders, feeding without alarm.

Larger animals move through the middle distance in crowded herds. They are squat, heat-adapted creatures with sloping backs and broad, soft feet, suited to ground that is neither fully land nor marsh. Their hides are kept glossy by humidity. Flies cover them, and smaller animals cover the flies. They graze not by clearing vegetation but by taking scattered mouthfuls, leaving the green density apparently unchanged behind them.

Predators are present everywhere, but panic is rare. A narrow catlike animal rests along a branch over a pool, almost touching a coil of snake below and a nest of wax-coated insects above. Tooth marks, shed skins, eggshells, and bones are quickly hidden, yet nothing gives the impression of decay advancing. Fungus covers the fallen; beetles enter it; roots pass through it. The body is incorporated so smoothly that death seems less an event than a transfer of occupancy.

Despite all this activity, the place never changes.

There is no season. The water does not rise or recede. The heat does not intensify. No storm clears the vapor, though droplets always fall. Flowers remain open beside fruit already ripe, while new buds wait on the same stem. Nests contain eggs, hatchlings, and empty shell halves together. Tracks appear in the mud, but old tracks do not soften or fill. A branch may bend beneath the weight of animals and return to precisely its former place when they leave.

The raw geology never grows old. Water passes over the sharp clay edges without rounding them. Roots press against stone but do not crack it. The mineral shelves remain forever on the verge of erosion, marked by the first faint runnels of runoff, never by the second. Likewise, the forest never conquers the open slicks entirely. Seedlings crowd their borders, eternally rooted at the threshold.

This gives the basin a peculiar stillness beneath its noise. Every creature moves, feeds, calls, mates, hunts, and rests, but the total arrangement is held in equilibrium. One flock departs and another of equal size settles into the same crown. A frog is taken from a pool; another surfaces beneath the vacated leaf. Fruit falls continuously, yet the branches do not empty and the ground does not accumulate fruit. Nothing is restored because nothing is ever truly diminished.

Across the region, the same balance repeats in different forms. To one side lie wide reed flats, their shallow water hidden beneath stems and floating leaves. Beyond them, low black stone islands support tangled groves. Farther still, pale mineral terraces exhale warm seep water into fern-filled depressions. There are corridors where large animals pass shoulder to shoulder, and quieter pockets beneath leaning trunks where the sound becomes muffled but never absent.

No path is made by passage. No den becomes a structure. No species reshapes the basin enough to leave a lasting design. There are no cut branches, stacked stones, fires, vessels, snares, or signs that anything has conceived of an object separate from its living use. Shells remain shells; sticks remain branches; stone remains unstruck.

At the dim horizon, the vapor thickens until vegetation and cloud become indistinguishable. The basin may end there, or continue indefinitely in low, crowded folds. It offers no summit from which to see its whole extent. Any vantage point is quickly occupied by leaves, perched bodies, dripping growth, and wings.

The landscape is embryonic but not young. It is a first moment held open: wet stone newly surfaced, life already abundant, erosion just beginning and forever beginning. Beneath the unchanging heat, the world is crowded with motion that leaves no history.
