### The Stillworks Plateau

The plateau stands above the weather.

Far below, storms move through the lower atmosphere, but they cannot climb this high. Their clouds appear only as a faint pressure beneath the horizon, occasionally illuminated from within by distant electrical discharges. From the plateau they resemble a second, buried sky. No rain has ever reached the summit. The stone holds no channels, no mineral stains, no memory of runoff. Water exists here only inside welded tanks and old chemical diagrams, where its symbol is marked with the same hazard signs used for acids.

The upper world is warm, dry, and almost entirely dark.

There is no sun in the ordinary sense. A broad black body occupies part of the sky, distinguishable from the surrounding darkness only because it conceals the sparse stars. Its radiation warms exposed surfaces evenly. Nothing casts a proper shadow. At most, edges acquire a weak graphite sheen, and certain metals answer the invisible heat with a dull red detectable only at close range. The city was therefore built without windows. Sight was never considered reliable infrastructure.

The land beneath it is among the oldest stable surfaces on the planet. Once it may have been mountainous, volcanic, violently folded. Time has reduced all of that to long, patient inclines. Ridges have become low backs of basalt. Craters are shallow saucers filled with powdered glass. Across hundreds of kilometers, the plateau rises and falls by only a few dozen meters, although its cliffs descend nearly vertically into the storm-dark atmosphere below.

Nothing continues to erode. The loose grains have already found their final hollows. The air is too thin and still to move them. Every pebble rests where the last meaningful disturbance left it, perhaps millions of years ago.

The city occupies this nearly level expanse without filling it.

Its districts are separated by wide tracts of bare mineral pavement. A foundry may stand alone three kilometers from the next building, connected by perfectly straight rails. Banks of storage cylinders gather in sparse constellations. Gantries cross empty ground and terminate at sealed loading platforms where no vehicle waits. There are towers, but few are tall; height has little value in a place where there is no sunlight to claim and no living crowd to accommodate. Most construction spreads laterally, embedded in the plateau like a circuit diagram drawn at municipal scale.

The oldest structures are made from local black stone, cut into massive interlocking blocks. Their corners have softened, though not from wind. The rock itself has slowly relaxed under its own weight, giving walls the faintly swollen look of wax. Later buildings are iron ceramic, nickel lattice, and panels of dark glass. Pipes run everywhere—over roofs, through trenches, along bridgework—but they carry no water. They transport powdered minerals, inert gases, liquid sulfur, hot salts, and electrical charge stored as slow-moving chemical imbalance.

Many of these systems are still active.

They do not clang or roar. Their mechanisms were designed for duration rather than speed. A refinery may take nine years to complete a single rotation. Ore advances through a furnace by the width of a finger each month. Counterweights descend so gradually that their movement cannot be witnessed, only inferred by comparing survey marks left generations apart. Warmth leaks from buried industrial chambers and spreads through the streets, giving the whole settlement the temperature of skin.

The machines have reached an equilibrium in which operation and stillness are nearly the same thing.

No smoke rises. No valves vent. Production continues because stopping was never included in the design. Deep under the plateau, pressure drives turbines through chambers whose bearings have polished themselves into near-frictionless rings. The power feeds electrochemical works, maintenance systems, and long bands of low-frequency transmitters. What the factories produce is difficult to determine. At certain terminals, dense black ingots appear at intervals of several centuries. Automated carriers collect them and take them into warehouses already full of identical ingots.

The warehouses are larger inside than their footprints should permit. Surveyors disagree on whether this is a defect of geometry or accounting.

There are inhabitants, though they are rarely encountered.

Some occupy sealed residential vaults beneath the administrative quarter. Their doors open onto narrow public concourses lined with tactile inscriptions. The inscriptions are not names but descriptions of pressure, texture, and duration: **warm flange after six cycles**, **corridor with the distant pulse**, **the room where weight is less certain**. Navigation here is performed by touch and vibration. Handrails carry coded tremors. Floors answer footsteps with identifying tones too low to be heard so much as felt in the teeth.

Other inhabitants may not belong to the city at all.

On the outer flats, one sometimes finds paired tracks pressed into the mineral dust. They run straight for tens of kilometers, never wandering toward shelter or machinery. Whatever makes them has a narrow body and only two points of contact with the ground. It moves chiefly during the longest darkness, although all darkness here is nearly absolute. Maintenance records refer to these creatures as *intervals*, because sightings are logged not by location but by the silence that precedes them.

An interval feeds, if feeding is the right word, on residual magnetic alignment in ancient iron. After it passes, compass instruments point nowhere for several hours. No specimen has been captured. Traps remain untouched, but the ground beneath them sometimes becomes subtly convex, as though the plateau had inhaled.

Smaller life persists in furnace seams: thin colonies of metallic cells that replicate by encouraging crystals to grow in particular errors. They have no soft tissue and use no liquid solvent. To an untrained observer they look like tarnish. Over centuries they form branching silver geometries inside hot pipes, not unlike frost, though there is neither cold nor moisture. The city’s maintenance engines remove most of them, yet preserve selected colonies in sealed galleries. It is unclear whether this is research, reverence, or part of the industrial process.

Sound is exceptional enough to have civic significance.

Most streets are silent. Even footsteps are muffled by the fine glass powder settled between paving slabs. Now and then a deep impact passes through the ground: one of the buried presses completing its stroke. The noise arrives as a single soft concussion, followed minutes later by a second, weaker response from the far edge of the plateau. Bells mounted on abandoned towers do not ring in the air. Their clappers strike only when the stone beneath them flexes, perhaps once every few decades.

In the central district stands the Hall of Constant Conditions. It contains instruments measuring temperature, pressure, ground level, atmospheric composition, electrical potential, stellar position, and the minute strain of the plateau under the city’s weight. Their needles have not visibly moved within recorded history. Each is watched by a separate machine. Each watching machine is watched by another.

The city’s fundamental law is inscribed there:

**Nothing may be altered without proof that it has already changed.**

As a result, collapsed structures are supported exactly where they fell. Broken rails remain in service if their separation is constant. Obsolete factories continue their cycles because their output is predictable. New construction is permitted only in preexisting gaps whose boundaries can be demonstrated to have always anticipated a building.

Beyond the last industrial district, the plateau continues into darkness. Rails run out across it, spaced kilometers apart, their surfaces faintly warm. Some lead to quarry basins. Some end at the cliff. Others continue beyond the mapped region without branching or destination markers.

Standing there, one feels neither abandonment nor active occupation. The landscape is not dead, because death would constitute a change. Nor is it waiting; waiting implies expectation.

It has simply arrived at a form so ancient and exact that time can pass through without finding anything to move.
