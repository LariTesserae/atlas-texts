The plain begins below sea level, though there is no sea and perhaps never has been. It occupies a shallow continental basin whose rim is visible only on exceptionally clear days: a pale, serrated line far beyond the crowded green. The air is warm, bright, and nearly motionless. Shadows fall with ordinary clarity. Colors remain where they belong. At first glance, this may be the least strange thing about the place.

Underfoot lies a fresh mineral crust, charcoal-gray and glass-hard. It has split into sharp polygons without weathering at their edges. Needles of translucent stone stand along the fractures, clean as newly broken crystal. There is no dust in the hollows, no rounded gravel, no stain left by rain. Nothing here has been softened. The whole basin appears to have surfaced yesterday from a cooling flow, yet every measurement—growth rings, isotope clocks, the slow memory encoded in the ground—claims it has remained exactly this way for longer than the surrounding mountains have existed.

The vegetation is lavish.

It crowds every fissure and covers much of the open country in waist-high thickets, dark canopies, folded blades, and immense radial blooms. But none of it contains water. Cut a stem and no sap beads out. The interior is a dry architecture of carbon foam, metallic fibers, and translucent membranes stretched across vacuum-tight cells. Leaves are warm to the touch and rigid at their edges. Some ring softly if struck, though there is seldom anything here to strike them.

These organisms do not drink. Their roots clasp the mineral crust molecule by molecule, disassembling feldspar, basalt, salt, and trace metals into useful parts. They draw carbon directly from the air and harvest sunlight with almost perfect efficiency. Where chemistry would ordinarily require a liquid medium, their tissues use shifting electromagnetic lattices: reactions pass through the body as ordered changes of charge, more like computation than metabolism. A grove may look tangled and exuberant, but beneath the apparent disorder every leaf sits at a precise angle to every other leaf. No two cast competing shadows.

The dominant growth is called, by those who have seen it, glassleaf. It rises in dense fans two or three times the height of a person, each blade broad, emerald-black, and finely ribbed with silver. The surfaces do not flutter. There is no wind to move them, and their hinges lock in place once grown. Between them spread low carpets of saffron filaments. These are not soft: each filament is a bundle of ceramic whiskers, delicate enough to bend under a fingertip and strong enough to score steel.

Farther across the basin stand pale crown-plants with hundreds of limbs arranged around empty centers. They resemble trees built to surround trunks that are absent. Looking through one circular crown reveals another, then another, aligned across kilometers. The alignment persists regardless of where one stands. Walk sideways and the distant openings shift to remain nested. Surveyors would call this an optical effect if instruments did not make the same correction.

There are no roads, ruins, pylons, discarded tools, or deliberate clearings. Nothing suggests settlement. Yet the country is saturated with an engineering more advanced than any visible civilization could explain.

It is not constructed upon the landscape. It is the landscape.

The mineral pavement repairs fractures before they propagate. Roots exchange information without signals crossing the space between them. Each plant maintains a microscopic boundary within which certain physical constants differ slightly from those outside: enough to allow its dry biochemistry, not enough to disturb the ordinary warmth of the air. A severed leaf does not die. It simply ceases to have been attached. The cut face is smooth, and after a few moments both pieces possess uninterrupted histories.

This correction is quiet. There are no flashes or mechanical movements. One notices it only through consequences. A snapped crystal is whole when looked at again. Footprints cannot be made because the ground selects a version of itself in which it was not compressed. A traveler may bend the saffron carpet while passing, but behind them every filament stands at precisely its former angle. Even heat does not accumulate. A shaded stone and a sunlit stone remain equally warm, as if temperature here were a local law rather than an exchange.

The basin therefore never truly changes.

Its luxuriance is not growth in the familiar sense but a completed state continuously asserting itself. Buds are present, but they do not open. Flowers are open, but they do not fade. Along every branch are stages that should belong to different seasons: folded shoots, mature leaves, dry seed-vessels, all held together in one permanent abundance. Nothing advances from one condition to another. The country contains processes without progression.

This gives the greenery an unsettling freshness. There are no fallen leaves. No humus gathers beneath the thickets. No rot sweetens the warm air. The scent is instead dry and electrical: crushed stone, hot resin, ozone too faint to sting, and something like the inside of a newly opened machine. Pollen hangs between certain flowers as a golden haze, each grain suspended at a fixed point. It can be brushed aside, but when the disturbance passes, the grains occupy their old positions again.

From the low center of the basin, the land seems almost level. In fact it is divided into shallow shelves and recent-looking faults. Black escarpments rise no more than a few dozen meters, their faces raw, angular, and unworn. Forests press right to their edges. Some plants grow horizontally from the cliff walls; others root on the underside of overhangs, indifferent to gravity until they extend beyond a particular invisible plane and abruptly bend downward.

The most open tracts are fields of mineral flowers. Millions of hexagonal cups emerge directly from the crust, close enough that their rims almost touch. Their interiors are lined with green-gold mirrors. By day they face the sun. At night—if night can be trusted here—they face points below the horizon, following sources of light that no eye can see.

Daylight itself is otherwise normal. The sky is a warm, washed blue. The sun has the expected size and brilliance. Shadows sharpen toward noon and lengthen toward evening. This ordinariness makes the basin’s errors more pronounced. One can use a shadow as a clock, but the clock returns contradictory answers. Crossing a field may take three hours by the sun and eleven minutes by the body. A distant ridge grows nearer at a reasonable walking pace, then remains at the same distance through an entire afternoon. Turning back does not necessarily retrace the route, although every landmark remains visible and apparently unmoved.

Space here is not fluid or chaotic. It is overdetermined.

The basin seems to preserve several geometries at once, each stable, each locally convincing. A grove can be five hundred meters across from the outside and many kilometers across within. Two adjacent crystal needles may be separated by a handspan, while the narrow interval between them contains a dry ravine under a separate sky. Certain clearings have more perimeter than area. Certain cliffs possess two upper edges but no summit between them.

Nothing flickers. Nothing warps theatrically. Reality breaks with perfect composure.

Animal life is uncommon. Now and then a long-legged creature can be seen standing between the glassleaves. Its body is narrow, matte white, and almost featureless except for a dark seam where a face might be. It does not browse. It does not drink. It may remain motionless for days, though “days” are unreliable. If approached, it is not seen to flee; instead, the arrangement of plants gradually proves that it had always been farther away.

Small six-winged things inhabit the crown-plants, but they are encountered mostly as absences: leaf surfaces from which reflected light is missing, moving slowly against the sun. They produce no wingbeat. Their nests, if they have them, cannot be found. On rare warm evenings, a single dry clicking passes across the basin from horizon to horizon. It is answered once, from directly beneath the listener’s feet.

Otherwise the silence is broad and layered. There is no running water, no insect chorus, no rustling canopy. The still air does not sigh through the leaves. One hears clothing shift, breath enter the lungs, the faint granular sound of one’s own joints. Those noises do not carry far. Beyond a few paces, sound seems to be taken apart and stored. Some groves are believed to preserve every noise made within them, not as echoes but as latent arrangements in their branching. Touching a certain silver rib may release a footstep uttered centuries ago—or a word the listener intends to speak tomorrow.

Despite its density, the basin never feels enclosed. The vegetation forms walls, galleries, radial corridors, and vaulted tangles, yet long sightlines repeatedly open through it. Black shelves march toward distant green masses. Mirror-fields flare under the sun. Pale crowns align across the plain like apertures in an immense instrument. The whole region gives an impression of purpose without any readable objective.

Perhaps it is a machine that has learned to resemble an ecosystem. Perhaps it is an ecosystem that has matured beyond the need for time, water, predation, decay, or reproduction. Perhaps the distinction has no meaning here. There are no makers in evidence and no interface waiting for a hand. The basin offers no central chamber, no explanatory monument, no privileged point from which its design becomes clear.

Its rules can only be inferred:

Nothing may erode.

Nothing may be lost.

No state may replace another when both can be retained.

Every disturbance must be reconciled with the completed whole.

And so the place remains forever young, warm, dry, and flourishing—an untouched lowland of sharp stone and impossible leaves, extending under common daylight. It does not spread into the surrounding world. The surrounding world does not wear it down. At the rim, ordinary dust lies against immaculate black crystal, and neither crosses.

The boundary has no fence.

It does not need one.
