Beneath a pale, stony plain lies a single hollow called, by those creatures capable of naming it, the White Room.

It is not deep. In places the surface is only the thickness of a mature tree above the chamber, though no trees grow there. The ceiling consists of limestone, salt, and a translucent mineral resembling cloudy amber. Daylight passes through it in a broad diffusion. There is no visible sun below, no opening onto the sky, yet the whole chamber shines. At midday it is nearly shadowless, bright enough to make the floor painful to look at. Toward evening the ceiling turns honey-colored, then rose, then slowly gray.

The chamber is cathedral-sized but almost entirely empty. Its floor slopes gently from dry shelves at the perimeter into a shallow central depression. Nothing interrupts that long surface except a few stone ribs, a scattering of low plants, and several basins no wider than sleeping animals. The emptiness gives every isolated thing unusual importance: one dark pebble, one upright stem, one pale shell can be seen from the opposite wall.

The rock has been weathering for a very long time. There are no fresh breaks. Every edge has softened into a lip, a saddle, or a shallow runnel. The walls fold inward like cloth pressed by an enormous hand. Mineral skins lie one over another: chalk white, old yellow, bruised violet, and thin greenish stains where water sometimes passes. Some surfaces are smooth from condensation; others have been fretted into thousands of tiny cups. The chamber no longer seems to be enlarging. Dissolution and deposition have reached a slow accord. What water removes during the wet season, the dry season returns as a film of salt.

Rain reaches the White Room without falling into it directly. When storms cross the plain overhead, moisture seeps through the translucent roof. At first the ceiling merely darkens. Then beads appear everywhere, each bright as polished glass. They grow for hours before dropping.

The drops fall separately, sometimes minutes apart. Each one strikes with a precise, enlarged sound.

The stone swallows the echo.

After sustained rain, the central depression fills ankle-deep. Narrow channels become moving threads. For several days the room contains a clear, warm lake, so shallow that its rippled light runs along the ceiling. Then the water withdraws into cracks and porous beds beneath the floor. Pockets remain in shaded hollows, slowly shrinking and becoming mineral-rich. In the driest months, moisture survives only as dawn condensation and as coolness held beneath flat stones.

Vegetation is sparse and low. Around the temporary pools grow jointed gray reeds, seldom more than knee-high. Their stems are hollow and translucent, with only a few narrow leaves held close to the stalk. They do not flower every year. Most of the time they resemble bundles of fine bone. After deep wetting, however, their tips open into blue-black fans that absorb the diffuse light and warm noticeably beneath the hand.

Elsewhere there are coin-sized mats of velvet moss, chalk lichens almost indistinguishable from the floor, and solitary bladder plants rooted in cracks. The bladders store seepage behind clear membranes. Small animals pierce them during dry periods, and the plants respond by sealing the wound with a milky resin. An old bladder plant may carry hundreds of such scars.

Despite the bare appearance, the chamber is crowded with animal life. Most of it occupies surfaces and fissures rather than open space. Minute glass mites graze on the ceiling’s mineral algae. Pale, long-legged runners patrol the walls. Soft-shelled beetles sleep under flakes of stone, emerging after condensation to drink from individual droplets. Hundreds may gather around a pool without making an audible sound.

The largest common animals are low, warm-bodied creatures shaped somewhat like lizards, though they have six limbs and no visible eyes. Along their backs lie rows of light-sensitive pits. In full brightness they become almost white; at dusk they darken and move into the open. They feed on reed shoots, beetles, and the soft mineral crust around fresh seepage. Their padded feet leave prints so faint that they disappear as the salt rises again.

These animals use simple tools. They select thin chips of limestone and hold them between the smaller front limbs to open plant bladders or scrape shelled prey from cracks. Some carry a useful chip for several days. None shape the chamber, build nests, or keep permanent stores. Their tools are found rather than made, and when abandoned they dissolve back into the damp floor. Intelligence here leaves no ruin. At most, one may notice that certain stones have been turned over repeatedly, or that the bladder plants near favored resting places bear unusually neat cuts.

There are also ribbon-winged fliers, abundant after rain. They hatch from cysts embedded in the dry mud and rise in silent, wavering columns toward the luminous ceiling. Their wings are too soft to buzz. For two or three evenings the upper air fills with them, though from the floor they look like faint scratches moving across milk glass. Ceiling predators wait with mouths open beneath the stone, attached by broad adhesive feet. When satiated, they release their grip and descend slowly on stretched membranes, like falling leaves.

The chamber remains mostly quiet. Water supplies its loudest events. A drip. A brief runnel whispering through grit. The papery collapse of a dried reed. Occasionally one of the six-legged grazers makes a low throat-click, answered from far across the room after a long pause. Sound does not travel cleanly. The porous walls absorb it, so that even nearby movement can seem distant.

Time is measured less by days than by stains. Wet years leave dark arcs around the basins. Dry decades lay white crust over them. Reed colonies advance a hand’s width and retreat again. Channels shift by fractions. A fallen body may be consumed overnight, but its mineral outline remains for generations: a delicate hollow bordered in salt.

The strangest feature appears only when the central basin is almost dry. Beneath the last film of water, the stone shows a pattern of shallow depressions resembling footprints—hundreds of them, all directed toward the center. They are too old and rounded to identify. Some have six toes, some four, some no recognizable anatomy at all. None lead away.

At the exact center is a narrow hole into deeper darkness. Water drains through it without sound. No animal willingly crosses its rim, although insects sometimes gather around it facing inward. The diffuse light does not enter far. A person lying beside it would feel warm air rising at intervals, each breath carrying the scent of wet clay and something green growing far below.

Nothing emerges. Nothing has emerged within the memory of the chamber’s oldest creatures.

Still, after every rain, the footprints become a little clearer.
