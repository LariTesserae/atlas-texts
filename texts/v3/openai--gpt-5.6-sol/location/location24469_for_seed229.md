The high place is called the Rattlecourt, though there is no court and no one remembers who named it.

It occupies a shallow basin near the crown of a black mesa: a bounded hollow perhaps three hundred paces across, large enough to feel like a world from its center and small enough that every edge can be reached before the light changes. Around it stand low cliffs of pumice, rust-red basalt, and pale mineral crust. The cliffs have been weathered into fluted pillars and blunt alcoves. Their forms are old and settled. Nothing here looks newly broken, although things are always breaking.

There is no water.

The stone contains no dampness; the air carries no mist. Dust does not cake or darken. It remains powder forever, flowing in dry sheets around ankles and roots. Life in the Rattlecourt is built from oils, waxes, mineral salts, and a hot amber solvent that circulates slowly through the native organisms. When exposed, this sap does not drip. It pulls itself into trembling beads, hardens, and leaps toward the nearest source of static charge.

The basin is warm even in darkness. Heat enters through cracks in the mesa, breathing from the rock in irregular pulses. The ground is a mosaic of warm plates separated by narrow seams of colder glass. A bare foot can cross from comfort to pain in a single step.

Vegetation is lush, but not crowded. The largest plants stand far apart, each claiming a generous circle of dust and stone. Their separation gives the hollow the grave spacing of an orchard or a ruined ceremonial grove. Between them lies open ground, yet every plant is extravagant.

There are fan-trees with squat, cork-colored trunks and crowns of thousands of black-green vanes. Each vane is thin as hammered leaf metal and edged with pale cilia. They do not seek sunlight so much as motion. In the constant wind they open, fold, invert, and clap. A single tree can produce the sound of heavy cloth shaken above a fire; the whole grove makes a rolling, percussive thunder.

Elsewhere grow spindle-bushes, no higher than a person’s knee. Their branches terminate in translucent bulbs filled with golden resin and captive grains of magnetite. As the bushes sway, the grains strike the inner walls of the bulbs. The resulting chiming is delicate at close range, but hundreds of bulbs, excited by a gale, create a shrill metallic cloud that can be heard from the slopes below.

Broad mats of scale-lichen cover the shaded stone. They are layered in violet, ivory, and soot-blue plates, each plate hard and glossy. During quiet periods they seem inert. When the ground warms, the plates hinge upward one by one, exposing scarlet undersides to the air. From above, the change moves across the basin as a complicated signal. No one knows whether the lichens are feeding, speaking, or warning the things beneath them.

The light is dim and coppery. A permanent belt of airborne grit circles the summit, driven by opposing winds that meet over the mesa. The sun appears only as a pale coin behind this moving veil. Shadows have soft edges, except during the electrical flashes that pass between tree crowns. Then every trunk, animal, and standing stone is printed in black against the ground.

The air is almost never still.

Gales descend one wall of the basin, strike the warm updrafts, and split into spinning columns. These whirlwinds are small enough to cross the Rattlecourt without escaping it. They comb the fan-trees, uproot temporary growths, and carry loose husks high into the dark. At times three or four vortices circle the hollow at once, colliding and rebounding as though they possess weight. Their paths have polished shallow rings into the rock.

This turbulence is not merely weather. It is part of the ecology.

Seeds here are many-legged things. They scuttle when the air is calm and become airborne when it strengthens. Some bury themselves; others climb exposed stone and wait to be thrown. A mature seed may spend years traveling around the basin before choosing a crack in which to root. Plants that cannot move their offspring quickly are stripped by grazers.

The animals are abundant, though few are large. The ground shivers with nail-mites, copper-bodied creatures that travel in dense processions between hot fissures. Kitebacks leap from the cliffs on six jointed legs, unfold membranes along their spines, and allow the crosswinds to fling them from tree to tree. Their calls are hard, abrupt knocks made by snapping their jaw plates.

In the upper branches live choirs of throatless, pale animals called bellows. Each is no larger than a fist. They breathe through pleated sacs along their flanks, and when alarmed they expel air in booming notes much deeper than their bodies should permit. A disturbed colony sounds like doors slamming in a distant stone hall.

Predators follow vibration rather than scent. Long, almost weightless needle-cats crouch beneath the scale-lichen, their bodies supported on eight reed-thin limbs. They emerge during the loudest storms, when prey cannot distinguish footfall from the drumming trees. Their mouths are circular grinding plates. They drink nothing. They rasp resin, fat, shell, and bone into powder, then press the powder into warm pellets inside the body.

Above everything circle the crown-wheels. They may be animals, or airborne portions of some rooted organism. Each consists of a ring of black cartilage hung with featherlike vanes. They turn endlessly in the rising heat, steering without visible effort. Occasionally one descends around a spindle-bush and remains there, spinning, until either the bush dies or the wheel grows large enough to rise again.

Amid this life are scattered marks of habitation.

Against the northern wall stand three small shelters made from fitted basalt and fan-tree cork. Their roofs are weighted with slabs to resist the wind. None has a door; each entrance bends twice so the gales cannot enter directly. The walls are scarred by stone tools and patched with glossy resin. Inside are shallow sleeping hollows, racks of bone pegs, chipped knives, woven vane-fiber masks, and clay vessels filled not with liquid but with powdered salts.

A low circular structure occupies the basin’s center. It is only waist-high, assembled from upright stone ribs. Threads of dried tendon run between them. The wind plucks these threads continuously. Their tones change with heat, charge, and direction, making the structure both instrument and instrument panel. Those who understand it can hear a dangerous updraft before it arrives, tell when the amber sap is rising in the trees, and recognize the hunting rhythm of needle-cats beneath the ground.

Near the western rim, a ladder of antler and braided fiber climbs to a ledge covered in handprints. The prints were made with soot, sulfur, crushed lichen, and metallic dust. Many have six fingers. Some are far too small. One set begins on the wall and continues across the underside of an overhang where no ordinary climber could have reached.

The inhabitants, when present, move quickly and crouch often. They wear layers of barkcloth tied close to the limbs, goggles cut from smoky crystal, and broad collars that prevent kitebacks from fastening onto the neck. Metal is rare: a few beaten copper hooks, needles, and resonant strips kept as treasured tools. Most work is done with basalt flakes, horn wedges, abrasive dust, twisted fiber, and patient leverage.

Fire is used cautiously. Open flame consumes the dry, oil-rich air too eagerly. Cooking is done by placing food between geothermal stones. Light comes from jars of charge-moss, whose pale filaments glow when shaken. At night—or during the deeper dimness that passes for night—the shelters pulse faintly whenever the wind strikes them.

The Rattlecourt has several rules.

Nothing dead remains quiet. Husks roll, bones whistle, shells become nesting chambers, and abandoned tools are soon threaded with lichen or carried aloft by vortices.

Nothing living depends on stillness. Plants fold before impact. Burrowing animals surface when the ground closes around them. Eggs roll toward heat. Even the stone seems to participate: thin mineral sheets expand, snap free, and sail across the hollow like blades.

Sound is more reliable than sight, but sound also lies. Echoes circle the basin several times. A call may return from the wrong wall, altered in pitch, long after its maker has moved. Some creatures imitate these returning echoes rather than the original voices, producing calls that seem to have no source.

And no shape is guaranteed to persist. A storm can strip every vane from a fan-tree in minutes, leaving a black skeleton. By the next warm interval, the fallen vanes may have rooted where they land, forming a low forest of clattering blades. A lichen field can hinge open and reveal a stairway that was invisible the day before. One of the whirlwinds sometimes gathers dust, husks, bones, and static-bound resin into a temporary walking figure. It may cross the basin in a dozen deliberate steps, collapse at the cliff, and leave behind a neat pile of objects from different centuries.

The place is old enough that these transformations no longer feel like ruin. They are its equilibrium.

From the rim, the Rattlecourt appears sparse: a few dark crowns, scattered structures, open mineral ground beneath a dim sky. From within, it is overwhelming. Vanes hammer. Resin bulbs ring. Bellows boom in the branches. Kitebacks crack their jaws. Stone threads hum. Dust races knee-high across the ground, and crown-wheels turn above like pieces of a vast, invisible machine.

There are brief pauses, sometimes lasting five or six breaths, when the winds cancel one another.

In those pauses every organism becomes still.

The fan-trees hold their vanes open. The mites stop in their paths. The airborne wheels hang without turning. Even the geothermal cracks seem to cease breathing.

Then, from somewhere below the basin, comes a single immense knock.

The whole Rattlecourt answers at once.
