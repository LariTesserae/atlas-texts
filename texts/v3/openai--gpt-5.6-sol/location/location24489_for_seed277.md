### The Blackworks Plateau

The plateau begins above the weather.

Its rim rises through a permanent belt of cloud, but the upper country is warm: a vast, dark tableland holding the day’s heat beneath a sky that rarely brightens beyond deep violet. No sun is visible. Light arrives indirectly, sifted through miles of high haze and the broad ash-colored rings of the planet overhead. At its brightest, the landscape resembles iron seen just before cooling—forms discernible, distances uncertain, every color reduced to rust, charcoal, and bruised blue.

The ground is old and fully weathered. Long erosion has not softened it so much as taught it a stable shape. There are shallow basins hundreds of kilometers across, stair-stepped scarps, fields of low black ridges, and dry channels winding with the confidence of rivers despite carrying water only a few nights each season. Nothing looks newly broken. Even the cliffs have rounded lips and fluted faces, their fallen stone already fitted into dense pavements below.

Much of the plateau is basalt, but not inert basalt. Heat moves under it in slow, regulated currents. The crust expands after dark and contracts during the dim interval that passes for day, opening hairline vents in repeating sequences. Through these vents the plateau breathes.

Warm vapor rises first. Then metallic fumes, faintly luminous. Then a fine black dust that drifts close to the ground before settling in parallel bands. From far away, entire valleys appear to smoke with the disciplined spacing of industrial exhaust stacks, though there are no stacks, no buildings, and no makers. The machinery is geological. Deep chambers separate minerals by heat and pressure; subterranean rivers turn stone turbines grown from crystalline vanes; electric charge accumulates in salt seams and discharges along buried networks. The land refines its own ore.

These processes are almost silent. Most of their force passes below hearing, felt through the feet as a slow pulse or in the rib cage as a pressure without sound. Now and then a vent seals with a soft iron click. A cliff gives a distant granular sigh. Condensation ticks from stone needles into hidden pools.

Water is never plentiful, but it is rarely altogether absent. It gathers where darkness is deepest: beneath overhangs, inside lava tubes, along the cold faces of mineral fins. During the plateau’s brief rains, enormous clouds condense almost at once, releasing warm, heavy drops over isolated districts while neighboring basins remain dry. The rain may last ten minutes. It may last two days. Channels fill abruptly, spreading into shallow copper-colored sheets that travel for hundreds of kilometers and vanish into porous ground.

Where a flood has passed, life appears with alarming speed.

Vegetation is sparse enough that each plant seems solitary and deliberate. Low mats of black-green tissue lie sealed beneath translucent crusts, unfolding when wet into broad pleated fans. Tall spindle-plants stand kilometers apart, their trunks mineralized and hollow, their crowns no more than a handful of silver blades. They do not reach toward light. They orient toward electrical gradients in the ground, slowly turning over decades to face the nearest active fault.

Along drainage lines grow thickets of wiregrass: stiff, conductive filaments that collect static until their tips glow dull red. When rain touches them, current passes from stem to stem. A whole valley may illuminate in branching patterns for a few seconds, revealing animals everywhere.

The abundance of fauna is otherwise easy to miss.

Most creatures are dark, low, and widely dispersed, spread over distances suited to the scale of the plateau. Herds can number in the millions and still seem sparse. They move along ancient migration corridors, following underground warmth rather than surface water. Seen from a high escarpment, they resemble drifting grains: six-legged grazers with heat-reflective shells; narrow-bodied runners whose feet divide into fans on loose dust; broad, slow animals carrying colonies of pale symbionts beneath their armor.

Many species feed not on plants but on what the earth vents. They cluster around fumaroles to strain metallic particles from the air. Some drag magnetized mouthparts through black sediment, separating edible bacterial films from powdered stone. Others press their bellies against warm rock and graze directly on electrochemical reactions, their internal organs arranged like stacks of living cells.

Predators follow the electrical wakes of their prey. They are nearly blind and make little noise. A hunting animal may remain motionless on a ridge for three nights, reading tremors through a fringe of sensitive limbs. When it attacks, there is no roar—only a brief scrape, a rush of displaced grit, then stillness again.

Above them, the sky has its own migration. Vast floating organisms cross the plateau’s upper air, buoyed by sacs of hot hydrogen. Their undersides trail feeding tendrils through the haze. Small winged scavengers ride these cables, climbing and dropping between the aerial herds and the ground. During storms, the floaters descend until their bodies pass just above the ridges, luminous organs pulsing through the cloud like furnace windows.

Nothing here has built a structure, yet the landscape repeatedly produces the impression of one.

Basalt columns form halls that extend beyond sight. Mineral terraces resemble loading platforms. Hexagonal sinkholes open in ordered fields. Vents activate in sequence from horizon to horizon, and streams of red-hot brine travel through channels lined with glass. There are cycles of intake, separation, transport, cooling, and waste. Slag-like stone accumulates in long embankments, though no hand has piled it. Crystals grow into toothed wheels around geothermal shafts and turn by fractions of a degree each year.

The plateau has reached this state over immense time. Its apparent industry is not a ruin and not a beginning. It is an ecology of pressure: geology borrowing the logic of organs, organisms borrowing the logic of engines. Nothing dominates for long. Vents clog; burrowing animals open them. Mineral feeders become too numerous; the electrical fields weaken and their breeding stops. Floods wash conductive dust into the lowlands; wiregrass blooms there and draws lightning, fusing the soil into fresh glass.

The darkness is part of this balance. Bright light is dangerous. On rare occasions the haze thins and the hidden sun strikes the plateau directly. Animals flatten themselves beneath ledges. Plants fold into mineral sheaths. Pools begin to steam. The exposed ground shines with unbearable bronze brilliance, and chemical activity races across it. For a few hours the Blackworks operates too quickly: vents roar, charge leaps between ridges, spores burst, hunters gorge, and flash floods rise from sudden meltwater in the distant high scarps.

Then the sky closes. The land cools back to warmth. Motion becomes measured again.

At ordinary times, a traveler could stand in one place and hear almost nothing. Yet the silence would not feel empty. The ground would pulse underfoot. Far off, a red line would move along the base of a cliff as subterranean heat found a new seam. A herd would cross the horizon over the course of hours. Vapor would gather on the stone, bead by bead, until one drop became heavy enough to fall.

The drop would strike dust.

Around that tiny wet mark, dormant things would begin to open.
