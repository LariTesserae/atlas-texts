### The High Green

From a distance, the country appears almost level.

Its horizon is a long green line, softened by warm haze, with no obvious mountains rising above it. Only when approached does its true elevation become clear. The apparent plain is the worn summit of an ancient massif, lifted nearly into the upper air and then eroded for so long that its peaks have been reduced to broad tables, shallow domes, and immense terraces. The valleys are not visible until one reaches their edges. There the land simply ends.

Beyond each rim lies a depth filled with light: ravines several thousand meters deep, their walls rounded and furrowed by ages of weather. Far below, rivers show as brief silver threads before passing under foliage or into shadow. Across the gaps, other green plateaus seem to float at the same height, separated by miles of warm, empty air.

The rock is old enough to have lost most of its sharpness. Cliffs curve inward like the sides of weathered bones. Harder strata remain as ledges, but even these have been softened into benches and overhangs. In places the original stone is exposed: dark, fine-grained, and polished by mist, with shallow channels that have carried intermittent rain for uncounted seasons. Elsewhere, deep erosion has hollowed the mountain from within. Wind moves through galleries and vertical shafts, making the plateau sound larger than it looks.

Yet very little bare stone remains on top.

The green is not a covering laid over the country. It is the country.

A continuous vegetal mat spans the summit: roots, braided stems, corky rhizomes, mosslike cushions, and enormous flattened creepers layered over one another until they form a ground several meters thick. Soil exists only in pockets within this living mass. When cut, the surface reveals pale fibers, water-bearing tissues, old brown layers, and dark cavities occupied by insects. A footstep falls not on earth but on a resilient weave that yields slowly and then rises again.

The largest plants grow sideways. Wind and exposure discourage height, so trunks crawl along the plateau, joining and separating in a network without clear individuals. Their upper surfaces carry groves of leaves; their undersides hang roots into fissures or trail them over cliff edges. Some runners are broad enough to serve as roads. Others arch across narrow chasms, forming bridges that thicken with age as new growth wraps around the old.

In the open interior, vegetation rarely rises above the knee. This gives the high country an astonishing emptiness. One can see for many kilometers across undulating fields of silver-green blades and low, leathery fans. There are no conventional forests to interrupt the view, no dense ranks of trunks. The mass of plant life is compressed into the surface, while above it opens an enormous volume of sky.

Near escarpments, the pattern changes. There the living ground spills downward.

Curtains of vine descend the cliffs, sometimes for hundreds of meters, adhering to damp seams and spreading into suspended gardens. Root cables hang free where the rock has fallen away. They twist in the rising air, bearing leaves only where condensation is reliable. Old cables may be thicker than towers, yet they can hang with nothing around them except swallows, mist, and the distant wall of another plateau.

Water is present, but rarely abundant in one place. Rain comes in brief warm bursts, usually in the afternoon. It drums hard upon the plant mat, fills every leaf-cup and root hollow, and vanishes quickly into the living substrate. For several hours afterward the plateau gleams. Shallow pools collect in the sagging centers of giant leaves. Clear water rises underfoot when the mat is pressed. Along the margins, runoff gathers into sudden waterfalls that pour into the ravines and are blown apart before reaching the bottom.

During dry intervals, moisture comes from cloud.

Warm air ascends the cliffs each morning and condenses beneath overhanging leaves. Droplets gather on roots, travel along fine hairs, and fall into hidden reservoirs inside swollen stems. Many animals drink without ever seeing open water. At certain places the plant floor has been opened deliberately, exposing natural cisterns: cool chambers lined with white root tissue, holding rain from weeks before.

The climate is warm without being oppressive. Sunlight is bright and ordinary, though the extreme height lends distant objects an unusual clarity. Clouds pass at eye level. Their shadows move visibly across entire districts, cooling the plants and setting off waves of leaf-folding. Nights are mild on the sheltered terraces and abruptly cold only where wind pours over the rims.

The emptiness of the plateau is deceptive because almost every surface is inhabited.

The fauna is beyond counting.

Small creatures live within the green floor in such numbers that the mat is always faintly in motion. Beetles polish tunnels between roots. Pale grazers rasp dead tissue from lower layers. Thumb-sized crabs occupy the rain pockets, sealing themselves into wet chambers when drought returns. Antlike colonies cultivate soft fungi inside hollow runners, while long-bodied burrowers move beneath them and occasionally cause whole stretches of ground to tremble.

Above the surface, the great herds are insects.

They emerge in regional waves. Black-winged seed eaters pass low over the plateau in dense, streaming fronts. Copper-colored pollinators fill flowering grounds until every blossom seems to vibrate. During the warmest weeks, immense translucent fliers climb from the ravines on rising air. Their wings catch the sun like thin glass, and their bodies cast moving shadows over the fields.

Birds follow them in numbers nearly as great. Hook-billed hunters patrol the cliff rims. Long-winged flock birds remain aloft for days, sleeping in the upward currents. Heavy grazing birds walk the plant plain in loose companies, clipping new shoots with broad beaks and carrying entire communities of mites and bright parasites in their plumage. At dusk they gather around warm vents in the root mat, their calls passing from one plateau to the next.

Larger animals are fewer, but only by comparison. Six-legged browsers move slowly across the open country, spreading their weight over wide, padded feet. They travel between water pockets by remembering the taste of particular root roads. Low predators hide not behind trees but beneath the ground-cover, lifting flexible plates of vegetation and slipping through its hollow interior. Cliff-dwelling animals descend root curtains with turned-back claws, feeding on fruit that grows along the vertical face.

Because so much life occupies thin layers or open air, the land still looks vacant. A traveler may see nothing nearby except low green ground and sky, while millions of animals pass beneath their feet and a migration darkens the air five kilometers away.

The noise is constant and, at its height, deafening.

There is wind in the chasms, booming through cavities in the rock. There is the hiss of leaves turning together, the crack of contracting stems, and the deep groan of root bridges under strain. Waterfalls roar briefly after rain. Birds call from the cliffs, their cries multiplied by echo.

But the insects dominate.

At dawn the hidden colonies begin with a low vibration felt through the legs. By noon it has risen into a metallic, overlapping shriek. Different species pulse at different intervals, so the sound seems to move across the plateau in broad bands. When a cloud shadow passes, one chorus stops and another begins. During mass emergence, speech is possible only mouth to ear. The plant mat itself resonates, carrying the sound farther than air would.

The scattered people of the heights build accordingly.

Their dwellings are few and widely separated, usually placed where an old stone outcrop breaks through the living floor. Walls are woven from dried root strips around frames of bent branchwood. Roofs are layered with waterproof leaves and weighted against the wind by stone disks. Nothing is dug deeply: to cut too far into the mat risks opening animal galleries, draining a cistern, or injuring one of the great structural runners that holds a terrace together.

Metal is rare. Tools are made from chipped cliff stone, shell, tooth, fired clay, and the hard silicified knots found in ancient stems. Root fibers become rope, baskets, ladders, sandals, and suspension lines. Narrow bridges are maintained by grafting living cables together rather than by constructing dead spans. A well-made crossing may outlive generations, growing stronger so long as its leaves are pruned and its water channels kept clear.

There are also older signs of habitation. On isolated ledges stand roofless circles of stone almost swallowed by vines. Handholds descend cliff faces toward caves no longer accessible. Lines of pale posts cross some plateaus, each post grown from a deliberately trained plant, marking routes between cisterns. No one is certain who began them. Since the posts still bud every wet season, they do not resemble ruins so much as customs the landscape has continued without people.

Life here obeys several practical rules.

Weight must be spread. Fire must be small and raised from the living ground. Water may be taken from a stem, but the cut must be sealed with clay. Root bridges are crossed one traveler at a time, except when the wind is high, when the bridge itself becomes more stable under distributed weight. No shelter is built in a silent patch, because silence means either poisoned sap beneath the surface or a buried predator close enough to suppress the insects.

Most importantly, one watches the clouds below as carefully as those above.

A white filling of the ravines means calm weather and reliable condensation. Dark streamers rising along the walls announce rain. A sudden clearing beneath the plateau warns of descending air: within minutes, warm winds may reverse and rush outward through every chasm, stripping loose leaves, scattering flocks, and setting the hanging gardens swinging against the cliffs.

The country is ancient, but not still. Its stone has reached an almost final shape, worn beyond grandeur into vast, quiet curves. Upon that old foundation, life is urgent and excessive. Every shower rearranges the surface. Every migration strips one region and fertilizes another. Bridges grow, terraces collapse, roots catch the fallen debris, and new ground forms suspended over emptiness.

From afar, it remains a simple green line.

Up close, it is a continent-thick living skin stretched across the summit of a nearly vanished mountain, warm under the sun, hollow beneath the feet, and roaring with more lives than the eye can hold.
