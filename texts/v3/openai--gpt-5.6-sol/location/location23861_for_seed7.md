At first it seems to be a cavern, though its boundaries are too deliberate for geology. The floor extends perhaps two hundred paces from wall to wall, low in places and rising elsewhere into shelves of newly broken black stone. Overhead, the ceiling disappears into heat and darkness. Only when condensation gathers along certain straight seams does the architecture reveal itself: enormous fitted slabs, rusted trusses, pipes thick as tree trunks. The chamber may have been excavated, built, or enclosed around some older hollow. None of those possibilities quite accounts for its shape.

The air is hot enough to make breath feel borrowed. Moisture does not fill the place evenly. Most surfaces are dry, warm, and faintly greasy, but water collects wherever cold pipes pass behind the walls. There it beads, swells, and falls in brief local rains. A patch of floor may receive a shower for three minutes and remain dry for days afterward. Shallow basins stand under the most reliable drips, their rims bright with mineral crust. There is no stream, only a system of temporary wetness: black puddles, sweating metal, damp seams, and narrow channels cut to carry water that seldom comes.

Plant life occupies these wet places extravagantly.

Great leaves rise around the basins, dark green to the point of blackness. They are broad, ribbed, and glossy, and many have grown vertically to catch falling droplets rather than light. Pale root-columns descend from overhead conduits. Mats of copper-colored creepers wrap valves and broken insulators, while soft fernlike crowns flourish inside open machine housings. In one corner, a bank of vegetation has swallowed a stairway so completely that only the regular angle of its slope remains.

Yet the chamber is not crowded. The lushness occurs in islands, each separated from the next by long stretches of bare clinker, fractured concrete, and sharp extrusions of recent basalt. A single dripping joint may support a grove dense enough to hide a person; ten paces away there is nothing but dust and heat. The emptiness between these groves makes each one seem self-contained, almost intentional, like a collection of gardens maintained by an absent intelligence.

There are no animals. No insects crawl beneath the leaves. No worms disturb the damp soil. Flowers, where they occur, are closed structures without scent or nectar: waxy cups that collect condensation and feed it directly into their stems. Nothing here requires pollination. Seeds are rare. Most plants propagate by runners, fragments, and patient division. A broken leaf pressed against wet metal will slowly grow roots along its wound.

Some may not be plants at all.

Certain upright forms resemble reeds until their skins peel and show braided filaments inside, green tissue threaded through tarnished wire. Black fronds emerge from ceramic junction boxes without having forced their way in; they appear connected to the terminals. When residual current passes through the old grid, these fronds stiffen and turn their faces toward the nearest live conductor. Hours later they relax. Their growth follows electricity as ordinary vegetation might follow the sun.

Light is scarce and never broad. Tiny amber lamps survive behind wire cages, their filaments pulsing at intervals too slow to be useful. Along the floor, mineral films emit a low bluish haze where warm condensate crosses rust. Neither source illuminates more than a few nearby objects. The chamber is therefore assembled in glimpses: a wet leaf, the edge of a gantry, a red valve wheel, a cliff of fresh stone. Beyond each glimpse is depthless dark.

The industrial remains are scattered rather than dominant. A pump stands alone in the center of the floor, mounted on a concrete plinth and surrounded by emptiness. Its casing has split along a clean, recent crack, exposing an impeller furred with roots. A short rail line emerges from beneath a collapse, runs forty paces, and ends at an unbroken wall. Three narrow service huts cling to one side of the chamber. Their doors are open, but their interiors contain only stripped control panels, mineral deposits, and old heat.

Nothing appears ancient. Rust exists, but it has not yet eaten through the heaviest steel. Broken rock keeps sharp edges. The basalt ridges are glassy and raw, as though pushed up only recently from below. Some columns have bent without gathering dust. Leaves cover machines quickly, but beneath them the paint remains red or yellow. The whole place has the uneasy freshness of a ruin created ahead of schedule.

Its machinery is not entirely dead. Far below the floor, turbines or pistons maintain a nearly imperceptible cycle. Every several hours, the concrete warms by a fraction. Pipes lengthen with soft ticks. Pressure accumulates somewhere unseen, and a valve exhales once—quietly, almost politely—releasing a ribbon of vapor. Condensation then begins along the ceiling seams. A few minutes of rain follow. The basins deepen; leaves bow beneath droplets; mineral channels shine. Then the pipes cool, the dripping stops, and the chamber returns to stillness.

These events are the loudest things here.

Otherwise there is only the occasional fall of water and the minute creak of roots tightening around metal. Sound does not travel naturally. The walls seem to absorb it, or the hot air bends it upward. A dropped tool produces a flat impact with no echo. Voices, if raised, become strangely small. Across the chamber, moving water can be seen without being heard.

The place has its own slow rules. Water follows machinery before it follows gravity. Growth gathers around voltage, cold, and trace metals. Heat comes from beneath but does not rise evenly; invisible pockets remain cool enough to turn breath white for an instant. Any unattended object acquires a pale root fringe along its lower edge, provided it contains iron. Glass remains untouched. Copper disappears fastest.

At the far end stands the chamber’s most complete structure: a narrow tower of riveted plates, no taller than a house, built around a vertical bore. Vegetation forms a circular grove around it but does not cross its threshold. Inside, a shaft descends into a dim red warmth. The bore gives off no sound. At long intervals, however, the tower settles downward by a few millimeters. The movement is so slow that it can only be inferred from scraped paint, buckled roots, and fresh metal exposed along its base.

Perhaps the factory is sinking. Perhaps something beneath it is growing.

Either way, the chamber changes by accumulation rather than event. Stone lifts. Steel lowers. Water arrives in brief permissions. The dark groves thicken around their isolated sources, while the dry spaces remain broad and bare. There is no struggle between ecology and industry here. With no animals to consume, scatter, or disturb it, life has entered into a quieter arrangement with the machines: leaf, wire, root, current, condensation, heat.

Nothing hurries. Even collapse seems to be proceeding one rivet at a time.
