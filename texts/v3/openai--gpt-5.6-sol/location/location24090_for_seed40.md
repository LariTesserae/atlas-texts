The plateau has no ground.

What passes for ground is a continuous assembly of pale ceramic plates, riveted iron, vitreous slag, and immense load-bearing members whose lower ends are hidden in luminous depth. The plates overlap like scales. Between them run gutters, inspection trenches, rails, cable channels, and seams packed with black sealant. Some plates are broad enough to hold a district; others flex faintly beneath a boot. None are old. Their edges remain sharp, their stamped numbers legible. Even where weather has begun its work, the damage is shallow: orange freckles around a bolt, a fan of mineral stain beneath a leaking joint, a few millimeters of glass worn cloudy by condensation.

The whole constructed tableland stands high in a cool atmosphere. Its outer reaches vanish into white distance, not because the landscape is empty, but because there is too much of it. Towers, pressure stacks, suspended roads, reflector fields, reservoirs, and scaffolded ridges crowd every horizon. They are packed closely, layer behind layer, yet long alignments through them create startling vistas. One may look down a service avenue and see forty kilometers of repeated arches, each framing the next, narrowing to a bright needle.

There are no natural mountains. The mountains are machines that have forgotten the distinction.

They rise in stepped masses of clinker block and ribbed alloy, their slopes composed of heat exchangers, abandoned assembly halls, switchback conveyors, and ranks of sealed doors. Some summits carry rotating mirrors. Others are cut flat and crowded with ventilation organs like forests of silver trunks. Their profiles are too angular to be mistaken for geology, though at great distance the pipes blur into cliffs and the clustered chimneys become serrated peaks.

Everything shines.

The sky is thin and almost colorless, and the light arrives without warmth. It strikes the pale surfaces until the plateau seems dusted with salt. Nickel roofs throw hard rectangles onto neighboring walls. Pools of standing water become sheets of white fire. Shadows are intensely blue and astonishingly clean. There is seldom mist except inside the lower works, but halos gather around certain towers even on clear days, as if their outlines are slightly misplaced.

Rain comes infrequently and with peculiar precision. It may fall along one narrow corridor for six hours while adjacent roofs remain dry. Sometimes it descends inside buildings but not outside them. The drops are cool, heavy, and slow. They collect in bolt sockets, warped panels, elevator pits, and the cupped palms of maintenance statues. Every depression becomes a temporary pond. Gutters carry the water toward thousands of cisterns, though not always downhill. In places, streams climb sloping channels in trembling silver threads, drawn toward pumps that were removed generations ago.

The plateau is damp without being lush. Condensation beads continually on the cold sides of pressure vessels. It slicks ladders, darkens concrete, and hangs under bridges in long rows of drops. Deep in the machinery, where air is compressed and released, water forms chambers of its own: flooded turbine vaults, cistern caverns, submerged tram tunnels, little black lakes crossed by corroded catwalks. Above, the exposed districts can remain dry for weeks.

Plants survive in these wet pockets, but sparsely. Black moss grows in the grooves of north-facing plates. Fine red grass appears around warm exhaust joints, each tuft isolated as though planted by measurement. Pale reeds stand in reservoir gutters, stiff enough to ring softly against one another, though the sound rarely carries. A few trees have rooted in old filter beds. They are narrow, metallic-looking things with translucent bark and only a handful of leaves. Their roots do not seek soil—there is none—but wind around pipes, enter cracks, and drink from the condensation inside walls.

Nobody agrees whether the plants are biological. Their leaves carry serial patterns. When cut, some reveal copper-colored veins.

Animals are everywhere.

They do not crowd the open concourses in obvious herds. Their abundance is discovered gradually: thousands of small presences concealed in the density of the works. White cliff-swifts nest in ventilation louvers. Six-legged hares sleep under cable trays, their fur stained green by insulating grease. Long-necked reservoir birds pace the edges of settling tanks and spear translucent eels from water that seems only centimeters deep, though the eels vanish downward for minutes before returning.

Colonies of warm-bodied moths cover inactive control panels, folding themselves into the shapes of switches. Beetles polish rust from bolts. Thin foxlike animals move along elevated pipelines, placing each foot precisely on the painted safety stripe. Beneath the plates are larger creatures: blind, slow, and known by the pressure of their backs against the structure. Their passage lifts whole neighborhoods by the width of a finger. Cups tilt. Water moves from one side of a basin to the other. Months later, the district settles again.

The fauna exploit the machinery, and the machinery appears to exploit the fauna in return. Birds carry ceramic washers in their crops and deposit them in sockets. Moths close circuits with their wings. Eels gather around broken electrodes and restore a weak current. In several factories, production continues only because migrating animals enter at one end each autumn. What emerges from the other end has never been identified. The factories are sealed, and the animals return the next year in undiminished number.

Despite the scale of the industry, the plateau is mostly quiet.

Its machines move slowly, and their mechanisms are hidden behind thick walls. A flywheel the size of a town may take three days to complete a rotation. Gantry cranes migrate across the skyline so gradually that their motion is visible only by comparing yesterday’s shadows. Conveyor belts advance one link at dusk. Pistons descend over the course of a season, compressing unknown material in chambers below.

The expected roar never comes.

Near an operating stack, one may feel vibration in the teeth without hearing it. Steam pours from vents in immaculate white columns, silent as light. Heavy carts pass on rails without wheel noise. Even impacts are subdued: a dropped tool seems to strike at a great distance, and the echo may return before the tool lands.

Small sounds therefore become landmarks. Water ticking into a sump. Claws on sheet metal. A loose wire tapping a mast. The delicate crackle of frost retreating from a sunlit pipe. In some valleys between the machine-mountains, every sound is absorbed; in others, a whisper spoken at noon can be heard again after sunset from a completely different direction.

There are settlements, though they are difficult to separate from the industrial fabric. Houses are fitted into unused chambers, under ramps and along the inner faces of cooling towers. Bright cloth hangs between ducts. Kitchens occupy former instrument bays. Public squares are circles painted around access hatches that no one opens.

The inhabitants build almost nothing from raw material. They rearrange. A wall becomes a bridge; a pressure door becomes a table; a bank of obsolete processors becomes a warm nursery for eggs. Every object has already been part of something larger. Even clothing is cut from filter fabric and signal pennants. People speak of fabrication as an ancient condition rather than an act. To make a thing from nothing is considered impossible, perhaps indecent.

Beyond the inhabited bands stand the new regions.

These are not new because anyone has constructed them. They have emerged whole during the last few decades: fresh districts found beyond familiar railheads, bright and unworn, already connected to utilities. Their surfaces bear no dust. Their bolts remain factory-blue. Their channels hold clear rainwater, though no rain has fallen there. There are offices with warm lamps, mines with no substance to mine, and warehouses filled with identical boxes whose interiors are larger than their exteriors.

The edges of these regions are sharp. Old catwalks meet new corridors at impossible angles. A stair may rise through fifty meters while returning to the level where it began. Two neighboring windows can show different weather. There are bridges whose far ends arrive several years later than their near ends, and loading docks where shadows continue working after the workers have gone.

Reality fails here without violence.

Distances become conditional. A tower may be five kilometers away when approached on foot and within arm’s reach when seen in a puddle. Rooms repeat, but each repetition is slightly wetter. Numbers stamped into the plates change when counted aloud. The sun sometimes appears reflected in a surface before it rises, and on certain mornings every reflective object shows the same unfamiliar animal watching from behind the observer.

The inhabitants mark unstable places with yellow triangles, although the triangles themselves tend to migrate. Animals cross such zones freely. The pipeline foxes step from one disconnected gantry to another. Birds enter vents in winter and emerge in summer. Once each year, the reservoir eels swim upward through the bright sky, following invisible channels toward cisterns hanging beneath the highest bridges.

Nothing here changes quickly. Even the breakdown of the world is patient.

An impossible corridor takes years to bend. A duplicated building begins as a second door, then an extra wall, then a whole annex visible only in rain. Rust spreads in precise constellations. Pools evaporate over months, leaving delicate rings of salt and machine oil. Moss advances one stamped digit at a time. The vast structures settle, align, and misalign according to cycles no calendar fully captures.

At sunset—when sunset occurs—the glare softens but does not disappear. Stored light seeps from ceramic roads and reservoir walls. The plateau remains pale beneath the darkening sky, crowded with silent machinery and innumerable lives. Far away, mirrors on the summits turn by fractions of a degree. A chain of lamps illuminates itself across the region, one white point every few minutes, tracing an industrial constellation over the fabricated earth.

By midnight the whole high country seems suspended: a dense, bright mechanism without foundation, damp in its hidden joints, open to the cold stars.

Below the plates, something enormous continues to breathe.

No one hears it.
