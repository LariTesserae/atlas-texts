At first the plateau appears almost empty.

It lies high above the cloud country, a broad, cool tableland whose horizons have been worn down to long horizontal lines. There are no young peaks here. Wind and immense age have reduced the mountains to terraces, shallow domes, and pale escarpments rounded like old tools. Valleys are wide and barely concave. Rivers have become chains of damp hollows connected by dry channels. Rain comes infrequently, but when it does it travels sideways, combed into silver threads by the plateau wind.

The emptiness is deceptive. Everything underfoot is alive.

What resembles soil is a compressed vegetable stratum thousands of years deep: root felt, woody plates, dormant bulbs, translucent membranes, and black layers of ancient leaf tissue that have never entirely died. Stone occurs only as grit caught in this growth or as mineral deposits secreted by plants. The cliffs themselves are rooted. Their faces are built from successive generations of load-bearing trunks, hardened by cold and pressure until their grain resembles sedimentary rock. Where erosion opens them, rings appear the size of streets.

Vegetation does not merely cover the landscape. It constitutes it.

Yet the plateau is not lush in the ordinary sense. There is little crowding, little underbrush, no profusion of separate stems. Life has consolidated into large, widely spaced forms. A single mat of blue-green ground tissue may run uninterrupted for kilometers. A grove may consist of only seven trees, each one broad enough to shelter a district. Their crowns stand far apart, leaving immense lanes of open sky between them. Photosynthetic skins stretch over the old terrain in smooth sheets, broken by ventilation pores and the occasional upraised rib. The sparseness belongs to objects, not biomass: there are few things because each living thing has become a place.

Across this plateau stands a city.

It is difficult to say where the city begins. No wall marks it, and there is no distinction between foundation and ecosystem. Its districts are grown from columnar plants whose trunks divide into regular floors. Streets follow the grooves between great root plates. Bridges are braided stems trained across ravines, continually tightening themselves as they age. Towers rise as hollow, tapering boles with windows cut into layers the plant can afford to lose. Their surfaces are gray, sage, faded copper, and the dull violet of leaves adapted to cold light.

The city is extensive but open. Buildings gather into islands separated by wind fields, cultivation flats, and broad plazas of bare living membrane. Some avenues are so wide that weather develops inside them. Fog can occupy one end of a street while the other remains dry and sharply sunlit. Between districts stand old infrastructural trees, solitary and monumental, bearing cables, pipes, signal flowers, lifting platforms, and banks of ceramic insulators among their limbs.

The industrial quarter is audible long before it is visible.

Its factories are not separate constructions so much as organs coaxed into specialized growth. Long fermentation halls lie beneath the plateau skin, warm cavities where resin, fuel alcohol, fiber, medicines, and structural pulp are produced. Above them, ribbed chimneys open and close like stomata. Turbines turn in the strongest passes, their blades made from laminated leaf-veins. Heavy presses compact harvested root fiber into rails and panels. Sap pumps labor in deep shafts with a slow reciprocating violence.

The city is loud.

Wind makes the greatest sound: a continuous high-altitude pressure that fills every exposed space. It booms in hollow towers, rattles dry seed vanes, and draws organ tones from ventilation trunks. Beneath that is the industry—the hammering of compression mills, the bass pulse of pumps, chain drives clattering through wooden housings, electric relays snapping in sealed cabinets. At regular intervals an entire district emits a low groan as its support roots alter their tension.

There are also sudden botanical noises. Pods burst with reports like distant artillery. Bark plates separate and fall across roofs. Vast leaves turn toward the sun with a leathery thunder. In wet weather, the city’s millions of collection cups unfold at once, producing a rolling crackle that passes from the western terraces to the eastern rim.

Water is never abundant, but it is everywhere anticipated.

Roofs are shaped to gather condensation. Fine hairs line the exposed surfaces of towers, combing mist from the wind and feeding it into green cisterns. In the mornings, beads of water hang under bridges and along the lower edges of leaves. Public reservoirs occupy shaded root chambers, black and cold, their surfaces crowded by pale floating tissue. The old riverbed crossing the city carries water only a few weeks each year. Most of the time it serves as a sunken boulevard, lined with valves, sluices, and deeply eroded stairs.

When rain arrives, urban life changes within minutes. Channels that looked decorative reveal their purpose. Streets tilt almost imperceptibly as thirsty tissues contract. Doorways swell shut. Reservoir plants open white throats in courtyards. Water races through the dry river avenue, carrying leaves, tools, pollen, and reflected windows. Then the flow sinks through the porous city and is gone. For days afterward the lower districts sweat. Condensation runs down interior walls. Machinery slips belts. Fungal lights appear in old service tunnels and fade as the plateau dries.

The light itself is ordinary: clean daylight, pale under cloud and strong beneath open sky. It casts recognizable shadows. Noon looks like noon. This normality makes the deeper irregularities harder to dismiss.

Distance is unreliable here.

A tower viewed across a plaza may be reached in ten minutes on one day and not before evening on another. Streets do not visibly move, yet survey lines refuse to close. Two parallel avenues sometimes meet without curving. The interior circumference of certain trunk buildings exceeds their exterior circumference, although only while the plant is photosynthesizing. After sunset, rooms become smaller and furniture must be shifted toward the walls.

The oldest districts suffer the worst distortions. Their living structures have accumulated too many growth instructions, repairs, grafts, and memories of former shapes. A stair may remember being a branch and fork unexpectedly. A sealed factory may continue producing goods in an interior that no longer has an entrance. Windows occasionally open onto weather from centuries earlier: harsher rain, lower clouds, forests of juvenile towers where the eroded plateau now lies.

These are not treated as supernatural events. They are municipal problems.

The city employs route readers who test streets with spools of marked fiber. Surveyors carry synchronized clocks because time can pool in concave courtyards. Buildings are pruned not only for structural stability but for logical stability; contradictory branches are cut away before they produce incompatible rooms. Every major intersection has a fixed stone at its center—not native stone, but dense mineral concretions grown slowly in vats. These inert masses anchor locality. Around them, distances behave.

There are practical rules.

Never enter a building while its leaves are turning.

Do not cross a root seam if you can hear machinery beneath it but feel no vibration.

If the same signal flower is visible at both ends of a street, wait for cloud cover before proceeding.

Never sleep in an unnumbered room. By morning, it may belong to an older version of the building.

During rain, follow the water. During drought, follow the cables. The water knows the current shape of the city; the cables remember the official one.

Animal life is scarce. The plateau’s vegetation has assumed so many ecological roles that little room remains for fauna. Pollination is usually performed by wind, electrical discharge, or mobile tendrils exchanged between distant plants. Nutrients circulate through root grafts. Dead tissue is digested internally or peeled away into industrial harvest yards.

Now and then a long-winged black bird crosses the city without landing. Tiny glass-shelled mites live in condenser beds. Colorless worms inhabit the deepest reservoirs, where they are regarded almost as civic treasures. Workers sometimes report seeing pale, many-legged silhouettes moving through the dry river channel at dawn, but these may be detached root clusters searching for moisture.

Human—or approximately human—inhabitants are present, though thinly distributed. The city is too large for its population. Figures appear singly on the tremendous avenues, wrapped in fiber coats, faces covered against the wind. A tram grown from a hollow runner-vine may cross kilometers of elevated track carrying only three passengers. Entire tower districts are maintained by one household, one factory crew, or no one at all.

This low population does not make the city feel abandoned. Its infrastructure is busy. Hoists ascend. Valves breathe. Signal blossoms change color. Root-haulers move along recessed lanes with heavy electric motors whining. Workshops cut, steam, braid, and laminate vegetable material. The living city performs maintenance on itself continually, with or without witnesses.

Beyond the urban terraces, the plateau extends into a regional mosaic of enormous plant forms: low silver mats, isolated wind trees, sunken orchards, and ridges armored in dark scales. Roads run outward as pale graft lines. Some lead to quarry-gardens where mineral-rich tissue is harvested. Others end at cliffside condensation farms suspended over the clouds. Farther still are the old flatlands, where the vegetation has settled into a near-final state—vast, slow, and almost featureless.

There, a century may pass between visible changes.

But the city remains active because it is grafted across too many ages. Young industrial tissues pulse beside trunks older than the plateau’s present contours. New transit vines bore through petrified roots. Rain systems wake channels cut by vanished rivers. The living substrate remembers mountains that erosion has erased, and sometimes, under pressure, attempts to restore them.

On such days the ground rises by fractions. Towers lean inward. Ancient contour lines darken beneath the streets. The machinery becomes deafening as pumps reverse and load-bearing roots pull against one another. Residents gather at the locality stones and wait while the city decides which shape it occupies.

Usually the plateau settles before nightfall.

The wind continues. The factories resume their measured impacts. Water ticks from condenser leaves into hidden tanks. Across the open districts, immense trees stand apart beneath a plain, believable sky, while their roots quietly rearrange the world below.
