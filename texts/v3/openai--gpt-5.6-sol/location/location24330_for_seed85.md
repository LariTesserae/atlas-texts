### The High Ward of Still Water

The city stands at the summit of a mountain no air has ever touched.

Its foundations occupy a narrow basalt crown nearly six kilometers above the surrounding abyssal plain, though another vast depth of ocean lies overhead. From the trenches below, the High Ward is elevated, exposed, almost alpine: a cluster of structures perched among fresh black pinnacles. From the surface—which may no longer exist—it is only another darkness under darkness.

The summit is small enough to cross on foot, if walking were practical here. A central court, several radial streets, and perhaps forty buildings fit within a shallow bowl of volcanic glass. Beyond the last construction, the terrain breaks sharply into cliffs. These cliffs are young and scarcely eroded. Their edges retain vitreous fins and needle points that ordinary currents would have blunted. Fractures in the rock look newly struck, pale mineral interiors exposed beneath black skins.

Nothing moves strongly enough to wear them down.

The ocean here is held in an engineered equilibrium. The water remains liquid, cool, immensely pressurized, and almost perfectly still. There are no tides, convection cells, descending particles, or slow rivers of brine. Heat does not noticeably rise. Salt does not settle. A diver disturbing the water would feel resistance without current, as if pushing through an invisible structure made of countless fitted planes.

The Ward’s builders called this structure the **hydrostatic frame**. It is not a physical lattice, or not one instruments can locate. It imposes relationships between neighboring volumes of water, preventing differences from propagating. Pressure exists, but pressure waves do not travel normally. Temperature exists, but cannot readily diffuse. Every cubic measure of the sea is permitted to be itself and discouraged from influencing the next.

As a result, the city has no weather.

There is no marine snow. No sediment plume rises from the streets. No bubbles ascend from vents. A loose object, once released, remains approximately where it was left. Dust hangs in old constellations between buildings, each grain fixed by an equilibrium too subtle to be called suspension. Here and there, interrupted trails of grit suggest that something passed through centuries ago. The trails have neither dispersed nor settled.

The buildings are sparse, arranged with more empty water between them than an ordinary city would tolerate. Each is tall enough to suggest a tower but broad enough to seem like a sealed civic machine. Their surfaces are formed from seamless gray ceramic, pressure-grown diamond, and panes of a dark transparent substance that reveal no interiors. The architecture is urban without being crowded: boulevards, thresholds, plazas, conduits, docking cavities, and public chambers all remain recognizable, but they serve a population no longer evident.

There are no doors. Approaching certain walls causes their material to reinterpret itself as open water. Passing through produces no seam, no sound, and no visible change behind the entrant. The same surface may admit one object and remain solid to another. Streets similarly decide what counts as traffic. Some repel drifting matter a few millimeters above their surfaces. Others allow stone, metal, and bodies through as if the pavement were only an image laid across a deeper shaft.

The central court contains the city’s only conspicuous monument: a vertical absence about twenty meters high. It resembles a column cut out of the ocean. Light, particles, sonar, and touch all fail to occupy it. Water presses against its cylindrical boundary but does not enter. Looking across it reveals the buildings beyond, displaced by an angle that depends not on the viewer’s position but on the viewer’s age.

Around this absence, low benches form three incomplete circles. Their seats are clean.

Illumination is scarce. The city emits no general glow. Thin indigo lines appear at the bases of some buildings, too dim to illuminate anything beyond themselves. At intervals along the streets, suspended points of blue-white radiance mark intersections. They produce no beams or halos. Each resembles a distant star inserted into the water, visible from every direction yet casting almost no light on nearby surfaces.

Most of the Ward is perceived through outline and occlusion: the deeper black of a doorway against a black wall, the shine of a mineral fracture, a faint geometric edge where the hydrostatic frame bends stray photons around a tower. Distance is unreliable. A structure across a plaza may seem kilometers away until touched. The outer cliffs sometimes appear inside the city, looming between buildings that are in fact uninterrupted.

Sound is equally constrained. The frame suppresses long transmission, so the ocean has none of its usual remote groaning. There are no whale calls, propeller murmurs, ice cracks, or low seismic rolls. A blow struck against a wall is audible only near the point of impact. The noise is brief and dry, strangely unwaterlike, and ends without echo.

The city itself produces three sounds.

One is a faint click from somewhere beneath the central court, repeated at an interval of thirty-one hours. The second is a soft, descending tone heard only inside sealed chambers. The third is not strictly a sound: at certain intersections, the bones of a living creature register a pressure like a distant chord. All three have continued unchanged for as long as measurements exist.

There is no vegetation. No algae films the towers; no bacterial mat clouds the vents; no pale frond rises from a crack. Organic accumulation is absent even where mineral chemistry should support it. The basalt is sterile down to the limits of sampling. The Ward appears not merely uninhabited but exempt from the ordinary persistence of life.

Rarely, however, something crosses the outer streets.

These creatures are small, translucent, and difficult to classify. They have neither fins nor obvious musculature. Most resemble folded sheets of clear tissue wrapped around a hard, many-sided kernel. They do not swim. One moment a creature hangs motionless beside a tower; later it occupies another location, with no observed transition between the two. Their bodies contain no recognizable digestive tract and almost no carbon. The central kernel is usually a crystal bearing microscopic voids arranged like portions of the city map.

They avoid the monument and gather singly near unfinished structures. Never more than four have been recorded within the Ward at once. A dead specimen has never been found. Whether they are fauna, instruments, or errors produced by the hydrostatic frame remains unresolved.

At the summit’s perimeter, streets terminate in balconies overlooking the abyss. No railings separate them from the drop. Below, sharp ridges descend into absolute black, their slopes sprinkled with isolated metal pylons. The pylons continue down farther than light can reach, spaced hundreds of meters apart. Each points not upward but toward the central absence, regardless of the angle at which it was installed.

The mountain itself may be artificial. Its basalt contains no weathered layers and no settled chronology. Samples taken near the summit appear geologically newborn. Samples from the lower cliffs are the same age. Crystals inside the rock all align toward a point above the city, as though the mountain solidified under a gravity field different from the present one. Yet there are no signs of eruption: no ash, no lava lobes, no displaced seabed. It seems to have arrived already cooled and fractured, carrying the Ward on its crown.

Nothing here decays at a normal rate. Metals do not corrode. Ceramics do not fatigue. Exposed rock does not soften. The few physical marks left by visitors remain sharp indefinitely, although the city sometimes ceases to acknowledge them. A cut made in a wall may still be measurable while becoming impossible to see. A dropped tool may remain in place but no longer cast a sonar return. The Ward preserves matter more faithfully than information.

Its principal rule is not that time has stopped. Clocks operate. Organisms breathe. Thoughts proceed. Rather, change is kept local and prevented from becoming history. Heat from a hand does not meaningfully warm the surrounding water. A footprint does not fill with sediment. A sound does not travel far enough to become an echo. Causes occur, but the landscape refuses to distribute their consequences.

Thus the city remains perpetually recent.

Its sharp mountain never ages. Its streets never accumulate debris. Its dark towers continue attending to functions that leave no visible product. Above, kilometers of unmoving ocean bear down like a roof without a ceiling. Below, the abyss waits without approaching.

And at the center, the column of absence persists—empty, upright, and exact—while all the water in the world stands silently around it.
