### The Throat Above Weather

The place is not large, though it takes effort to understand its boundaries.

It lies near the crown of an almost vertical mountain, higher than the ordinary flight of birds and higher, on most days, than the visible weather. From below, the mountain appears to end in a broken black needle. Inside that needle is a narrow basin: not quite a crater, not quite a gorge, but a fresh separation in the rock where the summit has begun to pull apart.

The basin is perhaps the size of a cathedral precinct. Its walls rise sharply on every side, crowded so close that the sky is reduced to a crooked seam. In some places the walls nearly meet overhead. Their stone is young and unsoftened—dark basalt, greenish glass, pale mineral ribs—split into blades, wedges, and angular shelves. There are few rounded surfaces. Nothing has had time to become gentle.

The floor descends in overlapping steps toward a fissure too narrow to enter. Cold air breathes from it.

There is little direct light. At noon, a diluted grayness may descend through the summit opening, revealing the upper ledges while leaving the lower cleft in blue-black shadow. On cloudy days the place is dark from rim to floor. At night there is almost no distinction between stone and empty space except where living bodies move.

Moisture comes without abundance. Clouds strike the mountain from one side, spill over the rim, and unravel within the basin. Their passage leaves beads of condensation on every cold face. Water gathers along mineral seams and drops patiently from ledge to ledge. Some hollows hold clear pools no wider than a hand; others contain only a skin of water over black grit. After rain, thin silver threads appear on the walls, but within hours they vanish into cracks. The basin never becomes a lake. It remains damp by accumulation, by breath, by fog, by innumerable small wettings.

Every surface seems occupied.

At first the walls look shaggy, as if furred with soot. Then the soot shifts.

Millions of small animals shelter here—not in open swarms, but packed through the rock’s depth. Pale moths fold themselves into fissures three bodies deep. Black, six-legged gliders cling under shelves, their translucent membranes wrapped tightly around them. Dense colonies of soft-shelled beetles plug warmer cracks. Long-bodied cliff mice sleep upright in seams too narrow for a human hand. Tiny hook-footed birds hang head downward beneath overhangs, stacked in rows like dark leaves.

The pools have their own inhabitants. Colorless shrimp circle slowly in them, feeding on mineral film and fallen dust. Flat worms no thicker than thread knot themselves around droplets. When a drop becomes heavy enough to fall, some of these creatures fall with it, descending through the basin in a private rain of water and life.

The air is similarly crowded. It is never empty enough to cross without contact. Wings brush stone, whiskers, other wings. Animals move through known corridors only a few finger-widths wide, each stream passing another in the darkness without collision. From a distance the basin might appear still, but close at hand every inch trembles with breathing, grooming, hatching, feeding, and minute negotiations over space.

The sound is immense.

It does not resemble a single cry. It is a layered pressure: wing-hiss, shell-click, throat-rattle, claws ticking against glassy rock, the soft concussion of whole colonies adjusting position at once. Low calls travel through the mountain rather than through the air. Some animals press their jaws or breastbones against the stone and hum into it. The resulting vibration moves along the fresh fractures, so that a colony on one wall answers another colony hidden far below.

These exchanges are slow. A note may last several minutes. Its reply may not begin until an hour later.

The deepest sound comes from within the fissure: a pulse below hearing, felt through teeth and joints. It may be wind moving through the mountain, or rock settling under its own height. The animals respond to it. When the pulse strengthens, every wing in the basin closes. Every clicking mouth falls silent. For a few seconds the place becomes so quiet that individual droplets can be heard striking the lower pools.

Then the noise returns all at once.

Vegetation survives mainly where water and animal waste coincide. There are no trees and almost no upright plants. Rust-colored moss follows drip lines. Black lichens colonize stone that receives a few minutes of light each day. In two protected hollows, thin translucent fronds emerge from guano and lie flat against the ground, opening only when fog enters the basin. Their surfaces are adhesive. Moths land on them and are slowly dissolved, so that the little vegetation feeds on the fauna rather than the reverse.

The most conspicuous plant is a gray, cordlike vine that grows downward from the rim. It has no leaves. Its roots begin in a patch of summit gravel, but its stems descend into darkness, drawing moisture from condensation. Animals use the cords as routes between ledges. Some chew through them; others carry the severed lengths in their mouths, not to build, but to probe narrow cracks for larvae. The vine regrows over years. Its use is learned across generations.

There are no shelters, walls, paths, or hearths. Nothing here has been shaped into architecture. Yet tool behavior appears everywhere in fleeting forms. Hook-footed birds select sharp volcanic flakes and hold them in their beaks to open beetle cases. Cliff mice wedge pebbles into wet cracks, returning days later to lick the mineral-rich moisture that accumulates behind them. A large, slow arthropod uses a shed wing casing as a scoop, then abandons it after feeding. No object is carried far. No artifact lasts. The basin’s technology is temporary and opportunistic, inseparable from the body using it.

Predation happens constantly but without spectacle. There is too little room for pursuit. Hunters wait with mouths open at the intersections of traffic routes. A wing, a tail, or an antenna touches the wrong surface and disappears. Several species have evolved false edges: mouths that resemble cracks, eyelids that resemble lichen, teeth that resemble crystals newly exposed by fracture.

The largest animal is seldom seen whole.

It lives below the narrowest part of the basin, where the walls converge over the central fissure. At intervals, a pale jointed limb rises from beneath the rocks and remains motionless for days. Small creatures roost on it. Moss begins to grow across it. Then, very gradually, the limb bends, drawing its accumulated passengers downward. Whether it feeds on them or merely relocates them is unclear. Its other limbs can sometimes be felt moving through connected cracks hundreds of paces apart, suggesting an animal shaped less like a body than a root system.

It is possible that there is only one.

The ecology follows long pulses rather than seasons. The summit receives cold, fog, and occasional rain, but little snow reaches the enclosed floor. Change is governed instead by the mountain’s slow opening. Every few years, a fracture widens enough to admit a new ribbon of air. A colony migrates toward it. Predators follow. Condensation begins to gather on the newly exposed surfaces, then lichen, then moss, then the layered multitudes.

Other passages close. Whole communities become sealed into the rock. Their humming may continue for decades after they vanish from sight.

Erosion has barely begun. Fallen fragments remain sharp where they land. Fresh mineral faces shine wetly in the dimness. The mountain is still assembling this place from tension, pressure, and fracture. Its ledges are temporary, but temporary on a scale longer than any inhabitant’s life.

The strange rule of the basin is that altitude does not make it empty. Here, height compresses life. The surrounding world falls away into wind and cloud, leaving this small dark interior as the only shelter for a vast aerial region. Creatures arrive from hundreds of miles of open sky and converge upon a chamber scarcely large enough to contain them. Generation after generation accumulates in the same crevices until every hollow is warm with bodies.

From the outer summit, nothing announces this abundance. There is only a black split in the stone and a faint exhalation of cool, animal-scented air.

Lean close, and the mountain sounds full.
