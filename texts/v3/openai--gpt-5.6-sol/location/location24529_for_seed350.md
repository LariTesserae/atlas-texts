The basin lies below the old sea level, though there is no longer a clear distinction between sea, weather, and ground. Warm water fills everything. It presses through streets, turbine halls, root systems, elevator shafts, and the long trenches where freight lines once ran. Above, if there is an above, a ceiling of rain descends without surface or impact: the water simply grows denser overhead until distance becomes black.

The land is young. Ridges of recently extruded basalt rise from the silt in hard, glassy folds, their edges still sharp enough to split drifting cable and sheet metal. Vents open along these ridges and discharge mineral clouds at violent speed. They smell, somehow, of hot iron and cut stems. Pale precipitates build chimneys in hours, then collapse under the vibration and begin again in slightly different places. Nothing has had time to weather. Even the ruins look newly broken.

Across this low drowned region stands an industrial complex so extensive that it can only be understood in pieces. A pump house here. Three cooling towers farther off, their upper halves lost in murk. Rail gantries crossing a ravine that did not exist yesterday. Concrete silos lean together like cups abandoned in a sink. Most structures are scattered and incomplete, with wide distances of flooded woodland and fresh stone between them, yet their buried conduits connect beneath the mud. When one machine starts, lights wake miles away.

No one tends them.

The machinery is not quiet enough to be called abandoned. Submerged presses strike at irregular intervals, each blow passing through the water as a bodily event. Turbines howl in drowned chambers. Cavitation rattles through pipes with the violence of gravel poured onto steel. Warning sirens continue inside sealed control rooms, too distorted by pressure to resemble alarms; they have become immense, mournful chords that bend as walls flex. At the basin’s center, something turns slowly below the sediment. Its bearings produce a continuous metallic shriek, while each revolution lifts the ground by several centimeters and lets it fall.

There are quieter sounds, but they are buried inside the louder ones: the hiss of hot water entering warm water, the tearing of roots from masonry, relays ticking in boxes where no air remains. Sound travels strangely here. A blow heard at one’s feet may have occurred beyond the horizon, while a collapsing tower nearby may remain silent until hours later, when its noise arrives all at once from every direction.

The vegetation is lush, although it has no settled shape.

Dense forests of ribbonweed fill the factory aisles, dark green at their bases and nearly black at their tips. Broad translucent leaves grow from cracks in concrete and spread across walls in overlapping fans. Their veins glow faintly amber whenever an electrical surge passes through the reinforcement bars. Cable-thick vines descend into shafts, knot around flywheels, and are wound through machines until plant and mechanism become one tense, spinning mass. Beds of red moss coat switchboards. Pale stalks stand in assembly lines, each stem emerging exactly from the center of a painted workstation.

There are no animals. No fish move between the leaves, no crustaceans clean the vents, no insects shelter in trapped pockets of gas. Flowers open and close without pollinators. Fruit forms, ripens, and dissolves untouched into clouds of sugar. Some plants appear to listen for the machinery: they flatten when the presses strike and rise during the brief gaps. Others grow toward vibration rather than light. Their roots crowd around active motors, feeding on warmth, dissolved metals, or the repeating certainty of rotation.

The absence of animal life is absolute enough to feel enforced. Anything that begins to move with appetite, fear, or intention soon loses its boundary. A swimming body would blur into the water. A heartbeat would fall into rhythm with the pumps, then slow to match them. The basin permits motion, but not creatures.

Light comes from below and from within things. Sunlight, if it survives, is only a dim green pressure overhead. Sodium lamps burn behind algae-filmed glass. Arc flashes pulse in ruptured substations, briefly revealing whole avenues of vegetation bent sideways in the current. Some leaves carry soft blue luminescence, but never continuously; the glow travels from plant to plant like a thought passing through a nervous system. Between flashes, the landscape collapses into silhouettes: chimneys, roots, cranes, mineral spires, all layered so densely that open water is difficult to find.

Currents race through the complex without regard for slope. They climb stairwells, pour upward from drains, and sweep horizontally through intact walls. At certain junctions, several currents cross without mixing. Leaves caught there are pulled in incompatible directions until they divide into copies, each copy continuing along a different flow.

This is common. The basin does not maintain one version of itself.

A warehouse may stand whole when approached from the east and lie flattened when viewed from the west. Doors open onto rooms larger than the buildings containing them. A service tunnel descends for six kilometers and ends at a window looking back into the same basin from overhead. Fresh basalt sometimes erupts in the shape of manufactured objects: valves the size of houses, perfect threaded bolts, lengths of black chain fused link by link to the bedrock. Conversely, machinery sometimes softens into geology. Steel tanks acquire crystal grain. Conveyor belts sag, harden, and become terraces from which forests sprout.

Creation and destruction occur too quickly to separate. A wall cracks; vines enter; the pieces reassemble around the vines; the vines become pipes; the pipes carry boiling sap to a newly formed tower. Minutes later, the tower folds downward and vanishes into a patch of knee-high leaves. The water never clears enough to reveal the full sequence.

The warm temperature is nearly uniform, but there are pockets of startling heat around vents and engines. These regions shimmer despite the absence of air. Water there becomes thick and glasslike. Plants passing into it are preserved for a moment with impossible clarity, every cell visible, before emerging on the other side several years taller. Elsewhere, cold does not exist so much as time slows: bubbles stop, sediment hangs motionless, and the industrial roar drops into a note too low to hear but heavy enough to bend steel.

Scattered through the basin are signs of former occupation. A maintenance hut bolted to a ridge. A row of lockers with their doors standing open. Ceramic cups fixed to a canteen table by mineral growth. Painted arrows that keep changing direction. There are no bodies, portraits, names, or handprints. Written instructions remain, but the words have rearranged themselves into descriptions of processes the machinery has never performed:

**RETURN THE RAIN TO LINE FOUR.**  
**DO NOT ALLOW THE ROOT PRESSURE TO REMEMBER YOU.**  
**IN CASE OF HORIZON FAILURE, REMAIN BELOW WATER.**

The largest visible structure is a pumping station built across a fault. Six intake mouths gape along its front, each screened by bars tangled with foliage. The pumps draw water inward at catastrophic volume, yet the basin never drains. Behind the station, the discharge pipes open into the same water a fraction of a second earlier than it was taken. This produces a rolling discontinuity—a transparent wall moving slowly through the region. When it passes, leaves reverse their growth, rubble leaps into ruined ceilings, and sirens inhale their own sound. Then ordinary turbulence resumes.

Nothing remains still long enough to accumulate age. Sediment is laid down and stripped away in the same hour. New islands of slag rise from the flooded flats, jagged and steaming, only to be consumed by vine forests whose roots were already waiting inside the stone. Towers collapse before corrosion can stain them. Cracks are fresh, surfaces bright. The entire basin has the exhausted violence of a place being born continuously.

Its boundaries are uncertain but not infinite. Beyond the drowned works lie low plains thick with black reeds and half-submerged transmission pylons. Beyond those, perhaps thirty or forty kilometers away, a ring of dim red lights marks the rim of the depression. Sometimes the lights are close enough to touch. Sometimes they appear beneath the ground. No matter their apparent position, they blink in sequence with the deep central rotation.

There is one dependable rule: the sequence must never complete.

When the final red light illuminates, every engine stops. The sudden silence is more overwhelming than the noise. Water loses its currents. Leaves hang suspended. Mineral clouds freeze around their vents. For several seconds the entire basin becomes perfectly visible, even though no source of light has brightened it: every factory, forest, trench, ridge, and buried pipe appears at once, extending far beyond what the basin should contain.

Then a sound like an ocean striking the inside of a bell passes through the water.

The lights begin again from the first. The pumps seize, the roots tighten, the young rock splits open, and the drowned industrial garden resumes making itself.
