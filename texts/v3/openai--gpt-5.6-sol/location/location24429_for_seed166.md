Below the surface of an industrial cooling reservoir lies a shallow stone basin no larger than a village square. Nothing has been built within it. There are no pipes, grates, cables, pilings, or discarded tools—only the influence of machinery beyond its boundaries, expressed through the water itself.

The basin is enclosed on three sides by low shelves of dark limestone. Its fourth edge falls into a narrow, deeper channel leading toward the reservoir’s circulating works. The limestone has been submerged long enough to lose every freshly broken angle. Ridges are rounded, hollows softened, and pale seams of calcite stand slightly proud where the surrounding rock has worn away. A floor of compact gray silt occupies the center. It is so settled that it resembles poured material, though no one laid it there.

Cool water fills all available space. It is clear but faintly green, treated and continuously filtered elsewhere. Suspended particles are scarce. Light descends from the open reservoir surface in broad, ordinary bands, sufficient to reveal the whole enclosure: the shelves, the empty floor, the occasional mineral glint. Surface ripples break the illumination into slow-moving meshes, but the movement is repetitive and nearly identical from hour to hour. There are no storms here, no tides, and no seasonal flooding. Pumps maintain the level to within a few centimeters.

Vegetation survives only in scattered, disciplined patches. Thin olive films occupy sheltered cracks where circulation is weakest. A few strands of filamentous algae lie flat along the stone, all oriented in the same direction by the maintained current. Nothing grows tall. There are no reeds reaching down from above and no underwater thickets. The treated water, cool temperature, and unvarying flow hold growth at a low equilibrium: enough life to stain the rock, not enough to conceal it.

There are no animals. No fish enter from the channel. No snails mark the algae, no larvae hover over the silt, no shell fragments collect in depressions. Even the smallest visible swimmers are absent. The emptiness is not dramatic; it has persisted too long for that. The reservoir’s filtration screens, controlled chemistry, and isolated circulation have made the basin a place where plantlike films can endure but animal life cannot establish itself.

Its industrial character is therefore sensed rather than seen. The water arrives at a regulated temperature. Its clarity is mechanical. Its weak current never wanders. At intervals too regular to be natural, a low vibration passes through the limestone as distant pumps change phase. The pulse is felt more in the ribs than heard. Then the basin returns to quiet: a slight pressure hiss in narrow fissures, the muted rubbing of water over stone, and far above, an occasional flat disturbance at the surface.

No debris accumulates. Any leaf that falls onto the reservoir is drawn away before it can sink here. Silt does not drift into dunes because the current is too uniform; it remains level, except around a few embedded stones where shallow crescents have been established over many years. These marks no longer noticeably deepen. Erosion and deposition have reached agreement.

The rules of the place are simple. Water must keep moving, but nothing within the basin may appear to change. Temperature remains cool. The level remains fixed. The same quantity of light returns each clear day. Mineral films thicken only as quickly as they are scoured away. The algae grow only as quickly as the treated water suppresses them. Everything rests inside a maintained balance whose machinery is elsewhere.

From within, looking up, the surface resembles a flexible ceiling of silver-green glass. Beyond it may be concrete towers, switchyards, steam plumes, warning lights, and long buildings full of turbines. None of those enter the submerged hollow. Down here there is only old rock, sparse growth, measured water, and the quiet consequence of an industry powerful enough to make constancy.
