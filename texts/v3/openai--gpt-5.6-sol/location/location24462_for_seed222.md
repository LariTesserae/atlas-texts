The high country begins above the last continuous vegetation, but it is not barren. It is a broad, warm tableland lifted into a white sky, its distances arranged in shelves, shallow basins, and long eroded scarps. The rock has been exposed for so long that nothing appears freshly broken. Cliffs have softened into immense stairways. Boulders sit low in their own collars of dust. Channels wander through the stone with the unhurried confidence of rivers, though most carry water only a few days each season.

At noon the plateau is intensely bright. Pale mineral crusts return the sun in flat sheets, and every upright thing—an animal, a thorn tree, a marker post—owns a compact pool of blue-black shade. The air is warm despite the altitude, thin enough that exertion quickly becomes deliberate. Far ridges look close and sharp until a traveler tries to reach them.

Water survives here by refusing to remain in one form.

There is little open water. Rain comes in brief, localized curtains that can be seen crossing the country without touching most of it. A cloud may soak one ravine while bare stone a kilometer away stays hot. By morning, condensation has gathered beneath overhangs and along the undersides of certain metallic leaves. It threads down hairline grooves into shaded pockets: thumb-sized cups in the rock, deep cisterns under talus, narrow seams where roots and small animals wait.

The dry riverbeds are the plateau’s principal roads. Their floors are packed with rounded stones, old floodwood bleached to the color of bone, and mats of translucent husks left by aquatic organisms that awaken only when the channels run. After rain, water advances through them as a dark moving edge. Insects rise almost immediately. Soft-bodied grazers emerge from cracks. Small silver fish appear in pools that were dust the day before, though no one has determined whether they sleep beneath the riverbed or are assembled there by some older machinery.

Vegetation stands far apart. Low thorn crowns anchor themselves at channel bends. Gray grasses occur in rings around condensation wells, never forming meadows. There are columnar plants with pleated skins and hard ceramic bark; during the day they seem dead, but at dusk their upper surfaces open into shallow dishes. A few trees grow in the deepest gullies. Their trunks are twisted and nearly horizontal, polished by centuries of sediment-laden floods. Their foliage consists of sparse black-green blades that turn edge-on to the sun.

The emptiness between plants is not lifeless. Tracks cross it everywhere.

Long-legged browsing animals move between shade islands in loose, quiet groups. Their feet are broad and soft, leaving only dim impressions in the mineral dust. They carry reflective scales along their backs, not armor so much as thermal apparatus: at midday the scales flare white, and at evening they darken to shed stored heat. Smaller creatures travel beneath them, feeding on mites and fallen skin. Flat-bodied lizards cling to sunward cliffs. Burrowing colonies ventilate the ground through thousands of tiny chimneys, each no taller than a finger. Pale winged hunters circle high overhead, seldom calling.

Even predation here is subdued. The plateau’s principal hunters do not chase. They select a route—usually a game path through a narrow cut—and lie against the rock until their skin takes on its grain and temperature. When they move, it is once and decisively. The herds respond not with cries but with a sudden coordinated widening, as if space itself has opened among them.

This abundance creates almost no clamor. There are no choruses, no constant buzzing. Wings are thin and efficient. Hooves are padded. Many animals communicate through color shifts, ground-borne pulses, or changes in the faint electrical field that lies across the plateau. A human listener hears wind touching stone, the dry click of seed cases, an occasional pebble displaced on a slope. Before rain there may be a low tone at the limit of hearing, less a sound than pressure in the teeth.

Scattered through the region are structures too sparse to form a settlement and too deliberate to be ruins.

Some resemble narrow houses built of fused local stone, one-room shelters placed a day’s walk apart. They have no doors, only shaded recesses that become permeable when approached slowly. Inside, the air is cool and faintly damp. Condensation gathers on seamless walls and runs toward basins in the floor. Nothing visibly powers them. Dust accumulates on the thresholds but not within.

Others are more difficult to name. Black hoops stand upright on distant ridges, each large enough for an animal to pass through. Needle-thin pylons rise from ravines where floods should have destroyed them long ago. There are fields of ankle-high glass pegs arranged in patterns too extensive to understand from the ground. Most appear inactive until weather crosses them.

When rain falls, the technology wakes gently.

The hoops bend the storm’s path by degrees, guiding part of it into old watersheds without dispersing the clouds. Pylons pull lightning sideways in silent, branching threads and store it somewhere that cannot be found beneath them. The glass fields alter the rate at which water enters the soil. A flood passing through one may slow without becoming shallower, its surface hanging at a slant for several minutes while sediment settles out in carefully separated bands.

Nothing here violates physics openly. Instead, physical law seems to have been persuaded into local exceptions.

Mass is slightly negotiable near certain cliff dwellings. Shade can persist after its source has moved. A cistern may contain more water than its dimensions permit, but only while no one attempts to measure it. Travelers learn practical rules: never mark the waterline; never cross the same hoop in both directions on the same day; do not sleep where two shadows overlap after sunset. These are not superstitions. They are field procedures, inherited from whoever maintained the plateau before maintenance became indistinguishable from ecology.

The machines do not dominate the landscape. They have entered into balance with erosion. Mineral skins have grown over them. Animals use their foundations as dens. Roots follow the microscopic cooling gradients around buried conduits. In some places the engineering may no longer exist as separate objects at all. The watershed itself could be computational: ravines as channels, migration routes as moving signals, seasonal pools as temporary memory. The plateau gives no indication that it distinguishes between animal behavior, weather, and operation.

Across centuries, the old systems have also learned to fail gracefully. A collapsed pylon becomes a condensation spine. A broken shelter wall curls inward and slowly fuses into a windbreak. Devices whose original purposes are lost continue performing smaller tasks—sorting seeds, discouraging landslides, keeping one shaded hollow three degrees cooler than the surrounding stone. There is no debris in the usual sense. Every remnant has been weathered into a new function.

Toward evening, heat lifts from the flats in wavering layers. Distant scarps duplicate themselves in the air, and the bright plateau becomes momentarily crowded with false ridges and inverted ravines. Animals descend toward the water pockets. Dark plants unfold. Condensation begins first on the ancient structures, then spreads to nearby rock as if taught.

The sun goes down abruptly behind the western escarpment. For several minutes the upper air remains brilliant while the ground lies in violet shadow. Thin lines of light move through buried networks under the dry channels—not glowing cables, exactly, but seams where the dust forgets to be opaque. The lines converge beneath watering hollows. Creatures gather around them without alarm.

Then the light drains away, and the plateau becomes almost perfectly quiet.

Somewhere far off, water begins running through a riverbed under a clear sky. It may be rain arriving from beyond the horizon. It may be water released from storage. It may be the landscape revising, in darkness, the shape it will take tomorrow.
