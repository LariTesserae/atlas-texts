The city occupies a shallow bowl near the top of the mountain, though “mountain” is almost too young a word for the land beneath it. This height is the remnant of something immeasurably older: a continent worn down, uplifted, worn down again, until its stone has lost every sharp intention. The surrounding summits are broad-backed and low, their exposed rock polished into whale-smooth humps. Cliffs occur only where whole shelves have quietly parted. Scree is rare. Even the loose stones are rounded, dark, and fitted patiently into hollows as if the weather has been arranging them for ages.

The bowl itself is no larger than a walled town. From rim to rim it can be crossed in an hour, if the paths are passable and the rain has not awakened the channels. Yet it feels empty. Structures stand far apart, with plazas of bare stone and expanses of low vegetation between them. One tower may be visible from another across several hundred paces of open ground, but nothing occupies the interval except wind, moss, and the occasional black pool.

There are no walls made by hands.

The buildings grew here.

They resemble an old city because they have streets, chambers, arcades, stairways, wells, and towers. But each is a continuous mass of pale, fibrous stone—not masonry, not timber, and not quite bone. Their surfaces show growth rings where rain has worn through outer layers. Buttresses divide and branch like roots. Doorways pinch and widen with the seasons. Roofs are overlapping plates, thick as shields, beneath which condensation gathers and falls in a steady interior drizzle.

Some structures are alive, though slowly. In the cold months they contract, closing their lower entrances and opening narrow vents high above the ground. During the brief thaw they swell with absorbed water. New rooms bud as soft gray blisters beneath old arches, hardening over decades. No tool marks exist anywhere. There are no hinges, nails, glass, furnaces, carts, lamps, or carved signs. The city has organization without invention, architecture without architects. Its streets follow drainage lines as much as any plan. What looks like a grand avenue is sometimes simply the space between two enormous root systems.

At the center of the bowl stands the oldest growth, a hollow trunk or tower nearly fifty paces across. Its crown broke away long ago and the remaining rim has been rounded by frost and rain. Looking down into it reveals a circular court carpeted in silver moss. Looking up from the court, one sees only an oval of sky and, on clear days, the pale passage of clouds. Narrow galleries spiral through the walls, but they do not connect reliably. A corridor open in summer may seal by winter; another may appear after a week of rain. The place continually revises itself, but never quickly enough to be seen moving.

Water is scarce in quantity and abundant in effect. It does not form a lake or lasting river. Instead it travels in episodes.

Rain arrives sideways, driven across the high plateau in brief, hard curtains. Every surface beads at once. The roofs begin to stream, channels fill, and the streets become a branching river system scarcely ankle-deep. Black water races through grooves polished over thousands of years, drops into sinkholes, and disappears under the city. For an hour the entire bowl glitters and runs. Then the rain passes. Within a day the streets are dry except beneath overhangs and in deep stone sockets, where water remains dark and still for weeks.

On clear mornings, condensation is more important than rainfall. Mist collects against the outer slopes and spills over the rim in slow white folds. The living walls comb moisture from it. Droplets gather along thousands of fine mineral hairs, fall onto roofs, and are directed inward through fluted channels. Thus the city drinks from clouds even when the ground around it is dry.

Its hidden cisterns are not constructed reservoirs but natural organs: smooth internal cavities lined with blue-green membranes. Some hold water clear enough to show every vein in the lining. Others contain an opaque, faintly warm broth in which pale filaments coil. When a cistern overfills, pressure forces water through pores in the walls. Entire buildings then appear to sweat.

Vegetation gathers wherever this moisture lingers. It is lush not in height but in closeness. Moss lies several hands deep in protected courts, packed into green cushions and terraces. Liverworts spread glossy fans over the lower walls. Tiny ferns occupy every drain. Black grasses, no taller than a finger, grow so densely along some streets that the ground resembles dark fur. Under the arches hang curtains of translucent lichen that become rigid in frost and soften again in mist.

There are almost no trees. One species survives in three sheltered places: low, twisted groves whose trunks lie against the ground, rising only where a wall blocks the wind. Their leaves remain folded most of the year, revealing white felt beneath. In the short growing season they open all at once, turning the groves an unexpectedly deep green. Small yellow flowers appear beneath them, crowded together for warmth. The whole flowering lasts perhaps six days.

The cold never entirely leaves. Even in the brightest season, ice persists in shaded seams. At night, shallow pools skin over from the edges inward. The living stone tolerates freezing by emptying its outer chambers; just before dusk, water can sometimes be heard withdrawing through the walls with a long internal gulp.

This is not a quiet place.

Wind strikes the bowl from several directions at once. The smooth surrounding uplands funnel it toward the openings in the city, and the towers behave like pipes. Each has its own note. One produces a low, continuous pressure more felt in the ribs than heard. Another begins to howl whenever the western mist arrives. Rows of small apertures in the long eastern structure whistle in shifting chords, their pitch changing as frost narrows them or rainwater fills their lower edges.

The city’s loudest voice comes during storms. Water drops through vertical shafts and drums on stretched membranes inside the buildings. Roof plates clap against one another. Air is driven through wet galleries, making sounds like bellowing animals, distant horns, or crowds calling from underground. The central tower answers with a deep pulse every few seconds as its hollow interior compresses and releases the wind. Nothing in the bowl remains acoustically separate. A drip in one chamber may emerge as a knock three streets away.

Even in calm weather there is cracking: ice loosening, walls contracting, old plates splitting from roofs and sliding to the ground. These fallen plates do not accumulate. Soil organisms and acidic moss slowly soften them, and the buildings absorb the resulting mineral paste through their foundations. The city consumes its own ruins.

Animals are rare, perhaps because the noise conceals approach and warning alike. A few white-backed grazers visit during the thaw. They are small, long-legged creatures with blunt faces and broad, gripping feet. They move between the moss courts in groups of three or four, biting off only the uppermost growth. At any sudden change in the wind’s song they freeze—not at silence, but at unfamiliar sound.

Tiny shell-bearing insects inhabit the rain channels. For most of the year they remain sealed into cracks, indistinguishable from grains of stone. When runoff begins, they emerge in immense but brief numbers and are swept through the city, feeding as they travel. Their shells strike the channels with a dry, rushing hiss. By the time the water vanishes, they have anchored themselves elsewhere and closed again.

A single kind of bird nests here. It has no song of its own. Its feathers are soot-black, its wings narrow, and it spends much of the day circling above the bowl without landing. It breeds in the warm cavities near the cisterns and navigates the interior by repeating fragments of the city’s noises: one whistle from a tower, two hollow knocks from a drain, a low imitation of the central pulse. Hearing one in darkness is disorienting. A familiar architectural sound may suddenly move past your face on wings.

There are predators, but they are seldom seen. Something leaves wide, soft impressions in the moss after rain. The tracks begin beside one building and end at another without crossing the bare ground between. The grazers avoid doorways when the city is wet.

Nothing here is young except weather.

The surfaces have been reduced to their most durable curves. Every path has reached the bedrock. Every exposed edge has already been tested by frost, dissolved by mist, and polished by countless films of running water. Yet the city is not dead or still. Its activity is cyclical, inward, and patient. It grows by thickening; it moves by opening and closing; it changes streets over centuries. The mountain erodes, but the structures feed upon that erosion, drawing loosened minerals into themselves. They may be the final shape the mountain has taken—a settlement formed not upon the old stone, but from the old stone’s last remaining life.

There are rules to the place, although no one wrote them.

Open chambers face away from the prevailing wind. Water always moves toward the central tower but never collects there. A doorway wide enough to enter will eventually narrow; a passage too tight to cross will eventually open. Nothing grows on a surface that has never heard running water. Moss thrives where the city’s voice is deepest. Animals do not sleep in structures that whistle.

And when the whole bowl falls silent, which happens perhaps once or twice in a year, every living thing leaves the streets.

The silence comes before a particular kind of frost. Wind stops. Drips cease almost together. The tower notes collapse one by one until the bowl becomes unnaturally vacant, an empty room at the top of the world. Then the buildings draw inward. Their pores close; their plates tighten; water drains into depths below the reachable chambers. The moss darkens and folds. Birds rise beyond sight. Even the hidden predators withdraw.

By morning, the city is glazed in clear ice. The ice contains bubbles arranged in lines resembling avenues, courts, and towers—another city suspended over the first, finer and more crowded, with structures where the real city has open ground.

Sunlight passes through it normally. Nothing glows. Nothing announces a miracle. The high bowl simply stands under a pale sky, cold and lucid, while the wind gathers again beyond the ancient rim.

Then one tower begins to hum.

Another answers.

The ice cracks from end to end, and water starts moving through the streets.
