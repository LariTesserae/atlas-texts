At first there is no landscape, only sound.

Water falls somewhere below in volumes too large to judge. It does not splash so much as sustain a continuous, subterranean concussion. Against this deeper note come nearer noises: the ratcheting of insects, the cough and restart of distant engines, leaves slapping under heavy runoff, metal cables singing as the wind tightens them. Every few minutes something vast moves through the forest canopy with a sound like wet canvas being dragged over stone.

The darkness is complete. It is not night in the ordinary sense, not a temporary passage between lights. No moon rises here. No stars disclose the shape of the sky. Even lightning, when the warm upper air discharges, travels invisibly inside the clouds. Thunder walks the highlands without revealing them.

The land must therefore be learned by pressure, echo, and proximity.

It is an immense uplifted country: a high plateau broken into long escarpments, drowned ravines, and shelves of old stone. The plateau is so broad that weather is born upon it and dies before reaching its farther edge. Warm seas of cloud press against its cliffs. Rain moves across the uplands in wandering fronts hundreds of kilometers wide, and in the intervals after rain, the rocks continue weeping for days. Water lies close beneath every surface. It swells the soil, fills root hollows, moves through porous beds of mineral, and emerges wherever the slopes have been cut.

The erosion here is ancient and composed. There are few raw faces. Cliffs have been softened into fluted walls and deep overhangs; boulders have worn sockets into one another; streams occupy channels so old that their beds have become polished troughs. Whole mountainsides are pleated by drainage. Basins nested inside larger basins collect peat, black water, fallen timber, and the slow rain of shells from creatures overhead.

Nothing appears newly ruined. Even collapse has a settled geometry.

Along the escarpments, waterfalls descend through darkness in stages. Their spray can be felt far away as a cool thickening of the air. Some plunge into forests rooted on intermediate ledges; others vanish into shafts without a bottom audible from above. In the largest gorges, falling water generates winds of its own. These winds climb the walls laden with mist, feeding hanging gardens that never touch soil.

Vegetation crowds every available plane. Trees rise in tiers, their lower trunks swollen into buttresses, their upper branches joined by vines as heavy as bridge cables. Their leaves are broad, tough, and almost black, shaped less to gather light than to shed water and sense vibration. Some fold instantly at the approach of an animal. Others maintain shallow cups where pale larvae live in warm rainwater.

Without daylight, photosynthesis is not the governing bargain. The forests feed on chemistry and heat.

Deep beneath the plateau, hot mineral water circulates through fractured stone. It rises along faults carrying sulfides, iron, and dissolved salts. Root systems descend toward these flows in braided masses, enclosing colonies of bacteria that fix carbon from the mineral-rich seepage. Fungi spread the products upward. Trees barter sugars, nitrogen, warmth, toxins, and structural tissue through networks that may extend across entire watersheds. A grove is not merely a collection of plants but the exposed portion of a buried metabolism.

In some districts the roots themselves are warm to the touch.

Elsewhere, vegetation grows around industrial exhausts.

The machines are scattered, but their influence is continental. A pumping station may stand alone for fifty kilometers: a low block of ceramic and corroded alloy sunk into a mountainside, its doors sealed, its roof furred with moss and nesting animals. Yet pipes leave it in every direction. Some are narrow enough to hold in one hand; others cross valleys like elevated roads. They vanish beneath bogs, enter cliffs, or descend through shafts cut before the present forest took root.

No cities accompany this system. No roads connect its visible parts. There are only isolated works: a turbine house beside a cataract, a switchyard suspended beneath an overhang, a row of pressure vessels standing in a reed marsh, an engine tower whose upper platform is lost in the low clouds. Their lights, if they ever possessed any, are gone or deliberately shuttered. Maintenance is conducted by touch, sonar, heat, and transmitted vibration.

The machinery still operates.

It catches the endless descent of water and converts it into pressure, current, heat, and motion. Turbines turn behind stone walls. Pumps force mineral brines uphill. Compressors inhale the saturated air and exhale it into deep chambers. Vast flywheels, hidden inside the plateau, store the violence of the rainy season and release it gradually through the drier months. The forest has grown into this industry so thoroughly that it is difficult to say whether the system was built to exploit the ecosystem or whether the ecosystem was cultivated to sustain the system.

Roots wrap pipes but seldom crush them. Certain vines seek warm conduits and seal small leaks with rubbery growth. Snails graze corrosion from electrical contacts. Dense mats of bacteria refine metals in drainage pools, laying down conductive films that machines harvest with comb-like electrodes. When a valve fails, animals gather around the change in sound days before any repair mechanism arrives.

The repairers are seldom seen, though in perfect darkness “seen” has little meaning.

They announce themselves as groups of precise clicks moving against the background roar. Some have many articulated feet and carry replacement parts beneath plated bodies. Others travel inside pipes. Larger units crawl along the cliffs on tensioned lines, stopping to brace weakened sections of aqueduct. Their outer surfaces support moss, mites, frogs, and small fruiting fungi. Predators stalk them for the warmth of their joints.

The fauna is abundant enough to give the darkness texture.

There are gliding animals that launch from high trees and navigate by listening to rain strike the canopy. Their membranes are ribbed to alter the pitch of passing air. They do not glow. Almost nothing here does. Light would carry too far and reveal too much. Communication instead occurs through clicks, drumming, scent, heat pulses, and low-frequency calls transmitted through trunks and stone.

Flocks of hollow-boned grazers move across the treetops, cropping fresh leaf folds. They keep contact by knocking their beaks against branches, producing rolling sequences that pass from one animal to the next. Marsh creatures inflate resonant sacs beneath the water and make whole basins hum. Small hunters run along vines using rows of pressure-sensitive pads; they freeze whenever a turbine changes speed, because that shift often precedes a surge in the waterways.

In the flooded ravines live long-bodied animals that surface only to breathe. Their breaths rise as warm bursts through the mist. They graze microbial films from submerged machinery and scrape deep, regular grooves into old stone. During heavy rain they climb spillways, and the sound of their armored bellies on metal can be heard for kilometers.

Higher still, above the main forest but below the unseen cloud ceiling, enormous slow-winged creatures circle in the updrafts. Their wings are too broad for rapid flapping. They remain aloft by reading the pressure systems generated by waterfalls and industrial vents. When one passes overhead, the rain briefly changes direction. Smaller animals fall silent until it is gone.

The loudness of the country is not uniform. It has territories.

Near the great escarpments, the waterfalls dominate everything. Speech is possible only by contact: jaw to shoulder, hand to throat, tool against bone. In the forests, sound breaks into thousands of local signals—drips, wingbeats, warning taps, seedpods bursting, branches unloading water. Near industrial works, the rhythm becomes mechanical. Pistons beat beneath the soil. Relays snap inside sealed cabinets. Cables murmur. Somewhere beyond the nearest ridge, ore mills turn with a patient grinding like distant weather.

And there are quiet places, but they are considered dangerous.

Silence may mean that a pressure line is about to rupture. It may mean the forest has detected a hunter. It may mean that a vast concealed machine, normally present as a vibration in the soles of the feet, has stopped. Animals flee such absences before the cause becomes apparent.

Movement is continual. Rain fills high reservoirs; gates open; waterways rise. Floods carry fruit and eggs into lower basins. Mineral blooms spread downstream in rust-colored fans that cannot be seen but can be tasted in the air. Root bridges sag under accumulated water, then spring upward when they drain. Slopes occasionally release—not in fresh, chaotic landslides, but along old, lubricated planes that have moved a little every rainy season for millennia. The forest rides these motions. Trees lean together, roots interlock, and entire groves descend by a few meters without breaking apart.

The machines account for these shifts. Pipes telescope. Foundations float in mud. Towers stand on clustered feet that adjust one at a time, making the structures seem to walk very slowly uphill.

There are traces of occupants, though not enough to explain the works.

Beside some stations stand small shelters with inward-sloping walls, rain chains, and raised sleeping ledges. Their entrances are narrow and marked by patterns cut deeply enough to be read with the fingers. Inside are tool racks, empty hooks, ceramic maps rendered in relief, and speaking tubes that carry sounds from distant chambers. A person placing an ear to one might hear water, a running engine, or the faint coded hammering of another station far across the plateau.

Some shelters are maintained. Their floors are swept by little jointed devices. Fresh woven mats appear. Reservoirs remain potable. Yet whoever uses them does not remain for long, or does not wish to be encountered.

The relief maps suggest that the known plateau is only one high region among many. They show chains of uplands separated by warm inland seas, each covered in watersheds and buried conduits. Certain lines continue off the maps without narrowing, implying distances beyond any practical journey. The industrial network may encircle the world. It may be the reason the world remains warm. It may regulate the hydrologic cycle, lifting water from abyssal basins and distributing it through the upper stone.

Or the network may itself be the landscape’s largest organism: an old metallic vascular system whose builders merely encouraged what was already trying to form.

There are indications that the darkness, too, is maintained.

On the highest exposed ridges stand sparse arrays of enormous vanes. They do not gather light; they turn toward pressure changes in the upper atmosphere. Their surfaces are cold and slick, their edges humming. Above them, beyond reach, the cloud layer has a density almost like fabric. The arrays release aerosols and heat into it, continually repairing its depth. When storms tear openings in the cover, the openings close before anything brighter than a dull thermal warmth reaches the ground.

No creature living here responds well to sudden illumination. Old shelter inscriptions give the same instruction in several tactile languages:

**If the sky thins, descend.  
If the water shines, do not touch it.  
If shadows appear, remain still until they are gone.**

The word rendered as *shadows* may also mean *copies*, *hunters*, or *things made separate by light*.

Most of the time, this warning is irrelevant. The world remains without visible horizon or color. Distance is an acoustic phenomenon. A valley is the delay before an echo returns. A mountain is the long upward angle of wind. A forest edge is where rain changes its note.

Warmth rises through the soles of the feet. Condensation beads on skin, bark, stone, and steel. Everything carries moisture; everything exchanges it. Even within sealed structures, walls sweat faintly in rhythm with distant pumps.

To travel here is to pass through overlapping bodies: the body of the rain, the body of the plateau, the fungal body under the soil, the rooted forest, the distributed machinery, the migrations of animals, the pressure of the cloud sea overhead. None has a clear boundary. Each uses the channels of the others.

The place is ancient enough that its violence has become cyclical, and engineered enough that its engineering has become weather.

Far off, perhaps beyond several watersheds, a bank of turbines comes online. The onset moves through the ground as a sequence of deep impacts. Flocks lift from the canopy. Marshes answer with booming calls. Valves open in buried galleries, and warm water begins climbing toward reservoirs on the highest ridges.

Then the rain strengthens.

It arrives first as a hiss in the leaves above, then as a dense and universal beating. Streams thicken. Rock faces begin to run. Creatures call to one another through the rising noise, and the high dark country—already crowded, already awake—gathers itself into greater motion.
