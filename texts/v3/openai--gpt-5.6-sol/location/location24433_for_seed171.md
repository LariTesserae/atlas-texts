The land is high, though almost nowhere does it feel mountainous. It is a raised continent of living matter: a broad, cool elevation held above the lower atmosphere on roots too deep and numerous to distinguish from geology. Its horizons are flat only at great distance. Nearby, everything rises and folds—boles, buttresses, ribbed leaves, peat swells, reservoirs cupped in green tissue—yet these variations belong to one immense plateau rather than to separate hills.

There is no bare ground.

What seems at first to be soil is a densely woven surface of black rhizoids, pale fungal-looking filaments that are themselves plant organs, and millions of overlapping leaves no larger than scales. Beneath them lie older layers, compressed but not dead: green-brown strata carrying slow currents of sap and rainwater. They have not yet become coal, stone, or even true earth. The continent is young in that sense. It is still deciding what substance it will be.

Minerals arrive dissolved in mist. Plants draw them out molecule by molecule and set them into their tissues as translucent ribs, flexible silicates, conductive veins, and cold metallic nodules. When the oldest growth collapses, these inclusions remain in soft heaps, but streams have not had time to carve them. There are no mature valleys, no rounded gravels, no long scars of erosion. Water travels through capillary roads, temporary seams, and sudden pressure fractures that close behind it. A ravine may open for half a day, fill with silver runoff, and then knit itself shut with roots before evening.

The entire upper surface is wet.

Rain is frequent but rarely violent. More often the air simply thickens until every edge begins to bead. Water condenses on the undersides of vast suspended leaves and travels along them in deliberate lines. It weeps from green cliffs, runs upward through grooved bark under capillary force, gathers in elevated marshes, and hangs in gelatinous globes between branching structures. Shallow pools cover much of the plateau, but their bottoms are not mud. They are breathing membranes, dark and faintly warm, stippled with pores that open when the water becomes rich in dissolved minerals.

The pools are crowded too. Floating plants overlap until water can be seen only through narrow seams. Some resemble mats of tiny cups; others form continuous violet skins that flex under the rain. Tall hollow stems rise through them in forests so dense that they touch and knock together. Their crowns vanish into low cloud. There is hardly an empty volume anywhere—not in the water, not in the air below the canopy, not beneath the surface. Every possible interval has been entered by a tendril, a blade, a vapor-root, a hanging bladder, a net of photosensitive threads.

Yet it is dim.

The primary canopy stands kilometers above the marsh surface in places, supported by columns braided from thousands of mutually grafted trunks. Its leaves spread in continental fans and intercept most daylight. What reaches below has passed through layer after layer of living filters. White light becomes green, then blue-gray, then a deep diluted indigo. During the long overcast periods, the lower landscape is lit chiefly by its own physiology: weak pulses along mineralized veins, pearly gleams inside water bladders, and broad, slow flashes that travel through root tissue beneath the marsh like distant lightning under skin.

The temperature remains cool. Mist lies everywhere, and surfaces are chilled by perpetual evaporation. No snow settles on the plateau, though crystals sometimes form along the highest leaf margins. They grow into delicate combs overnight, then are absorbed at dawn before they can fall. In sheltered hollows, exhaled vapor drifts between trunks in dense white rivers. The vapor does not always descend. Local electrical fields and the pumping of the great plants can lift it, fold it, or hold it motionless in midair.

From a distance, the whole continent appears to manufacture its own weather. Banks of cloud circle the canopy margins. Rain systems move according to the opening and closing of leaves rather than the prevailing wind. Vast stomatal plains inhale, and air bends toward them from hundreds of kilometers away. When they exhale, fog fronts roll outward in concentric walls.

The sound is overwhelming.

There are no animals: no insects, no birds, no hidden swimmers, no voices calling between trees. Nothing runs, hunts, grazes, nests, or burrows. Still, the place is almost never quiet enough for the absence to be felt.

Water drums on the upper canopy with the continuous force of surf. Hollow stems resonate in the wind, each at its own pitch, producing chords too large to hear as music. Hydraulic pressure moves through the plateau in booming sequences. A deep concussion begins beyond the horizon, passes underfoot, and continues for minutes as reservoirs empty into one another. When mineralized fibers tense, they emit shrill, glassy cries. When old support columns split, the noise is like ice breaking across an ocean, though the fracture is quickly filled by pale, foaming growth.

At intervals, entire regions synchronize.

The first sign is a low vibration in the water. Floating leaves turn their undersides upward. Trunks tighten. The dim internal lights extinguish one by one until kilometers of marsh have gone black. Then the continental root network discharges accumulated electrical potential into the cloud deck.

There is no single bolt. The sky becomes a branching white ceiling, every cloud illuminated from within. Thunder does not follow; it arrives simultaneously, a physical wall of sound that flattens mist and drives waves through the suspended pools. Conductive veins flare beneath the ground-skin. For several breaths, the landscape can be seen in impossible depth: not as a forest of individuals, but as one continuous system extending farther than sight.

Its sophistication is not visible as machinery. Nothing here has been built. There are no straight roads, towers, ruins, tools, or debris. Yet the vegetation performs operations that resemble technologies beyond any simple biology.

Leaves measure atmospheric chemistry and alter the rain. Root junctions route electrical signals around damaged regions. Mineral lattices embedded in wood store patterns longer than the tissues that made them. Some groves bend heat, drawing it from one watershed and releasing it many days later beneath another. Certain black fronds manipulate condensation so precisely that droplets arrange themselves in hovering sheets, each drop held apart from its neighbors by tuned charge. These sheets act as lenses, antennae, and perhaps memory.

The landscape does not merely respond to weather. It predicts and composes it.

Nor is its growth random. Across the immense plateau, branching patterns repeat at scales from the microscopic to the continental. A hair-thin vein in a leaf has the same geometry as a thousand-kilometer drainage system. The resemblance is functional: information and water follow related laws here. To redirect one is to influence the other. A change in salinity at a distant marsh may cause a canopy opening weeks later on the far side of the continent. A shadow passing over one region can be stored as a pressure pattern and replayed elsewhere as rain.

Because there are no animals, pollination does not occur through visitation. The plants exchange genetic material through the rain itself. During certain cold periods, the upper leaves release transparent chains of cells into the cloud. The chains travel for months, sampled and altered by electrical storms, before returning in precipitation. Each raindrop may carry instructions from forests beyond the horizon.

Death is equally diffuse. A stem does not simply die and fall. Its neighbors drain its useful compounds, inherit its stored electrical patterns, and brace its shell with new tissue. The hollow remains as an acoustic organ. Thus the deafening voice of the landscape is partly the resonance of its own retained dead: billions of empty trunks, leaf ribs, and root chambers sounding in pressure waves.

At the plateau’s outer margins, the living mass descends into cloud. There are no exposed cliffs. Curtains of roots hang for kilometers, drawing water directly from the air. Behind them, the continent’s underside glows faintly with vascular light. New material accumulates there in soft mineral buds. They harden slowly, not yet shaped by erosion, and become the first tentative bones of future mountains.

Now and then, a bud grows too heavy. It breaks away and falls into the obscurity below.

The separation is announced by a roar deeper than thunder. Nearby roots recoil, then reach after it, continuing to grow into the vacancy. Within hours there is no open space. Within days the torn margin has become a wet garden of translucent shoots. Within a season it has begun to extend outward again.

This is one of the governing rules of the place: emptiness is treated as damage.

Open water films over. Cracks close. Clearings fill with airborne threads. Even shadows are occupied by organisms adapted to nearly no light. Space exists only briefly, as an event between one form of growth and the next.

Another rule is that stone comes after life. The plants are not rooted in geology; geology precipitates from them. Their bodies trap dust, order crystals, concentrate metals, and compress their own discarded layers. The ground beneath them is an unfinished consequence. Rivers, valleys, and cliffs may exist one day, but they are embryonic now, still contained as possible shapes in hydraulic flow and vascular design.

The result is a world at once ancient in complexity and newborn in form: a vast, elevated wetland with no true soil, a forest with no gaps, an infrastructure without builders, a planet-scale intelligence that may not think in anything resembling thoughts.

It is cool beneath the canopy. The rain thickens. Miles away, unseen columns begin to resonate, and the vibration passes through every wet surface until droplets tremble on the leaves.

Then the marsh lights, one vein at a time, and the weather changes its mind.
