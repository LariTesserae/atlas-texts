The city occupies a high basin where mountains have not yet finished becoming mountains.

Their slopes are blunt and dark, pressed upward in broad shelves of basalt and blue-black glass. There are few gullies. The stone is too new for that. Water has only begun to discover where it is allowed to go, so it lies everywhere in undecided forms: trembling sheets over level rock, beads crowded beneath overhangs, shallow black pools with no outlets, films climbing walls under forces that resemble capillarity but are not quite capillarity. Along the basin rim, the peaks weep continuously. No rain can be seen falling. Moisture simply condenses out of the darkness and gathers until every edge acquires a silver lip.

It is bitterly cold. Exposed water should freeze, and some of it does, but the ice is inconsistent. One pool seals beneath a transparent lid while the next remains fluid at a lower temperature. Elsewhere, water hardens into vertical threads or soft, folded membranes. The city’s machines maintain fields that alter freezing, weight, and surface tension locally; after centuries of operation, no one can clearly separate engineered weather from the basin’s natural laws.

There is no plant life. Nothing roots in the cracks, no moss stains the masonry, no lichen softens the exposed stone. The few colors are mineral: iron violet, wet graphite, salt white, the green-black of pressure glass. Pale fungal-looking veils sometimes appear on submerged walls, but they are colonies of microscopic animals locked mouth-to-tail in branching chains. When disturbed, the entire veil contracts and swims away.

The city itself rises in dense tiers from the basin floor to the lower mountain shelves. From a distance it resembles a geology of narrow towers, retaining walls, bridges, sluices, stacked chambers and immense hollow piers. Nearly every surface is occupied. Buildings touch or almost touch, separated by slots through which water, sound, animals, and maintenance mechanisms pass. Higher districts cling beneath cantilevered platforms, so that streets have streets overhead, and those upper streets carry reservoirs on their undersides.

Nothing shines brightly. The city has little use for visible illumination. Its inhabitants—or whatever intelligence arranged it—built by pressure-sense, echo and thermal contrast. The darkness is therefore not interrupted by windows. At most, seams of deep red appear where transit membranes flex, or dim blue rings awaken around flooded entrances. In open squares, frost gives off a wan phosphorescence when stepped upon, recording traffic as bruised constellations that fade over several hours.

The loudness is continuous and almost architectural.

Water booms inside concealed conduits. Vast pipes turn corners beneath the streets, transmitting low notes through the foundations. Towers answer one another with infrasonic pulses too slow to seem like signals: each tone swells for minutes, reaches a pressure that can be felt in the teeth, then subsides. From the mountain walls comes the sharper clatter of condensation striking metal ledges. Ice membranes crack with rifle-like reports. Ventilation throats breathe in drawn-out cycles, and somewhere beneath everything is the soft but immeasurable rasp of countless bodies touching.

The fauna are everywhere.

They fill the squares, the canals, the undersides of bridges, the warm cavities around machinery. They have not displaced vegetation; there was never vegetation here to displace. The basin’s ecology begins with mineral gradients, electrical leakage and chemical films condensed from the water. Small grazers scrape charged particles from walls. Larger creatures eat the grazers. Others plug directly into the city’s energy fields with combs of conductive bone.

In the lower streets, shoals of translucent, many-legged animals flow ankle-deep through the black water. Each is no larger than a hand, but there are millions. Their shells are clear and their organs opaque, so they look like moving fragments of smoky glass with white knots trapped inside. They travel very slowly as a mass, advancing a few streets each day. The front ranks climb obstacles and anchor themselves; the ranks behind pass over them. A migration can take a year to cross a plaza.

The walls are furred—not with hair or growth, but with roosting creatures packed so closely that stone is visible only when they open around a doorway. These animals have flat, hinged bodies and six hooked limbs. At rest they tessellate perfectly, sharing warmth and forming a living insulating layer several bodies thick. When a tower releases its deep pulse, thousands unfold at once. They do not fly. They leap outward, spread into thin disks, and descend through the city’s dense air in silent spirals before climbing another wall.

In the reservoirs live much larger things.

Their backs occasionally rise beneath the water, broad enough to alter the level along an entire embankment. They move with such patience that they are often mistaken for submerged architecture. Towers have been erected on some of them. When one shifts position—perhaps once in a generation—the buildings above lean together, bridges tighten, and whole districts spend years adjusting their geometry. The city accommodates these movements rather than preventing them. Flexible foundations soften. Streets lengthen by adding folded sections. Water is redirected through new gates.

No creature here is quick unless injured. Predation proceeds through enclosure, convergence and waiting. A hunter might spend months closing a ring of limbs around a colony. Prey respond by gradually changing the chemistry of their shells. The resulting contests resemble geological processes more than chases. Yet their combined sound is immense: rasping mouthparts, pressure calls, shell against shell, the wet detonation of air released from deep-bodied swimmers.

Above the rooftops, the sky is less a visible space than a cold, saturated ceiling. Clouds settle into the basin and become indistinguishable from streetside vapor. They carry airborne fauna: long chains of bladder-like creatures that float by regulating internal vacuum. In calm periods they hang between towers like slack cables. Some are mistaken for infrastructure until they detach, turn with immense slowness, and feed on condensation around antenna fields. Tiny animals live inside the larger chains, and still smaller ones inhabit their breathing pores.

The city’s most advanced structures do not look advanced. There are no exposed mechanisms, no bright displays, no obvious vehicles. Technology resides in altered relationships between materials. A bridge may be a single slab of stone whose atoms have been instructed to bend only when a particular animal crosses it. Doors recognize patterns of vibration rather than bodies. Elevators are rooms in which the direction of falling rotates. Reservoirs are deeper inside than their outer dimensions permit, folding long columns of water into compact black chambers. Waste heat is stored in the past and withdrawn during severe winters. Messages travel through entangled seams in the mountain, though the seams also seem to breed small silver parasites.

The settlement does not appear maintained in any ordinary sense. Repair is built into its substance, but repair happens slowly. A cracked wall draws dissolved minerals from the water and thickens around the wound over decades. A collapsed arch softens, spreads across the street, and rises again in a new shape. New buildings begin as dark puddles extruded onto prepared foundations. Over years they crystallize upward, first as rounded stumps, then as hollow towers. During this embryonic stage, animals nest inside their warm, half-liquid walls. Their movements leave cavities that later become rooms and corridors.

Thus construction and ecology cannot be separated. The city provides heat, fields and mineral food; the animals provide insulation, circulation, excavation and pattern. Some species are clearly engineered, though their original purposes have become obscure. Others may be descendants of maintenance systems that crossed some forgotten boundary into life. There are hinged scavengers that collect loose bolts and incorporate them into their skeletons. Blind tunnelers align superconductive filaments in the mud. Great reservoir animals filter impurities from folded water. Pale swarms gather around structural stress and sing until the stone changes phase.

There are inhabitants in the conventional sense, but they are difficult to distinguish from the overwhelming animal presence. They move inside dense coats assembled from small living plates. Their buildings open for them, and the wall fauna part, but these responses may indicate recognition, kinship or simply a useful vibration. Figures cross the elevated causeways in groups of three or four, sounding the pavement with staffs. They rarely speak above the city’s noise. Instead, they carry resonators that persuade nearby structures to repeat their low, patterned knocks over long distances.

At the basin’s center lies the Unfinished Hollow, a depression several kilometers wide. It is not an ancient crater but a new absence, still being made. The rock there is smooth and uneroded, curving down into darkness. Water pours into it from hundreds of urban channels, yet its level rises by only a finger’s width each century. Around its rim stand dense banks of instruments shaped like monumental tuning forks. They sustain a chord so low that it is experienced as weather: nausea in some species, migration in others, ripples crossing water that has otherwise gone still.

What the Hollow is for remains uncertain. It may be a harbor for something not yet arrived, a machine intended to lower the basin, or the first stage of a manufactured sea. Some say the mountains are spoil pushed up from its excavation. This would explain their newness, their unweathered faces, their confused strata and the absence of mature valleys. It would also mean the city is not perched among natural peaks at all, but along the rim of a work site whose scale has been misunderstood.

The terrain changes, but with immense restraint. Water tests a groove in the basalt for a hundred years. Frost lifts one plate of paving and holds it raised for fifty more. A reservoir beast turns beneath its district. A tower flexes. An airborne chain drifts across the basin. Millions of small bodies alter course around a newly formed trickle.

Nothing is still, yet almost nothing can be seen moving if watched directly.

In the darkest hours—though no day is ever bright—the condensation thickens until the city seems submerged. Sound becomes close and directionless. Towers vanish a few spans above the streets. The animal layers stir on every wall. Black water rises over the paving, carrying flakes of new ice and crowded transparent bodies. Far below, the Unfinished Hollow answers the tuning forks with a single prolonged vibration.

Then every creature in the basin pauses.

The conduits continue booming. The peaks continue weeping. The city’s folded chambers shift infinitesimally around their impossible volumes.

After several minutes, or perhaps after much longer, the fauna begin again: billions of limbs, shells, membranes and mouths resuming their patient labor in the cold.
