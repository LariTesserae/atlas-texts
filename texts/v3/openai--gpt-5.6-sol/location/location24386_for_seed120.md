The place is no larger than a crowded courtyard, yet it occupies an altitude at which continents appear as stains beneath the clouds.

It is hidden inside the summit of a manufactured mountain.

There is no bedrock. There is no soil in the ordinary sense. Beneath every root, puddle, and kneeling place lies another crafted surface: split cane tied with black fiber, tiles pressed from ash and shell, lattices of fired clay, shelves of woven reed plastered with warm mud. Beneath those are older lattices, and beneath the older ones are still smaller constructions, fitted together with pegs, knots, resin, and wedges of green wood. Nothing has been quarried here because there was never anything to quarry. The mountain itself was assembled upward, one hand-span at a time, into the killing thinness of the upper air.

The hidden courtyard lies in a fold near its peak, enclosed by walls that lean together like cupped palms. From outside it would be impossible to find. The entrance is a slit behind a curtain of steaming roots, barely wide enough for a shoulder. Passing through it does not seem to lead inward so much as downward, although the chamber on the other side is demonstrably higher than the entrance.

Heat gathers here.

It is a close, bodily heat, trapped among damp walls. Water beads on every cord and leaf. Condensation forms on the undersides of roofs, runs along carved channels, and falls in large, slow drops into crowded basins. Rain is frequent but seldom arrives from above. It seeps from joints, emerges from the pores of ceramic pillars, or appears suddenly on the smooth faces of objects that were dry a moment before. Some water climbs threads of twisted bark and collects in hanging cups. Some lies just under the woven floor, visible through finger-width gaps as a dark, trembling gleam.

At the center is a pool only three paces across. It has no open edge. Shelves, planters, footbridges, drip-spouts, ladders, shrines, and bundles of roots press against it from all sides. Even the water is crowded. Lily-like leaves overlap in several layers, their undersides crimson and their upper surfaces almost black. Pale threads of fungus stitch one leaf to another. Tiny floating gardens have been built from corky stems and sealed baskets, so that patches of moss and peppery fern drift across the pool without ever touching whatever passes for its bottom.

The pool is deeper than the mountain is tall.

A hand lowered into it vanishes at the wrist, not from darkness but from distance. Ripples spread downward instead of outward. Sometimes a drop falling from the ceiling strikes the surface and makes the whole chamber sway, while the water itself remains perfectly still.

Everything here is under construction.

Wet clay bulges from molds tied to the walls. New steps, still soft enough to take a thumbprint, descend into corners that did not exist the previous day. Reed ribs are bent into arches and held in place by cords until they remember their curves. Fresh plaster shines beside ancient-looking murals, though the murals may have been painted that morning. Small scaffolds occupy nearly every free space. They are crude but ingenious: forked sticks, rawhide lashings, shell scrapers, stone-headed mallets, bronze needles used until they are thin as grass.

There are no ruins because nothing has yet had time to become one. There are no worn thresholds, no rounded corners, no channels cut by long-running water. The world does not permit erosion here. A surface may crack, sag, soften, or split, but before weather can abrade it, the crack buds. Damp fibers emerge from the fracture. Clay swells into fresh ridges. Knots tighten themselves. Broken tiles produce small, unfired replicas along their edges. Destruction does not lead toward smoothness or decay; it leads back into fabrication.

The place is forever younger than its materials.

This gives every surface a raw, provisional character. Walls carry fingermarks. Floors flex underfoot. Roofs are green with newly cut leaves. Pegs bleed sap. Mortar remains warm. Even structures that appear immensely old have the unfinished urgency of something made minutes ago and not yet tested.

Vegetation fills whatever the builders have failed to occupy, and much that they have. Broad leaves unfold from ceramic sockets. Vines are threaded deliberately through ladders, then depart from their intended routes and lace the air into dense green mesh. Ferns grow from baskets suspended inside other baskets. Moss pads every knot. Thick, translucent roots hang from the ceiling and pulse faintly as if swallowing.

No plant grows from natural ground. Each is rooted in a made thing: a jar, a wall pocket, a packed sleeve of fiber, a cracked figurine, a woven mask filled with black compost. The compost itself is manufactured from leaf pulp, charcoal, ground pottery, hair, and the fine dust shed by old ropes. The greenery is lush but intimate, compressed into layers. Leaves touch walls, water, tools, and one another. To cross the chamber is to push through warm foliage and emerge lacquered with rain.

Flowers are uncommon. When they appear, they resemble small apertures more than blossoms. Their petals open onto colors that do not exist elsewhere in the courtyard. A blue flower may show daylight at its center even in the middle of the night. One species produces the sound of distant surf. Another opens only when no one is looking directly at it; its presence is known from the sudden perfume of burnt sugar and wet stone.

The air ought to be thin at this elevation, but it is dense enough to drink. Breathing feels like drawing steam through cloth. At intervals the outer altitude asserts itself. The chamber inhales sharply. Leaves flatten. Flames turn narrow and violet. Ears pop, water boils without heating, and for several breaths every hanging object points toward a different notion of down. Then the hot, saturated atmosphere closes again.

Light enters reluctantly.

There are slits in the upper walls, but they do not agree about the hour. One shows a dim red dawn; another reveals stars; a third emits the greenish light found under deep water. Most illumination comes from bowls of luminous mold and from small oil lamps set into niches. Steam catches their glow, filling the chamber with amber halos. Beyond each halo the packed structures become uncertain: railings repeat, ladders continue too far, and walls seem to fold behind themselves.

The chamber is loud despite its concealment.

Water knocks on clay. Bamboo valves clack open and shut. Cords creak under the weight of wet gardens. Somewhere below, hundreds of hand-built pumps thud in overlapping rhythms, lifting water through the body of the mountain. Wind moans through narrow flutes embedded in the walls. Loose shells chatter. Leaves slap one another whenever the room changes its gravity.

There is also the making-noise: tapping, scraping, twisting, tamping. It continues even when no maker is visible. A mallet left on a shelf may rise a finger’s breadth and strike a peg. A basket slowly braids its own unfinished rim. Clay figures with featureless faces pass wet bricks from hand to hand, though their arms are part of the wall and cannot possibly reach one another. Their movements are patient, primitive, and exact.

Living animals are rare.

A few heat-drunk moths cling to the undersides of leaves. They have wings like pieces of thin hammered copper, though no one here knows how to hammer copper that finely. Tiny frogs sometimes appear in water jars after storms, one frog to a jar, always facing east even when the directions turn. They never call. Their throats swell instead with a faint orange light.

The largest creature is a white, many-legged lizard that lives among the scaffolds. It is seldom seen whole. A tail crosses one bridge while, impossibly, its head drinks from a basin on the opposite wall. It eats the small knots that form spontaneously in cords. If it did not, the lashings would tighten until the entire chamber crushed itself.

There are no birds, though wingbeats frequently pass overhead.

The rules of the place are practical, local, and unstable. Water must always be given a route, or it becomes depth. Empty space must be filled before dusk, or it begins duplicating nearby objects. No ladder may have the same number of rungs on two consecutive days. A roof tied with an even number of knots sheds rain; a roof tied with an odd number remembers rain and releases it weeks later. Nothing may be measured twice with the same cord, because the second measurement alters the first.

Most important: no structure can be completed.

A finished wall seals itself from the present and becomes an entrance to some earlier stage of the mountain. A finished vessel begins filling with the sky. A finished bridge arrives at both ends before anyone steps onto it and thereafter cannot be crossed. Therefore every object retains one deliberate flaw—an open seam, an unpainted mark, a missing peg, a loop waiting to be tightened. The entire summit persists through incompletion.

At irregular intervals, the mountain grows.

There is first a pressure in the floor, then a sound like green timber splitting under an ocean. The woven platforms lift. Clay ribs push out of walls. New rooms bud behind old shelves. The central pool draws into a narrow, bottomless funnel, feeding water through channels that are still being made. Plants unfurl at frantic speed to occupy the new surfaces. Roots stitch across gaps. Scaffolds assemble themselves badly and must be corrected by hand.

For several minutes the courtyard becomes almost impassable. Every object is active. Tiles hatch from clay. Rain rises. The dim lamps burn downward. The little frogs turn west. The white lizard appears in several places at once, devouring knots before the cords strangle the newborn architecture.

Then the growth subsides.

Steam settles. Water resumes dripping. The chamber is again no larger than a crowded courtyard, though it now contains another staircase, three additional walls, a grove of broad-leaved plants, and a narrow balcony overlooking a valley that cannot exist inside the mountain.

From that balcony, if the leaves are parted, one can see the manufactured peak continuing upward without limit: terraces of cane and clay, wet scaffolds, hanging gardens, ladders, pumps, warm roofs, and dark reservoirs, all packed together against a dim sky.

Far below, lightning moves silently through the clouds.

Far above, the same courtyard can be seen again, hidden in the summit, still being built.
