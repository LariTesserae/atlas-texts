The city occupies a high, shallow continent of stone.

It does not rise in a single cluster. It spreads from ridge to ridge, across distances large enough for rain to fall in one district while another remains under pale sun. From the western escarpment, the roofs and terraces continue almost to the limit of sight: gray steps, dark courts, walls the color of wet ash, occasional towers worn down to broad stumps. Valleys pass through it without interrupting it. Streams enter beneath the walls and emerge miles away. Whole neighborhoods have become watersheds.

The elevation is not severe enough for permanent snow, but the air is consistently cool. Clouds travel low, dragging hems of mist across upper streets. Rain is frequent and usually light. It beads on stone, gathers in old chisel hollows, and runs down stairways in threads so clear that each submerged edge remains visible. Even after several dry days, water continues to weep from retaining walls. The foundations stand on a broad shelf of porous rock, and the hidden water table lies close beneath every pavement.

There are no working devices here. No wheels turn, no lamps burn, no pumps raise the buried water. The city’s surviving systems are simpler than machinery: slope, weight, fitted stone, open air. Gutters cut along roof edges guide rainfall into cisterns. Overflow passes from basin to basin, then into narrow canals lined with dark slabs. Gates once lifted by hand have rotted or vanished, leaving only grooves in their frames. Water now chooses its own routes through the inherited geometry.

The construction is dense. Houses touch houses, sharing walls two and three bodies thick. Passageways narrow until shoulders would brush both sides. Courtyards lie behind courtyards, each entered through an offset doorway that keeps the wind out. In the oldest quarters, later buildings have been raised directly upon earlier ones, so that a street may run over buried rooms and under exposed foundations at the same time. The place has the compression of something inhabited for longer than its inhabitants could remember.

Yet no people remain.

Their absence is old enough not to feel like an interruption. Doors are gone. Roof beams have softened, fallen, and become dark seams in the rubble. Rain has rounded the stair treads. Corners have lost their sharpness beneath centuries of touchless weather. Where walls collapsed, the debris no longer looks newly broken: every fragment has settled into a stable position, fitted by frost and gravity among the fragments below it.

The city is not being dramatically reclaimed. Vegetation is too sparse for that. There are no forests forcing their way through plazas, no vines engulfing towers. The plateau’s thin soil supports low, severe growth: silver grass in drain channels, black moss under north-facing ledges, pale crusts of lichen spreading across roofs. In sheltered courts stand a few twisted shrubs with small blue-green leaves. Their roots follow mortarless joints deep into the walls, but they grow slowly and seldom rise above a person’s waist.

This scarcity makes every plant distinct. A single tuft of red sedge can mark a square from a great distance. A line of moss reveals the buried path of a water conduit. After rain, dormant films turn green over whole paving stones, then fade again within hours of sunlight. The city seems briefly carpeted and then bare.

Beyond the central districts, construction follows the shape of the land. Long terraces contour the hillsides. Stepped reservoirs occupy natural bowls. Causeways cross marshy saddles where water spreads among flat stones and stands ankle-deep. At the plateau’s margins, buildings become less complete and the streets broaden into quarry roads, though it is difficult to tell where extraction ended and settlement began. Cliffs have been cut into chambers; chambers have weathered back into cliffs.

The stone itself is layered volcanic rock, fine-grained and nearly blue when wet. Builders split it along natural planes, producing blocks of broad, uneven regularity. No two are identical, but their accumulated fit gives the city a grave coherence. On exposed heights, iron in the rock has weathered to muted rust. Lower walls remain almost black from moisture. Pale mineral deposits hang beneath arches in blunt folds, slowly turning architecture into cave.

Water is the chief inhabitant.

It taps from ceiling cracks into empty rooms. It lies in rectangular cisterns beneath open sky. It seeps invisibly through fill and appears again as a cold spring in a lower court. During heavier storms, dry alleys become temporary streams. The runoff carries no great burden of mud because the land has long since stabilized; instead, it moves fine gray grit, lichen flakes, seeds, and the occasional whitened bone. By the following morning the channels are clear again.

In the largest plaza, rain gathers over a pavement subtly bowed by age. The resulting pool may be hundreds of paces across but only a hand deep. Clouds and roofless facades are reflected in it with greater apparent solidity than the ruins themselves. Wind travels across the water in long dark bands. When the pool drains, it leaves concentric rims of silt, recording the storm.

There is weather within the city and weather above it. Warm air rising from the western lowlands condenses against the plateau. Fog enters the outer districts through broad avenues and divides at walls, pouring into courts and stairwells. Seen from a tower, the lower neighborhoods disappear first, while ridges of roofs remain like islands. Elsewhere, sunlight may pass through breaks in the clouds and move slowly across entire boroughs, bringing out the dull mineral colors: greenish slate, brown iron, cream calcite, wet black masonry.

The light is ordinary, which makes the scale more convincing. Nothing glows without cause. The days are neither permanently dim nor unnaturally brilliant. On clear mornings, shadows lie sharp inside doorways. At noon the city appears exposed and almost simple. Toward evening, its depth returns as thousands of walls cast thousands of overlapping bars of shade.

Sound is scarce despite the continual movement.

Rain is usually too fine to roar. It whispers on shallow water and ticks under projecting stones. Wind rarely howls because the dense construction breaks it into small currents. One passage exhales; another carries a faint, steady murmur from a hidden channel. Drops fall at intervals in underground rooms, each one followed by a silence large enough to seem architectural.

Thunder is uncommon but immense. It travels along the plateau and returns from distant escarpments minutes later, softened into something less like a crack than a shift in the ground. In winter, freeze and thaw loosen blocks. A stone may fall somewhere beyond sight, strike twice, and settle. Nothing answers it.

Animals are present mostly as traces. Small gray swifts nest in a few high crevices during the warmer months, feeding over the marshes at the city’s edge. Pale newts live in cisterns that never dry. There are beetles beneath loose slabs and tiny translucent crustaceans in the underground water. Occasionally a lean mountain deer crosses an open terrace at dawn, stopping often, its hooves making almost no sound on the moss-dark pavement. Predators are rarer still. A solitary fox may range through several districts, known only by tracks in silt and the disappearance of nesting birds.

There are no herds, no constant calls, no busy colonies. Life survives in separated pockets connected by water.

The active processes are mostly impersonal and persistent. Fog wets the stone. Sun warms it. Night cools it. Water enters cracks, freezes, and widens them by fractions. Mineral skins thicken around outlets. Moss advances on one wall and dies back on another. A blocked drain diverts a stream through a house; years later, a collapsed floor opens a new route and the old streambed dries. Marshes expand across low plazas during wet decades and retreat during dry ones. The city changes continuously without ever seeming hurried.

Its stability is not preservation. It is balance.

Many structures have already lost everything they were going to lose. Their remaining walls are thick, low, and well seated. Rubble buttresses them. Soil has filled their interiors to the level of the outer streets. Elsewhere, intact vaults endure because the pressure above them has become evenly distributed. Erosion has ceased to act like destruction and become a form of maintenance, removing weak edges, rounding exposed surfaces, and laying the pieces into new slopes.

Far below the upper city lies a second, drowned arrangement. Through cracks and wells, one can glimpse water several stories down, occupying chambers, streets, perhaps entire quarters. The flooded spaces are not dramatically deep or mysterious; they are simply the lower consequence of the plateau’s long saturation. Their ceilings sometimes collapse, creating circular ponds in courtyards above. Reeds colonize these openings, though never thickly. In clear weather, submerged stairs can be seen descending until perspective and reflection erase them.

At the eastern boundary, the plateau breaks into a succession of broad escarpments. The city descends with them. Each lower level is wetter and more ruinous than the one above. Springs burst from cliff faces beneath old foundations, forming veils that tremble in the air before collecting in marshy ground. Mineral stains spread downward like faded banners. The water eventually gathers into rivers, but from above those rivers are narrow silver threads crossing a country otherwise hidden by distance.

No single vantage reveals the whole place. Hills conceal central basins. Fog removes entire regions. Roads branch, rejoin, and sink beneath later construction. What appears to be the edge of the city is often only a sparsely built ridge, beyond which dense walls begin again. Travelers could spend weeks moving through it and remain within the same watershed, hearing only rain, runoff, and their own passage.

The city has no obvious center now. Perhaps it never had one. Its great spaces are distributed: reservoirs, market courts, quarry amphitheaters, stair fields, roof terraces large enough to hold villages. Some seem ceremonial until rain reveals their function. A monumental avenue becomes a flood channel. A ring of chambers proves to be a retaining structure around a spring. A vast paved depression, apparently a gathering ground, fills every wet season and becomes a lake for a month.

Purpose and landscape have worn into one another. The builders followed the water, and then the water followed what they built. Neither has fully prevailed.

Under an overcast sky, the place can seem colorless and finished. Then the clouds open. Light moves over the wet roofs, every surface answering differently: dull walls, bright pools, black arches, silver grasses, small mirrors held in thousands of hollows. The city does not wake, exactly. It has never been asleep.

It continues in rain, seepage, settlement, frost, and the slow exchange between stone and air—vast, nearly silent, densely made, and no longer dependent on anyone to remain a place.
