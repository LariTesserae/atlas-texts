At first it appears to be a high, dark plain.

It lies above the lower cloud decks, broad enough to bend weather around itself, its horizon softened by warm mist. There are no peaks in the ordinary sense. Whatever mountains once stood here have been worn down past grandeur into long swells and shallow basins. The land rises and falls by degrees too gentle to notice while walking. Only after hours does one realize that the water has begun to run in another direction, or that the clouds now lie below rather than alongside.

There is almost no exposed earth. The plain is made of vegetation—not merely covered by it, but constructed from it.

Its uppermost surface is a quilt of low, close-growing plants: liver-colored pads, waxy rosettes, black mosses with silver tips, and warm mats of tiny leaves that close when touched. Beneath these lies a deep accumulation of roots, compressed stems, waterlogged bark, pollen, husks, and slowly collapsing trunks. This living substrate is hundreds of meters thick in places. It bridges old ravines, buries stone, and sags into the hollows left by vanished rivers. Walking on it produces no clear footfall. The ground gives slightly, as if breathing in sleep.

Here and there, erosion has opened immense wounds in the mat. At their edges the plateau reveals its age in layers: pale root-felt over red peat, red peat over fibrous black bands, black bands over wood turned glossy and dense without becoming stone. Water leaks from every seam. Some escarpments weep in thousands of separate threads, each one too thin to become a waterfall, so that the cliff seems hung with trembling glass. Ferns grow downward from these faces. Long white roots descend into cloud and disappear.

Rain is frequent but seldom violent. It arrives as condensation first. Droplets gather on leaf edges, on fur, on the fine hairs of stems. The air thickens until every surface begins to bead and run. Only then does a soft rain fall, usually without visible clouds overhead. Water lies close beneath the ground everywhere. A heel pressed into the moss may fill its own hollow. Pools form in old footprints and remain for years.

The pools are dark, warm, and astonishingly deep. Their visible margins may be only a few paces across, yet below the woven surface they connect to flooded chambers within the root mass. Some have no mineral bottom. Soundings would pass through layers of suspended leaves and pale submerged stems before entering black water beneath. In certain basins, entire groves float, their roots braided into rafts thick enough to hold small ponds of their own.

Nothing grows alone.

The smallest plants live on larger leaves. Vines carry mosses; mosses carry flowering threads; flowering threads shelter translucent molds that glimmer faintly after rain. The trunks of the taller growths cannot be seen directly. They are wrapped in old bark, strangling stems, aerial roots, bowls of humus, and generations of plants that have sprouted in one another’s crowns. A single column may be tree, garden, reservoir, nesting cliff, and bridge.

The largest of these columns rise sparsely from the plateau, wide enough to alter the local weather. Their crowns spread horizontally rather than upward, pressing into the low ceiling of vapor. Beneath them the light is green-black and directionless. Rain takes hours to descend through the layered leaves. It pauses in cups and folds, spills from one canopy into another, and reaches the ground as irregular drops large enough to startle animals.

Between the columns are regions of lower growth: reed plains, fern hollows, orchid thickets, and slow-moving carpets of floating vegetation. From a distance these seem open. Up close, they are crowded with stems and small lives. Warm vapor occupies every gap. Tendrils cross at face height. Seed filaments drift like smoke and cling to wet skin.

The plateau is ancient enough that its drainage has become indecisive. Rivers do not cut brisk valleys. They wander through broad channels lined with spongewood, split into a hundred shallow streams, vanish under root shelves, and emerge days later on the far side of a basin. Their water is tea-dark but clear, stained by immeasurable ages of leaf-fall. In the dimness, reflections look more substantial than the sky.

Along the outer margins, where the living plain ends, the water gathers into slow curtains and pours over cliffs nearly hidden by vegetation. The falls rarely strike bare rock. They descend through hanging forests rooted in the escarpment, separating and rejoining around trunks, becoming mist before reaching the unseen lowlands. The plateau loses itself grain by grain, but the plants rebuild its edges almost as quickly. Roots advance into empty air, catch debris, thicken, and become new ground. Erosion and growth have reached an old agreement.

The warmth is constant rather than fierce. Days are humid and mild; nights are scarcely cooler. There are no sharp seasons. Instead, the place passes through long conditions: decades of heavier condensation, centuries in which certain basins deepen, intervals when one kind of flowering spreads across thousands of kilometers. A bloom may begin beyond the horizon and take twelve years to arrive. When it does, the plateau changes color so gradually that no resident animal ever sees the transformation, only the state before and the state after.

The fauna are abundant, though the land often seems empty.

Most creatures are small or hidden. Flat-bodied grazers move beneath the top layer of leaves, making the vegetation rise in quiet traveling humps. Glass-winged insects cling under fronds, their bodies patterned like drops of water. Long-limbed amphibians live inside the cups of giant plants and may spend their whole lives without touching the ground. Pale, eyeless swimmers pass through the flooded root chambers. Warm-blooded animals with dark, absorbent coats travel along branchways in family groups, freezing whenever rain stops.

Larger beings leave signs rather than appearances. Wide trails close behind them as stems slowly stand upright. Bark is polished at shoulder height. Pools are found emptied of floating flowers. On rare clear mornings, one may see a procession moving along a distant ridge: tall, narrow animals stepping with impossible care, each bearing colonies of epiphytes on its back. Their outlines resemble walking trees until one bends to drink.

Predation is quiet here. There is little pursuit. Hunters wait at crossings, mimic rotting wood, or allow harmless species to nest on them as camouflage. Some pools have flexible rims that tighten by degrees. Some blossoms are mouths only during the warmest part of the night. Death tends to occur by patience, enclosure, or failure to notice that the landscape has changed its shape.

For all its life, the plateau is subdued in sound.

The vegetation absorbs echoes. There is the close ticking of condensation, the occasional heavy drop from a canopy, the soft friction of leaves turning toward moisture. In reed basins, invisible insects produce a low pulse for a few minutes at dusk, then stop together. Distant water is more felt than heard, a continuous pressure beneath the quieter sounds. Animal cries are rare and short. A call may cross the mist once, receive an answer hours later, and not be heard again for a season.

Thunder occurs below the plateau. Its light flickers upward through the cloud sea, illuminating hanging roots from beneath. The sound arrives muted and late, as though something enormous were turning over under the world.

Scattered across the plain are structures, though it is difficult to decide who built them.

In the drier swells stand small roofed chambers woven from living branches. Some are no larger than nests; others could shelter several large animals. Their walls continually bud and leaf. Openings narrow during wet years and widen during dry ones. No cut marks exist, no bindings, no shaped tools. The branches appear to have been persuaded by repeated pressure over generations, or perhaps they grew around bodies that no longer come.

There are also rings of upright trunks surrounding empty pools. Each trunk belongs to a different species, yet their roots interlock beneath the water. Low causeways cross certain bogs in lines too straight to be accidental, made from old root bundles overgrown with moss. At intervals along them stand hollow columns filled with clean rainwater. Whether these are abandoned works, inherited animal habits, or organs of the plateau itself is unknowable. Nothing here distinguishes architecture sharply from ecology.

The strangest structures occur near the oldest erosion faces. Small rooms are exposed within the black vegetal layers, sealed between ages of buried forest. Their surfaces are smooth and ribbed like the insides of fruit. Door-shaped gaps open onto solid peat. Roots enter them but do not cross their centers. Sometimes a chamber contains a heap of pale seeds that remain soft after tens of thousands of wet years. Sometimes it contains only warm air.

The place seems governed by a few slow rules.

Bare surfaces do not remain bare. Any opening becomes habitat, then garden, then ground.

Water never takes the shortest path. It is delayed, divided, lifted through roots, stored in flesh, breathed into mist, and returned elsewhere.

Nothing truly falls while something living can catch it. Dead trunks hang for centuries in vines. Soil gathers on them. New groves grow in suspension. Even the cliffs are crowded with arrested descent.

Distances are deceptive because the landscape repeats its forms at every scale. A pool resembles a valley seen from above. A leaf vein resembles the plateau’s rivers. A branching root chamber may extend farther than a surface watershed. Travelers can cross a hundred kilometers and feel they have circled one grove, or step through a curtain of hanging stems and enter a basin with weather unlike anything behind them.

And age does not simplify the land. It layers it.

Beneath every living surface is an older surface, still damp, still partly intact. Beneath every forest lies another forest folded into darkness. The plateau has been settling toward a final form for longer than any creature can remember, but its final form is not stone or dust. It is an ever-deepening body: warm, saturated, dimly lit, inhabited throughout.

From the cloud sea below, the entire highland might look like a single dark continent floating in vapor.

From within, there is no continent, only leaf after leaf, water beneath water, and the patient sense that the ground underfoot is alive farther down than imagination can follow.
