The land lies low beneath a broad, warm sky. It is not quite marsh and not quite plain, but an old surface worn so nearly flat that water has difficulty deciding where to go. After rain, it remains everywhere: in shallow sheets over dark stone, in long braided pools no deeper than an animal’s ankle, in soft depressions filled to their rims, and beneath thin crusts of clay that shine when pressed. Even the exposed rock seems wet from within. Along its faint ledges, clear drops gather slowly and fall without any visible spring.

Nothing here is young except the day’s hatchlings.

The ground is among the oldest exposed surfaces of the region, weathered past roughness. Once there may have been ridges and channels, but ages of heat, rain, and patient chemical decay have reduced them to low swells, shallow basins, and scattered shelves of iron-dark stone. Pebbles have lost their corners. Larger rocks sit like the last teeth in an ancient jaw, rounded almost to disappearance and stained black, red, and violet by minerals and long wet seasons. In places the bedrock has softened into mottled clay, pale gray under the water and ocher where it dries. Thin films of green algae occupy the boundary between the two.

The horizon is open. No forest interrupts it, and no steep rise gathers the eye. The plain extends through a sequence of wet gleams and muted earth colors until heat and moisture erase the distance. Here and there, low islands stand a handspan or a body-height above the flooded ground. These are not hills so much as remnants—slightly harder patches the rain has not yet brought level. Sparse grasses grow on them in wiry clumps. A few broad-crowned trees appear far apart, each isolated enough to cast its own complete shadow. Their trunks are twisted and pale, the bark loosened by alternating saturation and heat. Between them, thorny shrubs and reed beds occur only where some small difference in soil or water permits them.

The emptiness is deceptive. Plant life is sparse in height and mass, but the shallow water is crowded with small bodies.

Frogs cling to half-submerged stones, their skins matching the wet clay. Others remain unseen in cracks and call with booming, hollow notes disproportionate to their size. In the grass islands, insects rasp and pulse. Cicadas build a metallic roar during the warmest hours, rising together until the air itself seems taut. Mosquitoes and midges turn above the pools in narrow columns. Water beetles write temporary circles on the surface. Tiny crustaceans cloud the warmer puddles, and translucent larvae hang head-down beneath films of water.

Birds are scattered across the plain: long-legged waders stepping with ceremonial care, small plovers running in quick starts, dark ibises probing the soft margins, and pale egrets standing motionless until something moves. Their calls carry far over the level ground. A flock lifting from one pool can be heard before it is clearly seen—the clap and rush of wings traveling outward across the flats. Farther away, broad-backed grazing animals move between water patches in loose groups, never dense enough to resemble a herd from a distance. Their trails are shallow, old, and repeatedly softened by rain. Mud closes behind each hoofprint over days.

Despite the noise, the landscape changes slowly. There is little current. Water advances by seepage and withdrawal rather than flood, spreading a few finger-widths overnight, retreating under several days of sun. Pools join, separate, and join again. The deepest basins may persist through the dry interval, becoming warm and tea-colored, ringed by mineral stains that record former levels. Where water evaporates, the clay tightens into many-sided plates. The next rain smooths those cracks without entirely erasing them.

Clouds pass in ordinary procession. Their shadows are among the quickest things here. Under full daylight, the plain is neither gloomy nor dazzling: wet rock reflects pieces of sky; brown water absorbs the rest. Humidity softens distant forms but does not make the place mysterious. Everything has the plain clarity of a warm landscape after rain—the smell of opened soil, damp mineral, algae, animal musk, and bruised grass.

There are no cut paths, no walls, no worked edges, no objects whose shape argues with weather. The only crossings are made by bodies seeking firmer ground. The only boundaries are changes in mud, depth, salt, and stone. Even nests are temporary: cups pressed into grass, scrapes on bare islands, woven reed platforms already sagging toward the water.

Near evening, the heat releases its hold by degrees. Condensation gathers again on the oldest stones. Birds become more vocal as they choose their resting places, while frogs take possession of the lower register. Calls overlap across miles of shallow water until no single creature can be located. The sound is immense, but the land beneath it remains nearly still.

In darkness, the pools hold the last gray of the sky. Warm water touches warm air. Fish rise where hidden channels connect the basins; wings flicker low over the surface; something heavy enters the mud with a soft, collapsing step. By morning the mark will have rounded edges. After enough mornings, it will vanish entirely.

That is the governing law of the plain: not sudden destruction, but endless reduction. Every prominence settles. Every sharp thing is softened. Water does not carve dramatic gorges here; it lingers, dissolves, stains, and levels. Life returns loudly in each wet season, breeds in the temporary abundance, and withdraws when the pools contract. The ground receives all of it without appearing to change.

Yet it is changing—by films, droplets, softened grains, and distances too small to watch. The plain has spent ages becoming this open, this low, this patient. It is not empty and not finished. It is simply very near the shape that time gives to everything when time is allowed to continue.
