The high country lies beneath an ocean.

It is not the seafloor in the ordinary sense, but a drowned mountain region: a broad shelf of ridges, saddles, and steep-sided valleys suspended far above the true abyss. Below its outer cliffs, the bottom falls away for kilometers into pressure-darkness. Above, equally distant, a ceiling of pale ice drifts over the unseen surface. Now and then the ice thins enough to admit a dim, blue-gray stain of day, but most of the upland remains in darkness.

The water is almost freezing. It is clear where it stands still and clouded wherever it moves. Long currents descend from cracks in the overhead ice and pour through the valleys like invisible rivers, revealed only by streaming mineral dust and the backward bending of plants. Other currents climb the escarpments, driven upward from warmer depths, carrying silt, dissolved salts, and the faint metallic taste of the abyss.

There is no animal life. Nothing swims, grazes, burrows, drifts, or watches. The absence becomes one of the landscape’s strongest qualities. No sudden body crosses the open water. No shells cling to stone. No tracks disturb the gray sediment. Even the smallest moving specks are fragments of leaf, crystal, or ash.

Vegetation nevertheless thrives.

It grows in separated provinces rather than as an unbroken forest. Wide plains of bare rock and blue-black silt lie between lush groves. On the sheltered sides of ridges stand forests of ribbon plants, their trunks formed from tightly braided fibers rooted in cracks. They rise thirty or forty meters into the water, then divide into thousands of translucent straps. The straps are dark green by the weak light from above, but when disturbed they show a deep violet radiance along their veins. A passing current can illuminate an entire slope, not brightly, but as a slow bruise of color traveling from plant to plant.

Lower down grow heavy fronds plated with silica. They overlap like wet glass shingles and chime when they strike one another. Mats of black moss cover the stone around warm mineral seeps. The moss grows in geometric terraces, each level catching what falls from the one above. In the deeper hollows are pale root-fields without leaves. Their branching forms spread over the sediment for hectares, feeding directly on heat and dissolved minerals. At intervals they raise hollow white stalks, release clouds of green dust, and collapse. The dust hangs in the still water for days.

With no animals to carry pollen or seeds, the plants depend on the country’s violent circulation. Reproduction occurs during storms in the water: periodic reversals when the cold descending currents meet the rising deep flows. Whole valleys fill with spiraling sediment. Leaves tear loose. Seed-capsules knock against the rocks until they split, and their contents are swept through passes and over cliffs. The ecology advances by inundation, fracture, and chance.

The terrain is old and settled into its erosion. Sharp forms have long since been worn away. Ridges are rounded; ravines are polished into deep channels; basalt outcrops have smooth shoulders where centuries of silt-laden water have passed. Yet nothing is still. Fine sediment continually slumps from the higher slopes. Frost acts even underwater: mineral-rich water enters cracks, precipitates, expands, and slowly levers slabs apart. When one falls, the impact can be heard through the water from the far side of the region.

Sound carries too well here.

The ice ceiling groans at all hours, a vast pressure-noise with no clear direction. Distant fractures arrive as blunt detonations. Currents roar in narrow passes. Silica leaves ring and scrape. Loose stone rolls endlessly in deep channels, pushed uphill by one current and dragged back by another. In some valleys, long basalt pillars resonate under the flow, producing low sustained notes. Several pillars sounding together can make the water tremble inside the cavities of the old structures.

Those structures are scattered across the upland.

They are primitive in material and method: dry-stacked stone, split timberlike stalks, braided root cord, wedges of polished bone-white mineral. There is no worked steel, no glass except what the plants make, no machinery. The largest construction is a stepped enclosure on a ridge above the central valley. Its walls are built from flat slabs, carefully balanced and weighted with boulders. Water passes freely through gaps in the masonry. Inside stand rows of forked posts, each holding a bundle of stone tools tied with fiber that has somehow not decayed.

Elsewhere there are solitary huts sunk into slopes, their roofs held down by cairns. Some have doorways so low they seem intended for people crawling against a powerful current. Others are no more than circles of upright rock around patches of cultivated moss. Narrow paths connect them, marked by stones of contrasting color. The paths make little practical sense underwater: they climb directly over ridges where it would be easier to drift around them. Their persistence suggests they were laid when movement obeyed different rules—or that those who used them considered touching the ground essential.

No builders remain. Nothing living here appears capable of tool use. Yet the sites do not feel entirely abandoned.

Stone piles sometimes change between current cycles. Root cords found snapped after a storm are later discovered knotted again, though the knots are crude and uneven. Sediment is absent from certain doorways. The tools in the ridge enclosure slowly rotate in their bindings, all eventually pointing toward the abyssal drop.

The strangest feature of the upland appears only in the darkest periods, when the ice above thickens and the last surface glow vanishes. The vegetation then begins to lean—not with the current, but toward particular empty places in the water. Groves separated by kilometers incline toward the same moving point. Their violet veins kindle one after another. Pale stalks open. Moss terraces release strings of bubbles containing no gas, only tiny pockets of perfect darkness.

These dark pockets rise until they reach a certain height above the ridges. There they stop.

Over weeks they gather into an inverted lake: a black, level-bottomed mass suspended within the surrounding water. Currents divide around it. Sound entering it does not emerge. Beneath this floating absence, the old structures resonate, and the marked paths become visible as faint green lines through the silt.

Then the water reverses.

The suspended darkness breaks apart. The valleys thunder. Forests bend flat against the slopes, scattering luminous leaves across the open plains. Mineral chimneys crack from sudden cold. Silt pours upward over the outer cliffs in immense curtains, giving the impression that the mountains are eroding into the sky.

Afterward, clarity returns slowly. The groves stand upright. The root-fields settle. The stone buildings remain in place, altered only in small ways: a wall lowered by one course, a cairn raised, a tool shifted to another post.

Beyond the shelf, the abyss waits on every side. Above it, the drowned upland continues its old cycle of growth and abrasion—cold, dark, fertile, and loudly empty.
