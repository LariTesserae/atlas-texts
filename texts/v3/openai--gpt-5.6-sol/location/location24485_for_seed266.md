The inhabited world lies on the summit of a single elevation so large that no one living there thinks of it as a mountain.

It is called the Roof, though it has no edge visible from its interior. From the central districts, the land appears almost level: a warm, tawny expanse of mineral flats, low scarps, and dark pavements stretching beneath an ordinary amber-white sun. Only instruments reveal that the ground slopes away for thousands of kilometers. Far out, beyond the freight meridians and the old extraction fields, the Roof breaks into terraces. Those terraces descend through layers of atmosphere toward depths from which nothing returns.

There is no sea below. There are no rivers, aquifers, clouds, or buried lenses of ice. The planet’s oxygen and hydrogen were separated long ago, driven into stone or lost to the upper dark. What passes for weather is made of dust, pressure, static charge, and the migration of gases between heights.

The air is warm and thin. It smells faintly of hot metal, sulfur ceramic, and the clean mineral odor released when stone fractures. At noon, exposed surfaces become painful to touch. At night they retain their heat, and the cities glow only a little warmer than the surrounding plateau. Temperature changes come reluctantly here. A shadow cast by a tower may take hours to cool the ground beneath it.

The plateau has had time to settle into itself.

Its oldest surfaces are black and lustrous, polished by uncounted ages of dust. Broad fields of volcanic glass have been rounded not into pebbles but into low, fused waves, each ridge aligned with winds that may no longer exist. Elsewhere stand forests of stone without anything that could be called a tree: columns of porous basalt, spaced widely apart, their bases narrowed by abrasion until they resemble enormous dark bones balanced upright.

The erosion is mature and patient. Nothing is being freshly carved. Cliffs have withdrawn into long ramps. Craters have softened into shallow bowls. Broken machinery disappears not under soil but under a varnish of electrostatically sorted powder, pale on its upper surfaces and iron-dark below. Even footprints endure only briefly—not because wind erases them, but because the dust grains slowly turn in place, aligning themselves to the planetary field until every disturbance lies flat.

Across this immense, subdued terrain is a city.

It does not gather around a river or harbor. It has no dense center. It is an urban system dispersed across a continental summit: habitation shafts sunk into thermally stable rock, factories built into old quarries, transit lines stretched arrow-straight between distant pressure towers. A district may consist of six buildings separated by thirty kilometers of empty pavement. Streetlights stand along routes no pedestrian would ever walk in full.

The largest structures are the atmospheric mills. They rise from the high scarps where gases ascending from the lower world spread into slow horizontal currents. Each mill is a skeletal ring several kilometers across, carrying thousands of thin metallic vanes. They do not visibly spin. Over days, a ring changes its angle by a few degrees. Over months, its internal weights climb their tracks. The mills harvest pressure differentials, static charge, and the almost imperceptible settling of heavy gases.

Their power feeds the refineries beneath the plateau.

There, industrial heat loosens useful elements from stone. Ceramics are cast, metals condensed, old composites unmade and remade. Since nothing grows, all food, cloth, insulation, sealant, medicine, and lubricant must be synthesized or gathered from the native fauna. Almost every factory is also a recycling shrine. Matter is never discarded casually. A cracked vessel may be older than the district that melts it down. Human remains, if humans are what the builders are, pass through the same careful inventories as machine parts.

Yet the Roof is not lifeless. It is crowded, in its sparse way, with animals.

Most are small enough to hide in joints of stone. During the day, the flats seem empty. At dusk, seams begin to move.

Copper-backed grazers emerge first. They are many-legged creatures, flat as coins, with undersides fringed by conductive hairs. They do not eat plants or drink. They cross mineral surfaces in slow herds, scraping up films of reactive salts and microscopic colonies of catalytic crystal. Behind them, the rock is briefly duller, marked with branching feeding paths.

The crystal films are not quite alive by the definitions used in the cities. They do not reproduce as organisms do. They propagate defects through mineral lattices, passing arrangements from grain to grain. Some chemists call them weather. Ecologists insist that weather does not adapt to traps.

Above the grazers drift the veil animals.

A veil is a translucent sheet of silicone-like tissue stretched across a web of hollow ribs. The largest span hundreds of meters but weigh less than a cargo vehicle. They rise on thermal gradients at morning and descend near evening, steering by changing the electrical charge along their edges. From below they resemble scratches in the sky, or pieces of clear architecture that have come loose.

They feed on airborne ions, metallic dust, and each other’s shed membranes. Because the atmosphere carries sound poorly, the passage of a great migration is nearly silent. Only its shadow moves across the ground: slow, fractured, and immense.

Predators follow beneath. These are taller, jointed things called walkers, though they spend much of their lives motionless. Their limbs are mineral tubes grown around tensile filaments. Their heads contain cavities tuned to tiny changes in electric field. A walker may remain beside a transit pylon for eleven years, indistinguishable from discarded equipment, then strike when a veil descends too low.

The cities tolerate them. Some districts even maintain designated hunting pylons outside their pressure walls. Walkers keep veil swarms from fouling the mill rings, and their discarded limb casings are valuable structural material. Warning paint around a motionless walker is renewed more often than the creature itself moves.

Farther from settlement, life becomes larger and less easily classified.

In the old collapse basins, herds of hollow-bodied animals travel along lines of magnetic inclination. From a distance they look like boulders rolling uphill, though each is carried on hundreds of concealed feet. They cross the same routes generation after generation, polishing broad tracks into the bedrock. Their bodies contain chambers of dense, non-aqueous solvent kept under pressure. When one dies, the chambers rupture and evaporate in minutes, leaving a lacework shell and a dark chemical frost.

These migrations supply much of the Roof’s raw biology. Collection towns stand beside the tracks, built high on armored piers. They do not hunt. They wait for natural deaths, sometimes for decades. A single carcass can keep a synthesis works active for a year.

Despite the number of creatures, the landscape seldom feels busy. Life is distributed over too much space. A million animals may inhabit a basin and still leave kilometers between visible bodies. Movements are economical. Calls are rare. Nothing wastes moisture because there is none to waste; nothing wastes energy because every metabolism depends on weak gradients and difficult chemistry.

Silence dominates.

Close to a factory, one feels machinery through the soles before hearing it. Compressors work in sealed vaults. Freight vehicles glide along magnetized rails without wheel noise. The atmospheric mills produce a pulse below hearing, a pressure sensation in the jaw that comes and goes with the upper currents.

The loudest natural sound is usually stone.

At irregular intervals, a sun-warmed slab gives a single sharp click. Dust pours from a scarp with a dry whisper. Somewhere far away, a hollow animal vents its internal gas after death, producing a note like a bottle being blown across the mouth—deep, pure, and brief. The note may travel astonishingly far through particular atmospheric layers. Settlements record these tones and use them to estimate migrations beyond the horizon.

The light is unremarkable enough to make everything else stranger. The sky is pale blue near noon, yellow toward the horizon, black only at the highest observatories. Shadows have clean edges. There are no fantastic auroras every night, no permanent eclipse, no glowing oceans. Buildings, animals, and impossible geology stand under familiar daylight with nowhere to hide their peculiarity.

From the upper districts, the scale of the Roof becomes apparent.

Observation towers rise from already immense elevations, their foundations drilled through kilometers of ancient lava. Looking outward, one sees successive atmospheric horizons: the nearby ocher plain, a distant band of violet dust, then the curvature of lower haze. The terraces beyond appear to float above one another. Freight lines vanish into the air before they visibly bend.

At the true rim, the ground falls away for tens of kilometers.

The escarpment is not a wall but an entire geography of descending shelves, abandoned industrial ledges, pressure forests, and weathered elevator anchors. Heavy gases pool in the depths, forming invisible lakes without surfaces. Anything entering them loses buoyancy and sinks. Creatures adapted to the summit cannot survive there; machines must descend inside sealed bells, winched slowly from station to station.

The lowest visible shelves hold the oldest constructions. No current city claims them. Their towers were built for a denser atmosphere and have broad fins like the bones of buried ships. Some still draw power. At certain times of year, remote grids illuminate in sequences too regular to be accidental and too slow to be a message anyone recognizes. One line of lights may take nine years to cross a ruin.

Nothing on the Roof happens quickly unless it is breaking.

Districts are planned in centuries. Mill rings turn with seasonal pressure tides. Animals migrate along routes older than the factories. Mineral ecologies spread a finger’s width through stone during a lifetime. The plateau itself continues to rise as heat accumulates below, but so gradually that survey monuments, not inhabitants, perceive it.

Even the city’s politics are shaped by this patience. Ownership is measured less by area than by duration: the right to occupy a place through three cooling cycles, to gather from a migration for seven passages, to maintain a rail until the underlying magnetic seam wanders away. Maps include future ruins as carefully as current buildings. Every structure is designed with instructions for its weathering.

At evening, the sun lowers across thousands of kilometers of inhabited emptiness. Heat shimmer subsides. Copper-backed herds pour from the cracks. Veils descend, catching the last light in their clear ribs. Windows brighten in scattered clusters so distant that each might be mistaken for a separate world.

Then the atmospheric mills adjust themselves by one slow increment.

A tremor passes through the bedrock, too deep to be called sound. Across the plateau, animals pause. Factories briefly dim. Dust lifts in fine vertical threads and arranges itself along invisible fields.

For a moment the whole Roof reveals its order: city, weather, industry, and fauna joined not by water or growth, but by pressure, mineral appetite, electrical charge, and the immeasurable downward pull of the heights.
