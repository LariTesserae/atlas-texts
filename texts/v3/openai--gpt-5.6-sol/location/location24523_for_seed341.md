The district occupies a shallow bowl no more than nine streets across, bounded by an old floodwall of black brick. Beyond the wall there may be a larger city, but it cannot be seen. Heat and vapor erase the upper stories of anything distant, and the few gates have been sealed for so long that banyan roots have entered their hinges and thickened there.

Inside, the land is almost level. Streets descend by imperceptible degrees toward a central pumping court, their gutters broad as canals and permanently half full. Rain falls often, though rarely in clean drops. It condenses first on cables, pipes, leaves, and iron balconies, then releases all at once in localized showers. One side of a street may be hammered by water while the opposite pavement remains merely slick. Buildings weep continuously through their masonry. Mortar sweats. Rusted bolts gather trembling beads. The undersides of bridges drip even when the air above them is dry.

It is intensely hot. The heat does not blaze; there is no visible sun. It accumulates under the low ceiling of cloud and steam, soaked into brick, standing water, and ironwork. Every surface seems to return warmth to the air. Breath would feel used here, already breathed by engines and wet walls.

The district was built around machinery that has never stopped.

At its center stands the pump house, a broad, low cathedral of riveted steel and green-black stone. Its roof has partly collapsed into a garden of ferns, creepers, and broad leaves, yet the engines beneath continue their rotation. Their pistons are hidden below the flood line. Only the rods can be seen, rising and falling through collars in the floor with the patience of well-established tides. Each downward stroke sends a shudder through the district. Window glass ticks in its frames. Puddles arrange themselves into concentric rings. A second later, water erupts from relief spouts along the floodwall and falls back into unseen channels outside.

The noise is enormous, but not chaotic. There is the bass concussion of the pumps, the long metallic groan of flywheels, the clatter of slack chains, and the continuous rush of water through culverts. Steam escapes from cracked joints with a shriek that may last ten minutes, then cease abruptly. Rain drums on sheet-metal awnings. Overflow bells strike when cisterns fill, though many bells are warped and produce only blunt, mournful blows. The district has composed these sounds over decades into a cycle. After a while, silence becomes imaginable only as a disaster.

The streets are sparsely built despite their urban order. There are empty lots between structures, but they are not vacant. Some have become shallow reed beds. Others hold tanks, pipe manifolds, settling pools, or solitary towers crusted with mineral deposits. Tram rails pass through beds of moss and disappear under tea-colored water. Utility poles stand at irregular intervals, each bearing too many porcelain insulators and too few intact wires. Dim lamps still burn on several corners, enclosed in fogged glass. Their light is amber and weak, less illumination than discoloration—a stain laid across the mist.

Vegetation is lush where it has found purchase, but it does not cover everything. Growth occurs in islands separated by flooded stone and expanses of bare, wet brick. Giant ferns occupy stairwells. Pale vines lace factory windows without entering them. Rubber-leaved shrubs rise from drainage grates, their roots combing the current below. On rooftops, dense mats of moss swell during rain and contract in the hot intervals, slowly lifting tiles from beneath.

There are no flowers, or none that open visibly. Pollination, if it occurs, is done by warm air, vibration, and spray. Seedpods hang over the canals and split when the pumps strike their deepest note. The released seeds are flat and metallic-looking. They skid across the water, lodge in rust seams, and germinate within hours.

Nothing moves with intention.

There are no birds on the cables, no rats in the warehouses, no fish beneath the opaque water. No mosquitoes rise from the marsh lots. Even the deepest leaf litter contains no beetles or worms. Fallen vegetation is softened instead by films of heat-loving microbes: pearly skins that spread across dead leaves, dissolve them, and retreat when rainwater becomes too fresh. The absence of animals gives the greenery an unfinished quality. Leaves grow without being bitten. Fruitlike bodies ripen and collapse untouched. Seedlings rise in dense, mathematically neat clusters because nothing disturbs them.

The water is similarly empty. It carries oil rainbows, brick dust, spores, flakes of paint, and occasional sparks from submerged cables, but no swimming life. When a canal’s surface dimples, the cause is always mechanical: a pressure valve opening below, condensate falling from a great height, or trapped gas escaping the mud.

The buildings have reached a durable accommodation with decay. Brick corners are rounded by generations of runoff. Iron stairs have thinned but no longer seem to rust quickly, protected by stable skins of black oxide. Flood marks stripe the walls at regular heights, making each structure resemble a measuring instrument. Where facades have fallen, their rubble has been sorted by water: heavy stone nearest the collapse, brick farther downhill, glass gathered in glittering fans at drain mouths. Nothing looks newly ruined. Even breakage has settled into architecture.

Every few hours, the district changes state.

First, the lamps dim. Then the underground pipes begin to knock, one after another, proceeding east to west. Steam pours from pavement vents and fills the lower streets to waist height. Moisture condenses on every exposed surface. Leaves bend beneath the sudden weight. For several minutes the whole district rains inward: walls, ceilings, cables, and vegetation surrender stored water simultaneously.

The central pumps accelerate.

Canals rise over their edges and spread across intersections in thin, fast sheets. Water turns staircases into cascades. Old elevator shafts become temporary wells. The marsh lots fill until only the tallest reeds remain visible. At the peak of the cycle, the overflow bells strike and the floodwall trembles as sluice gates open somewhere inside it.

Then the water drains almost as quickly as it came. Gutters roar. Whirlpools form over grates. Mud flats emerge steaming. Fine roots tighten around exposed pipes. Within half an hour the district is merely wet again, its reservoirs reset, its bricks dark, its machinery returning to the slower rhythm.

No operator can be found. Control rooms stand open and empty, their consoles furred with mineral bloom. Gauges remain active, but their scales have been overwritten by hand with unfamiliar intervals: not pressure, temperature, or depth, but symbols resembling phases of growth. In one room, a wall-sized diagram shows the entire district as a single engine. Streets are marked as cooling channels, buildings as valves, gardens as filters, and the central pump house as a heart only in the broadest mechanical sense.

The diagram suggests that the plants are not intruders. They are replacement components.

Roots tighten seals around cracked mains. Moss regulates evaporation from cistern roofs. Vines pull corroded shutters open when ventilation is needed and drag them closed before the heavy condensation. Reed beds strain grit from the returning flood. Even the microbial films have a place, feeding on residues that would otherwise clog the system. The city and its vegetation have been in balance long enough that it is impossible to say whether the machinery cultivates the garden or the garden maintains the machinery.

The strangest structure stands at the district’s lowest edge, where three narrow streets meet. It resembles a small station shelter made from cast iron and cloudy glass. Inside is a single dry bench. No rain enters. No condensation forms. Sound is muted there, though the pumps can be felt through the soles of the feet.

Opposite the bench, behind the glass, water rises upward.

It emerges from a drain in the floor as a smooth vertical column, passes through the ceiling without wetting it, and vanishes into the fog above the roof. Leaves, rust flakes, and droplets sometimes enter the column and ascend. Nothing has ever been seen to come down.

Around this shelter the paving stones are cracked into a precise circle. The cracks are full of tiny white roots. At the deepest stroke of the pumps, all the roots contract together, and the upward water hesitates.

For one second, across the entire district, every moving thing pauses: flywheels, rain, runoff, steam, trembling leaves.

Then the engines strike again, louder than before, and the hot, dim city resumes.
