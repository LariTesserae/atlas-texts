The city stands in a high basin of black stone, where a young mountain range has risen too recently to have softened.

Its ridges are narrow and angular. Cliffs break along clean planes, their faces dark gray, blue, and faintly green where minerals have not yet weathered. Scree lies beneath them in sharp, interlocking fans, but no stone is ever heard falling. The channels cut through the slopes are shallow and unfinished, as though erosion began its work and then paused. From the basin floor, the mountains look less carved than assembled: enormous slabs leaned together around a cold, open sky.

There is moisture, though at first there seems to be little water. The air is cool and carries the mineral smell of rain. Condensation beads on every shaded surface—under bridge rails, along the undersides of leaves, inside the hollows of masonry. Thin streams occupy some of the mountain seams, descending without turbulence in narrow silver threads. They feed scattered pools among the rocks and a modest river that passes through the city. The river is broad enough for reed boats but rarely deeper than a person’s waist. Its water is clear, darkened by the basalt beneath it, and so steady that the same ripples remain behind the same stones.

Rain clouds sit against the northern peaks. They are always there. At a certain hour their lower edges turn pearl gray, and a fine rain crosses the basin. Each drop seems newly fallen, yet the soil never becomes wetter. The pools do not rise. The river neither floods nor dwindles. By evening the exposed paving is dry again, although there is no perceptible warmth and no wind strong enough to explain it.

The city follows the river in a loose chain rather than filling the whole basin. Its districts are separated by orchards, vacant terraces, pasture, exposed rock, and long strips of alpine meadow. Seen from the heights, it resembles several towns that have agreed to share walls, roads, and a name. Buildings gather densely at bridgeheads and around public cisterns, then thin into isolated compounds with broad spaces between them.

Most structures are made from fitted stone. The rock splits naturally into flat-sided blocks, allowing walls to rise without mortar. Their workmanship is patient rather than advanced: wedges, hammers, levers, braided ropes, timber rollers. Copper exists but is too soft and scarce for heavy construction. It appears instead in needles, fishhooks, small knives, mirror discs, and green-stained fastenings on ceremonial doors. Roofs are layered with stone shingles or tightly woven mats coated in clay. Upper stories are rare, but the buildings spread widely, enclosing roofless courts where moss, herbs, and dwarf fruit trees grow.

The streets are urban in organization but not in crowding. They are paved, drained, measured, and named. Low marker stones stand at each crossing. Covered channels bring water from springs above the basin, dividing it by means of wooden gates and ceramic pipes. There are granaries, bathhouses warmed by compost rather than fire, animal yards, public ovens, rope walks, carving courts, and long market shelters. Yet between these works lie stretches where only lichen covers the paving and hoofed animals graze beside abandoned-looking colonnades.

Nothing is actually abandoned. Nothing decays far enough to become a ruin.

Wood darkens but does not rot. Roof clay cracks in the same places and no farther. Moss advances to the edge of particular stones and stops. The oldest walls are indistinguishable from those completed yesterday, except that the people know which is which. This is a young landscape held in an ancient condition: fresh mountains, fresh fractures, fresh streets, all denied the next stage of their lives.

Vegetation is richest wherever shade and condensation coincide. Ferns crowd the northern sides of walls. Pale moss forms thick cushions beneath aqueducts. Juniper, mountain plum, and a silver-leaved tree resembling willow grow along the river, but never form a continuous forest. There is always open ground between them: grassland, gravel, bare shelves of stone. On irrigated terraces the city raises barley, bitter greens, peas, gourds, and low vines whose fruit has a cold, translucent flesh. The fields are lush by the standards of the heights, though each is sharply bounded by dryness. Beyond the last irrigation channel, green dwindles within a few paces to wiry grass and dark mineral crust.

Animals are everywhere, but the basin remains strangely quiet.

Small horned deer stand among the outer houses. Slate-colored goats travel the stairways of the cliffs, placing their feet on ledges scarcely wider than a hand. Marmot-like burrowers sit upright along terrace walls. Long-tailed birds crowd the orchards, and white waterfowl occupy the river in such numbers that distant reaches appear flecked with foam. At dusk, broad-winged moths gather beneath the eaves. Beetles shine in rain gutters. Pale fish hold themselves against the current under every bridge.

Their abundance produces almost no clamor. Hooves touch stone softly. Birds open their beaks and make only brief, wooden notes. Insects have no continuous drone. Even the city’s domestic beasts—shaggy cattle, narrow-faced sheep, patient pack lizards with harnesses of felt—seem governed by the basin’s restraint. The loudest regular sound is the hand-mill: stone turning against stone somewhere behind a wall. After that comes the tap of a mason’s hammer, the splash of a paddle, the short call used to announce an opening bridge.

Speech is usually quiet. Sound carries too well in the cool air and returns from the new cliffs with almost no distortion. A shout made in the central square may come back from three mountain faces, each echo clean enough to be mistaken for another speaker. For this reason, conversation has developed a language of small sounds and visible signs. Market prices are indicated with knotted cords. Ferry crews signal with polished boards. Children learn early not to cry out toward the eastern ridge, where an echo can continue at intervals for much of the afternoon.

The city has no clocks. It does not need them.

The same cloud takes the same shape above the northern pass. Rain begins when its edge touches a notch in the summit. The same flock crosses the river after the rain. Shadows reach the same doorways. Fruit ripens but does not over-ripen; grain becomes ready but does not shed. Animals are born, yet the herds do not grow. People age in ways too subtle to measure, and no one agrees whether they truly do. A child becomes an adult, but elders remember other adults who seemed to change no more than the mountains. Death occurs, though rarely, and births answer it with exactness. Every household keeps genealogies because bodies provide unreliable evidence of time.

Work is not done to alter the city so much as to confirm it.

Each year—or what is called a year—the western bridge receives one new paving stone, while one old stone is lifted and carried to the quarry. A house may be expanded only if another courtyard is opened elsewhere. Trees are pruned branch for branch. Hunters take no animal unless tracks show that one has newly appeared. The river channels are cleared of silt, though the quantity removed is always the same: seven baskets from the north sluice, five from the south, one shallow bowl from beneath the council bridge.

No law explicitly demands balance. Balance is simply what survives. Attempts at greater change fail gently. A wall begun outside the established districts is found each morning reduced to its laid foundation, its stones resting again beside the builders’ tools. A field planted beyond the last wet terrace germinates beautifully but never grows a second pair of leaves. If too many boats are made, one older hull loses its seams without rotting, each plank separating cleanly and floating to shore.

The people do not consider this magical. To them, change is the unusual and frightening thing.

High above the city is the newest feature in the basin: a line of pale rock exposed along a summit crest, sharp enough to shine at noon. It has always been called new. Scholars climb to examine it with bone gauges, charcoal rubbings, and plumb lines. Every measurement agrees with the previous one. The ridge has not eroded by a grain. A single split runs down its face, and in that split rests a seed.

The seed is glossy, red, and unlike any plant in the basin. It is always damp. It has never sprouted.

Birds visit it. Rain touches it. Frost films the stone around it each night and withdraws each morning. From the city below, no one can see it, but everyone knows it is there. Some say the basin’s stillness is waiting for the seed. Others say the seed is the one thing being prevented from waiting.

Meanwhile, the river keeps its fixed ripples. The quiet animals move through their appointed abundance. Condensation gathers beneath the bridges, drops into the water, and leaves the level unchanged. Across the open terraces, beneath ordinary daylight, the young black mountains remain forever at the beginning of becoming old.
