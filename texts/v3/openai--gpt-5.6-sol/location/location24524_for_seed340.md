The cavern is not hollow so much as inhabited through.

Far above, beyond miles of compacted salt and dead basalt, there may be a surface, but nothing here has seen it. The inhabited layer lies in a warm fracture beneath the planet’s crust: a buried country several valleys wide, its ceiling lost behind overlapping crowns, wing-clouds, and pale mineral haze. There is no groundwater. No dripping stone, no underground river, no dampness gathering in crevices. Exposed surfaces feel warm, dusty, and faintly waxed.

Yet the place is green—green, amber, violet, black-green—so completely vegetated that “ground” is only the name for whichever living surface bears your weight.

Great load-bearing plants span the depths. Their trunks rise both upward and downward, fastening themselves into ceiling and floor like pillars in a mine. Some are broad enough to contain chambers. Others split repeatedly into braided scaffolds, producing terraces, bridges, cages, and hanging plains. Bark grows in overlapping ceramic plates. New plates are soft and luminous; old ones harden into something like fired clay. Between them run dry vascular threads carrying not water but warm oils, alcohols, liquid sulfur compounds, and milky silicone-rich latex. When wounded, a trunk does not bleed so much as smoke. The escaping fluid flashes into vapor, and the wound seals beneath a blister of resin.

Every surface supports another growth. Blade-thickets stand on the backs of boughs. Coiled epiphytes root into bark pores. Fan-shaped colonies unfold from old scars. There are plants whose roots never touch mineral at all, feeding on heat, airborne carbon, and the waste gases of animals. There are plants growing upon those plants, and smaller plants growing within the breathing chambers of the second layer. Seeds circulate like dust.

The light is unexpectedly ordinary.

Across the cavern ceiling spread vast cream-colored membranes, thin as onion skin and many acres in extent. They are living reflectors. Heat rising from deeper faults excites mineral-bearing cells within them, and they radiate a broad, steady white-gold illumination—not the lurid glow of cave fungus, but something close to a hazy afternoon. Shadows are soft. Colors are clear. The membranes brighten and dim in slow regional cycles, producing a day without sunrise and a night that never becomes fully dark.

From a distance the buried country appears pastoral: pale canopy, green distances, low golden light.

Then the sound reaches you.

It is continuous and enormous.

Millions of animals rasp, strike, saw, hiss, boom, and chatter at once. Winged grazers pour between the trunks in dense migratory sheets, their transparent pinions making a roar like wind through canvas. Burrowing herds move inside the vegetation itself, and the pillars vibrate with their chewing. Armored creatures no larger than fists drum courtship messages into hollow branches. Long-bodied browsers in the upper crowns produce foghorn calls by inflating ribbed sacs along their flanks. Somewhere below, unseen among root vaults, something beats on the hardened floor with the regular violence of quarry machinery.

There are quiet places, but only in the way that an eye has a pupil. Sudden circles of silence open when predators arrive. The hush travels faster than the predator can be seen: chirring stops, branches tighten, seed-dust falls undisturbed. Then there is a crack, a rush of bodies, and the deafening world closes again.

The fauna seem excessive even by the abundance of the plants. Every niche is occupied, and most are contested. Creatures graze the leaves; others graze the grazers; still others live on shed armor, cast-off wings, dung powder, resin crust, heat, vibration, or noise itself. Some animals have become almost architectural. Shell-backed colony beasts anchor themselves between trunks and remain there for generations, filtering spores from the air. Plants grow across them until only their breathing vents show. When they die, their shells become plazas.

Nothing here depends on water. The chemistry of life is built around oils, waxes, volatile hydrocarbons, and salts that pass directly between solid and vapor. Flesh is fibrous and warm, more like packed felt than meat. Animals do not pant or sweat. They cool themselves by raising reflective scales or by releasing puffs of stored gas. Their mouths are grinders, piercing cones, heated chisels, solvent brushes. Their eggs are kiln-dry capsules buried in insulating pollen.

Decay does not produce rot. It produces dust, perfume, and fire.

A fallen limb is quickly stripped by rasping swarms. What remains is colonized by pale furnace-moss that oxidizes the wood in slow, smokeless heat. Sometimes the process runs away. Blue combustion races along a branch network, not consuming it entirely but baking the outer tissues into black glass. Many plants require such fires to germinate. Their seeds split at a precise temperature and drive root-spikes into the newly hardened surface before it cools.

The terrain is young, though the cavern itself may be ancient. The living world is still constructing it.

There are few settled channels, few old valleys, no rounded stones. Erosion has only just begun here, and it is biological. Roots prise mineral slabs from the walls. Herds wear grooves into bark terraces. Acid-breathing larvae etch lacework into salt outcrops. Falling seed masses punch through thin crusts and open new pits. Instead of a landscape slowly being reduced, this one is being shaped for the first time—cut, reinforced, abandoned, overgrown, and cut again.

Cliffs can appear in a week when a support plant withdraws its internal pressure. Valleys close when creepers knit across them. A plain may rise as countless buoyant pods collect beneath it, lifting the whole mat toward the ceiling. Elsewhere a pillar dies and its dependent ecosystem descends over several days in a majestic, noisy collapse. Animals flee ahead of the sinking crowns. Opportunistic plants flower in the newly exposed light before the debris has stopped moving.

Navigation is therefore provisional. Paths are not places but agreements.

The scattered inhabitants—small, upright tool-users with heat-reflective hides—mark routes by hammering ceramic wedges into the oldest trunks. They build rarely and lightly. Their shelters are baskets woven between load-bearing roots, kiln-hardened against gnawers and hung high enough to avoid migrating herds. A settlement may consist of six suspended chambers, a smoke shelf, a seed store, and a platform from which sentries watch the moving canopy.

They possess blades of chipped salt-glass, bone awls, resin cord, fired bark vessels, and crude copper gathered from the teeth of mineral-feeding animals. Open flame is too dangerous for casual use. Heat is carried in sealed clay bulbs containing slow-reacting powders. Their most important craft is listening: they press forked sticks to trunks and interpret the vibrations. A changed rhythm may mean an approaching herd, a splitting pillar, a predator boring upward, or a root system preparing to convulse.

Their oldest structures are not houses but braces. Here and there enormous timber crutches support a favored terrace, each one assembled by generations and almost swallowed by new tissue. The plants accept some braces and reject others. A poorly placed beam will be enveloped, crushed, or pushed out. A well-placed one becomes part of the plant’s body, budding leaves and eventually forgetting its artificial origin.

This makes building less an act of construction than negotiation.

The same is true of travel. The bridges flex. Ladders grow. Doors close for seasonal reasons. A chamber that welcomed sleepers yesterday may fill tonight with mating moths the size of cloaks. Nothing remains merely usable. Every object is tasted, inhabited, pollinated, gnawed, warmed, or recruited.

The atmosphere is dense with breath and suspended life. Pollen moves in ocher veils. Tiny ballooning animals drift on thermal currents, drawing in their limbs when struck by sound waves. Resin vapor gives the air a sweet, medicinal sharpness. Deeper down, near the exposed faults, sulfur and hot stone dominate. Breathing is possible, but never neutral; each breath is participation. Spores cling to the throat. Aromatic compounds alter alertness and dreams. Travelers wear woven filter masks and chew bitter bark to prevent germination in the lungs.

Storms occur without wind or rain. They begin when one region of the forest releases gas in response to heat. Pressure builds beneath the canopy mats until whole districts belch upward. Branches whip. Pollen explodes into the light. Flying animals are thrown against trunks, and gliding predators use the turbulence to cross miles in minutes. The luminous ceiling flickers as membranes fold and reopen. Static crawls over waxy leaves. For an hour, the cavern becomes a green machine shaking itself apart.

Afterward, it is never arranged as before.

New clearings shine under the ceiling light. Broken pillars vent fragrant smoke. Swarms arrive so thickly that the wreckage seems to move. Seedlings rise with visible speed, unrolling hot, translucent leaves. Scavengers court, feed, and lay eggs within minutes. The inhabitants emerge from their hanging shelters and find that a familiar road now ends above a new abyss, while elsewhere a forest has woven a bridge across open space.

There is no stable distinction between disaster and growth.

The deepest law of the place is that emptiness cannot persist. A crack gathers spores. A shadow attracts feelers. A discarded tool acquires a rind, then roots, then mouths feeding on the roots. Silence summons hunters; light summons wings; warmth summons germination. Even bare mineral is only a brief phase before something learns its shape.

And beneath all the clamor, the whole buried country is slowly spreading. Roots work into the surrounding salt. Furnace-moss warms the fissures. Animals grind the loosened rock and carry it away in their bodies. The cavern is not merely occupied—it is being excavated by appetite.

It grows its own space as it needs it.
