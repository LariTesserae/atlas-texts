The place lies only a short distance below the surface, though there is no obvious way down. Rain finds it more easily than people do. It passes through porous stone, old root channels, and seams of engineered gravel until it gathers on the ceiling in bright, trembling beads. Every few seconds a bead grows heavy enough to fall. In quieter places this would sound delicate. Here each drop is caught by the architecture, amplified through hollow walls, and returned as a low metallic note.

The underground settlement is no larger than a hamlet. From one boundary wall to the other might be a ten-minute walk, if the walkways were direct. They are not. Streets divide, descend, cross through buildings, and emerge above themselves. Narrow towers hang from the ceiling rather than rise from the floor. Bridges span empty chambers only a few meters wide. Staircases fold flat when unneeded, leaving abrupt ledges slick with condensation.

It is unmistakably urban, but almost entirely vacant.

There are plazas, transit grooves, drainage courts, vertical gardens, public reservoirs, and ranks of doorways. Yet there are no crowds, no abandoned possessions, no nests or droppings, not even the husks of insects. The emptiness feels designed rather than catastrophic. Most structures stand far apart, with conspicuous volumes of warm darkness between them. A single kiosk occupies an entire square. One pale bench faces a wall of running water. Along the largest avenue, lamps are set at intervals so wide that each illuminates only its own island of pavement.

The city is young. Its surfaces have not had time to round or soften. Stone ribs meet at sharp angles; newly exposed mineral faces shine black, green, and rust-red beneath the wet. Channels cut by runoff remain narrow and clean-sided. Where water has begun to score the plazas, the marks resemble scratches in glass. There is little sediment. The settlement seems to have arrived faster than ordinary geology could weather it.

Some of it may have been excavated, but there are no tool marks. The local stone appears to have been persuaded apart along boundaries too precise to be fractures. Buildings merge continuously with the bedrock, their walls passing from basalt to translucent ceramic without a seam. In places the city is still forming. A façade shivers almost imperceptibly, grains migrating through it like schools of dust, closing one doorway while opening another several meters away. Street edges creep overnight. Pipes bud from damp walls, fill with water, then withdraw once the pressure has changed.

The machinery responsible is mostly invisible. Its effects are not.

Water sometimes climbs. It beads on the floor, gathers into strings, and rises toward ceiling cisterns in slow, vertical streams. Heat moves with similar independence, flowing along engraved lines beneath the pavement. A person standing over one line would feel tropical warmth around the ankles while their face remained cool with mist. The city does not violate gravity so much as negotiate locally with it, assigning different directions to different materials. Loose stones still fall. Water obeys the channels appointed to it. The architecture, apparently, is exempt.

This continual adjustment makes the settlement loud. Deep pumps—or organs resembling pumps—beat inside the rock with the irregular cadence of distant construction. Spillways roar behind walls. Tension cables hum at pitches that shift when the chambers warm. Every hour or so, the whole place gives a tremendous resonant groan as some load is redistributed through the hanging towers. Then comes a succession of sharp reports, one after another, as wet surfaces contract and lock into new positions.

Between these events, the falling drops maintain their scattered percussion.

Vegetation has entered wherever the city permits it. Ferns crowd the drainage courts, broad and dark, their fronds lacquered by constant moisture. Moss rises in thick cushions around the bases of lamps, feeding on their weak amber glow. Fine roots occupy hairline cracks but do not widen them; when they press too strongly, the crack relocates, carrying the root tip sideways into a prepared cavity.

There are also pale, leafless plants in the deeper streets. They grow as braided ribbons, drawing energy from thermal gradients in the walls. Their upper surfaces are silver, their undersides wine-red. When a reservoir discharges, the ribbons uncurl toward the sudden humidity, filling an otherwise empty arcade with slow motion. Minutes later they wind themselves tight again.

Nothing pollinates them. Nothing grazes them. There are no fish in the reservoirs and no larvae in the standing water. Even rot proceeds strangely slowly. Fallen fronds are dismantled by gray fungal veils and by the city’s microscopic maintenance systems, which separate organic matter into clear droplets and dark mineral flakes. The plants reproduce through runners, division, and spores carried by ventilation pulses. It is a complete ecology with one kingdom conspicuously absent.

The air is warm, but not uniformly so. Low courtyards hold breathlike heat. Tunnels opening toward the upper rock release cooler drafts scented with rain and clay. Mist condenses in the transition zones, obscuring streets after every surface storm. Because the lamps are dim and widely spaced, the mist does not glow so much as erase. Towers vanish halfway up. Bridges continue into a gray opacity and return without revealing what supports them.

There may once have been a sky simulated across the ceiling. A few chambers still show traces: moving fields of deep blue, a blurred white disk, patterns like clouds. The system is damaged or unfinished. The false sky appears only in strips, and its weather does not correspond to the weather above. At times it shows dawn for several minutes. At others it displays rain falling upward into a black sun. Most residents, had there ever been residents, would have known it was an instrument rather than an illusion—a municipal map of pressure, water, and structural stress translated into meteorology.

The central plaza contains the only object that suggests a purpose for the settlement. It is a shallow circular basin surrounded by seven empty platforms. The basin holds perfectly still water despite the vibration underfoot. Its surface is darker than the chamber around it and reflects structures that do not exist: crowded balconies, additional towers, cables heavy with gardens. No figures appear in the reflection. The alternate city is as uninhabited as this one, only denser.

When surface rain intensifies, the reflected buildings change. Whole districts rise in the basin’s black image while the physical settlement rearranges itself by millimeters. Gutters rotate. Walls breathe outward. Reservoirs fill with water pulled from saturated rock. The noise swells until conversation would be impossible. Ferns bow beneath artificial wind, and silver plants open all at once.

Then the storm above passes. The ascending streams thin. The pumps slow, though they never stop. Water drains into unseen depths, leaving fresh edges and wet black pavement.

The city returns to its dim, warm emptiness—not abandoned, exactly, and not waiting. It is occupied by processes rather than inhabitants. Its streets are for the movement of pressure. Its towers house differences in temperature. Its plazas give water somewhere to become still. The settlement continually maintains the shape of a community, even though no animal life has ever come to use it.
