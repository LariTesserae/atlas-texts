Beneath the ordinary surface lies a second country: low in the earth but not enclosed in any simple sense. Its ceiling is lost behind vegetation. Its horizons run for many leagues through a hot, bright interior, opening into basins, galleries, and broad hollows joined by green corridors. There is no bare cavern wall by which to judge its dimensions. Everything mineral has been covered, entered, or replaced by growth.

The light comes from the vegetation itself, though not as scattered points of luminescence. Vast, translucent leaves spread beneath the unseen roof, layered like panes in a living skylight. Their tissues hold a steady white-gold radiance. Veins appear as darker rivers overhead, and where many leaves overlap the light acquires a green depth, but there is no true dusk. The brightness is almost excessive. It reveals the smallest hairs on stems and the clear beads of condensation held at their tips.

Heat fills the whole region evenly. It is the close, breath-warmed heat of a greenhouse without doors, heavy enough to make distance shimmer. Yet there are no winds and scarcely any gradients of temperature. The air does not cool beside water or rise from exposed ground. It rests in place, warm and saturated, among millions of motionless surfaces.

There is little ground in the usual sense. What seems at first to be soil is a deep interlacing of roots, cork, fungal threads, compressed husks, and pale cellular mats. It bears weight with a faint resilience. Low ridges are the backs of immense buried rhizomes. Valleys form where sheets of root descend into the raw stone, and broad plains consist of packed rosettes whose leaves fit together without gaps. Some regions are supported entirely by the woven crowns of plants growing upon older plants. One can cross miles without touching mineral matter.

The vegetation is dense but strangely ordered. Pillar-trunks stand close together in some districts, each smooth and swollen, without branches until far overhead. Elsewhere, forests give way to fields of tall blades, all equal in height and all held at the same slight curve. There are terraces of waxy cups, forests of jointed tubes, vaults grown from arching roots, and enormous horizontal leaves strong enough to form bridges across luminous pits. No stem strains toward a brighter place; brightness is everywhere. No vine searches. No flower opens or closes. The country has the appearance of growth brought to the exact brink of completion and then retained there.

Moisture exists in isolated pockets. Condensation pearls every surface, but the droplets do not readily fall. They cling beneath leaves in long, bright rows, each bead full and perfectly rounded. In the deeper basins, clear pools occupy depressions among roots. Their margins are not eroded: the water meets soft new stone and living tissue along edges as crisp as poured glass. A few narrow rivers lie between the basins, though “river” is only a description of their shape. Their surfaces remain smooth, and their courses show no evidence of having cut the land. They seem to have been placed at the same moment as their banks.

The exposed geology is embryonic. Where roots part, one finds pale mineral bulges, unfractured and almost tender-looking, with no scree beneath them and no scratches from passage. Crystals have begun to form but possess only blunt first facets. Ridges rise without weathered sides. Cavities retain the rounded impressions of whatever withdrew from them at the landscape’s beginning. Nothing here has been worn down. Nothing has yet accumulated the small damage by which the age of a place is usually read.

This gives the country its deepest strangeness: it is ancient in extent and newborn in condition. Decay does not proceed. Fallen leaves exist, but they are not collapsing into rot; they lie intact in the root mat, their colors fixed at bronze, green, or black. Trunks display no rings of injury. Pools neither shrink nor overflow. Condensation neither increases nor clears. The whole ecology occupies an equilibrium so complete that it resembles stillness, though every surface is alive.

Animals are rare enough to seem accidental. In the broad cup-pools there may be a colorless swimmer, no longer than a finger, suspended among the roots. On certain pillar-trunks rests a flat, six-legged creature whose body imitates a patch of lichen. It does not visibly feed or travel. Once in a great distance, a pale winged form can be seen high among the luminous leaves, held against the brightness as if embedded there. There are no swarms, no burrows, no spoor, no calls crossing the forest. Pollination, if it occurs, belongs to the plants themselves: neighboring flowers touch, roots exchange tissues, airborne filaments remain stretched from crown to crown.

The silence is not absolute. Close to the water, there is a low pressure-sound, the almost inaudible hum of tension inside stems. Thick leaves occasionally give off minute creaks, though they do not move. One hears moisture held in narrow channels, a sound like a distant stream muffled behind a wall. Footsteps are absorbed by the living substrate after a single soft compression. There is no rain on the canopy, no wind in the fronds, no chorus at the pools.

Scattered through the region are things that resemble buildings. It is difficult to decide whether they were constructed or cultivated. A small chamber may stand between two trunks, formed from six ribbed roots joined into a dome. A row of alcoves may have been hollowed—or simply grown—along the side of a giant tuber. Farther on, narrow platforms spiral around a column, each step a shelf-fungus hardened to wood. There are no tools, hinges, vessels, roads, or machines. The structures possess no separable parts. They are architecture in the way a nest or shell is architecture, except that few animals here could have made them.

Some are empty. Others contain arrangements of seeds: black ovals set in concentric patterns, or pale pods stacked in niches, all untouched by germination. A long hall near one of the pools is lined with leaf membranes so thin that the steady light passes through them. At its center stands a low growth shaped like a table. There is nothing upon it but a single depression filled with clear water.

No path remains worn, yet routes are easy to follow. The vegetation repeatedly leaves corridors wide enough for passage. Arches recur at regular intervals. Root bridges meet opposite banks precisely. Openings through the dense growth align across improbable distances, giving occasional views into the bright interior: green colonnades, white pools, red thickets, and smooth young ridges repeating toward a horizon that never darkens.

The place seems prepared for use but never used, finished but still at the first instant of formation. It neither advances nor retreats. Its rain is held as condensation; its rivers are held in their channels; its forests are held at full growth. Even the heat does not fluctuate.

To enter it is to feel that one has gone not merely underground, but beneath change itself—into a vegetal world spread over raw, unworn earth, bright enough to have no shadows in which age might gather.
