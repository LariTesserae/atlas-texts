The high country has been worn almost past the point of being terrain.

It is a broad, warm tableland suspended above distant haze, its old summits reduced to low backs and shallow basins. Nothing rises sharply. Ridges have the bluntness of bone handled for generations; escarpments descend in enormous, softened steps. Even the loose stones are ancient-looking—dark, close-grained pebbles polished not by any present river but by vanished floods, lying so densely that bare ground rarely shows between them.

From a distance the plateau appears empty. Its vegetation is only a sparse stippling of gray thorn-cushions, copper grass, and low plants with leaves like folded leather. Up close, however, there is almost no unoccupied space.

Life is packed into the landscape in small, patient forms. Pale lizards lie fitted into cracks as if poured there. Beetles rest beneath every flake of stone. Moths sleep in overlapping rows on the shaded sides of boulders, their wings patterned exactly like mineral stains. Finger-length tortoises crowd the hollows, often stacked shell against shell. In the warm dust are soft-bodied burrowers, scale-backed worms, transparent mites, and colonies of dry-skinned frogs that can remain folded and motionless for years. The thorn-cushions contain nests within nests: birds in the outer branches, insects in the stems, pearl-colored grubs inside the roots.

At noon, when the light is plain and nearly vertical, all this abundance becomes almost invisible. The creatures lower their heads. Membranes close over eyes. Wings flatten. The plateau enters a silence so complete that distance itself seems muted.

There is no wind-song through forests because there are no forests. There is no constant water. Usually one hears only the occasional tick of a cooling stone, the faint spill of grit from beneath a moving shell, or a single dry chirp answered minutes later from another ridge. Yet wherever one kneels, something is breathing.

Moisture comes intermittently and seldom from obvious clouds. On certain mornings, warm air rises from the lowlands and condenses against the upper plateau. Water beads along the undersides of stones, inside empty shells, and on the waxy seams of plants. The land drinks from below and in shadow. Shallow pits fill one drop at a time, becoming dark lenses no wider than a hand. Every such pool acquires a crowded rim of tracks before sunrise.

Rain is rarer and more dramatic, arriving as widely separated curtains. One basin may flood while another, visible across the open country, remains dusty beneath clear light. The rain does not run far. It vanishes into a deep mantle of broken rock and reappears days later in isolated pockets: a seep beneath an overhang, a warm pool in a hollowed slab, a line of dampness along a cliff far below its source. There are old channels everywhere, but most have not carried a continuous stream within the memory of anything living.

The plateau’s age is visible less as ruin than as completion. Erosion has exhausted the easy shapes. Soft strata have gone. What remains is resistant and settled: pavements, rounded shelves, shallow bowls, and immense recumbent stones whose upper surfaces have become miniature countries. Crusts of orange, black, and bone-white lichen divide them into territories. Tiny predators hunt across these colored plains. Rain pools occupy their depressions. Whole migrations take place from one side of a boulder to the other.

In the broadest basins stand scattered structures, though none were made by hands. Tall mud chimneys rise in loose groups, some fresh and warm, others mineralized into stone. They resemble roofless towers softened by immense age. Birds have opened chambers in their sides; snakes sleep in the abandoned passages; condensation gathers in the coolest interior cells. Elsewhere, generations of shell-bearing animals have piled their dead into low white walls around seasonal pools. The walls curve and branch without plan, enclosing pockets of darker soil where the plateau’s few flowering plants grow.

Some formations are harder to explain. Across several ridges, lines of flat stones stand tilted in the same direction, each slab separated from the next by the width of a small animal. They may be exposed edges of buried strata, though they resemble processions halted in place. Under them are polished tunnels, densely inhabited and older than the current surface. Nothing here uses tools, yet the repeated labor of claws, mouths, roots, and bodies gives parts of the country an architectural order.

The overwhelming fauna follows rules of stillness. Movement invites heat, and sound invites attention, so most species survive by waiting. Predators do not patrol; they occupy thresholds. Prey do not flee far; they become indistinguishable from the ground. A hunt may consist of two creatures remaining motionless within a handspan of each other from dawn until dusk. At sunset, one turns its head. The other is gone.

Only after a rare rain does the silence break. Buried frogs unfold. Winged insects emerge from sealed chambers. Tortoises climb over one another toward the new pools, and long-legged birds descend from altitudes where they had seemed like dust against the sky. For an hour or two the basins rustle, click, pulse, and softly croak. Flowers open directly from what had appeared to be dead sticks. The air smells of hot stone, wet mineral, and something faintly sweet.

Then the water sinks. Wings are shed. Songs cease. The great population returns beneath the surface, leaving the open plateau apparently vacant once more.

At the margins, the land falls away into warm, immense valleys. From there the tableland seems like a single eroded summit. From above, it reveals itself as a dense mosaic of basins and stone fields extending to the horizon, each with its own hidden congregation. Nothing changes quickly except during rain, and even rain serves only to renew old arrangements. Paths deepen by the pressure of countless tiny feet. Shell walls grow a fraction higher. A ridge loses a grain at a time.

The place is not dying, though much of it resembles the final residue of a world. It has simply learned to keep nearly everything out of sight: its water beneath stone, its forests reduced to thorn and root, its multitudes compressed into cracks, and its history worn down until age itself has become the landscape’s most enduring material.
