The city occupies a shallow bowl near the top of a mountain, no wider than a small valley and enclosed on every side by pale, steeply folded stone. From above it might be mistaken for a patch of shattered ice caught in a hollow. There are hundreds of roofs, ledges, cisterns, bridges, gutters, pens, ladders, retaining walls, and narrow courtyards, all fitted so closely together that almost none of the original ground remains visible.

Water is everywhere.

It runs beneath the paving in stone-lined veins. It beads on walls that have not seen rain for days. It seeps from joints in the cliffs and hangs in clear drops from lintels. Every stair has a groove worn down its middle, and every roof is tilted to pour into another roof, then another, until the water reaches one of the deep communal basins at the center. The air itself seems close to condensation. Breath clouds instantly, but so do doorways, shaded niches, ropes, feathers, and the backs of animals. At dawn the whole settlement wears a skin of frost; by midday, under an almost intolerably bright sky, that skin breaks loose in glittering scales and goes rushing through the drains.

The cold never lifts. Even in full sun, exposed metal bites the hand. Pools remain dark beneath floating plates of ice, and the small waterfalls descending from the bowl’s rim are half liquid, half translucent architecture. Their shapes alter by the hour. A frozen lip grows outward, cracks, falls, and is replaced. Fine spray rises and freezes against nearby walls, slowly building bulbous white buttresses which the inhabitants chip away with antler picks before they crush a roof.

Nothing here is still, though very little is loud.

Water races. Ice splits. Animals stream along the parapets. Suspended footways sway in the mountain wind. Whole ranks of bright prayer-flags turn and strain, yet make no snapping sound. The stone of the bowl has a peculiar softness to sound—not to touch, for it is hard enough to blunt a bronze chisel, but acoustically. Noise enters it and does not return. A dropped bowl produces a brief clay note and then silence. Voices carry only a few paces. Even the collapse of a cornice seems distant: a white movement, a puff of crystals, a low pressure felt in the teeth.

Because of this, the city’s turbulence has the character of a dream witnessed through thick glass.

Its buildings are made from fitted slabs, packed clay, hide, bone, woven reed, and ice. Timber is rare. What wood exists has been repaired so many times with horn splints and rawhide bindings that the original branch survives only as a dark core. The oldest houses are carved directly into the eroded folds of the basin. Newer structures cling to their faces or stand above them on stacked arches. There are no broad streets, only steep passages squeezed between wet walls. Some are shoulder-width. Others dive through houses, cross roof terraces, or vanish under curtains of falling water.

Construction never ends. It cannot. The mountain constantly rearranges the conditions of the city.

A spring opens beneath a storehouse, and by evening the floor has become a bridge. An ice dam forms in an upper channel, diverting water through three courtyards. Frost lifts a stair from its foundation. A retaining wall sags and must be unpacked stone by stone before it fails. Families move their sleeping platforms higher or lower according to the behavior of the week’s seepage. There are houses that migrate around fixed hearths, walls rebuilt on opposite sides as runoff changes course. Property belongs less to parcels of ground than to water paths, sunny ledges, and rights of passage through neighboring roofs.

The inhabitants possess simple tools but intricate knowledge. Their hammers are stone, horn, and soft copper. Their drills are bow-driven. Their measuring instruments are knotted cords, plumb stones, shallow water trays, and the trained fingertips of masons. Yet they can read a wall’s moisture by color and predict the opening of a spring from the taste of frost. Children learn the basin’s drainage songs before they learn the names of distant places, though the “songs” are mostly gestures: hooked fingers for a blocked channel, flattened palms for thin ice, two fists rotating for water moving unseen under stone.

Few plants survive the height and cold. Those that do are pressed low, almost secretive. Black moss rims the warmest seep mouths. Rust-colored lichen maps the oldest walls in branching continents. In protected cracks grow thumb-sized cushions of silver grass, each blade stiff as a needle. There are no trees and scarcely any shrubs. Fuel comes from dung, oil, dried fungus, and oily cakes rendered from small mountain animals.

The scarcity of vegetation makes the abundance of animal life seem impossible.

They occupy every unclaimed surface.

White-backed rodents crowd the warm drain covers, sleeping in braided heaps. Long-legged water birds stand in the cisterns, each balancing on one foot among the ice plates. Pale lizards, warm-blooded or something near it, emerge at noon to drink condensation from copper nails. Flocks of tiny black birds move through the alleys like thrown handfuls of ash. They land without calling, pick mineral insects from the walls, and rise all at once.

There are translucent eels in the underground channels. Their bodies show blue organs pulsing beneath the skin, and they swim equally well upstream or down, sometimes bracing themselves in vertical flows. The people consider their migrations more reliable than calendars. When the eels climb toward the upper terraces, a deep thaw is coming. When they bury their heads in silt and leave their tails waving in the current, the mountain will shake.

Larger beasts use the roofs as roads. Shaggy, six-horned grazers descend from the barren ridges, though no one can say what they graze there. They lick mineral films from the masonry and scrape frost with broad lower teeth. Their hooves are divided into many soft pads, allowing them to cross ice almost silently. They belong to households only in winter. In spring they walk away, pushing through gates and courtyards with calm entitlement, and return months later wearing loops of unfamiliar woven grass around their horns.

Predators come as well. Thin silver foxes pass through drainage openings too narrow for their skulls. Broad-winged kites circle beneath the level of the city, using updrafts from the cliffs to hang apparently motionless in the brightness. At certain hours their shadows climb the walls independently of them. No one points this out. Shadows are understood to be less firmly attached at high elevation.

The light is relentless. Clouds often lie below the basin, making the city an island above a glowing white plain. Sun strikes snow, water, ice, pale stone, mica, fish scales, wet hide, and polished bone until there is scarcely anywhere for the eye to rest. The inhabitants darken the skin around their eyes with soot and wear slitted masks carved from bark or horn. Doorways are hung with black wool. Interior rooms are dim not for warmth but for mercy.

In this brightness, depth becomes uncertain. An alley flooded with reflected sky can look like an opening straight into the air. A sheet of ice may appear solid until a bird dives through it. Distant cliffs seem close enough to touch, while a neighboring stair may disappear into glare. The city’s many handrails are not merely practical; they are a second map, read by touch when vision fails.

At the center of the bowl lies the White Court, a roofless enclosure crowded with cistern mouths. Its paving has been worn into smooth basins by generations of feet and overflow. Here the city’s waters meet, though never in the same arrangement twice. Channels are redirected each morning with clay gates and fitted stones. Water destined for drinking is separated from water used for washing, tanning, animals, cooling hearths, softening reeds, and carrying waste. This work involves dozens of people moving continuously among one another, but they communicate almost without speech. A lifted elbow, a cord laid across a channel, a pebble placed white-side-up: each changes the flow.

Beneath the court is something older than the settlement.

The lowest cisterns are not built. They are cavities weathered into the mountain, round and smooth, joined by submerged passages. Their walls bear concentric terraces like the insides of enormous shells. Water entering them sometimes returns warmer than it went in, sometimes colder, sometimes faintly luminous. Objects dropped into one chamber may rise days later in another, coated in frost or soft blue mud. Once or twice a year, the cisterns produce finished shapes: a hollow stone ring, a ladderlike length of calcite, a cluster of thin mineral cups nested one inside another. The people use these things if their purpose becomes apparent. If not, they place them on the eastern wall, where thousands have accumulated.

That wall is the densest place in the city. Rings, forks, spheres, perforated stones, animal-like concretions, and nameless mineral tools fill every recess from paving to parapet. In sunlight they sweat clear water. At night they slowly change position.

This is not considered alarming. The city changes position too.

Not visibly, and not all at once, but the number of stairs between the White Court and the northern gate is never constant. A passage that descended yesterday may rise today. Certain rooms become wider during storms. Occasionally two unrelated houses discover a shared door between them, its hinges ancient and its threshold worn, though neither family remembers building it. Such openings are treated as temporary civic spaces. Tea is brought. News is exchanged. No one sleeps across the threshold, because by morning the doorway may lead into running water or open air.

The mountain’s erosion is ancient and composed. Nothing in the surrounding forms looks raw. The cliffs have been rounded, channeled, undercut, and polished into an equilibrium of curves. Yet this settled appearance hides ceaseless internal revision. The bowl is old enough to know how it wishes to be shaped, and all weather merely assists it. The city survives not by opposing that shape but by remaining unfinished.

Its only absolute laws concern obstruction.

No person may seal a seep without providing it another path.

No wall may be built without an opening large enough for a bird.

No roof may retain more snow than three people can clear in silence.

No animal may be driven from a warm drain during the longest night.

No newly appeared doorway may be barred before water has been poured across its threshold.

Everything else is negotiated.

Toward evening, the sun lowers but does not soften. It passes through banners of ice mist and fractures into columns, halos, and inverted arcs. Every wet edge burns. The sparse moss darkens to almost black; the animals become moving cuts in the glare. Thousands gather in the roofs and channels—birds, rodents, horned beasts, silver foxes, pale lizards, people carrying jars—yet the city remains uncannily hushed.

Then the daily freezing begins.

It travels from the upper rim downward, not as a gradual cooling but as a visible boundary. Water stills. Films whiten. Gutters choke and spill into alternate channels. Bridges tighten in their sockets. Cistern lids rise as ice forms beneath them. The inhabitants hurry through the narrow ways, shifting gates, hammering wedges, releasing backed-up streams. Animals pour from one terrace to another ahead of the advancing frost. New icicles extend as one watches; old ones shear away. Walls glisten, crack, and settle. A stair closes. A tunnel opens. Somewhere below the White Court, the mountain manufactures another object whose use may not be understood for generations.

All this happens in a blaze of cold light, in near silence, within the space of a small town.

By darkness the city has become a different city, fitted closely into the same enduring bowl.
