At first it appears to be a warm tidal plain, though there are no tides and nothing beneath it could properly be called earth.

The plain extends farther than weather can see. It is assembled from dark hexagonal plates, each several kilometers across, fitted edge to edge with seams of pale metal. The plates are not flat. They sag toward their centers into shallow basins and rise at their margins in abrupt, unworn ridges. From the air the whole world resembles a vast hammered sheet: dim hollows crowded with water, sharp creases catching the sun, repeating outward until haze closes over them.

Everything is manufactured. Dig beneath the black surface and there is no sediment, no bedrock, no fossil depth—only additional layers: ceramic shock lattices, pipes packed as closely as nerves, sealed maintenance galleries, pressure reservoirs, and immense load-bearing members descending into warmth. Even the horizon is constructed. What seems to be a distant range of low hills is a line of ventilation housings, each broad enough to alter the clouds above it.

Water lies everywhere without being abundant enough to dominate. It fills joint channels, gathers in shallow trays, and hangs in trembling skins beneath overhangs. Warm droplets condense continually on the cooler alloys. Rows of weeping apertures bead and release water at intervals so exact that, if one watched long enough, the pattern would never drift. There has recently been rain—or the place gives that impression. Every surface shines. The hollows smell faintly of hot iron, salt, electrical insulation, and the clean mineral odor that follows a storm.

Yet there was no recent rain. The wetness is permanent.

Above the plain, a fixed ceiling of pearl-gray cloud admits an ordinary, generous daylight. There is neither sunrise nor evening. The light comes at an angle soft enough to show relief and strong enough to cast shadows, but the angle does not change. Bright edges remain bright. The same narrow faces of the ridges stay in shade. Pools retain the same fragments of white sky year after year. It is a world held at late morning.

The air is warm and nearly saturated. Breathing it feels like entering a machine room after rainfall. Condensation gathers on exposed skin and remains there. There is no breeze at ground level, only occasional broad movements high in the cloud deck, where moisture is redistributed through ducts too large to recognize as ducts. No thermal front advances. No season approaches. The atmosphere is managed into equilibrium.

The terrain is young in form but not in age. Corners are keen. Weld seams remain ridged and silver. Drainage slots preserve the burrs left by their cutting tools. Inclines end suddenly in lips rather than softening into slopes. Nothing has had the opportunity to round them. Water passes over the surfaces, but does not erode. The plates resist chemistry; the water carries no grit; every fallen particle is collected through microscopic sieves and returned to the foundries below. The landscape may have stood for a million years and still look newly unpacked.

Between the ridges lies the fauna.

It is overwhelming not because it surges or hunts, but because there is nowhere one can look without finding bodies.

They occupy every basin in ranks and interlocking colonies. Pale, seal-like forms lie fitted shoulder to shoulder in the warm drainage troughs, their smooth sides marked with serial patterns. Tall jointed animals stand in the seams, each with six narrow legs braced against opposing walls. Black shelled creatures cling beneath ledges in layers ten deep. In the pools, translucent disks crowd the water so completely that the surface seems paved with lenses. Long ribbed animals are wound around conduits. Tiny copper-colored mites fill bolt recesses. Great soft masses, broad as districts, occupy the deepest hollows and rise only a few meters above the water, breathing through fields of identical pores.

There are no plants. No moss greens the wet joints, no algae cloud the pools, no reed breaks the geometric shore. The fauna do not graze. They feed instead upon the systems that built the world: low electrical leakage, trace minerals delivered in water, warmth from circulating fluids, pressure differences between adjacent chambers. Some insert mouthparts into standardized ports. Others lie over heated grilles and absorb energy through dark membranes. The translucent pool-creatures open themselves to the fixed daylight, but their light-catching organs are animal: contractile, blood-fed, sleepless.

Birth, growth, and death have been reduced nearly to stillness.

Each creature is mature. Each occupies a designated niche shaped exactly to its anatomy. Reproduction occurs only as replacement. When a body fails, it does not rot. The surface beneath it opens soundlessly, lowers it into a reclamation chamber, and later returns an indistinguishable animal through the same aperture. The colony’s numbers never rise or fall. Predator and prey exist only as inherited forms: jaws remain, armor remains, camouflage remains, but pursuit has vanished. A hooked pale hunter stands forever among the lens-creatures, taking one at intervals prescribed by the machinery. Elsewhere, an armored animal opens its flank and permits smaller creatures to remove a measured quantity of tissue. By the time the cycle repeats, the wound is gone.

Nothing is wasted, and nothing learns.

This makes the multitude uncanny. Millions of eyes may face across a single basin, yet there is almost no commotion. The animals breathe in synchrony with the pumps below. Mouths open. Membranes pulse. Gill fans extend into channels and fold again. On the largest scale, these motions cancel one another. Seen from a ridge, the packed colonies appear motionless, a mottled biological pavement laid over the mechanical one.

The quiet is immense.

Close by, one hears droplets striking metal, the soft closure of valves, the damp click of shells, a communal exhalation passing through a colony like air through an organ. Far below comes the subdued industrial bass of turbines under load. It never grows louder or softer. There are no alarms, no hammering lines, no irregular engines. The factories are vast enough to have lost the character of work. They operate as geology might operate: continuously, impersonally, below hearing.

At long intervals along the plate margins stand factory towers. They are squat because the world is low, but their footprints are enormous. Their walls are ribbed with heat exchangers and curtained in condensation. Wide portals face the animal basins, although they never open. Inside, production lines maintain exactly the inventory already present: spare limbs, shell material, nutritive salts, valve assemblies, cloud nuclei, replacement sections of atmosphere. Conveyor belts run without visible cargo because each item is produced only when an equivalent item is removed.

There are roads, but no traffic. The roads are broad strips of cream-colored composite crossing the dark plates in perfect tangents. Animals avoid them without fences. Water does not collect on them. Their surface is warm, dry, and faintly resilient, preserving no footprint. They lead from factory to factory and finally converge upon the horizon, where perspective turns them into a single pale thread.

Occasionally the plain drops into a maintenance gulf. Its walls descend in clean terraces crowded with pipes, balconies, braces, and motionless cranes. Warm vapor fills the depths. Water runs down the walls in narrow, permanent streams, each occupying a machined groove. At the bottom, barely visible through the haze, are slow black reservoirs and the broad backs of aquatic animals designed for pressures unknown at the surface. Their breathing makes concentric rings that form, cross, and vanish in precisely repeated places.

There are no ruins. There is no rubbish. Nothing is abandoned, though much appears unattended. No builders remain in evidence. Perhaps the animals are the builders’ final product; perhaps they are workers whose tasks became bodily functions; perhaps the whole plain is one factory whose purpose is simply to preserve its own exact condition.

The governing rule of the place is not immobility but return.

Every droplet travels and comes back. Every calorie passes through flesh and reenters the grid. Every body is replaced. Every vibration is damped. Every scratch is filled before it can become history. Weather circulates but never arrives. The living multitude performs countless acts, yet the landscape does not accumulate their consequences.

Travel across it is possible, but progress feels doubtful. The basins differ in species, color, and architecture—one crowded with ivory towers grown from shell, another with low red animals nested among radiator fins, another flooded ankle-deep beneath a ceiling of suspended bridges—but the same fixed light falls everywhere. The same warm moisture closes around the traveler. The same ridges repeat. Behind, footprints disappear from the wet black plates as beads of water draw together and restore the shining surface.

Far ahead, beyond the manufactured haze, the plain continues: dense, wet, inhabited beyond measure, newly sharp and impossibly old, a complete world running at full capacity in order that nothing whatsoever will change.
