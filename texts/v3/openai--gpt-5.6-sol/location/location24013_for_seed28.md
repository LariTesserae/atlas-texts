The city occupies a crack no wider than a doorway.

From the salt plain above, nothing marks it except a seam of warm air and a continuous, subterranean roar. The plain is low, white, and nearly level, an old mineral basin pressed beneath a brilliant sky. Its crust has been weathered into broad plates with dark gaps between them. Wind pushes flour-fine salt across the surface in silver streamers. There are no roads, wells, or ruins in sight. Even the city’s entrance is difficult to recognize: one particular fissure among thousands, shaded at noon, exhaling heat.

Below, the fissure widens.

It opens not into a cavern exactly, but into a vertical hollow within the salt—a narrow, branching absence around which an entire city has been fitted. The place is pocket-sized by the measure of cities: a person can cross its inhabited breadth in an hour, and nowhere is the opposite wall more than a few hundred paces away. Yet much of that space is empty air. Buildings cling to ledges, hang from ribs of stone, or stand on thin pillars rising out of darkness. Bridges stitch one wall to another. Stairways descend, divide, double back, and vanish through slots barely wide enough for shoulders. The city has been built around its hollow rather than across it, preserving a great central vacancy.

That vacancy makes the noise.

Hot wind from the basin enters through hundreds of surface cracks and is forced down polished shafts. The shafts narrow and broaden according to old, carefully maintained proportions. Some howl. Some beat like enormous drums. Some issue tones too low to hear distinctly, though they shake dust from the walls and make teeth ache. Clay shutters clap in pressure changes. Reedless pipes moan. Stone vanes spin on wooden axles with a relentless knocking. Ropes thrash against bridge rails. The whole city is a wind instrument played by the desert.

Conversation is conducted at arm’s length, mouth to ear, or by hand signs. Houses have double doors and thick, soft inner walls woven from plant fiber. Public warnings are not shouted; they are struck into the structure. A particular hammer pattern travels through beams and bridge cables farther and more reliably than any voice.

There is no water.

Nothing pools here, nothing drips, and no dampness softens the dust. The pale surfaces sometimes resemble dried waterfalls, but they were shaped by windborne crystals and the slow migration of salt, not rain. The inhabitants have no native word for river. Their vessels are open baskets, porous jars, slatted bins—forms useless for holding liquid but ideal for keeping seeds, resins, powders, and lumps of wax.

The city’s lushness is of another chemistry.

Every sun-facing ledge is crowded with black-green fans. They are thick as leather, ribbed with silver capillaries, and warm to the touch. Instead of water, they circulate a light mineral oil produced in their buried tissues from atmospheric gases and salts drawn directly from the walls. Their roots are broad adhesive pads rather than searching filaments. They creep over masonry, mortar cracks, roofs, bridge undersides, and abandoned staircases, dissolving trace minerals with weak acids and leaving delicate, etched patterns behind.

Among the fans grow towers of translucent bladders, amber and gold in the fierce light. At dawn they inflate with warm gas. By midday they stand upright, crowding terraces like banners or flames. Their skins focus sunlight into small furnace-hot points, beneath which seed nodules ripen. In late afternoon they vent all at once. The release adds a dry hiss to the city’s thunder and fills the air with aromatic vapor: bitter resin, scorched citrus, hot stone.

Other growths resemble vegetation only by analogy. Copper-colored cords hang in curtains down the walls, branching wherever airborne dust strikes them. Low mats of jointed white scales spread across shaded floors. Clusters of brittle tubes bloom from old bricks and ring softly when the wind passes through. Nothing is moist, tender, or cool. Even the lushest gardens crackle.

Because usable air and mineral nutrients are abundant but space on the walls is not, cultivation is vertical and exacting. Gardeners climb on knotted cords, scraping away dead layers with shell blades and fitting new root-pads into shallow sockets. They prune with chipped stone, bone wedges, and soft copper knives. No pumps irrigate the terraces. Instead, vents are adjusted to feed plants particular mixtures of heat, dust, and gas. A garden is less a watered plot than a tuned breath.

The brightest districts lie near the upper slit. Sunlight enters in a hard white column and is passed downward by thousands of small reflectors: mica plates, polished salt, pale ceramic disks, and sheets of hammered copper. They are mounted on simple pivots and turned by hand throughout the day. Their reflected beams cross the hollow at every angle. They strike walls in moving squares and crescents, ignite the amber plants, and flash painfully from door lintels. In places the city seems brighter underground than the basin above.

Beyond those directed beams, darkness remains close. A person may stand in blazing light while an arm’s length away a stair drops into complete blackness.

The structures are dense in workmanship but spare in mass. Rooms are small, walls thin, and floors often little more than woven platforms stiffened with resin. Empty space separates every cluster. A neighborhood may consist of six houses fastened around a stone spur, with nothing beneath them for sixty body-lengths. The bridges between districts look impossibly delicate, though generations of repair have thickened their joints into smooth knobs.

There is little metal. The city is made chiefly from saltstone blocks, fired clay, fiber rope, hornlike plant laminates, and a glassy substance melted from basin sand in sun furnaces. Nails are rare. Buildings are pinned, tied, slotted, and balanced. Their flexibility is deliberate: rigid construction would crack under the constant vibration.

Age shows everywhere, but not as ruin. Steps have been polished into shallow bowls by bare feet. Handholds are deep and dark. Wind has rounded exposed corners while plants have engraved sheltered ones into lace. Broken bridges have been rebuilt in slightly altered positions so many times that unused anchor holes form constellations along the walls. Certain towers lean, yet have leaned for centuries, adjusted season by season with ropes and counterweights.

The city is settled into its erosion. It does not resist the hollow’s slow widening. When a wall becomes too thin, rooms are dismantled and moved inward. When a ledge falls, its stone is caught in nets far below, sorted, and lifted back piece by piece. Collapse is treated less as catastrophe than as a recurring civic season.

Animals are scarce. The most common are small sail-backed crawlers that live among the root-pads. Their bodies are almost weightless, supported by lattices of bone, and they open bright membranes to ride short gusts from one garden to another. They feed on resin flakes and mineral spores. Their landings make faint papery taps, usually lost in the surrounding tumult.

Higher up, one may occasionally see a hollow-wing circling in the sun shaft. It has no feathers and rarely flaps. It navigates pressure currents with a ring-shaped body, drifting for days without touching stone. The people consider its descent an ill omen, since a healthy hollow-wing should always be held aloft by the city’s breath.

There are said to be pale burrowers in the lowest salt, but proof comes only as parallel grooves found on newly exposed surfaces. No one hears them. Nothing living could be heard here unless it climbed inside the listener’s skull.

The great hollow has its cycles. Morning brings an upward draft and the lowest tones. At noon, heated air plunges through eastern shafts, setting whole banks of vanes spinning; bridges buck, gardens inflate, and loose crystals fly in glittering clouds. Near sunset the direction reverses. For several minutes opposing currents meet in the center, and the city reaches its daily crescendo—a deafening, layered blast in which every pipe, shutter, cable, tube-plant, and hollow chamber sounds together.

Then comes the pause.

It lasts perhaps three breaths.

In that interval the bridges stop trembling. Dust hangs motionless in the crossed beams. The rare sail-crawlers flatten themselves against the walls. People halt wherever they are, tools suspended, hands resting on railings. The sudden quiet is more alarming than the noise.

Afterward, the night wind rises from below, warm and scented with stone.

No one knows how deep the hollow goes. The lowest inhabited district ends at a platform called the Last Floor, though it is not a floor at all: only a web of black rope stretched above a shaft. Fragments dropped through its gaps never produce an audible impact. The city’s deepest pipes descend beside it, carrying up tones so powerful that the platform heaves like a chest.

The inhabitants say the noise is not created by the wind. They say the city itself is breathing, and the desert above is merely the mouth.
