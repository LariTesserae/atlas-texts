The mountains rise almost directly out of the hot lowlands, not as a single wall but as a broad, broken country of escarpments, hanging basins, knife-backed ridges, and immense green shelves. Their upper faces are lost at intervals in cloud. Between them, valleys open for many kilometers, each with its own weather: one bright under a white sun, another darkened by rain, a third filled to the rim with slow-moving vapor.

Despite the elevation, there is no cold season here. Heat comes from below as much as from the sky. The stone is geothermally restless, warm beneath the mosses, and rainwater sinking into fractures returns through springs almost too hot to touch. High plateaus steam after storms. Even on exposed ridges, the air is heavy and tropical, carrying the mineral smell of wet basalt, crushed fern, sulfur, and flowers fermenting where they have fallen.

Water is everywhere without quite taking possession of the land. It beads on waxy leaves and gathers under the translucent scales of lichens. It threads down cliff faces in silver lines, disappears into mats of roots, then emerges hundreds of meters lower as clear, warm cascades. The ground of the hanging valleys gives slightly underfoot, saturated but bound together by roots and fibrous fungi. Pools occupy old depressions in the rock. Their edges are crowded with bladder plants, reeds, and low palms whose trunks divide repeatedly into dense, fan-shaped colonies.

The largest rivers are not broad. They are steep, muscular things, confined to ancient channels worn deep into the mountains. From a distance they appear as white stitching along the valley floors. In shaded reaches, they run dark green over polished stone; where mineral springs enter, their beds bloom orange, cream, and metallic black. Mist rises continuously from the warmer tributaries, condensing on the underside of foliage and falling again in a patient secondary rain.

The mountains have been weathering for an age, and their forms have reached a kind of equilibrium. There are few fresh wounds. Cliffs are rounded at the brow and deeply pleated beneath, their weakness already found and exploited by roots and water. Fallen rock does not remain bare for long. First comes a skin of heat-loving bacteria, iridescent as spilled fuel; then black moss, then tiny ferns. Within a few seasons, rubble becomes an island of vegetation. In centuries, those islands merge with the forest and redirect the streams around them.

The vegetation is lush but not uniformly tall. On the lower slopes, forests rise in several crowded stories. Buttressed trees lean over one another, their bark concealed by orchids, bromeliads, climbing aroids, and curtains of blue-green moss. Vines run between trunks under tension, creaking faintly when the wind moves the canopy. Leaves are broad where they can afford to be, armored or deeply divided where rain strikes hardest. Many have narrow tips from which water drips with clocklike regularity.

Higher up, the forests shorten and thicken. Trees become muscular, with multiple trunks anchored into cracks. Their crowns interlock so tightly that entire hillsides move as one surface in a storm. On the most exposed ridges grow low thickets of red-veined shrubs, giant clubmosses, and cycads whose rigid leaves collect warm rain at their centers. Open ground appears only where the rock is too steep, too hot, or too frequently scoured by runoff.

There are no roads, walls, towers, ruins, tools, or signs that anything has ever laid out a plan here. Yet at times the mountains seem industrial.

The impression begins with the geothermal fields. Along certain terraces, hundreds of vents breathe in sequence. Pressure builds underground, tremors pass through the stone, and one vent after another releases a column of white vapor. Mud basins churn with slow, pistonlike regularity. Mineral valves grow around their rims, closing one outlet until another ruptures nearby. In caverns behind the cliffs, water is drawn through branching tubes of stone, heated, compressed, and discharged in pulses powerful enough to shake the forest canopy.

More striking are the living systems that have adapted to these cycles. Vast root networks descend toward the warm aquifers and move water upslope by capillary pressure and evaporation. At dawn, the trees of some high valleys begin to discharge accumulated moisture through specialized pores. Millions of droplets fall at once from leaf to leaf, and the forest produces its own rain even under a clear sky. The process runs for an hour, shuts down in the heat of midday, then begins again toward evening. Seen across a valley, the successive bands of condensation resemble machinery starting section by section.

Lightning also belongs to the ecology. Storm charge gathers on the saturated high ridges, where groves of iron-rich trees rise above the surrounding canopy. Their black, wet crowns attract frequent strikes. The current travels through conductive sap and interwoven roots without always killing the trees. For a few seconds after each impact, pale organisms beneath the bark glow along the branching path, and nearby flowers open abruptly. Beetles emerge from insulated hollows to feed on heat-softened resin. The whole grove behaves less like a forest damaged by electricity than like an apparatus made to receive it.

These events are dramatic but infrequent. Most of the time, the place is quiet.

Water provides the continuous undertone: dripping, seepage, the soft hiss of distant falls. Insects do not maintain an unbroken roar. Instead, different species sound at intervals dictated by humidity, pressure, and the temperature of the stone. A single high clicking may travel from one side of a basin to the other as colonies answer in sequence. Frogs call from hidden pools after rain, producing low notes that are felt through saturated ground more than heard through air.

Larger animals move carefully. Heat discourages wasted motion, and the abundance of cover makes stealth more useful than speed. Long-limbed browsers pick their way across root mats, feeding on new leaves and mineral crusts. Small primate-like creatures inhabit the middle canopy, where they drink from bromeliad cups and rarely descend. Gliding reptiles cross gaps between trees on membranes bright enough to resemble falling petals. In the rivers, thick-bodied fish hold themselves behind stones, waiting for fruit, larvae, or stunned insects to be delivered by the current.

Predators are present but seldom visible. One leaves oval impressions in the mud and scratches high on warm tree trunks. Another hunts in shallow water, lying beneath mats of floating fern with only its nostrils exposed. Above the escarpments, broad-winged animals circle on columns of geothermal air, almost motionless for hours. Their shadows pass over the canopy and briefly silence the smaller creatures below.

Decay is rapid but orderly. Fallen leaves soften overnight. Fungi thread them together into damp sheets, and pale crustaceans carry fragments into burrows. Dead trunks become hotbeds of decomposition, steaming in cool rain. Nothing accumulates except in the deepest pools, where black organic sediment lies meters thick and releases bubbles with a smell of iron and old vegetation. Even there, blind grazers and mats of microbes keep the boundary between rot and life in constant motion.

The weather moves vertically as often as it moves across the land. Morning heat pulls mist out of the ravines. By midday, clouds form around the mountain shoulders while the summits remain in blinding sun. Rain may fall upward along a cliff when warm drafts seize the droplets from a cascade. In late afternoon, thunderstorms march through the higher basins, their thunder muffled by dense foliage and deep folds of terrain. At night the clouds often descend, enclosing whole forests in warm gray vapor.

Distances are deceptive. Clear air after a storm reveals ridge beyond ridge, each crowded with trees, waterfalls, steam fields, and dark cuts where rivers descend. Yet the density of the near landscape makes travel slow. Ten meters may contain a stream, a wall of roots, a thicket, a pool hidden under leaves, and a warm fissure exhaling carbon-scented air. Open vistas exist from cliffs and landslip scars, but within the forest the world returns to layers: wet bark, hanging roots, shining leaves, moving eyes.

The governing rule of the place is circulation. Water descends, vanishes, heats, and rises again. Minerals pass from rock into spring, from spring into root, from root into animal, and from bone back into acidic soil. Electricity enters through storms and disperses through living tissue. Heat drives cloud; cloud feeds forest; forest captures cloud. No single element remains where it first appears.

Nothing here was constructed, but almost everything participates in a process that resembles production. The mountain manufactures rain. The forest concentrates minerals. The vents regulate pressure. Rivers sort stone, roots stabilize it, and organisms harvest every gradient of heat, moisture, and charge. It is an industrial landscape without industry: immense, efficient, and entirely without a maker, running quietly on sunlight, deep heat, gravity, and time.
