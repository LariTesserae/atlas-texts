### The Hollow Ward

The Hollow Ward lies far below any remembered surface, though “below” is not entirely reliable here. It occupies a single excavated chamber perhaps a kilometer long and half as wide: not a cavern incorporated into a city, but a city built in the shape of a cavern. Every wall is architecture. Every apparent cliff has windows, conduits, balconies, maintenance apertures, and sealed doors worn so thoroughly by mineral deposits that they resemble natural strata.

The chamber is mostly empty.

Its buildings do not rise from a floor. They sheath the perimeter in narrow, interlocking layers, leaving the central volume open and black. Streets run vertically along the walls or curl beneath overhangs like dry canals. Bridges extend a little way into the hollow and stop. Suspended stations hang at differing angles, connected by nothing visible. What looks at first like urban ruin remains functional; it simply obeys an infrastructure no longer apparent to the eye.

There is a floor, but it is far below the inhabited ledges: a broad basin of warm black silt called the Sink. Shallow water appears there after the condensation cycle, gathering in copper-colored pools between eroded ribs of masonry. Over several days the pools shrink, leaving salt membranes that wrinkle and peel. Occasionally one pool fails to evaporate. Such persistent water becomes a landmark and, eventually, an ecosystem.

The heat is constant but not oppressive. Walls hold the warmth of deep stone, while buried machinery adds slow pulses of fever. Metal rails are warm to the touch. Moisture beads on cooler ceramics and runs along hairline channels cut into almost every surface. There is no rain from above in the ordinary sense, yet every thirty or forty hours the upper dark fills with droplets. They form on unseen structures, gather weight, and fall in a scattered internal shower. Some drops descend the entire height of the ward. Others veer sideways or climb back toward the ceiling.

This is because gravity is parceled here.

The old municipal system divided attraction into routes, assigning weight much as another city might assign traffic. A pedestrian stepping from one district to the next once felt the ground rotate gently beneath them until a wall became a road. Freight moved through empty air along invisible gradients. The machinery still maintains these fields, but age has softened their boundaries. Dust hangs in slanted sheets. Water crawls around corners. A loose bolt may fall past a balcony, slow, describe a wide orbit through the chamber, and strike the same balcony years later from behind.

No one knows whether the system is deteriorating or merely adapting to the absence of its intended population.

Sound is the ward’s dominant weather.

At intervals, the entire chamber begins to roar. The source is distributed: deep fans, pressure exchanges, mass-routing engines, and resonance pylons buried in the architecture. A low mechanical note rises through the masonry until doors tremble in their frames. Pipes answer in higher voices. Empty towers amplify the vibration, and the central hollow becomes an instrument large enough to produce tones felt in the sternum before they are heard.

During the loudest cycles, old signs shake dust from their recessed lettering. Water standing in the Sink forms geometric ripples—hexagons, branching grids, concentric shapes whose centers lie in midair. The noise can continue for hours, changing so slowly that each new pitch seems permanent. Then it stops all at once.

The resulting silence is not gentle. Condensation ticks against railings. Warm stone contracts with tiny clicks. Somewhere in the darkness, a delayed echo arrives from a sound made days earlier.

The surfaces show tremendous age without looking ruined. Erosion has settled into the design. Corners have been rounded by mineral water and acoustic vibration; stair treads have become smooth troughs; ornamental panels are furred with pale calcite. Some walls were made of programmable matter and have responded to centuries of wear by growing thicker where water runs. As a result, drains resemble roots, and façades have slowly folded around them like bark around wire.

The city is weathering, but in equilibrium. Material leaves one place and is incorporated elsewhere. Rust travels through capillary channels and blooms as red glass. Dissolved stone is carried upward by gravity fields, then deposited beneath roofs in hanging terraces. Nothing collapses quickly. A failing structure redistributes itself grain by grain, sometimes over generations, until it becomes a buttress for the structure opposite it.

Vegetation survives in sparse, deliberate patches. Along condensation seams grow black fans of moss, each no larger than a hand. They open only during the internal rain and close when the roar begins. Their undersides are silver and reflect whatever little light exists: emergency glyphs, the blue flicker of active conduits, or the faint amber glow of microbial mats around persistent pools.

A few pale vines inhabit the warmer shafts. They have no true leaves. Their translucent stems cross stretches of bare wall to reach corroding electrical junctions, from which they draw both heat and trace metals. Where several vines meet, they form a loose cage around the junction and pulse at long intervals. These are the ward’s nearest equivalent to trees, though an entire grove might consist of six stems in a room the size of a nave.

Animal life is rarer. Thin, eyeless crustaceans occupy the Sink after a moisture cycle, emerging from cracks as soon as the first drops fall. They graze on films left by evaporation and bury themselves before the basin dries. Their eggs can remain dormant in salt for decades.

Higher in the chamber live the sailers. One may be seen only a few times in a year: a dark, flat-bodied creature that enters an active gravity route and lets itself fall sideways through the city. It steers with membranes stretched between six narrow limbs. Sailers feed on airborne spores and condensation insects, circling the hollow for days without touching a surface. When the machinery changes phase, they fold themselves into niches and grip the stone until the world decides which way is down again.

There are signs that people still pass through, though the ward does not feel inhabited. A handrail will be clean where all others are furred with dust. A service door may stand open for one cycle and sealed the next. Small lamps occasionally kindle in sequence along a vertical avenue, marking a route no visible traveler takes. The lamps are dim enough to preserve the darkness rather than dispel it.

Most illumination comes from the infrastructure itself. Hair-thin lines of green-white light run beneath translucent stone, branching like nerves. They brighten when approached but seldom reveal more than a few meters. In the great central space, distance swallows them. Opposite districts appear as faint constellations, so that a person standing on one wall can mistake the other side of the city for a night sky.

The Ward has boundaries. At either end, the chamber narrows into monumental gates made from overlapping rings. One opens toward deeper municipal levels; the other is filled with compacted sediment and warm water. Neither can be crossed when the resonance engines are active. During those hours the rings become impossibly heavy, anchoring the local geometry while the rest of the district flexes around them.

The city’s surviving rule is simple: movement belongs to the Ward before it belongs to the traveler. A thrown object, a running animal, a drop of rain, even a shout may be captured by routes laid down centuries ago. Paths recur. Momentum is stored. Echoes return. Nothing that moves here is entirely lost, but neither does it necessarily arrive soon.

Thus the Hollow Ward remains active despite its emptiness. Water gathers and vanishes. Roads tilt through the dark. Buildings exchange their worn material. Mosses unfold, engines bellow, sailers cross the abyss, and ancient systems continue directing the traffic of an almost vacant city.

Far above, there may still be a surface. Nothing in the Ward requires it.
