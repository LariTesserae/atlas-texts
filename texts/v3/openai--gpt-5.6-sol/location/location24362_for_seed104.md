The sea begins above the clouds.

It rests on the summit of an ancient tableland, a dark, warm mass of water thousands of kilometers across. No basin contains it. At its edges the water rises in sheer, trembling cliffs, held not by walls but by an old distortion of weight. Beyond those transparent escarpments lies open air and, far below, the pale weather of the low world. Storms crawl beneath the sea like shadows under glass.

Within, there is no obvious surface. Upward, the water merely becomes thinner and dimmer until distance turns it blue-black again. Downward, it thickens around the plateau’s worn stone. Depth and height have folded through one another. A swimmer descending for days may emerge higher than where they began, looking out through the vertical boundary at mountain ranges far below. A falling leaf may sink, slow, and return from above.

The drowned plateau is nearly flat. It is not young flatness—not mud laid down yesterday, not a plain awaiting rivers—but the final smoothness of unimaginable age. Once there were peaks here. Their roots remain as broad undulations scarcely steeper than sleeping breath. Stone has been rubbed until its grains no longer feel separate. Ravines have widened into shallow troughs; cliffs have become long inclines whose beginnings and ends are too far apart to see. The bedrock is red-brown beneath films of green and black, polished in places to a dull, depthless luster.

Everything loose has already been carried away, broken smaller than silt, and distributed through the water. That ancient dust gives the sea its dimness. Light penetrates without beams. It is a diffuse bronze-green presence, never coming from one direction, illuminating nearby forms while refusing to reveal their distances. On rare clearings in the current, the plateau seems to continue forever beneath a ceiling that does not exist.

The warmth is steady but not uniform. Vast slow bands of hot water migrate over the plain. They are invisible except where vegetation responds: fronds uncurling in sequence across hundreds of kilometers, as if a hand passes over fur. Cold wells open without warning in the stone, releasing dense clear water that pours upward. Around them, mineral gardens grow in concentric shelves—chalk, iron, sulfurous yellow, translucent crusts the color of old bone. These formations are among the few sharp things left.

Vegetation occupies almost every surface.

It is vegetation only in the broadest sense. There are forests of ribbon-plants rooted into microscopic seams, their leaves extending for hundreds of meters while remaining thin enough to show the shadow of one side through the other. There are low velvet fields that alter color under pressure, fading from green to bruised violet as currents lean across them. Hollow stalks rise in dense clusters and bend without touching, though there is scarcely a finger’s width between them. Their avoidance produces narrow, wandering corridors through the thickets.

Some growths have abandoned the ground. Buoyant mats hang in the water at every altitude: tangles of bladder-leaves, root veils, and broad parasols turned sideways to catch currents rather than light. Beneath them descend threads finer than hair, kilometers long, drinking mineral dust from the sea. The threads form curtains so dense that distant parts of the landscape appear in panels. Currents pull these curtains aside and disclose other forests, other plains, occasionally the same curtain seen from behind before it has moved.

There are no flowers. Reproduction happens through intervals of darkness. At irregular periods, whole provinces of growth release black spores simultaneously, and the sea becomes night. The spores travel in silent fronts. They adhere to exposed rock, to other plants, to the few remains of structures, and sometimes to empty water, where they germinate upon surfaces that cannot be seen.

Animal life is rare enough to feel accidental.

A pale, broad-bodied grazer may pass once in a season, nosing through moss with a mouth like a soft stone. Its wake arrives minutes before it does. Small transparent creatures live inside the hollow reeds, but they move only when unobserved; one sees their abandoned chambers, their delicate molts, never the animals themselves. Far out over the deepest troughs, immense shadows occasionally cross the ambient light. Nothing follows them—no disturbance, no sound, no displaced vegetation—and it is uncertain whether they are creatures, weather, or absences moving through the water.

There are no schools of fish, no continuous insect murmur, no reef clatter. The abundance belongs almost entirely to rooted and drifting life.

Yet the place is active. It is active at scales that do not produce noise.

Warm fronts comb the forests. Mineral springs build terraces grain by grain. Root mats migrate across stone, lifting and settling like slow lungs. In the plateau’s central reaches, tides travel vertically: the water compresses toward the ground for several hours, flattening every frond, then loosens and allows the forest to rise. Vast whirlpools form without rotation. Their centers are regions where distance itself drains away. A leaf entering one may cross a thousand kilometers while seeming motionless; another, caught beside it, may swim for years and advance no farther than its own length.

Sound is swallowed almost at birth. The water feels heavy with listening. A broken stalk makes only a soft internal click. Stones settling against one another produce no impact, merely a pressure felt briefly in the teeth and joints. During the strongest current changes, the entire plateau gives off a nearly inaudible tone, lower than hearing, which causes suspended roots to align in the same direction. Silence here is not the absence of events. It is the material into which events disappear.

Scattered over the plain are traces of building, though none suggest machinery or any sophisticated craft. Low circles of fitted stone stand among the moss fields, each scarcely high enough to mark a boundary. There are roofless chambers made from slabs leaned together, their entrances facing into currents that may not have existed when they were raised. Narrow causeways cross places that are no longer gaps. A few solitary pillars remain, worn so smooth that they resemble natural extrusions until one notices their regular spacing.

No tool marks survive. Perhaps there were never tools. The stones may have been persuaded into place by roots, hands, tides, or patience. Some shelters contain layers of pressed leaves, continually renewed by plants that grow nowhere else. Others hold dry air.

These pockets of air are impossible and ancient. They cling beneath stone roofs without bubbling upward. Inside them, the temperature is warm, the floor damp, and the silence changes from oceanic pressure to the frail quiet of a sealed room. Looking back through the entrance, one sees the submerged forest waving in dim green light. Looking through a second entrance—where one exists—may reveal the same forest in another age: bare stone before the growth, or a thicket so mature that its trunks have merged into walls.

The landscape does not preserve time in order.

Erosion has rubbed not only the rock but sequence itself. In some regions, the consequence of an event arrives first. Silt clouds gather, then a terrace collapses into the empty space prepared for it. A plant lies uprooted for days before the current comes to tear it free. Footprints—if anything crossed the plain to leave them—slowly deepen in undisturbed moss, and only later does a pale animal pass nearby, not quite placing its feet in them.

Toward the sea’s margins, the plateau narrows beneath forests that grow almost horizontally. Through the vertical wall of water, the outside world shines gray and distant. Rain falls below. Clouds drift underfoot. Lightning flickers silently in the atmosphere, many kilometers down, and its light enters the suspended sea as a dim pulse from beneath the stone.

Plants crowd the boundary but do not cross it. Their leaves press flat against the invisible division, making vast green windows. Now and then the wall extrudes a rounded mass of water into the air, a bulge the size of a mountain. It hangs there for hours, filled with trailing vegetation and bronze dust, then draws itself back without shedding a drop.

At longer intervals, part of the ocean overturns.

There is no violence to it. A region hundreds of kilometers wide simply changes its understanding of down. Forests bow sideways. Drifting islands pass through rooted meadows. Mineral rain rises from the plain. The few animals conceal themselves in stone hollows, while the vegetation yields, tangles, tears, and begins immediately to regrow. During these inversions, one may look into the murk and glimpse the eroded plateau overhead, its springs descending like pale smoke, its ancient structures clinging upside down to the roof of the world.

Then weight settles into another temporary direction.

The dust thickens. The forests resume their long movements. Somewhere beyond sight, a single stalk clicks as it breaks. The sound travels a few meters and vanishes.

Above the clouds, beneath an ocean with no surface, the oldest highland continues being worn smooth.
