There is no seafloor here, only workmanship.

Beneath miles of cold, nearly black water lies a plain assembled stone by stone: fitted basalt flags, drowned stairways, retaining walls, tiled canals, courtyards, cisterns, embankments, and broad terraces descending by increments too slight to sense. Sediment has softened every joint, but wherever the silt thins, the pattern returns. A chisel line. A row of peg holes. The lip of a drain. The worn center of a road.

The construction extends beyond any horizon. It is not a city surrounded by wilderness; there is no wilderness and perhaps never was. The whole low world was leveled, paved, partitioned, and given a gradient by hand. Its foundations are buried too deeply to find. Even the hills are masonry: shallow, many-sided rises made from courses of stone and packed clay, their slopes held by millions of low walls. Valleys are long settling basins. Ravines are collapsed conduits. What appear from a distance to be reefs are heaps of roof tile, brick, carved posts, and the remnants of bridges folded down upon older bridges.

No metal is visible. The surviving materials are those that patient hands could quarry, shape, fire, split, weave, and carry: dark stone, pale ceramic, timber made black and hard by immersion, braided reed sealed under mud. The scale is difficult to reconcile with the simplicity of its making. Individual blocks are small enough to lift. Steps are made for ordinary feet. Doorways, where they remain, are modest. Yet the doors recur for thousands of miles, opening into chambers that repeat with minor differences, as though generations built outward without ever deciding to stop.

Now everything is submerged.

The water is dense with cold and depth. It presses into halls, fills ovens and granaries, stands in wells that once descended through dry ground. There are no waves in the lower reaches. Currents arrive as almost imperceptible changes of pressure, taking days to bend a stem or carry a veil of silt across a threshold. Far above, the drowned world may have a surface, but it contributes little. At noon a dimness sometimes gathers overhead, less a light than a thinning of darkness. During long overcast seasons even that disappears.

Vegetation has inherited every made surface.

Forests of ribbon weed rise from cracks between paving slabs. Their leaves are broad, thin, and nearly black, arranged to intercept the weakest downward glow. They grow with extreme patience. A new leaf may take a season to unfold; a grove may require centuries to cross a plaza. Once established, however, the growth is luxuriant. Long fronds pour over parapets and hang through windows. Pale root mats fill stairwells from floor to ceiling. Mosses coat carved walls in dense velvet, turning inscriptions into rounded green shadows. In the open settling basins stand thickets of jointed water stems, taller than towers, their upper reaches lost in the dark.

There are no flowers in the familiar sense. Reproduction is quiet and dispersed: clouds of spores released at intervals measured in decades, drifting until they settle into mortar, porous brick, or the fibrous remains of old ropes. Some plants spread almost entirely by runners. A single colony may occupy an entire district, linked underground through miles of silted channels. What looks like a forest can be one ancient organism following the geometry of streets.

The lushness does not make the place colorful. Most growth is black-green, brown, or a deep blue visible only at close range. New tips are translucent. Damaged stems leak a faint milky sap that curls away in the water and vanishes. On higher roofs, where a trace of remote daylight still reaches, low mats take on a muted olive hue. These upper gardens are the most active places in the world, though their activity would be nearly undetectable to a human observer.

Nothing grazes them.

There are no fish among the columns, no shellfish on the walls, no worms in the mud. No animal eyes reflect from doorways. No burrows interrupt the sediment. Leaves grow old where they stand, collapse, and are slowly overgrown by the next generation. Dead vegetation gathers in rooms and channels, becoming felted layers of dark fiber. Without mouths to break it down quickly, decay is a long chemical fading. Whole groves subside into peatlike beds that preserve the shapes of floors beneath them.

This absence gives the landscape an unusual cleanliness. There are no bones, tracks, nests, husks, or sudden movements. Nothing darts when disturbed. Every motion belongs to water, vegetation, or the gradual failure of construction.

Sound is correspondingly scarce. In the deep districts, quiet has weight. At long intervals a roof tile slips from its bed and strikes the paving below: one hard knock, then several diminishing impacts carried astonishing distances through the water. A swollen timber beam gives a low internal crack. Silt whispers down a staircase. In the tall weed forests, current passing through thousands of narrow leaves produces a faint continuous rasp, like cloth drawn slowly over stone.

The loudest events are collapses, but even these are subdued by water. An arch may spend centuries losing grains of mortar before finally folding in on itself. Its fall comes as a muffled succession of blows and a rolling pressure through adjacent chambers. A cloud rises, conceals the ruin, and takes years to settle. When the water clears, roots have already begun testing the new surfaces.

Erosion has nearly completed its work without erasing the world. Corners are rounded. Relief carvings have become shallow, anonymous undulations. Statues, if they were statues, resemble upright stones draped in weed. Steps sag at their centers. Channels have widened as their walls slumped outward, producing slow rivers without banks. But because everything underneath is also constructed, erosion only reveals earlier layers of making. Beneath one plaza lies another. Beneath a fallen retaining wall is a tiled reservoir. Beneath that is a pavement of smaller, older stones, worn smooth before the upper world was begun.

There is no exposed bedrock to end the descent.

The lowest known places are enormous sunken courts where the water becomes colder and almost perfectly still. Their floors are laid in fitted slabs the size of tables, interrupted by circular shafts. These shafts breathe faint currents from deeper levels. Vegetation rims them but rarely grows across their mouths. Loose leaves descend into them and do not return. They are not machines, at least not in any active sense; they are drains, wells, ventilation passages, or accessways built according to the same simple principles as everything else. Their mystery comes only from age and depth.

Across the greater plain, differences in water temperature create a kind of weather. Cold streams creep along the buried avenues. Slightly warmer water rises from deep cistern systems, carrying dissolved minerals that feed pale gardens on the walls above. Once every several years, a broad density current moves through the region. It lays all the weed in one direction and sends curtains of fine sediment pouring from rooftops. For a few days the submerged districts seem to lean. Then the current passes, and the stems slowly lift.

Time here is counted by deposits rather than days. A green film thickens. A terrace settles the width of a finger. A conduit fills. A root enters a crack, swells, and separates two stones after a hundred years. The world changes constantly, but almost nothing changes quickly enough to watch.

There are indications that the flooding itself was not sudden. Doors were blocked with fitted stone. Low chambers were filled deliberately with rubble. Clay seals remain on some vents. Causeways were raised repeatedly, each new course laid over an older surface as the water climbed. Gardens were moved to roofs, then to towers, then perhaps abandoned. The builders seem to have met the rising water with the same methods by which they had built everything else: baskets, levers, wedges, kilns, ropes, rafts, and immense coordinated labor.

Eventually the water exceeded every wall.

What remains is not a ruin in the usual sense, because ruin implies an interruption of order. Here the old order persists as terrain. Roads guide currents. Courtyards collect silt. Aqueducts shelter root systems. Roofs have become plains, towers have become shoals, and canals have merged into a single cold ocean while retaining their influence on its movement. The constructed world continues to govern the flooded one long after its makers have disappeared.

From a high point—perhaps the top of a stepped mound, perhaps the roof of a buried hall—the view is vast but close. Darkness limits distance. Forms emerge only as degrees of black: a parapet beneath trailing leaves, a line of columns, the blocky rise of another terrace. Beyond them are more terraces, more drowned groves, more patient masonry. The water contains a sparse fall of particles, each catching the faintest trace of overhead gray before sinking out of sight.

Nothing approaches. Nothing calls.

The landscape simply extends: handmade, overgrown, cold, and old enough that its final shape has begun to seem inevitable.
