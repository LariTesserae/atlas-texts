The place occupies the crown of a mountain, though none of the mountain is visible.

It is enclosed on all sides by a ring of fabricated cliffs: warm, dark walls poured from mineral foam, furnace slag, powdered glass, and the pale shells of industrial algae. They rise perhaps thirty meters, curving inward slightly, so that the whole summit feels less like a valley than a room left open to the sky. From one wall to the other is only a short walk. Yet the density of partitions, hanging gardens, cisterns, scaffold-roots, and half-formed stone makes it difficult to see farther than a few paces.

Everything here was built. The rock was mixed in vats. The soil arrived as wet bricks on a freight lift. Even the shallow tarn at the center has a welded floor and a rim of interlocking ceramic teeth. No natural substrate lies beneath it—only service decks, pressure chambers, and the hollow steel crown of an immense tower descending through the clouds.

The landscape is still being made.

Along the inner cliffs, nozzles exude slow ribbons of gray-green lithic paste. These folds sag, wrinkle, and harden in the humid air, becoming ledges before they have had time to become weathered. Pebbles bud directly from seams. Stalactites are printed downward in careful increments, each one warm to the touch and faintly smelling of rain on concrete. Where the mixture is too wet, whole shelves slump by a finger’s width, leaving smooth raw scars. Small maintenance arms emerge from apertures to correct them, pressing the young geology back into place with padded metal palms.

There has not yet been enough time for erosion. Water travels over surfaces too new to bear channels. It spreads as bright skins, loses its way, collects behind unfinished lips. The cliffs weep everywhere, but their tears have not learned where rivers should go.

As a result, the floor is an intricate marsh of accidents.

Shallow pools occupy depressions between molded roots. Warm water trembles over black tile, amber resin, porous concrete, and plates of copper gone blue at the edges. In some places it is no more than a film reflecting the sky; in others it reaches the knee and hides bundles of insulated cable beneath floating leaves. Narrow walkways cross it, assembled from hexagonal grates. Several have already vanished under vegetation. The plants do not distinguish between designed habitat and machinery. Moss coats valve wheels. Ferns unfurl from gutters. Creepers thread themselves through fan housings and descend in curtains from the scaffold canopy.

The air is warm enough that every surface seems to perspire. Clouds pass close overhead, brushing the summit enclosure with their bellies. When they enter the ring, vapor condenses on the hanging cables and artificial branches. Drops gather, swell, and fall in a soft, irregular rain even under clear sunlight. Every few hours the atmosphere changes direction: warm air rises through floor vents, mist blooms among the ferns, and the opposite wall disappears. Then extraction fans begin turning somewhere deep below, and the enclosure clears from the bottom upward.

The plants are lush but young. Nothing has the deep authority of an old forest. There are no great trunks, only many slender ones—bamboo-like columns grown around composite spines, broad-leaved vines trained across tensile wires, waxy shrubs rooted in suspended troughs. Their abundance comes from speed rather than age. Leaves overlap leaves. Tendrils cross conduits. Pale root mats drift in the tarn like drowned cloth.

Some species were chosen to manufacture soil. They crush the soft mineral surfaces with swelling roots and fill the fractures with black humus. Others pull metals from the water and store them in iridescent veins. Their fallen leaves are collected by slow skimming belts, macerated beneath the walkways, and returned as warm compost through perforations in the floor. The system is circular, but not tidy. Seeds lodge in control panels. Pollen clogs optical sensors. Drainage mouths develop green beards.

There are flowers, though most are small and functional: white cups that condense mist, orange brushes pollinated by vibration, translucent bells that close when the pressure drops. One vine blooms directly against the cliff printers. Its roots detect the fresh calcium-rich slurry and follow the moving construction front, so that each newly made ledge acquires a fringe of purple within a day.

Animal life is scarce. The enclosure is too new, too isolated, and too thoroughly filtered. Occasionally a single dark moth appears under a work light, large enough to cast a wavering shadow. Tiny glass-backed snails graze algae from the cistern rims. There may be a gecko in the western wall; workers have found shed skin caught in a sensor grille, but no one has seen the animal itself.

The tarn contains three long silver fish. They were not introduced, according to the records. They have no visible eyes and rarely disturb the surface. On humid evenings they rise beneath the floating roots, and dim blue registration marks can be seen moving under their skin, as though they were manufactured components that escaped into ecology. Their number never changes. Eggs have been found, but never young.

Despite its activity, the place is mostly quiet.

Water ticks from cable to leaf, leaf to railing, railing to pool. Pumps produce a low pressure felt more in the soles than heard. A chain moves somewhere inside the eastern cliff with a patient, intermittent chuff. Every so often a printer arm releases pressure and the valley gives a brief mineral sigh. There is no continuous roar of industry. Most mechanisms are hidden, damped, or operating at the slow pace of growth.

The loudest events are the rain cycles. Sprayers mounted high in the ring activate without warning, throwing warm droplets through the canopy. For several minutes the entire summit hisses. Leaves bow. New stone darkens. The tarn dimples until its welded bottom is invisible. Overflow runs across the unfinished floor in broad, indecisive sheets, searching for slopes that have not yet been assigned.

Then the rain stops. Drain shutters open one by one with hollow clicks. Water sinks through reed beds and filter gravel into the machinery below, carrying spores, dissolved minerals, and fragments of leaf. The place begins immediately to dry, though never completely. Steam lifts from the darker walls. Condensation gathers above it. The next rain is already assembling.

There are signs of human use, but few people are present. A narrow operations booth clings to the upper rim, its windows shaded by enormous leaves. Ladders descend into the gardens. Yellow handrails appear and disappear under vines. Labels are stamped into almost every surface:

**SLOPE REVISION 8**  
**ROOT LOAD NOT YET CERTIFIED**  
**TEMPORARY WATERSHED**  
**DO NOT ENTER DURING CLIFF GROWTH**

At the south wall stands a small pavilion made entirely of pipes. It shelters no machinery now; its former tanks have been removed, and their circular foundations hold warm rainwater and reeds. A bench runs around the inside. From there, the enclosure resembles a miniature world undergoing accelerated creation: cliff, wetland, cloud forest, reservoir, and factory occupying the same confined volume.

Its rules are not quite those of an ordinary landscape. Height comes before age. Stone follows plumbing. Rain is both weather and scheduled maintenance. Succession is designed but never fully obeyed. Anything that falls is expected to become feedstock for something else. Anything that grows beyond its allotted mass is cut, pulped, and returned underground. Yet the system’s greatest productivity occurs in the places the plans failed to describe: beneath loose grates, inside dead vents, along the warm backs of dormant motors.

Each day the walls are fractionally higher. Each day the vegetation makes the interior fractionally smaller.

Eventually, the designers intend to stop the printers and call the landscape complete. By then, they expect the young cliffs to have hardened, the drainage to have found permanent routes, and the planted canopy to regulate its own weather. But the place appears to be developing a different intention. Roots divert water toward unapproved basins. Mineral paste hardens around stems, preserving them inside the cliff. The tarn’s silver fish gather whenever the construction arms move, as though listening for instructions.

Near the northern wall, one unfinished passage leads inward. Warm air exhales from it. Its surfaces are smooth and damp, printed only yesterday, with embedded lights shining through the mineral skin. The passage is supposed to become a drainage gallery, but for now it ends in raw scaffold and a drop into darkness. Water can be heard below, much farther down than the dimensions of the summit should allow.

Beside the opening, a fern has rooted in new stone.

Its fronds are still uncurling. Each is patterned with fine parallel lines, almost like the contour marks on a map. As the leaf opens, the lines rearrange themselves, showing pools, ledges, and channels that do not yet exist.

By morning, the cliff printers have begun building them.
