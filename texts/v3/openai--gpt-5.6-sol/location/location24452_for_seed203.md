## The White Undergreen

The place lies beneath a surface no one living has seen.

Far overhead, beyond a distance difficult to judge, the roof of the world shines. It is not sky. It has no sun, stars, or discernible curve, though its curvature becomes evident over journeys of several months. It is a continuous stratum of pale root-tissue pressed against translucent stone: frost-white, faintly green, and bright enough to cast sharp shadows. At irregular intervals the whole ceiling intensifies, passing through waves of brilliance as though enormous weather were moving through the buried layers above.

This light is cold. Nothing warms beneath it.

The inhabited space is a planetary hollow between two skins of living matter. The upper skin is the luminous root-vault; the lower is a continent-wide tangle of trunks, compressed peat, fungal wood, and pale structural fibers. Between them lies an abyss of clear air, so vast that distant landforms fade not into blue but into luminous milk. There are regions where the floor and ceiling are hundreds of kilometers apart. There are regions where they nearly meet, joined by solitary pillars of vegetation thicker than mountains.

Despite the abundance of life, the first impression is emptiness.

Most of the volume contains nothing at all.

The green world gathers into immense, widely separated forms: floating veils, root islands, upright forests with kilometers between individual stalks, and branching structures whose limbs are broad enough to carry valleys. Each living presence is enormous, but between them stretch clean gulfs of bright, cold air. A traveler may spend days crossing a single naked shelf of smooth cellulose, seeing only one black tendril on the horizon.

There is little soil. Plant is the soil, plant is the rock, and plant is the architecture on which all other things depend.

The ground underfoot may be the hide of a dormant organism: hard, fibrous, and faintly warm only where its inner fluids are moving. Young surfaces are bone-pale and sharp-edged. They split into geometric scales, curl upward, and harden before wind or water has time to soften them. Older surfaces darken toward green-black and accumulate a thin felt of moss, spores, shed hairs, and powdered bark. True mineral appears only in scattered inclusions drawn up from deeper strata—blue salt, glassy nodules, grains of iron—which plants encase as irritants and slowly grow around.

The landscape is continually making itself.

Great ribs push upward without warning, lifting plains and breaking them into tilted slabs. Hollow stems inflate beneath the floor and raise new ridges over the course of hours. Root bridges extend across voids, hesitate, fork, and sometimes withdraw. Entire forests unfold from tightly packed buds, stand for a season, then collapse into loose spirals of fiber. Elsewhere, broad leaf-basins invert with a soundless convulsion, spilling frost, huts, and accumulated debris into the light below.

These changes are violent but rarely loud. The world has an uncanny softness of substance. Fibers bend where stone would shatter. Miles of living wall can buckle in near silence. A storm may be visible for half a day—veils twisting, branches lashing, loose sheets of frost streaming sideways—yet from a sheltered ledge one hears only a dry hiss, an occasional wooden report, and the slow creak of tension moving through the floor.

When something truly breaks, the sound travels astonishingly far.

A severed pillar produces a single crack like a split in the world, followed hours later by diminishing knocks as its branches strike lower structures. Afterward the quiet returns so completely that droplets can be heard forming on the underside of leaves.

Water is present, but never generously. It travels as a pulse.

Condensation gathers when the ceiling dims and the air cools further. Beads form across the luminous vault, hang there like dark stars, and eventually fall. Some evaporate during their descent. Some freeze into clear needles. In certain regions the droplets combine into brief underground rains, narrow curtains descending through otherwise dry air. These rains may wander laterally as currents shift, watering a forest for several days and then leaving it untouched for years.

The lower growth catches what it can. Leaves are shaped less for sunlight than for water: broad cups, combs, gutters, nets of waxed hair. Reservoir plants swell into translucent bladders along the edges of plains. Inside them, water remains liquid beneath a skin of antifreeze sap. When punctured, they leak slowly, sealing the wound around whatever tool or beak made it.

There are rivers, but they are temporary and strange. A channel may appear when a distant reservoir ruptures, carrying black water through grooves in the living floor. Because the terrain is young and always moving, the river has no established bed. It cuts only shallowly before the substrate swells, tilts, or closes beneath it. The flow divides into glittering threads and vanishes into pores. Along these brief riverways, green growth erupts with such speed that a bare channel can become a tunnel of leaves before the last water has passed.

Cold governs everything. Moisture freezes in shaded folds; exhaled breath hangs close to the face. Yet the vegetation does not behave as vegetation should in cold. Its growth is driven by the overhead radiance and by chemical currents in the deep floor. Many structures are dry, porous, and almost weightless, conserving no heat because they have no need of it. Their living tissues retreat into hair-thin vessels, wrapped in meters of dead structural fiber.

Color is most intense where the world has recently opened. New growth appears white, lemon, or electric green. Mature growth turns deep violet or nearly black to absorb the cold brilliance. Wound tissue shines orange. When a continent of leaf tears, its exposed edge can make a burning line hundreds of kilometers long.

There are very few animals.

Those that exist are mostly solitary and difficult to distinguish from parts of plants. Glass-winged crawlers move along the ceiling roots, grazing luminous films. Long, six-legged things cross the lower plains on feet broad enough not to pierce the skin beneath them. They travel only after rain, following dampness with their heads lowered, and may remain folded and immobile for decades when conditions dry.

In the great open air drift bell-shaped organisms called listeners. They possess no visible wings. Gas chambers hold them aloft while rootlike sensory threads trail below. They turn toward distant fractures and descend upon fresh wounds, feeding on sap. A listener may be the only moving creature seen in a hundred days of travel.

Predation is rare and abrupt. Pale burrowers live inside the structural layers, emerging when they sense repeated footsteps. They do not chase. They open the ground.

More often, the plants themselves occupy the role of hazard, hunter, and scavenger. Some close around warmth. Some mimic dry, load-bearing surfaces until weight touches them. Others grow toward rhythmic vibration and will slowly surround a camp if people sleep in the same place too many nights. Fire is especially dangerous. Flame is difficult to sustain in the cold, humid air, but certain dry tissues ignite explosively, flashing along internal veins before vanishing. The plant may survive; those standing on it may not.

Human habitation, if the scattered settlements can be called human, is slight enough to disappear in the scale of the hollow.

Villages cling to old growth—never to new, because new growth still changes its mind. Houses are woven into branch-forks from bark strips, felted hair, dried membranes, and bone. Stone is too rare for walls. Metal is rarer still. A village may possess one cold-hammered copper blade, inherited through generations and used only to cut tissues that defeat flint.

Most tools are grown, not made. Hooks are thorns hardened in smoke. Water vessels are seed cases. Rope is twisted vascular fiber. Sled runners are polished rib-wood. People shape living walkways by pinching buds, scoring bark, and applying carefully mixed saps. This is not precise control. It is persuasion practiced over years.

The settlements leave modest marks: ladders stitched to vertical skins, rain-catchers stretched beneath condensation routes, blackened hearth pits, warning knots tied around deceptive shoots. On migration paths, shallow symbols are cut into the floor. They do not mark direction, since direction itself can change when the substrate rolls or folds. Instead they record behavior:

**This surface sleeps deeply.**

**This bridge pulls north during brightness.**

**Water came here three dimmings ago.**

**Do not drum.**

Ruins are difficult to recognize because the world digests them. An abandoned house becomes a swelling in a branch. A fence takes root. A corpse, wrapped in woven leaf, may be incorporated into the floor until only teeth remain in a seam of green wood. The oldest known structure is a ring of mineral blocks around a dry well, each block hauled from an unknown distance. The well descends not into earth but into a hollow vein that exhales warm, metallic air. No one drinks from it. The ring is maintained because it is one of the few things that does not grow.

Weather belongs to the hollow rather than entering it.

Brightness tides create convection on a continental scale. When one region of the ceiling flares, air beneath it cools and descends, driving slow horizontal gales through the lower reaches. Vast hanging forests begin to sway. Spores lift in silver clouds. Frost is stripped from the upper vault and falls in glittering fronts. In the open gulfs, these winds may reach tremendous speed, yet the air remains almost transparent, giving little warning except the distant bending of structures.

At times the vegetation contributes weather of its own. A coordinated flowering releases enough vapor to form clouds. A mass closure of leaves displaces air and sends pressure waves across whole territories. The opening of deep vents brings up warm mist, which rises until it strikes the cold roof and becomes sudden rain.

The greatest events are called turnings.

No one knows whether the lower world is one organism or many. During a turning, the distinction ceases to matter. Growth across thousands of kilometers begins to move in apparent concert. Plains tighten. Pillars lean. Suspended veils reel upward. Reservoirs burst in sequence, and temporary rivers race across surfaces that are themselves flowing in another direction. Settlements cut their anchors and trust themselves to migration sleds. Animals emerge from dormancy all at once. The luminous ceiling becomes unbearably bright.

Then the world rearranges.

A familiar valley may become a vertical wall. An abyss may close, its two sides knitting into a dark seam. New empty spaces open where solid mass had been. Forests arrive from the distance, carried on folding plates of leaf. The change can last weeks, and afterward every map is obsolete.

Yet even in the heart of a turning, silence often dominates. Motion happens too broadly for the ear. One sees horizons rise and does not hear them. Shadows sweep across the white vault. Frost lifts in columns. A river bends into the air as its bed rotates beneath it. Only nearby fibers complain, ticking and creaking like ropes on a ship.

There are several dependable rules.

Light comes from above, but life does not always grow toward it.

Water falls, except during turnings.

No surface remains dead forever.

Repeated sound attracts something, though not always an animal.

Fresh white growth is strong and unpredictable; old black growth is weak and honest.

Nothing truly discarded stays an object for long.

And there is no bedrock—not in any place reached by root, drill, burrow, or well. Beneath every floor is another chamber, another web of load-bearing growth, another brightness perhaps, and another enormous emptiness.

The people who live here do not imagine the world as a cavern. A cavern implies stone surrounding space. They say instead that they inhabit the pause between two acts of growth.

On clear, quiet intervals, one can stand on the edge of a leaf-cliff and look out across that pause. The radiant ceiling stretches beyond sight. Far away, a single stalk rises through the gulf, impossibly slender for its height. Condensation falls around it in a dim vertical haze. Below, the lower forest opens and closes in slow waves, black against the white light.

Nothing seems to move nearby.

Then the cliff underfoot gives a faint, muscular shiver. A new green ridge presses up along the horizon. Somewhere too far away to see, a pillar breaks, and minutes later its deep report crosses the emptiness.

After that, there is only the sound of water gathering.
