The city occupies a high basin where the mountains have been worn down to blunt black rims. From a distance it appears dense—terraces, towers, bridges, pressure pipes, stepped foundations—but within it there is almost nothing. Buildings stand far apart on enormous paved shelves. Roads broaden into plazas large enough to hold weather systems. Elevated rails cross kilometers of open air without passing a single inhabited window.

It is hot even at this altitude. Heat comes from below rather than from the dim sky. The paving stones sweat around their seams; iron handrails are warm enough to sting; deep shafts exhale mineral breath at regular intervals. The city was built over a thin, fractured crust through which geothermal water circulates. It rises as vapor, condenses against the upper walls, and comes down again in a perpetual local rain.

There are no plants.

The absence is initially difficult to see because every surface glistens as though it ought to be fertile. Water lies in gutter channels and shallow courtyards. Droplets hang beneath cables. Black runoff threads through orange deposits of iron and gathers in tiled reservoirs. Yet no moss softens the stone, no weed prises apart the paving, no root follows the wet cracks. The water is hot, acidic, and heavily mineralized. Where it cools, it leaves skins of pale silica, green copper salts, sulfur-yellow rims, and a dark red sediment fine as flour. These deposits form the nearest thing the city has to growth. They thicken around drains, creep over steps, seal doors, and build delicate terraces inside broken factories.

The sky is nearly always hidden. A low ceiling of vapor fills the basin, absorbing daylight until noon resembles late evening. Lamps burn along the main conduits, though many illuminate no path anyone uses. Their dull amber globes appear and vanish as clouds pass through the streets. Higher towers disappear entirely above the mist, leaving only their lower supports: enormous legs planted in flooded squares. Occasionally the vapor thins enough to reveal the upper city suspended across the slopes—gantries, reservoirs, ventilation crowns, and cableways stretched from ridge to ridge. Then the scale of the place becomes briefly comprehensible, and more unsettling.

The oldest districts have been shaped into the mountain rather than placed upon it. Their walls are weathered basalt, polished by centuries of hot condensation. Arches have sagged without collapsing. Stairways have become shallow ramps under accumulated mineral glaze. Rain gutters have cut channels through masonry and then filled those channels with fresh stone. Erosion and construction are no longer opposites here. The city wears away and rebuilds itself through the same process.

Much of its machinery still operates.

It does not operate noisily. The great industrial age of the basin seems to have passed beyond clamor into maintenance. Pumps turn deep underground, felt more often than heard. Pressure doors breathe open when reservoirs reach certain levels. Along the terraces, articulated sluices lift and pour dark water from one basin to another. Condensers tighten with a sequence of faint metallic clicks. Far off, once every hour or perhaps once every several hours, a turbine engages with a low note that travels through foundations rather than air.

Nothing supervises these systems in any visible way. Gauges tremble behind clouded glass. Painted arrows direct absent workers toward galleries sealed by mineral crust. Factory halls contain rows of hollow frames where machines have been removed, or where they were never installed. Conveyor belts cross bare floors and move slowly enough that a dropped object might take a day to reach the other side. In some buildings, pistons continue to rise and fall over empty molds, pressing nothing into nothing.

Water organizes the whole landscape. It moves through the city in a complicated vertical cycle: steam ascends from fissures, collects in towers, condenses onto cooling fins, spills into elevated aqueducts, and descends through cisterns and industrial baths before returning underground. After heavy condensation, the streets flood ankle-deep. The water is clear at first, but quickly clouds with dissolved metals. It flows downhill in broad, nearly silent sheets, reflecting lamps and bridge undersides. By morning—or what passes for morning—it has vanished through thousands of circular drains, leaving white tide lines on every wall.

There are stranger flows as well. On certain nights, droplets climb.

They gather on the undersides of bridges and creep upward along pillars in trembling chains, drawn by pressure changes in the buried system. At the highest points they detach into the fog and disappear. Old public notices depict this event with simple diagrams and advise citizens not to touch rising water. The lettering has been scoured away, but the warning symbol remains: a hand beneath an inverted rain cloud.

The basin opens toward the east through a monumental spillway. Beyond it, the land falls in a sequence of dark plateaus, each carrying fragments of the wider industrial region—cooling fields, abandoned extraction pits, isolated pumping stations, long straight canals gleaming in the dimness. The horizon is spacious but never clear. Vapor drifts between the plateaus, and geothermal vents send up columns that merge with the cloud deck. At intervals, a line of electrical discharge moves silently through the mist, briefly outlining distant infrastructure in violet.

Life, if it can be called that, is sparse and difficult to classify. In sheltered condensers, translucent films form over standing water. They have no green pigment and no stable shape. Under heat they divide into hexagonal patches; when cooled, they dissolve and reappear elsewhere in the same basin. Whether they are organisms, crystals, or a by-product of the machinery is unresolved.

Larger creatures are rare. One may sometimes see a long, pale animal crossing a plaza at the edge of visibility. It walks on four narrow limbs and pauses over drain mouths, lowering its head as if listening into the pipes. It has no obvious eyes. Its skin reflects the lamps like wet porcelain. These animals never appear in groups, leave only temporary impressions in the mineral slurry, and retreat before the periodic releases of steam. Deep inside the spillway, small black fliers cling upside down to warm concrete. They take wing only during electrical weather and seem to navigate by the vibration of substations.

The city’s emptiness does not feel abandoned in the ordinary sense. It feels deliberate, as though vacancy were one of its engineering requirements. Doors are large, halls are wide, and platforms extend hundreds of meters without shelter or furnishing. A person standing in one of the main squares becomes the only upright, moving object in a field of wet stone. Their footsteps travel a short distance and are swallowed by vapor. Even echoes seem scarce.

There are rules the place enforces without explaining them. Water may be followed downhill, but never to its final outlet; the channels divide faster than they can be mapped. A tower entered at street level can sometimes be exited on a lower terrace that overlooks the roof by which one entered. Bridges lengthen in fog—not infinitely, but enough to make crossing times unreliable. Machinery that is watched closely appears idle, while unattended machines change position. Painted numbers on the cisterns correspond neither to capacity nor sequence, yet the water always reaches them in numerical order.

Heat keeps everything near transformation. Metal expands and contracts. Vapor becomes rain and rain becomes stone. Pavement buckles, relaxes, and settles again. New mineral curtains descend from ceilings while old ones crack free and shatter in empty vestibules. Pressure moves invisibly beneath every district. The city is always active, but its activity has the patience of geology.

At the darkest hour, the lamps dim together. Pumps cease. The climbing droplets hang motionless along the bridges. For several minutes the basin becomes almost perfectly quiet.

Then, from far under the central terraces, comes a single immense impact—not an explosion, but the sound of something heavy being set into place.

Steam begins to rise again. Water beads on the walls. The conduits fill one after another, and the empty city resumes its work.
