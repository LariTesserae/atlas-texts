The place is called the **Low Garden**, though it lies inside no recognizable country.

From a distance it resembles a shallow depression in a salt field: an oval of dark green sunk below a white, level waste. Only when one approaches does the salt reveal its seams. The “field” is made from fitted slabs, each roughly dressed with stone tools and laid so tightly that even dust has difficulty entering the joints. Chisel marks remain on every surface. Some are fresh enough to catch the light.

There is no natural ground.

The rim of the garden is a low parapet of pale ceramic blocks, enclosing an area perhaps two hundred paces long. Beyond it, the constructed flats continue to an uncertain horizon. They do not erode, accumulate dunes, or bear tracks for long. At midday the horizon sometimes draws closer, making the garden seem like an island at the bottom of an immense empty room. At other times there is no visible horizon at all—only warm brightness standing upright around the parapet.

Steps descend into the oval. They are broad, irregular, and newly sharp. None has been rounded by passage despite the many clawed feet that use them. Each step is assembled from dozens of small tiles: fired clay, black glass, chalky lime, shell-like laminates, hammered copper no thicker than leaves. The technology visible in their making is simple. Everything has been cut, pinched, woven, fired, beaten, knotted, or stacked by hand. There are no saw marks, screws, poured beams, or machine-perfect curves. Yet the workmanship extends everywhere, down through every layer. Lift a tile and there is another decorated tile beneath it; under that, a lattice of finger-thick ceramic rods; under that, a packed bed of carved beads. There is no bedrock to reach.

The bottom is low, warm, and crowded.

At first it seems lush in an ordinary way. Dense fans of green rise waist-high between paths. Crimson runners lace the walls. Trees no taller than a person arch over one another to form dim galleries. Their leaves are wide, glossy, and abundant, overlapping so closely that very little of the fabricated soil can be seen.

But nothing here contains water.

The plants are not quite plants. Their roots penetrate meshes of porous pottery and draw up a warm, volatile mineral oil that circulates beneath the garden in sealed capillaries. Their tissues are built from waxes, resins, flexible salts, and thin membranes of carbon. A cut stem does not bleed sap. It exhales a brief amber vapor, and the severed surfaces soften, press themselves together, and seal.

Many leaves are cool to the touch despite the warmth. They regulate themselves by opening microscopic scales and releasing fragrant gases: camphor, pepper, hot clay, bitter orange, singed hair. The garden’s air is dense with these traces, though not unpleasantly so. Breathing here feels like entering a room where incense was burned several days ago.

There are blossoms everywhere, but they open slowly. A single flower may take a month to unfold. Their petals resemble lacquer, mica, folded leather, beaten gold, or translucent horn. Some blossoms are assembled rather than grown. Tiny leaf-colored animals arrive carrying flakes of material, place them around a bud, and remain to become its outer petals. Whether the flower commands them, feeds them, or merely remembers them into position is unclear.

The garden is full of animals.

They seldom make noise.

Small six-legged browsers move through the lower thickets, nibbling waxy growth with flat ceramic teeth. Their bodies are furred in gray, green, and saffron. When startled, they flatten along the paths and become indistinguishable from woven mats. Pale lizards cling to the underside of leaves. Beetles carry little flames under their wing cases, though the flames give no heat. Long-legged creatures like delicate deer step between the dwarf trees, lowering narrow masks to graze on aromatic crusts.

Above, flights of paper-thin animals pass from branch to branch without wingbeats. Their motion is more like being handed through the air. One branch releases them; another receives them. Between those points they are briefly absent, though their shadows continue across the ground.

Predators are present as well, but pursuit is rare. A black, low-bodied hunter may spend ten days approaching a browsing animal across a distance of six paces. The prey notices and continues feeding. Both become subtly older during the approach. Sometimes, before the hunter arrives, the prey molts into a different species and is no longer edible. The hunter then lies down and slowly grows a shelter over itself.

Almost every hollow contains a nest, cocoon, burrow, hive, egg case, or resting body. Life is packed into the garden in layers: mites under scales, silver worms inside the ceramic trellises, soft colonies between the leaves, dormant animals folded into fruit. Yet there is little chatter. No dawn chorus rises. Wings make almost no sound. Footfalls are absorbed by the tiled paths.

The loudest ordinary noises are isolated ones: a seedpod clicking open; a copper leaf contracting in the evening; the dry cough of an animal shedding its old mouth. After such a sound, the quiet seems to gather around the place where it occurred.

There is normal daylight, warm and slightly golden. It falls from a sky that appears convincingly blue when looked at indirectly. Looking straight upward reveals the roof.

The roof is high enough to feel like weather. It is a vast shallow dome of limewashed wickerwork, supported by no visible columns. Its weave is crude and uneven. Handprints remain in the plaster. Here and there, pinholes show a blackness beyond, and through these pinholes sunlight enters in broad shafts far larger than the openings that admit it. The shafts drift throughout the day, but not in parallel. Some rise from the floor toward the roof. Some bend around trees. Animals use them as warm resting places.

The temperature changes little. It is always the warmth of late afternoon on sun-baked stone. At the garden’s lowest point, in a circular court, the air is warmer still. The court contains no pond or fountain. Instead there is a basin filled with thousands of smooth red pebbles, all gently rotating in place. They circulate heat through the garden’s buried channels. If one is removed, it becomes angular and cold. Another pebble somewhere in the basin immediately becomes smooth, and the total remains unchanged.

Paths coil through the vegetation, but their routes cannot be mapped consistently. They are bounded by low walls and unmistakable turns, yet a circuit of forty paces may enclose a grove wider than the entire garden. Two walkers taking opposite forks can remain visible to one another while arriving hours apart. A gate in the eastern wall opens into the western thicket. A narrow passage between trellises is impassable in one direction but roomy in the other.

Distance here is not measured by length alone. It is also measured by attention.

Anything watched continuously remains nearby. Anything forgotten, even briefly, may withdraw into the deeper garden. This is how the place accommodates so much abundance within its small boundary. Nests, trees, courts, herds, and entire warm glades fit between adjacent tiles, provided no observer tries to see them all at once.

The inhabitants have adapted. Grazers travel in pairs, each remembering the other. Beetles leave chains of scent to pin their routes in place. Certain vines grow eye-shaped leaves around valued clearings. The black hunters close one eye at a time, allowing prey to recede only gradually.

There are structures among the growth: huts, shrines, bridges, walls within walls. They are made from the same limited means as everything else. Bent branches are lashed with gut. Clay figures are stacked into pillars. Roofs are shingled with shells, leaf-metal, and flat animal scales. No structure is large, but some have interiors that descend through dozens of rooms.

The builders are difficult to identify. There are no obvious people, no central settlement, and no tools lying idle. Yet repairs continue. A cracked wall acquires a clay brace overnight. A fallen tile is reset, with a thumbprint pressed into its wet surface. Trellises extend by one rung every few years. Occasionally a hooded, knee-high figure can be seen in the vegetation, carrying a basket or mallet. If approached, it resolves into several animals walking close together. If ignored, it may finish a doorway.

Nothing in the Low Garden is truly wild. The animals have joints tied with microscopic knots. The trees bear maker’s marks beneath their bark. The air itself passes through woven screens hidden in the dome. Even decay is crafted: dead matter is disassembled by pale, meticulous insects and sorted into pigments, fibers, oils, salts, and reusable teeth.

Nor is anything truly artificial, because there is no contrasting nature against which artifice could be judged. Construction is the local form of growth. Birth is a kind of assembly. Evolution proceeds by repair, imitation, and mistakes in copying.

The garden appears young. Its edges are crisp; its colors have not faded. Some sections are visibly unfinished. At the southern end, vegetation stops abruptly along a line of blank tiles. Beyond it stand skeletal frames awaiting walls or organisms. Half-made birds hang from cords, each with one wing and an open cavity where its song should be. Rows of unfired eggs dry in the warmth.

This unfinished margin advances very slowly. Perhaps once in a generation, another tile darkens into soil, another frame clothes itself in leaves, another silent animal wakes and steps down. The garden is still being made, though no first day can be located. The oldest shrine contains a relief depicting the garden already complete. The newest wall bears fossils.

At evening the light does not dim so much as become less certain. Colors remain visible, but shadows detach and settle separately beneath the trees. Animals return to them as if to nests. The warm mineral circulation deepens underfoot. The red pebbles turn. Resinous leaves fold with a soft succession of taps.

Then, for a few minutes, the entire enclosed place seems to inhale.

The paths narrow. The dome lowers. Every animal looks toward a different center.

Nothing follows. The garden exhales, slowly, and resumes its patient construction in the quiet.
