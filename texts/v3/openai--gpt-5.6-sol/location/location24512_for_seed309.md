### The Underfoundry

The Underfoundry lies just beneath the surface, though nothing within it feels close to the upper world.

Its ceiling is a shallow crust of stone and compacted roots, sometimes no thicker than the height of a house. Through cracks comes neither daylight nor a view of the sky, only a diluted green-brown glow. At noon above, the cracks brighten to the color of old brass. At night they vanish. The chamber remains warm throughout, holding the day’s buried heat in its damp walls.

The place is not large. From the central hollow, one can hear activity at every boundary: the mills at one end, the hatchery thickets at the other, the groaning cistern roots below. Yet its scale is deceptive. Passages turn back into themselves. Chambers that appear adjacent may be separated by dense, impassable tissue. A straight route across the settlement rarely exists.

Everything is plant.

Not merely overgrown, not ruined and reclaimed. The paving is plant, the towers are plant, the conduits, bridges, walls, sluices, cranes, gates, and furnace housings are all differentiated portions of a single ancient colonial organism. Its oldest trunks have hardened into something like timber, bone, and ceramic at once. Their surfaces are polished by centuries of traffic. Where they have cracked, pale cambium shows beneath, slowly knitting the fractures closed.

The streets are broad intervals between these living structures. They feel sparse despite the totality of vegetation: open beds of dark mulch, bare root plazas, long galleries where only a few pillar-stems rise to meet the ceiling. Space has been cultivated as carefully as growth. Any shoot emerging in a traffic hollow is immediately grazed, clipped, or chemically discouraged. The city survives by keeping certain emptinesses empty.

Its buildings resemble orchard forms trained into industrial purposes. Branches have been bent and fused into gantries. Buttress roots make warehouses with vaulted interiors. Bladder-like fruits hang in rows as storage tanks, their skins marked by bands of mineral dye. Flexible vine belts run over knuckled wooden wheels. Elevators climb by the contraction of bundled tendrils. Great flower-mouths open and close over grinding chambers, their ridged petals crushing seed husks, beetle shell, stone salt, and fungal cakes into usable powder.

No flame is permitted. Heat comes from fermentation vats and muscular engines.

The largest of these engines occupy the Foundry Nave, a cavern no bigger than a village square. Six colossal seedpods stand there on rooted legs. Their chambers rhythmically inflate and collapse, drawing air through reed pipes buried in the walls. The pods breathe for the whole settlement. With each contraction, they drive pistons of hornwood into resin-lined cylinders. The motion powers mills, sap pumps, ventilation vanes, and the low-voltage electrical organs cultivated along the eastern gallery.

The electricity is weak but constant. Blue-white nodules blink in cages of root fiber. Telegraph creepers tense and release messages through the city. Sensitive leaves laid along the streets fold in sequence when a warning is sent. At the machinery terraces, copper-loving moss grows in precise circuits, its black filaments carrying current between acidic fruit cells. The lamps are colonies of luminous bacteria fed on measured drips of nectar. Their light is dim, green, and unreliable. When machinery draws heavily on the system, whole districts darken as if blinking.

Water is never abundant.

Rain reaches the Underfoundry intermittently, filtering down through soil and cracks long after storms have passed overhead. It arrives first as tapping in the ceiling, then as a sudden multitude of drops. Gutters unfold. Funnel leaves rotate toward leaks. Streets tilt almost imperceptibly as water-bearing tissues swell beneath them. Every surface shines for an hour.

Then the rain is gone.

The collected water is pulled into cistern fruits or sent through capillary pipes to hatcheries and cooling beds. Shallow pockets remain in hollows worn into the root floor, warm and brown with tannin. Condensation beads continually on the breathing pipes, but even this is harvested. Small silver mites patrol the reeds, brushing droplets into channels with fans on their forelegs.

Moisture therefore moves through the city as an event rather than a condition. A district may be dripping at dawn and papery dry by afternoon. Gates open and shut according to humidity. Bridges become rigid in dryness and slack in wet weather. Doors are leaves that can only be opened after misting. Certain stairways appear when their cells swell and flatten again when the air dries.

The whole settlement has been shaped by this pulse for longer than any inhabitant can reckon. Nothing is raw. Nothing looks newly excavated. The chamber walls are scalloped by old seepage and polished by generations of shells, feet, claws, and dragging cargo. Drainage channels have deepened into smooth gullies. The oldest structural roots sag in graceful arcs, having yielded a finger’s width each century until their present forms became stable.

Even the machinery is weathered into equilibrium. Wooden bearings do not squeal because their grooves have become exactly as deep as the loads require. Resin pipes bulge at places of recurrent pressure, reinforced by intentional scar tissue. Where a mill wheel once cut into the floor, the organism grew a matching basin around it. Industry has ceased to look imposed. It resembles anatomy.

The fauna are beyond counting.

They are the citizens, workforce, waste stream, livestock, weather, and danger of the Underfoundry. The air is seldom empty of wings. Midge clouds occupy the lamp halos. Thumb-sized moths sleep tiled across the ceilings, then descend in avalanches when the ventilation pods exhale. Beetles move through the streets in organized files, hauling clipped leaves, mineral grains, eggs, carcasses, and resin drums.

Different species attend different mechanisms. Long-legged crickets regulate the tension of vine belts by chewing them to prescribed thickness. Blind, velvet-furred rodents turn capstans in exchange for fermented pulp. Wasps maintain the city’s paper valves. Snails coat moving joints with mucus. Caterpillars feed on dedicated hedge walls and are themselves harvested for silk, fat, and chemical dyes.

None of them rules the place entirely.

Authority passes through mutual dependencies. The pollinators can halt fruit production. The root-borers can open or sever conduits. The mill beetles control stored meal. The wasps can close ventilation gates. Predatory mantises are tolerated around hatcheries because they keep fly numbers below suffocating levels, but they are killed if they begin taking trained moths. Tiny frogs inhabit the condensation cisterns and consume anything small enough to swallow; during wet weeks they multiply until the gutters seem to croak.

There are larger animals too, though the city has little room for them. Pale lizards bask against fermentation walls. Root-pigs nose through compost galleries, each fitted with a harness of woven stem so its digging can be directed. Once every few months a tunnel cat squeezes in from the outer soil, long-bodied and nearly hairless. Its arrival shuts down entire streets. Alarm leaves snap closed. Beetle columns scatter beneath the paving flaps. The cat feeds until it can no longer fit through the opening by which it entered, and then lives within the city as a temporary, ravenous god.

Death is rapid and public. A fallen animal is covered within minutes: first by flies, then mites, then beetles, then probing root hairs. Bones are softened in acidic pits and pressed into the structural wood. Shells are ground for mortar. Wings become filter material. Almost nothing remains recognizable by the following day.

The noise is tremendous.

The Foundry Nave produces a continuous, bodily thunder: pod engines inhaling, piston roots striking, fermentation chambers belching gas through liquid seals. Over this comes the chainlike rattle of beetle caravans, the metallic sawing of cicadas, the clack of seed counters, the slap of wet leaves, the squeal of overloaded vine pulleys. Wasps drum on paper walls to announce pressure changes. Frogs pulse from the gutters. Somewhere, nearly always, a predator causes a brief shriek to pass through the smaller creatures like wind through grass.

Conversation, where it exists, is conducted through vibration, scent, colored wing flashes, or direct contact. Sound is too crowded to carry subtle meaning. The city’s deepest warnings are transmitted through the floor. When the root organism senses collapse, fire above, poisoning, or prolonged drought, every structural column gives a single synchronized shudder. All other activity stops.

The silence after that shudder is the most frightening thing the Underfoundry can produce.

Its ecology is fast but not chaotic. Every hour changes the balance. Flowers open in timed districts, releasing perfumes that redirect traffic. Fungal chimneys vent spores and are immediately mobbed by harvesters. The warmest walls migrate as fermentation beds are filled and emptied, and whole populations follow them. Water, when it comes, triggers breeding, swelling, corrosion, germination, and sudden construction. New rooms can be grown within days, though they may take decades to harden.

The upper surface matters only as pressure and rumor. Rain. Heat. The bitter seep of some spilled chemical. The tremor of heavy feet. Occasionally a root probe reaches upward and returns with sunlight stored in sugar. No inhabitant sees the sky. To them, brightness is a substance made elsewhere and sent down through living pipes.

This is one of the Underfoundry’s central laws: nothing may remain singular.

A wall must also carry water. A light must attract pollinators. A predator must prune the workforce. Waste must become structure. A street must serve as a dry-season air channel and a wet-season canal. Even empty space is machinery, preserving airflow and giving the swarms somewhere to turn.

The city is ancient not because it resists change, but because it has made change repetitive. It swells, drains, molts, blooms, breeds, consumes, and repairs itself. Its boundaries shift by inches. Its inhabitants are replaced by the thousand. Its engines wear down and are eaten, then regrown from the same rooted foundations.

Above, the land may appear nearly barren: a low mound, a few vents disguised as thorny shrubs, warm air trembling over cracked soil.

Below, in the green-black dimness, the small city labors without pause—alive in every beam and gutter, sparse in its deliberate clearings, crowded past reason with wings and mouths, forever awaiting the next thin rain.
