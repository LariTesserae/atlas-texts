### The Crown Cistern

The place lies high, though there is no sky from which to judge its height.

It occupies the uppermost chambers of an immense constructed mound, a mountain assembled block by block and long ago drowned beneath a warm, lightless sea. The cistern itself is not vast: perhaps the size of a small hamlet folded inward, or a cathedral divided into many low rooms. Beyond its boundaries, the artificial mountain descends through miles of buried stairs, sealed courts, rubble-filled galleries, and retaining walls. Above it hangs the remaining ocean, black and heavy.

There is no natural ground. Every surface was placed.

The floor consists of broad ceramic slabs bedded over older courses of brick. The walls are fitted from dark, hand-dressed stone, though even the stone was brought here and shaped; no native outcrop shows through. Vaults rise on squat pillars, their capitals worn down to smooth, blunt masses. Narrow alleys connect circular chambers. Stairs climb toward ceilings and end against them, because later builders enclosed earlier entrances without removing the steps. Channels pass through rooms at shoulder height. Doors open beneath the floor. The whole place has the layered illogic of something built by generations who inherited plans they could no longer fully understand.

Its makers possessed little that would now be called machinery. The masonry bears the evidence of wedges, abrasives, lever holes, cord measurements, and countless blows from simple hammers. Some bricks preserve fingerprints beneath mineral varnish. On the underside of one arch, where erosion has not reached, there are shallow tally marks made by a pointed bone or copper awl. Whatever the scale of the greater mountain, it was raised by increments small enough for hands.

Water fills everything.

It is warm, almost bodily, and so constant in temperature that entering it would feel less like immersion than being admitted into another organism. There are no waves here. The ocean’s great movements arrive only as changes of pressure: a slow tightening in the ears, a current that bends loose feelers for half a day, a nearly imperceptible exhalation through the wall seams. Some passages breathe inward for years and then reverse.

The darkness is nearly complete. There are no windows, or none that still communicate with open water. Light occurs only in small living signs: a pale blue seam traveling along the body of an animal; a congregation of red pinpricks beneath an arch; a soft white pulse from deep inside a drainage shaft. These lights do not illuminate the architecture so much as disclose brief pieces of it—a rounded threshold, a field of old chisel scars, the face of a pillar—and then withdraw.

Nothing green has ever lived here. Nothing branches toward light or lays roots in sediment. The closest thing to a meadow is a low velvet of bacterial and fungal film over the warmest tiles, colorless except where it turns iron deposits black. It feeds on minerals seeping from the ceramics, on dissolved compounds carried upward through the mountain, and on the slow rain of dead bodies. In places the film grows into translucent crusts like old wax. Whether these colonies count as growth or merely a kind of chemical weather is difficult to decide.

Animal life, however, is abundant.

The chambers are crowded with it, though almost none of it moves quickly. Thin, lidless fish rest vertically between the pillars, their jaws held open to strain microscopic food from the water. Soft shelled things occupy every unused socket in the masonry. Long white grazers, jointed like fingers, travel over the walls so slowly that their paths are known mainly by the clean trails they leave through the dark film. Bell-shaped swimmers hang beneath the vaults in dense clusters, rising and falling a few inches over the course of an hour.

Some creatures have incorporated the ruin into their bodies. Small armored scavengers cement ceramic flakes across their backs. Worms draw dissolved metals from the water and build delicate coppery tubes in the old lever holes. A species of broad, flat animal fits itself over carved inscriptions and remains there for decades, feeding on whatever gathers in the grooves. When it finally moves, the covered writing is revealed in startling freshness.

The largest inhabitants are the shroud rays. They are almost perfectly black and only slightly darker than the water. Each one drifts through a circuit of several connected chambers, folding its wide body to pass between pillars. They may take an entire day to cross a room. Smaller animals settle on them as though they were moving floors. Their passage can be recognized by the gradual extinguishing of distant points of living light.

Despite the crowding, the cistern is quiet.

There is no surf, no wind, no rain striking roofs. Soft bodies touch stone without a sound. Occasionally a shell taps against a tile: one dry, impossibly distinct knock transmitted through the water. From somewhere below comes the intermittent grinding of sediment inside a constricted pipe. It may occur twice in a night, then not again for several months. The walls themselves sometimes issue a low ceramic tick as pressure redistributes through the ancient structure.

The loudest event is the collapse of a tile, and even that is subdued. A slab loosens, hangs for days in its bed of mineral film, and finally settles onto the floor in a brown cloud. Animals withdraw a hand’s breadth. Within hours they return. Within a century the broken edges are rounded. Within a millennium the fragment becomes indistinguishable from the accumulated sediment, which is itself nothing but the mountain ground down: brick dust, powdered glaze, lime, metal salts, shell, and the patient remains of its inhabitants.

Erosion has passed beyond ruin here. It has become refinement.

Every exposed corner has softened. Stairs are shallow undulations. Carved faces have lost their eyes and mouths, then their heads, and now resemble smooth stones set deliberately into the walls. In the central court stands a ring of columns reduced to waist-high domes. They look unfinished, but fragments buried below the floor show that they were once tall and elaborately inscribed. Water has edited them toward simpler forms.

Yet the place does not feel destroyed. Its age has settled too deeply for that. Collapse is no longer an interruption but one of the cistern’s ordinary, almost ceremonial processes. A wall bows over ten thousand years. A channel silts shut over twenty thousand. Animals discover another route. Warm water enters through a new crack. A chamber that was barren becomes crowded. Elsewhere, a colony dims and vanishes.

At the highest point is a shallow domed room called, by any imagined logic, the Crown. Its ceiling is close enough to touch. Set into it is a single circular plug of pale stone, polished by innumerable bodies passing beneath. On the far side of that plug may be open ocean, another flooded chamber, or simply more masonry rising into the dark. A faint downward seep circles its rim, carrying water colder than that of the cistern.

The animals gather there in unusual density. They hang layer above layer, barely moving: transparent bells, threadlike fish, jointed grazers, black rays resting on the floor. No one creature appears to dominate the assembly. There is no visible feeding frenzy, courtship, or migration. They simply converge beneath the sealed aperture and wait.

Once in a very long while, the plug shifts.

It lifts no more than the width of a fingernail. A line of colder water enters. The whole cistern experiences a slow inward current, and every living light goes out at once.

For several minutes there is absolute darkness and something close to absolute silence.

Then the plug settles. One blue point returns beneath an arch. Another answers from inside a pipe. Gradually the crowded rooms reveal themselves again—not as a single landscape, but as scattered portions of an old, handmade world, suspended high in the ocean and still being worn toward its final shape.
