Below the surface, the world opens rather than closes.

The descent passes through tight shafts worn slick by generations of hands, then abruptly releases onto a ledge above an immense buried basin. Its farther walls are visible only in fragments, interrupted by pale columns, terraces, and the suspended haze of mineral dust. The ceiling arches high enough to have weather. Across it, broad sheets of luminous stone shine with a cold, unwavering brightness. There is no sun here, yet almost nowhere is truly dark.

The light comes from seams of compressed salts threaded through the roof and from mats of glassy microorganisms inhabiting the moist cracks. They are not plants and do not spread in leaves or stems. They form thin, lucid skins—milk-white, blue at the edges—which brighten when pressed, heated, or disturbed by vibration. Tectonic strain is always moving through the basin. Thus the ceiling glows in wandering districts: one quarter of the cavern paling while another becomes brilliant, avenues and towers passing through slow, shadowless dawns.

On the basin floor stands a city made almost entirely from what erosion left behind.

It is ancient enough that construction and geology have become difficult to distinguish. Rounded buildings rise from rounded shelves; broad stairs descend into natural gullies; arcades continue the lines of old water channels. There are no sharp corners except where repairs are recent. Walls have been built from chalkstone slabs, black volcanic glass, fired clay, bone-colored calcite, and blocks of compacted salt. Mortar is made from lime and fibrous fungal residue. Metal is rare, appearing only as treasured bronze hooks, small blades, rivets, bells that no longer ring, and patches hammered over cracked cisterns.

The city is large but never crowded. Structures collect in widely spaced precincts with open mineral flats between them. A cluster of domed houses may occupy one terrace, then nothing for half a mile except marker stones and the pale tracks of animals. Farther on stands a market court, a quarry stair, a district of empty towers, and a long bridge crossing a chasm so shallow and broad that it resembles a crease in cloth. The scale is urban, but the density belongs to ruins, camps, and constellations.

No vegetation softens any surface. The public squares contain no trees. Courtyards hold raked gravel, arrangements of resonant stones, shallow condensation basins, or upright shards of obsidian. Roofs are bare except for gutters and nets. The rare colors are mineral: sulfurous yellow, iron red, manganese violet, wet limestone gray. Against these, the blue-white radiance of the ceiling makes everything appear freshly uncovered, though nearly everything has been worn for ages.

Water is present as an event rather than a landscape.

Most days the ground is dry beneath the foot, cool and faintly powdery. Moisture gathers overhead, condensing around luminous seams until drops grow heavy enough to fall. These isolated droplets can descend hundreds of feet. Each strikes with a tiny dark mark, raising a spot of scent from the dust. During certain changes in pressure, condensation becomes a fine rain beneath one section of roof while districts only a short distance away remain dry. Citizens watch the bright ceiling for dim halos, which announce where the next rain will form.

Water also travels through buried channels. It emerges unpredictably from stone mouths at the bases of old walls, fills a cistern or floods a street, and vanishes hours later into cracks. Every neighborhood keeps ceramic jars stacked near likely outlets. Channels are continually cleared with antler picks and stone hoes. No one claims a spring permanently, because the underground rivers change their courses too often. A dependable well is regarded not as property but as a temporary guest.

The city’s inhabitants have simple tools and elaborate knowledge. They quarry with stone wedges, fire pottery in low draft kilns, spin cord from the dried membranes of cavern fungi, and move heavy loads on sledges greased with animal fat. Their maps are not pictures of fixed streets. They are sequences of conditions: *when the eastern vault is bright, pass below the three pillars; when mist rises from the red floor, do not enter the lower arch; after the seventh tremor, the salt stair may be used.*

This is necessary because the basin is never still.

Its turbulence is mostly silent.

Floors buckle gradually and then, without warning, settle by the height of a hand. Salt walls slump like exhausted wax. Cavities open in plazas, exposing rooms from older levels of the city. Elsewhere, cracks close and crush alleys out of existence. A tower may lean farther each day for a month, then right itself during a distant tremor as though reconsidering. Streams burst through dry chambers and rearrange entire districts before draining away. Curtains of dust descend from the ceiling in graceful, soundless folds.

Noise does not travel normally here. The cavern stone is riddled with microscopic pores and soft mineral laminations that swallow vibration. A collapsing façade may produce only a low pressure in the chest and a brief trembling under the soles. Hammer blows become dull taps a few paces away. Speech is intimate by necessity. Across public spaces, people communicate with raised hands, polished signal plates, and shutters placed over luminous walls. Even emergencies are strangely quiet: running figures, flashing mineral panels, animals streaming from an opening, all beneath an almost complete hush.

The fauna are everywhere.

Small translucent grazers—though there is nothing vegetable for them to graze—move over damp stone, rasping up microbial films too thin for human eyes. Their bodies resemble pale commas with many soft feet. They gather by the thousands after a condensation rain, making the floor appear to ripple. Long-legged salt crabs inhabit cistern rims and abandoned pottery. Colorless mothlike creatures hang under arches, their wings mineral-stiff and faintly luminous. Blind ribbon fish appear in flooded streets, sometimes remaining behind in deep wheel ruts when the water withdraws.

Higher in the cavern, flocks of narrow-winged gliders pass from column to column. They navigate by changes in electrical tension rather than sound. Their undersides mirror the ceiling, so a flock is visible mostly as a moving interruption in brightness. Predators follow: lean, six-limbed animals with sealed eyes and broad facial pits sensitive to warmth. They hunt without cries and kill quickly. In settled districts they are held back by rings of cold ash and bitter salts, renewed nightly around sleeping chambers.

Many species depend on the city. Warm kilns attract soft-shelled burrowers. Cisterns support colonies of pearl-bodied swimmers. Refuse pits breed tiny armored scavengers, which in turn feed domestic hook-beaks kept in roofless pens. The inhabitants do not divide animals neatly into wild and tame. A creature is classified instead by what change it predicts. One species leaves the lower tunnels before flooding. Another gathers where the roof will soon shed stone. A sudden silence among the usually rustling cistern crabs means dangerous gas is entering the district.

The oldest structures stand toward the basin’s center, where erosion has brought everything close to a final shape. There, broad monuments have been reduced to low, smooth masses. Inscriptions survive only in protected hollows. Processional roads are shallow troughs, polished by feet, water, and drifting salt until they shine like old ivory. Some foundations are believed to predate the cavern’s present ceiling. Others may never have been built at all, but simply resembled buildings long enough for later people to add doors.

At the center lies the White Forum, an open expanse several miles across. Sparse pylons lean from its floor, each separated from the next by long distances of bare stone. During dry periods it is a blinding plain. During condensation storms, it becomes a shallow lake no deeper than an ankle, perfectly reflecting the luminous roof. The city then seems suspended between two bright vaults. Boats cannot use such thin water, so people cross on high-soled clay sandals, moving with exaggerated care among shoals of newly awakened animals.

The Forum is also the most unstable place. Mineral pressure beneath it raises transient ridges, terraces, and hollow domes. In a single night, a smooth square can become a field of waist-high folds. These formations may last an hour or a century. Builders respond quickly but without surprise. Steps are cut. Ropes are fixed. Market shelters are dismantled and reassembled on the new ground. If a fissure reveals an older street below, ladders descend before the edges have stopped steaming.

Thus the city does not resist change so much as accompany it. Buildings are deliberately unfinished. Doorframes are made to be removed. Roof stones rest uncemented on sliding courses. Household possessions fit into carrying baskets. A family may occupy seven houses in a lifetime without moving more than a few hundred yards, simply following habitable ground as walls tilt, moisture shifts, and light migrates.

Permanence belongs only to routes of escape, stores of water, and shared memory.

There are laws governing the unstable world. No one sleeps below a newly bright seam. No cistern is sealed completely, because trapped water must be allowed to depart. No fallen tower is rebuilt in the same shape. Animals fleeing an area are never obstructed. An exposed lower doorway must remain open for three days before anyone enters, in case the old air within is poisonous or remembers a different pressure.

Most importantly, no map may be carved in stone.

Stone suggests certainty, and certainty is dangerous here.

At the far edges of the basin, the bright roof slopes downward into low regions where the city thins to watch posts, kilns, burial shelves, and animal paths. Beyond them lie drowned passages, warm fault chambers, and older basins whose lights operate by unfamiliar rhythms. Occasionally travelers emerge from these margins carrying black glass tools, preserved shells, or stories of streets that grow upward from the floor already furnished with empty rooms.

Whether these reports are true hardly matters. The known city is strange enough: brilliant yet buried, populous with creatures yet bare of greenery, ancient yet continually being remade. Its great motions happen beneath silence. Its ruins are inhabited, its newest buildings already rounded by mineral weather, and its people wake each day to discover which parts of the world have survived the night.

Above them, unimaginably distant, rain may be falling on some other surface.

Here below, one drop separates itself from the radiant ceiling, hangs for a moment like a dark star, and begins its long descent toward the city.
