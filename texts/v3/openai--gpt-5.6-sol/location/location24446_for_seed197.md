The place occupies a shallow depression no larger than a village, though its boundaries are difficult to cross and harder to remember. From outside it resembles a low thicket rising from warm, dun-colored flats. There are no hills, only slight changes of level: old channels, root-lifted pavements, sunken courtyards where rain lingers for a day or two. Yet upon entering, the thicket opens into something unmistakably urban.

Streets run between walls. Alleys narrow, turn, and rejoin themselves. There are arcades, towers, cisterns, plazas, bridges, and roofed chambers. None were built with tools.

The city is one organism—or perhaps an agreement among many plants. Its foundations are mats of woody rhizomes packed so tightly that they resemble weathered stone. Trunks have grown in colonnades, evenly spaced without being cultivated. Their upper branches knit into vaults, while hanging roots descend as partitions and screens. Leaves overlap in broad shingles. Seedpods lock together along roof ridges. Where a wall has split, pale cambium swells from the fracture like fresh mortar.

Despite the totality of its vegetation, the city feels empty. Plant life does not clutter the streets. It defines them. A square may contain nothing but warm air and a single black basin. A hall may be thirty paces long and entirely bare between its living pillars. The growth is concentrated into surfaces—floors, walls, roofs—leaving large, improbable hollows inside. Moss does not soften every edge. Vines do not hang randomly. The plants have withdrawn from open volume as though obeying an ancient civic law.

The ground beyond the living pavement is low, silty, and faintly saline. It bears the signatures of long erosion: shallow fluting, exposed root ribs, smooth bowls where centuries of seasonal rain have turned fallen leaves in circles. Everything has reached a settled form. Nothing looks new, even while it grows.

Water arrives intermittently. Warm rain falls in abrupt, heavy curtains, sometimes from a clear sky. For a few minutes every roof pours through its designated spouts—hollow stems with flared mouths—and the city becomes a network of flashing channels. Plazas fill ankle-deep. Stairways become cascades. Black cistern flowers open at ground level and swallow the runoff with muscular contractions.

Then the rain stops. The channels empty almost at once, and the city returns to dampness rather than wetness. Moisture remains in pockets: inside cup-shaped leaves, beneath translucent bark, in deep intersection wells where threadlike roots drink slowly. At dawn, condensation gathers on the ceilings of the larger chambers and falls in widely spaced drops. Each drop is claimed before it reaches the floor. Fine stems bend toward it. Moths turn in flight. Small mouths open in the pavement.

The temperature is warm without being oppressive. The air has the enclosed warmth of a greenhouse after sunset, carrying odors of green sap, mineral mud, fermenting fruit, and the peppery exhalation of disturbed leaves. Sunlight enters normally—gold in the morning, white at noon, red toward evening—but behaves strangely after touching the city. Shadows sometimes remain where an animal stood. Sunbeams bend at intersections. An open plaza may hold late afternoon while the alley beside it has already entered night.

The sound is overwhelming.

It begins before the boundary is crossed: a continuous pressure like a waterfall heard from inside the skull. Within the streets it separates into countless sources. Cicadas pulse in synchronized districts. Beetles hammer seed husks from inside the walls. Frogs boom from the cisterns. Broad leaves clap against one another though there is scarcely any wind. Hollow trunks amplify the footfalls of lizards and mice until each sounds like something enormous moving behind a partition.

The plants themselves contribute. Sap surges through vascular columns with the roar of underground rivers. Seed chambers crack open like snapping timber. Root systems grind slowly beneath the streets. At intervals, whole avenues exhale through thousands of stomata at once, producing a long, hoarse chord. The sound is not merely loud; it occupies space. Certain notes form invisible barriers. A deep drone may make an alley impossible to enter, while a higher tone opens a passage through what had seemed a solid wall.

Silence is the rarest and most dangerous event here. When one district abruptly falls quiet, predators freeze, flowers close, and every leaf turns its underside outward. The silence then moves through the city like a transparent animal. Whatever it passes over loses a small property of existence: color, weight, direction, sometimes memory. When the noise resumes, the missing thing may return elsewhere. Green lizards emerge blue. Fruit falls upward into the canopy. An avenue remembers being a river and becomes briefly full of fish.

Fauna is abundant, though seldom visible all at once. The city’s emptiness is crossed by flickers: geckos with fan-shaped toes, flights of metallic flies, transparent frogs tucked into drainage mouths. Ants travel in formal lanes beneath the bark, tending scale insects in chambers that resemble granaries. Palm-sized crabs leave the wells after rain and climb the walls to graze on lichen. Long-legged birds patrol the plazas, striking at prey hidden under the pavement. Their calls bounce between the root facades and return in altered voices.

Larger animals move through the architecture more cautiously. A lean, spotted cat sleeps in the warm hollow beneath a bridge. Arboreal grazers with split lower jaws prune the inner roofs. Pale constrictors lie along cornices, indistinguishable from vines until the vines flower and the snakes do not. Some creatures appear to be temporary coalitions of smaller lives: a dark animal crossing a square may disperse at the far side into a flock of beetles, several mice, and a ribbon of spores.

Predation is constant but strangely civic. Hunts tend to follow the streets. Territorial displays occur in plazas. Certain intersections are neutral ground, recognized by every species. At one such crossing, insects, birds, reptiles, and small mammals gather during the hottest hour and drink condensation from the same low leaf without attacking one another. The truce lasts only while the leaf remains in shadow.

There are districts, although their positions shift.

The Reservoir Quarter is cool and dark, its rooms descending into mud-lined cavities. The water there reflects not the ceiling above but weather yet to come. Tadpoles gather beneath images of future storms.

The Orchard Courts are brighter. Their walls produce heavy fruits shaped like bells, windows, or sleeping faces. When ripe, the fruits drop and split, releasing winged seeds and a brief scent of distant places. Animals eating them may vanish for several hours and return wet with seawater, dusted with snow, or carrying pollen from plants that do not exist in the city.

At the center stands the Bell Tower, the highest structure, though it rises only a little above the canopy. It is composed of six braided trunks around a hollow shaft. No bell hangs inside. Instead, a colony of immense cicadas lives in chambers along the upper bark. At noon they call together. The tower flexes, the streets tremble, and every loose object lifts a finger’s width from the ground. During this daily resonance, doors appear in otherwise seamless walls. Most open into ordinary chambers. Some open into the same room from which they are viewed. One reveals a night sky beneath the city.

No inhabitants govern this settlement. There are no tools, fires, wheels, inscriptions, or worked stone. Its order arises biologically: chemical signals passed through roots, pressure changes in hollow stems, fungal messages spreading beneath the floor. Damage is repaired. Routes are widened when migration increases. Chambers close around persistent parasites. New courtyards bud at the margins after unusually wet seasons, while old quarters are digested from below.

The city seems ancient, but age cannot be counted reliably. Growth rings inside fallen branches contain miniature streets instead of concentric lines. Excavating beneath a plaza reveals older plazas in layers, each still alive and faintly sunlit. Seeds found in the deepest mud sometimes germinate into mature, weathered trees within minutes, already scarred by storms they have not yet experienced.

There are rules, though they are learned only by consequence.

Never follow the loudest street; it leads inward, regardless of direction.

Do not step twice on a root that moves away from you.

When rain begins beneath a roof, remain there until the sky also becomes wet.

If an animal has no shadow, allow it to pass. It is carrying yours.

And if the whole city goes silent, close your eyes. Sight is one of the things the silence most often relocates.

Toward evening, the activity intensifies. Flowers open in the pavement. Bats pour from seedpods beneath the eaves. Warm mist rises from the cisterns and fills the lower streets to knee height. Predators call, prey answer with false locations, and the walls alter their pores to favor one scent over another. The city’s deafening chorus thickens until no single creature can be distinguished from rainfall, root strain, wingbeat, or echo.

From outside, however, the depression still looks like a modest thicket on an almost empty plain.

The sound seems to come from far underground.

And sometimes, after sunset, the entire green city is absent. In its place lies only a shallow patch of damp earth patterned with streets too small for an ant. By morning the towers stand again, dripping condensation, their old scars exactly where they were—except for one new alley at the edge, open and waiting, shaped to the dimensions of whoever saw the city disappear.
