The city lies inside a fold of the floodplain, no larger than a courtyard and almost impossible to find from the open country around it.

A low rim of silt encloses it. From outside, the rim resembles an ordinary rise beneath sparse warm grass: a place where flood debris has snagged, where a few broad-leaved plants stand apart in the heat. Only at one point does the ground loosen into a narrow passage between roots. Beyond that passage the land drops by the height of a hand, and the hidden settlement appears.

It is not a city made by hands.

Hundreds of small structures rise from the damp basin: mud turrets, hollow stems, woven chambers, leaf-roofed cavities, and low galleries cast from clay. None is much taller than a reed, but together they have the organization of dense habitation. Paths divide and rejoin. Drainage channels curve between neighborhoods. Root buttresses form walls and precincts. Small plazas open around shallow pools of rainwater. There are tiers, underways, nurseries, refuse hollows, hunting grounds, and cool interior vaults.

The builders are animals, fungi, roots, and water acting without a common plan.

Termites raise the oldest towers, though even these are young, their clay still dark and faintly glossy. Burrowing crabs excavate beneath them and heap fresh pellets along the lanes. Solitary bees seal round doors in sun-warmed walls. Frogs occupy abandoned chambers near the pools. Caterpillars draw neighboring leaves together with silk, making green awnings overhead. Fungal threads bind loose soil into soft, pale crusts, while roots continually push up through floors and redirect whole streets.

Nothing here has yet been worn down. The basin is too new for ruin.

It formed during the last season of overflow, when a slow sheet of river water laid down fine sediment behind a tangle of fallen branches. The water withdrew without cutting gullies or polishing stones. There are no old cliffs, no weathered faces, no rounded pavements. Every surface seems to be arriving. Clay bulges upward in moist seams. Seedlings split the ground cleanly. New chambers appear overnight as neat crescents of excavated earth. Walls slump, but even collapse looks constructive: a fallen turret becomes a dam; a dam catches humus; humus becomes a bed for moss and germinating seed.

The rawness gives the place a strange softness. Its terrain has not decided what it will be.

The basin floor alternates between firm, flour-fine silt and shallow depressions holding tea-colored water. Moisture is present everywhere but plentiful nowhere. It gathers as condensation under leaves, beads along silk, and darkens the entrances of burrows. Rain comes in short warm visitations. A shower may fill every plaza and channel for an hour, turning the city into an archipelago; by afternoon most standing water has sunk away, leaving only pockets beneath root arches and in the deepest clay cups.

Because the place is low and enclosed, the air remains close. It smells of wet mineral earth, bruised green leaves, fungal skin, and the faint animal sharpness of crowded nests. Warmth is held under the canopy even after sunset. There is little wind at ground level. What movement enters from above arrives as a slow shifting of dim green light.

Vegetation is lush in texture but sparse in arrangement. A few plants dominate rather than forming a continuous thicket. Their leaves are large, widely spaced, and sufficient to roof most of the basin. Between them are open tracts of bare clay, black liverwort, and thin moss. Seedlings stand alone like planted columns. Ferns occupy only the wettest margins. One creeping vine has crossed the entire settlement, but it touches the soil at long intervals, leaving loops beneath which the smallest paths continue.

The resulting city is spacious by the measure of its inhabitants. Empty ground separates one tower district from another. A beetle may cross a broad silent square before reaching the next shadow. The pools have clear margins. No growth fully chokes the channels, and no colony controls the whole basin.

Its life is abundant without being loud.

Most sounds are too small to carry beyond the enclosing rim: the papery adjustment of a leaf; the dry tick of a seed striking clay; mandibles working inside a wall; water slipping from one root hair to another. A frog’s throat pulses without calling. Tiny wings make brief pockets of vibration and then stop. Beneath the ground there is constant activity—tunneling, feeding, eggs being moved away from damp chambers—but the soil absorbs it all.

At long intervals, something larger enters. A ground bird steps through the root passage and becomes, for a few minutes, a towering disaster. Its toes breach galleries. Its beak opens sealed rooms in search of larvae. Then it leaves, and the city resumes. Workers close the ruptures with new clay. Crabs widen alternate routes. Flies descend to the exposed interiors. Nothing announces recovery; the broken district simply changes shape.

The rules of this place are simple and local. Water is both weather and architecture. Shade determines ownership. Any hollow may become a reservoir, any reservoir a nursery, any nursery a hunting pool. Walls belong to their builders only while they are occupied. Once abandoned, they pass immediately into common ecology. There are no monuments and no permanent boundaries. Structure persists, but purpose migrates.

By evening the basin grows very dim. The open sky remains bright beyond the rim, yet little of it reaches the floor. Moist surfaces hold the last light as dull silver flecks. Moths begin to visit pale flowers high above the streets. Small geckos descend the stems and wait beside busy openings. Mosquitoes hover over the remaining water, and bats pass unseen overhead, their presence indicated only by abrupt absences among the insects.

Then condensation begins.

Droplets collect beneath the broad leaves until their weight bends the tips. Each fall is a local event: a roof struck, a pool enlarged, a wall softened, a sleeping insect washed into another quarter. The city quietly rearranges itself around these impacts.

By dawn there will be fresh towers on the bare ground, new fungus along the flooded lanes, and a scatter of wings where the geckos hunted. A root will have lifted one district by a fraction. A clay arch will have sealed itself through swelling. The basin will be more complete than it was and no closer to finished.

It is an urban place before the invention of tools: a warm, dim, living settlement assembled from instinct, growth, rainfall, and the unfinished intentions of mud.
