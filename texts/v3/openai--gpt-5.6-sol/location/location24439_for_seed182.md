The place lies only a little way below the surface, though it feels much deeper. A roof of black stone hangs over it: not the softened ceiling of an ancient cave, but a fresh fracture, angular and glittering, its plates still seeming to fit the broken earth above. Drill marks remain in some faces. Elsewhere the rock has split on its own into narrow, glass-edged shelves. Heat seeps through every seam.

The whole hollow is no larger than a cathedral precinct or a tightly walled hamlet. It is difficult to see its boundaries because nearly every span has been occupied. Buildings press against buildings, stairs climb over pipes, pipes bend around roots, and roots stitch themselves through retaining walls. There are no open squares. The nearest thing to a street is a warm passage between tiled tanks, wide enough for one cart or two people turned sideways. Above it hang cables, ducts, drying racks, and leathery curtains of leaves. Below its iron grating run older passages filled with black condensation and slow machinery.

The city was made in a hurry, but has since learned patience.

Its lower structures are brick, dark red where dry and almost purple where damp. The brickwork stands on freshly cut basalt ledges and is reinforced with riveted bands of pale metal. Higher rooms have been added in whatever materials could tolerate the heat: ceramic panels, slabs of volcanic glass, woven mineral fiber, old boiler skins. Many walls are double-walled so that hot air can rise between them. Chimneys do not lead upward to the surface; they curl sideways, merge, and descend toward cooling chambers. From a distance the settlement resembles the dense interior of some mechanical organ, every component nested against another and nothing quite separable from the whole.

There are engines, but they are mostly quiet.

They operate on thermal differences too slight for haste. Broad pistons move once every few minutes. Flywheels turn at the speed of shadows. Pressure vessels swell with a barely visible pulse, absorbing heat from the surrounding stone and releasing it into long ceramic veins. A person can stand beside a machine for some time before realizing it has moved. The loudest industrial sound is often a single relay closing somewhere behind a wall: one dry click, followed by silence.

Manufacture happens by accumulation rather than impact. Metals are grown through electroplating in warm baths. Clay tiles cure in hot crevices without kilns. Mineral salts crystallize around suspended frames, building intricate machine parts grain by grain. Fibers are soaked, pressed, and left beneath weighted plates for weeks. Nothing is forged if it can be persuaded into shape. Nothing is pumped quickly if it will rise on its own.

The air is hot enough that metal handrails are wrapped in cork and braided root. Breathing feels thick, though not quite difficult. The settlement smells of wet brick, hot dust, leaf resin, copper, and the faint medicinal sweetness of fungal cultures. Moisture is never abundant, yet it is present everywhere in small forms. Condensation pearls under pipes. Rain from the world above arrives unpredictably through hairline faults, first as cool pinpricks, then as several hours of dripping. Every drop is intercepted. Leaves tilt beneath leaks. Stone gutters no wider than a finger lead into sealed jars. Cloth tongues wick dampness out of cracks. Beneath certain staircases are communal cisterns, shallow and black, with ceramic lids to prevent evaporation.

In dry intervals, which may last many weeks, the city seems to hold its breath. Green surfaces become dull and folded. Moss retracts into mortar joints. The few channels cease running, leaving bright lines of salt. Then, without warning, water begins tapping on the roof. Panels open. Cups appear under eaves that have never seen a sky. The vegetation unfurls so rapidly that by the following day some corridors have narrowed by half.

There is a great deal of plant life, but almost none of it resembles a forest. There is no room for trunks. Instead the city supports mats, curtains, fans, coils, and densely layered skins of green. Black-leaved vines cling to hot masonry, using the warmth rather than sunlight. Pale fronds spread around electric lamps. Fleshy reservoirs grow in wall niches, each plant swollen with a private ration of water. Their surfaces are dusted in silver hairs that catch condensation. In the hotter workshops, red algae inhabit transparent cooling tubes and turn the circulating water the color of diluted blood.

The largest plants are the ceiling gardens. Their roots grip the fractured roof and send down hundreds of narrow cords, some living, some hardened into woody cables. Leaves cluster at the lower ends, near lamps, cooking steam, and human breath. From certain angles the gardens appear inverted: a jungle growing downward from darkness, its canopy at head height and its roots hidden overhead.

The citizens prune paths through it rather than clearing it away. Growth is part of the infrastructure. Root bundles brace unstable stone. Vine skins insulate boilers. Fungal felt filters dust from air shafts. A species of broad, translucent leaf is cultivated over lamps, softening their glare and converting harsh white light into a dim green-gold radiance. Consequently, nearly all illumination seems submerged. Faces emerge from it in fragments—an eye, a cheekbone shining with sweat, the pale line of a scar—then sink back into shade.

True darkness occupies the spaces above the lamps and beneath the grates. It is not empty darkness. Things continue there: cooling water, deep roots, the slow exchange of gases, mineral growth.

Animals are rare. The heat, confinement, and careful accounting of water leave little margin for them. Tiny ash-colored moths sometimes gather beneath the leaf-shaded bulbs. Blind threadworms live in cistern sediment and are regarded as evidence that the water remains healthy. There are stories of a single lizard moving through the high pipes, seen years apart in different districts, always larger and always white. No one has caught it. No droppings have been found. Some believe it is not an animal at all but a length of insulating cloth carried gradually through the ducts by thermal expansion.

Pollination is performed by the city. At prescribed intervals, workers pass along the green corridors with soft brushes and small bellows, carrying pollen between flower chambers. They wear masks, not because the pollen is poisonous, but because some flowers bloom only in air recently breathed by no one. Those chambers are sealed for a day before being opened. Inside, the petals have rearranged themselves toward the door.

The settlement has little weather, yet it keeps seasons. They are determined by stone temperature, moisture, and electrical demand. There is the Sweating Time, when the roof drips though no rain has fallen. There is the Tight Time, when heat makes doors swell and metal joints lengthen, closing some passages while opening others. There is the Pale Bloom, when fungi sheet over cooling walls and workers scrape them into food vats. These seasons do not occur in a reliable order. Sometimes one district enters a season while the next, only a few rooms away, remains in another.

This is partly because heat does not travel normally here.

It pools in corners. It descends certain stairs. A sealed cabinet may be hotter inside than the boiler beside it, though no pipe connects them. Engineers chart these anomalies on ceramic maps, but the maps age quickly. Heat routes migrate at a rate of perhaps a handspan per year, and buildings are altered around them. An inexplicably cool patch becomes a pantry, then a bedroom, then—when it warms decades later—a nursery for heat-loving vines. Property is therefore measured not only in floor area but in gradients: a household may own the right to seven degrees of warmth along a particular wall.

Sound behaves strangely as well. The packed construction should make the city noisy, yet most vibrations are swallowed by root felt, layered brick, hanging vegetation, and the soft mineral deposits coating every surface. Conversation does not travel far. Footsteps become dull within a few paces. A dropped tool may strike the floor without echo.

But quiet sounds occasionally emerge at great distances. A whisper made beside one warm pipe can be heard days later from a cooling vent across the settlement, altered by its passage into a low, breathy murmur. The inhabitants call these returns. Most are meaningless. Some repeat fragments of old instructions: close the western valve; bring three cups; wait for the green light. No one knows whether the pipes preserve sound or merely generate phrases from pressure and coincidence. Either way, it is considered discourteous to answer.

At the center—if the crowded hollow can be said to have a center—is a shaft called the New Well. It is not deep. Its walls are sharp, raw, and scarcely stained, having opened only within living memory. The shaft contains no standing water, only a slow upward draft that smells of rain. Around its rim, the settlement is denser than anywhere else. Balconies overlap. Pumps and gauges crowd the masonry. Every available surface supports a jar, a coil of hose, a patch of edible lichen, or a shrine made from obsolete machine parts.

Once or twice a year, droplets rise from the Well.

They form on the stone below, detach, and drift upward through the shaft, cool against the air, then settle on the undersides of leaves. The event lasts for several hours and produces less than a barrel of water in total. Nevertheless, the entire city gathers as close as it can. Engines are disengaged. Work lamps are dimmed. No one speaks. In the near silence, the upward rain makes a soft ticking among the leaves.

Afterward, the collected water is not drunk. It is divided among the newest walls, one spoonful pressed into each seam. The builders say this keeps the city from becoming old too quickly.

Perhaps it does. The place remains oddly young despite its layers. Edges stay sharp. Fresh cracks appear without widening. Mineral skins grow over machines but not over the raw faces of the cavern. Buildings weather inward rather than outward: rooms gradually become smoother, darker, and more humid, while their exteriors remain angular and unfinished. Demolition is uncommon because there is nowhere to put the debris. Instead, unwanted structures are enclosed within new ones. A storeroom becomes the foundation of a kitchen; the kitchen is wrapped by a cooling tower; the tower becomes a trellis. There are sealed doors in many houses, not leading outside but into previous versions of the same house.

Change is therefore constant but nearly imperceptible. A stair tilts by a fraction each year. A machine’s product thickens. A garden lowers from the ceiling. A route once used daily becomes too narrow for shoulders, then too dense for hands, and is finally surrendered to roots.

Nothing here has room to vanish. It can only be covered, repurposed, absorbed, or remembered by the pipes.
