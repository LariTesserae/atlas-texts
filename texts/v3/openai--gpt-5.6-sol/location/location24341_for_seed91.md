Thirty metres beneath the tundra lies a cultivated hollow the size of a valley.

It is not a cave in the old sense. The walls are too straight in places, the shelves too level, the fractures too pale and sharp. Rotary cutters opened the first chambers only a few decades ago, following a shallow seam of soft stone. The excavation widened into a chain of immense rooms connected by low throats and vehicle ramps. Seen from one of the upper galleries, the main chamber resembles an underground plain: a dim floor extending beyond the reach of sight, interrupted by columns left to support the roof.

Nearly everything exposed has since been claimed by plants.

The floor is covered in a continuous, low-growing fabric of moss, winter rye, dwarf clover, sorrel, and thick-leaved fodder plants bred for cold and weak light. Roots do not descend into ordinary soil. They occupy compressed mats of fiber laid over the stone, or run through shallow channels filled with chipped bark and mineral grit. The greenery is only a handspan deep across much of the plain, giving the place its peculiar combination of abundance and emptiness. There is plant life everywhere, but little stands tall. Long distances remain visually open.

Where the roof rises, cable-trained vines climb toward the lamps. They hang in broad, widely spaced curtains, each one rooted in a cistern and spread over steel mesh. In older sections, the mesh can no longer be seen. Stems have thickened around it, incorporating braces, pipes, and access ladders into living walls. Some corridors are supported as much by bundled willow trunks as by construction. Their pale bark shines faintly with condensation.

Light comes from narrow industrial bars fixed high overhead. They burn in dull bands of violet, red, and exhausted white. To human eyes the illumination is twilight; to the selected plants it is sufficient. The lamps are separated by wide intervals, so the chamber alternates between murky fields of foliage and brighter cultivation lanes where leaves turn upward in disciplined ranks. Emergency bulbs mark the distant galleries with amber points.

The cold is constant. It comes from the surrounding ground and from ventilation shafts opening far above on the surface. Breath clouds immediately. Leaves near the air intakes grow hard and compact, edged with frost. Ice forms on bolts, handrails, and the shaded sides of irrigation pipes, though the root beds themselves are kept just above freezing by warm return water from the machinery.

Water is present but never plentiful. Nothing flows freely for long. Condensation gathers on the roof, creeps along the fresh drill scars, and falls in heavy, widely spaced drops. Each impact makes a dark coin in the foliage. Shallow collection trenches cross the floor, leading to covered reservoirs where meltwater and runoff are filtered and pumped back into the growing mats. In neglected recesses, small black pools form between ridges of spoil. They may remain for a month, skin over with ice, then disappear when a pump cycle or dry season lowers the water table.

The loudness of the place is overwhelming.

Ventilation turbines run without pause, forcing air through kilometres of planted chamber. Their roar fills the stone and seems to come from every direction at once. Pumps beat below the floor in slow, powerful strokes. Conveyor chains hammer over rollers. Somewhere out in the dimness, trimming machines pass through the rye beds, producing a metallic chopping that approaches, peaks, and recedes without ever becoming visible. Even when no vehicle is nearby, the support columns vibrate. Loose leaves tremble continuously. Speech is useless outside the insulated control huts; workers communicate by hand signals and flashing helmet lamps.

The few built structures are scattered far apart. A pump house stands on a concrete island above the main reservoir. Farther on, an electrical substation is enclosed in frost-clouded glass. There are loading platforms, tool cages, narrow bunkrooms, and occasional raised cabins from which the cultivation lanes can be monitored. None dominates the landscape. They appear as isolated human objects in a much larger planted system, often half-covered by runners or screened by hanging roots.

Old industrial markings remain on the rock: numbered excavation faces, red survey lines, black arrows indicating abandoned haul roads. Some end abruptly where a new bed has spread across them. Rails emerge from the clover and vanish again. A rusting drill head has been left beside one pillar, wrapped in bindweed despite the cold.

Animal life is scarce. Small pale moths gather around the warmest lamps, and tiny springtails live in the wet root mat. Once in a while a vole arrives in a freight crate and establishes itself near a grain-processing shed, but traps and the lack of edible waste prevent large populations. There are no birds, no pollinating swarms, no hidden predators. Most flowering plants are sterile cultivars or are pollinated mechanically by carts that blow measured pulses of air through the rows.

The place changes slowly. Growth is continuous but restrained by cold. A vine may take years to cover a support frame. Moss closes a tire track over the course of a season. Roots gradually clog channels, and maintenance crews cut them back according to maps revised only once or twice a year. Harvesting happens in broad rotations: one district clipped, another resting, another lying nearly dark while its lamps are replaced. From a distance, these cycles appear as subtle changes in color moving across the underground plain.

There are rules here, practical rather than mysterious. No exposed flame. No untreated wood. No cutting of structural willow without an engineer present. Every litre of water must return to a reservoir. Every doorway must close behind the person who opens it, so temperature and fungal spores do not pass freely between districts. If the ventilation alarm turns blue, workers climb to the nearest raised platform; cold carbon dioxide can pool invisibly along the floor.

Beyond the active fields, the newest chambers remain raw. Their stone is bright and splintered, their corners unworn, their machine grooves still crisp. Planting has begun only in scattered rectangles there. Black irrigation cloth lies over the pale ground, with young leaves rising from evenly spaced cuts. The green has not yet joined into a single skin. Pumps and lamps have been installed, but the spaces between them are bare enough to reveal what the entire valley once looked like: a new wound under the earth, cold, dry-edged, and resonant.

In time, the mats will meet. Roots will enter the fractures. Vines will soften the columns, and dripping water will stain the walls. The machinery will continue to thunder, but the visible work of transformation will remain almost imperceptibly slow—a patient green occupation beneath a surface where winter passes unnoticed overhead.
