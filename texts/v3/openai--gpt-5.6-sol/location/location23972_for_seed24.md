The basin lies below the surrounding desert by no more than the height of a tall tree. Its boundary is a low ring of limestone, broad and blunt, so weathered that it resembles the rim of an old bowl rather than a cliff. From one side to the other is perhaps half a kilometer: large enough for distance to soften detail, small enough that the encircling stone is always present.

There is no standing water and little sign that water has crossed the basin in living memory. The floor is a compacted field of pale clay, salt, and powdered gypsum. In darkness it does not shine so much as retain a dim pallor. The ground is faintly crazed into many-sided plates, but the cracks are shallow and rounded at their edges. Nothing here appears freshly broken. Even the scattered stones have settled deep into their own slight depressions, with skirts of dust gathered evenly around them.

The air is warm and nearly motionless. Heat rises from the mineral floor with none of the violence of noon; it is stored warmth, released slowly through the night. The surrounding rim prevents most wind from reaching the hollow. Now and then a weak current passes overhead, audible as a dry brushing along the limestone, but it seldom descends.

Darkness gives the place its proportions. There may be stars above, yet the basin receives little from them. The inner face of the rim merges with the sky, and individual rocks emerge only at close range: a chalk-colored shoulder, a seam of dull flint, a low shelf fretted into shallow cups. There are no fires, no lamps, no distant settlements. Nothing marks a path across the floor.

Vegetation survives sparsely and without obvious vigor. Low gray clumps stand far apart, each no wider than a sleeping body. Their stems are jointed and hard, more like bundled twigs than green growth, and their few leaves remain folded into narrow scales. They draw moisture not from rain or pools but from vapor held deep in the clay and from condensation too slight to wet the surface. Around each plant is a bare circle where its roots have taken nearly everything available. The oldest clumps have formed small mounds of trapped dust, generations of stems accumulating in place. No blossoms open at night. There is no scent except a faint bitterness when a stem is crushed.

Between them grow thin mineral lichens on the limestone rim, matte black against gray rock. Their spread is so slow that their edges seem permanent. A handspan may represent centuries.

No animals live in the hollow. There are no tracks, burrows, wings, shells, or small furtive noises. Seeds fall almost directly beneath their parent plants. Pollination, where it occurs, is accomplished by dust-fine grains shaken loose during the rare passage of stronger air. Decay is equally restrained. Dead stems bleach, split, and gradually abrade into fibers; there are few organisms to consume them. Life persists here chiefly by waiting.

The stone is old but not dramatic. Erosion has already done its work and arrived at modest, stable forms. The limestone rim is undercut in places into niches no deeper than an arm. Flint nodules protrude from softer beds, some naturally fractured into sharp flakes. They could be lifted and used for cutting, scraping, or striking, and the harder stems could serve as cords or needles after patient working. The basin offers the first materials of craft, but no one has taken them up. There are no worked edges, stacked stones, hearth-blackening, or discarded tools. Its technology exists only as possibility: a blade sleeping inside a fracture, fiber inside a stem, pigment in a patch of ocherous clay.

Sound does not carry richly. A footstep produces a short mineral crunch and then ends. Touching the hollow stems together makes a dry click. A loosened grain may whisper down the limestone, stop against another grain, and remain there. With no animals and almost no wind, silence is not an interval between events but the basin’s ordinary substance.

Change happens below the scale of notice. Salt migrates through clay. Rock gives up heat. Roots extend by fractions. Dust settles into old cracks without altering their shape. Night after night, the basin appears to have completed itself long ago: warm, low, enclosed, and nearly empty, holding its few dark plants at measured distances across a floor the color of bone.
