Far below the exhausted plateau lies a city without wells.

It occupies an immense limestone chamber, broad enough that its opposite wall is usually lost in dusk. The ceiling hangs low at the margins and rises toward the center in long, shallow vaults, less like a cathedral than the underside of a buried plain. Nothing drips. No stream has crossed this place within living memory. The stone is dry through its full depth, chalk-pale where fractured and darkened elsewhere by age, smoke, fur, and the touch of innumerable bodies.

The city is nearly as old as the cavern. Its builders worked without metal and left no machinery: only masonry, packed earth, bone pegs, braided fiber, and blocks shaped by abrasion against harder stone. Buildings spread across the chamber floor in low districts separated by broad empty ways. There are roofless courts, thick-walled sleeping houses, granaries with narrow mouths, and circular towers that never rose high enough to meet the ceiling. Most corners have softened. Stairways are worn into ramps. Carvings have become shallow stains. Entire neighborhoods have settled until their upper floors stand level with the surrounding ground, so that people and animals pass over buried rooms without knowing they are there.

It is an urban landscape reduced to its most durable forms: walls, thresholds, hollows, and routes.

The cavern floor is almost flat, not because it was made so, but because time has removed every vulnerable edge. Rubble has sifted into cellars. Dust has filled lanes to the height of old doorways. Roofs collapsed centuries apart and were gradually ground underfoot into the same gray-brown soil. Only the broadest monuments remain distinct: immense oval enclosures, squat gatehouses, and long retaining walls built around rises in the bedrock. From a distance they resemble natural terraces. Up close, regular joints and finger-polished stones reveal their origin.

There is no surface water, and scarcely any moisture in the air. Life depends on what the rock and atmosphere provide. Cold drafts descend through fissures too narrow to enter, bringing traces of vapor, spores, and windblown organic dust from far above. Porous clay vessels are set beneath these vents, not to catch drops but to gather faint condensation during the coldest part of the cavern’s cycle. Their interiors may become darkened after many months. The yield is so slight that it is reserved for mouths. Nothing is washed.

Yet the city is green in its own restrained way.

Vegetation grows wherever moving air meets mineral-rich dust. Low mats of lichen spread over north-facing masonry in gray, olive, and muted yellow. Tough, ribbonlike plants rise in loose clumps around the vent shafts, their leaves coated in pale wax and folded inward to protect the narrow tissues beneath. They do not drink from soil. Fine hairs comb vapor and suspended particles from the air; deep rootlike anchors pry salts from stone. In the older courtyards, these plants form knee-high thickets, dense enough to hide sleeping animals. Fungal fans grow under arches and in sealed rooms, feeding on ancient timber, dung, leather, and the slow rain of feathers from the vaults above. Their flesh is dry and corky rather than soft. When broken, it releases powder.

No tree grows here. The nearest equivalents are colonies of stalk fungi clustered around old refuse pits. They rise in branching, antlerlike masses and may take decades to reach the height of a person. Some are older than the families who harvest them. A cut made in one generation remains visible in the next.

The air is cool and remarkably constant. There are no seasons in the familiar sense. Instead, the city observes long breaths. Over several weeks, pressure changes in the upper world push air downward through one set of cracks and draw it away through another. Then the current slowly reverses. Districts that were still become windy; streets that carried scent fall silent. Doors are hung with fiber tassels so the movement can be seen. Animals migrate from quarter to quarter following these almost imperceptible shifts.

They are everywhere.

Pale hoofed grazers move through the outer avenues in scattered herds, cropping lichen from walls. Their eyes are large, their coats short and dense, their steps delicate on the loose masonry. Long-eared burrowers occupy collapsed houses, emerging by the hundreds to feed among the fungal beds. Small, nearly colorless reptiles lie against warm patches where deep stone releases stored heat. Beetles work continuously through dung and dust. Moths gather at the faintly luminous fungal crusts, not in clouds but in persistent drifts, each individual separated from the next by a hand’s breadth of darkness.

Above them hang the great colonies.

The ceiling is crowded with bats—millions across the full chamber, divided among ledges, seams, and soot-black hollows. Their numbers make the city deafening. Even at rest they produce a continuous dry thunder: claws scraping stone, wings adjusting, pups crying, adults quarreling, bodies shifting through layers of fur. At feeding time the noise becomes physical. Flights pour down the central vault and split along the major streets, using the city’s avenues as channels. Wingbeats press against the ears. Echolocating calls, mostly too high to distinguish individually, combine into a needle-fine pressure behind the eyes. Lower cries ricochet from wall to wall until direction disappears.

Other sounds survive beneath this uproar: the rasp of grazers stripping masonry; the tick of beetle shells; the collapse of a dust-weakened partition somewhere in the distance. Voices must be used at close range. The inhabitants speak with hands as often as with mouths, and many walls preserve a practical language of scratched arrows, open palms, animal silhouettes, and warnings against unstable ground.

There are people here, but sparsely. The city is larger than its population by an order of magnitude. Families occupy defensible clusters of rooms while whole districts remain to animals and dust. No new stone is cut if an old block can be recovered. No house is built when ten empty houses stand nearby. Construction means rearrangement: clearing a chamber, plugging a crack, narrowing a doorway against bats, rebuilding a fallen wall with pieces gathered from another ruin.

Fire is rare. Fuel accumulates too slowly, and smoke can poison a still district. Light comes from shallow bowls of animal fat, from smoldering fungal cords, and from pale colonies cultivated on interior walls. The illumination is never strong. Faces emerge at arm’s length and vanish beyond it. Streets are known by texture underfoot, by airflow, by smell, and by the pattern of echoes. A polished band along a wall can guide a hand for half a mile.

The city’s laws are shaped by dryness and age. Water, when obtained, cannot be owned. Vent gardens cannot be inherited by a single household. No one may block an avenue used by a bat flight or grazing herd, because the animals fertilize the fungal beds and carry spores between districts. Deep excavation is forbidden: buried rooms may contain pockets of foul air, and breaking into them can alter the cavern’s slow circulation for generations. Loudness itself is not prohibited—the cavern is already beyond silence—but sharp repeated impacts are avoided. They can panic the colonies or loosen ceilings that have rested undisturbed for thousands of years.

Change is almost invisible. A wall leans for a century before it falls. A lichen field advances the width of a finger in a year. A vent closes grain by grain, shifting an entire neighborhood’s ecology over several lifetimes. Paths migrate as buildings subside. Families follow them. The city is not maintained against ruin so much as inhabited within it, its people adapting to each slow surrender of structure.

At the far edge of the chamber, where the buildings thin, the ground becomes a salt field. It is an old, dry basin of compacted mineral dust, flat and faintly reflective. Nothing substantial grows there. Beetles cross it by night, and predators wait among the last walls. Beyond the basin stand eroded pillars that might be natural or might once have supported a gate. Their surfaces are too worn to decide.

This uncertainty is common here. The oldest architecture has entered the geology. The youngest geology has been made into rooms. Streets follow ancient fractures; plazas occupy collapsed domes; neighborhoods nest inside quarries whose purpose no one remembers. There are no miracles and few mysteries in the city—only things too old to retain a clear boundary between the made and the found.

The cavern continues past sight, district after district, alternating between habitation and emptiness. Its darkness is never complete, its silence never possible, and its life never hurried. Under the relentless roar of wings, everything else proceeds with immense patience.
