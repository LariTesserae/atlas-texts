The city occupies a shallow, circular depression scarcely wider than a large railway yard. Its boundary is unmistakable: a ring of low, weather-rounded basalt, black where rain has touched it and gray everywhere else. Beyond the rim lies an empty plain of salt and fine metallic dust. Within it stands the city—compressed, industrial, old enough that rust, stone, ceramic, and deposited minerals have reached a kind of agreement.

Nothing grows here. There are no weeds in the drainage cracks, no moss on the shaded walls, no timber, paper, cloth, or soil. Every surface is mineral or manufactured: basalt paving, riveted iron, glazed brick, vitreous slag, dull aluminum, copper gone black rather than green. The place is cool without being cold. Condensation gathers on pipes and under eaves, hangs there in orderly beads, and falls at intervals into narrow gutters. Rain comes occasionally, always fine and nearly vertical. It darkens the entire basin for an hour, fills a few cisterns to the same high-water marks, then drains away through the same grates.

The city was built around a central pumping hall, a many-aisled structure of soot-colored brick with a roof like the back of an armored animal. Around it stand squat factories, sorting towers, pressure houses, and rows of narrow dwellings whose doors are too small for people. Elevated ducts cross the lanes. Gantries terminate at sealed walls. Rails emerge from one building, curve through the open square, and return to the same building without branching. Every structure has been softened by long erosion: stair treads are hollowed, corners rounded, inscriptions reduced to shallow scars. Yet nothing appears ruined. The wear is complete rather than ongoing, as if each object has arrived at its final shape.

The machines still operate.

Flywheels revolve behind iron grilles. Pistons rise and fall in wells below the streets. Conveyor chains pass through archways carrying pellets of gray protein, porous blocks of salt, cylinders of compressed shell, and shallow trays of warm water. There are turbines under the pumping hall, though no river enters the basin. Their vibration can be felt through the soles of the feet and in the teeth. Electric lamps burn in wire cages, giving an ordinary yellow-white illumination even at midday. Outside, the sky is usually pale and evenly clouded, so the distinction between natural and artificial light is slight.

The animals fill everything else.

They are not visitors to the city. They are its population, its product, its labor, and perhaps its original architects.

On every roof sit thousands of pale, blunt-winged creatures resembling a compromise between moths and small seabirds. Their bodies are warm and furred; their faces are protected by thin mineral plates. They do not fly far. At fixed moments they lift together, circle the pumping hall once in a thunder of wings, and settle in the same places.

The gutters are occupied by jointed black swimmers, each the length of a finger, which move through films of condensation too shallow to cover them. They rasp mineral residue from the ceramic channels. In the factory yards stand herds of six-legged animals with translucent skin and dark internal skeletons. They queue beneath dispensing pipes, receive identical portions of gray feed, and walk away in parallel lines. Their hooves strike sparks from the basalt.

Every wall cavity contains nests of red, eyeless things that click continuously against the brick. Cable conduits pulse with soft-bodied burrowers that conduct electricity through chains of wet tissue. Under the rails lie colonies of coin-flat animals, layered by the tens of thousands, which tremble whenever a cart passes above. In the cisterns, white eels are packed so densely that the water seems to have grown muscular.

Predation exists, but without pursuit. At the north wall, armored feeders wait with their mouths open. At regular intervals a stream of smaller creatures enters those mouths. The feeders close their jaws, digest, and resume waiting. Nothing flees. Nothing is surprised. Elsewhere, animals lay eggs into heated molds; the eggs hatch; the young are sorted by brass gates according to size and directed into separate buildings. Old shells are ground into insulation, paving aggregate, and feed. Waste descends through grates to warm chambers below the city, where blind, many-mouthed organisms reduce it to gas and mineral paste. The gas powers the furnaces. The paste coats the walls. The walls flake into the gutters. The gutter animals eat the flakes.

There is no visible plant life and no sunlight-dependent foundation to the ecology. The city feeds on its own circulation, supplemented by something drawn from beneath the central hall: a steady upwelling of warm, iron-rich water and combustible vapor. The deep supply never weakens. The machines never accelerate. The animal numbers never noticeably rise or fall.

This constancy is the strangest quality of the place. It is not silent or frozen. On the contrary, the basin is deafening. Belts slap rollers. Wings beat. Valves hammer shut. Hooves ring on stone. Sirens sound two low notes from towers with no attendants. Millions of throats chirr, bark, rasp, whistle, and grind. But the noise never develops. It has no crescendo and no lull. Stand in the square for an hour or for a year and the same composite roar surrounds you, with every apparent irregularity recurring at its appointed interval.

Even the weather belongs to the equilibrium. Condensation falls from the same pipe joints. Rainwater reaches the same depth in the sunken lanes. Mist forms each morning at knee height and disperses before touching the roofs. No sediment accumulates beyond a certain thickness. No beam rusts through. No carcass remains unprocessed. No species escapes the basalt ring, although there are no fences at its gates.

Time can be measured here, but not witnessed. Clocks in the pumping hall have hands and ticking mechanisms, yet each dial shows a different hour and returns to it after one full rotation. Fresh scratches made in the slag paving become rounded overnight, indistinguishable from ancient wear. A displaced object—a loose bolt, a shell, a fragment of glazed brick—will be carried through feet, mouths, gutters, belts, and sorting drums until it returns to the place from which it was taken.

The city does not resist change violently. It absorbs change as material.

At the basin’s center, beneath the largest flywheel, there is a dry circular court. The noise is strongest there, but oddly impersonal, as if heard through a wall. Thousands of animals stand around its perimeter facing inward. They blink, breathe, molt, and replace one another, yet the ring itself never opens.

In the middle of the court lies a shallow depression polished into the basalt. Rain sometimes gathers there, no more than a hand’s depth. Nothing drinks from it.

When the water is still, it reflects not the pumping hall or the clouded sky, but the city exactly as it is: every factory lit, every animal in motion, every wheel turning.

The reflection does not ripple when the rain falls.
