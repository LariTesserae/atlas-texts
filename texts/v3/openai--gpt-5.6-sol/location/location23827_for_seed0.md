The land lies low beneath a ceiling of unbroken cloud. It is not night, exactly. Day passes above the cloud mass, but only a bruised violet seepage reaches the ground, too weak to cast shadows. At intervals the entire sky whitens soundlessly, revealing the plain in a single flat glimpse: black ridges, luminous leaves, innumerable moving bodies. Then darkness folds over it again.

This is young ground. Long, shallow swells of basalt have risen recently from beneath an older alluvial basin, splitting the soil without yet giving wind or water time to soften the breaks. Their edges remain sharp enough to cut hide. Fresh vents exhale warm mineral breath through seams crowded with roots. Elsewhere, rafts of dark earth have tilted and settled at odd angles, carrying whole thickets with them. Trees lean but continue growing, their crowns turning upright while their trunks remain diagonal. The plain seems caught midway through being made.

There is not much open water. Rain comes rarely, but when it comes it arrives violently: warm drops as large as fruit stones, battering the canopy for a few minutes and then stopping. Most moisture is made closer to the ground. At dusk, vapor rises through volcanic fissures and condenses on every leaf, shell, feather, and tooth. Vegetation combs water from the air. Broad leaves funnel it into their own hollow stems; mosses swell into dripping cushions; pitcher-shaped flowers overflow into root basins. By the darkest hour, every surface is wet. Before the next dim day, much of it has been drunk, swallowed, or shaken loose.

Thus the country is both lush and almost riverless. Water exists in pockets: in the cups of plants, in hoofprints, beneath mats of fallen foliage, inside the translucent bodies of animals. Shallow streams appear after rain and vanish by morning, leaving writhing shoals of small creatures to bury themselves in mud or climb into the vegetation. The only permanent channels are narrow black trenches warmed from below. They carry no visible current, yet their surfaces tremble continually as gases rise.

The vegetation is dense without making the landscape feel small. It spreads for leagues in low, interlocked layers rather than closing into a tall forest. Dark fan-palms grow shoulder to shoulder, their leaves glossy enough to catch the faintest skyglow. Between them stand forests of jointed reed, copper-red and twice the height of a large animal. Creepers bind these growths together, but their cords do not hang passively: they contract at touch, drawing neighboring stems close. A creature forcing passage may find the opening sealing behind it.

Many plants make their own light, though none shine steadily. Pale nodules along the roots flash when trodden upon. Spores flare green as they are released, tracing gusts and wingbeats. Certain flowers hold a dull blue radiance until something approaches, then extinguish themselves all at once. The result is not illumination but signaling. Across the plain, points and veils of color answer one another in patterns too broad to read from the ground.

The animals are everywhere.

They fill every available layer: burrowing beneath the thin soil, nesting in leaf axils, clinging to the undersides of fronds, swimming in the temporary cups of rainwater, coursing through reed corridors, and migrating overhead in such numbers that their wings seem to thicken the clouds. The vegetation itself often appears to move until one notices that much of the apparent foliage is folded wing, plated back, or a congregation of leaf-shaped grazers.

Nothing here waits quietly.

Armored herd-beasts shove through the low palms in files thousands long, their flanks lacquered with condensation. Six-legged browsers climb over one another to reach new shoots. Flights of membrane-winged scavengers descend wherever a tree falls, stripping bark and soft wood before the leaves have stopped trembling. Pale, eyeless hunters move through root tunnels and burst from the ground under concentrations of footfall. When temporary pools form, dormant eggs hatch within minutes. The water clouds with mouths.

The noise has no distance. It is equally immediate from every direction.

Reeds knock together like vast racks of bones. Wingbeats create sustained thunder. Herds produce a continuous deep percussion that can be felt in the teeth. Burrowers rasp beneath the soil. Small creatures pulse their hollow abdomens and make the air ring. From the warm trenches comes a bubbling roar, joined at irregular intervals by explosive exhalations from the vents. Calls overlap until they lose all resemblance to individual voices: shrieks, booming throat notes, metallic chirrs, wet claps, whistles too high to locate. Silence occurs only as a warning. When a wide circle of creatures abruptly stops, something large is moving through it.

Predation is not the landscape’s central drama, despite the abundance of teeth. The greater struggle is for position. Every organism must remain in motion to keep its place in air, soil, or canopy. Plants crawl by root extension. Colonies migrate. Pools shift. Grazing fronts erase thickets, and fresh growth rises behind them almost immediately, fed by dung, shed skins, carcasses, and mineral heat. A clearing may open in the morning, become a marsh after rain, swarm with hatchlings by night, and be choked with red reeds two days later.

There are no paths in any lasting sense. Repeated passage does not tame the country; it provokes it. Trampled plants send runners into the compacted ground. Burrowing animals follow the vibrations of travelers. Predators learn the temporary corridors cut by herds. What resembles a road is usually a moving boundary between two migrations, and by the time it has been recognized it is already closing.

Among the animals are small upright creatures with grasping hands and large reflective eyes. They do not build. Building would require a place to remain stable, and stability is nearly unknown here. Instead they carry knowledge in gestures and carry tools only until the tools become burdens.

They split fresh basalt into black flakes, wedge shell blades into naturally forked branches, and use hollow reeds to draw water from guarded plants. Their implements are made quickly and abandoned just as quickly. A cutting edge discarded at dusk may be swallowed by roots before dawn. A sharpened stick becomes a nursery for eggs. Nothing remains an artifact for long enough to look separate from the ecology.

These tool-users travel within the migrations of larger animals, reading the tumult as others might read landmarks. One herd signals firm ground; another precedes rain. A certain deafening insect chorus means the condensation is beginning. A sudden wash of blue spores marks an approaching pressure front. They navigate by sequences of events rather than fixed places.

The land obeys this principle generally: location matters less than timing.

A safe hollow is safe only between the departure of the burrowing grazers and the arrival of their hunters. A fruit is edible during the brief interval after its shell splits and before its interior fills with larvae. The warm trenches can be crossed when their gas bladders subside, but no visible sign predicts this; one must listen for a change in the pitch of the bubbling. Even the sharp basalt shelves are temporary. Roots force into their cracks, vents widen beneath them, and whole slabs tip without warning into the soft basin soil.

Storms intensify everything. The cloud ceiling descends until its underside drags across the tallest reeds. Static crawls over shells and leaf edges. Animals climb, dig, mate, molt, flee, and feed simultaneously, as though each species has received a different command. Rain smashes down. New rivulets race across the unweathered rock, unable to find established channels. They collide, divide, disappear underground, and erupt elsewhere carrying blind fish and steaming mud.

In the storm-dark, living lights spread across the plain. Each footfall flashes. Every branch beaten by rain answers in green fire. Migrating wings burn blue at their margins. For a few minutes the regional shape of the land becomes visible—not by reflected light, but by disturbance. Valleys appear as flowing constellations of fleeing animals. Ridges show as dark, sharp absences. The warm trenches coil in lines of orange microorganisms. Great circular voids move through the brightness where the largest predators pass.

Then the rain stops with the same abruptness with which it began.

The noise, somehow, grows louder.

Water runs from a billion leaves. New pools boil with hatching life. Roots tighten in softened ground. Herds turn toward fresh growth, and the reeds bow beneath the pressure of their passage. Behind them, the black stone gleams naked for a moment: newly risen, sharply broken, almost untouched.

By the next dim cycle it will be hidden again.
