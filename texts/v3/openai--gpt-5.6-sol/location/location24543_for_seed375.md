The city occupies a shallow basin no wider than a small valley, though from its center it feels larger. Its streets multiply distance. They turn without curving, meet at acute angles, and repeatedly open into vacant courts whose far walls seem farther away than the boundaries allow.

There is no water here—not underground, not in the air, not even held in living tissue. The basin floor is a pale, cool mineral flour that does not clump beneath pressure. What passes for weather is a slow exchange of gases between the ground and the dim atmosphere. At certain hours the soil releases a heavy violet vapor, ankle-deep and nearly transparent. Later, as the temperature falls, the vapor is drawn back into hairline fissures. Nothing becomes wet. No dew forms. No stain spreads.

The buildings are young.

Their planes are sharp enough to cut skin, their corners still bearing translucent growth-edges like fresh crystal. They stand one or two stories high around broad, empty squares: roofless chambers, narrow towers, arcades made from repeated mineral ribs. Yet there are no tools, no fitted blocks, no hinges, drains, hearths, or machines. The city has not been constructed in the ordinary sense. It is an arrangement produced by the basin itself.

Below the streets lies a branching colony of stone-soft organisms. They have no eyes and scarcely any distinction between flesh and geology. Each is a membrane wrapped around a chemical gradient. As gases move through the ground, the colony deposits thin walls along invisible lines of pressure. Intersections thicken into columns. Trapped pockets become rooms. When a chamber reaches a certain proportion, its ceiling splits open and lifts into fins. Thus the place possesses architecture without architects: ordered construction arising from appetite, avoidance, and blind repetition.

The colony is active, but its activity is nearly impossible to hear. Walls lengthen by the width of a fingernail during the dim part of the day. Doorways narrow. New stairs push themselves upward one sharp tread at a time, leading to platforms that do not yet exist. Whole façades subtly lean away from the violet ground-vapor, then straighten when it recedes. In the outer streets, newborn partitions emerge as black seams and unfold into standing slabs.

The city therefore has no ruins. Even its abandoned quarters look unfinished rather than old. Erosion has barely begun. Cornices retain brittle needles. Street edges have not rounded. Fallen fragments lie exactly where they separated, surrounded by the shallow impressions of their impact. Nothing washes them away, and the weak winds lack the force to roll them.

There is vegetation, though little that would be recognized as such. In the courts grow sparse stands of hollow grey blades. They are stiff, jointless, and rooted in cracks where two kinds of stone touch. They do not photosynthesize. During the vapor tide, they open microscopic pores and take in reactive gas; afterward they close and spend hours slowly twisting toward the ground’s residual warmth. Their growth forms austere gardens: seven blades in a row, then none for twenty paces; a dark tuft at the center of a plaza; a single branching stalk occupying an entire room.

Some stalks produce brittle lantern-shaped bodies. These hold no seeds. When the temperature reaches its nightly low, they fracture and release flat mineral flakes that glide for several meters before standing upright in the dust. Most remain inert. A few send down threads and begin the century-long process of becoming another blade.

The light comes from the sky, though the sky has no visible sun. It is a low, uniform pewter radiance, brightest near the horizon and weakest overhead. Buildings throw only vague shadows. Within arcades, surfaces seem to generate a dull afterimage of their own, so that an empty doorway may remain visible after everything around it has faded. Distance is difficult to judge. Pale streets merge with pale walls, while black cracks appear nearer than they are.

Animal life is rare and solitary. A small six-legged creature sometimes crosses the open avenues, its body enclosed in overlapping plates of chalky shell. It contains no fluid; flexible powders slide within it, carrying chemical charge from limb to limb. It feeds by scraping the newest edges from buildings. Because the walls grow faster where they are damaged, the creature is followed over weeks by ridges, alcoves, and blind little chapels. Its grazing becomes ornament.

There are also the vault-dwellers, if they are animals at all. They hang beneath ledges as folded scraps of dark membrane. Once every few days one releases its grip and drifts across a court on the heavy exhalation from the soil. It lands elsewhere and remains motionless. No one has seen one feed. Their positions, taken together, map the breathing channels beneath the city.

Nothing calls. Nothing buzzes. Feet make almost no sound because the powder of the streets swallows impact without shifting. Even a broken wall falls quietly: first a tremor, then the graceful separation of a slab, then a soft displacement of mineral dust. The greatest noise is an occasional dry ticking inside the façades as new crystal tensions settle. It may occur once, then answer from another district several minutes later.

For all this activity, the city feels empty. Its architecture encloses vacancy more often than shelter. Rooms are too tall for the rare plated grazers and too narrow for any imagined crowd. Stairways climb to exposed ledges. Gateways frame blank walls. Avenues divide districts in which only three or four structures stand. The settlement is urban not because many beings inhabit it, but because the ground insists on making streets, thresholds, plazas, and neighborhoods.

At the basin’s edge, construction stops abruptly. The final houses present smooth, windowless backs to an outer salt-dark plain. Beyond them lies almost nothing: low ground, dim atmosphere, occasional shards pushed up by subterranean pressure. From outside, the whole city resembles a cluster of fresh teeth.

Its boundaries migrate. On some cycles the outermost walls dissolve—not by rain or decay, but by sublimating directly into the air. Elsewhere, new blocks rise overnight from the plain. The settlement slowly walks around the basin, dismantling itself behind and rebuilding ahead. Its central square remains approximately central, though no individual stone stays there long.

The governing rule of the place is simple and strange: emptiness attracts structure. Leave a court vacant and walls begin to form around it. Clear a room and a column rises near its center. Crowd a passage with debris and the surrounding architecture thins, withdrawing into the ground. Presence repels the city; absence gives it shape.

Perhaps this is why it remains uninhabited. Anything numerous enough to fill it would cause it to vanish.

So the avenues wait beneath the pewter sky, cool and sharply new. Violet vapor advances through the intersections. Grey blades turn in their ordered gardens. A plated grazer passes through a distant gate, and behind it the bitten stone begins, silently, to build.
