The city lies across a high basin where the mountains have been worn down to broad, mineral shoulders. From a distance, it appears almost white: terraces of pale ceramic, salt-streaked retaining walls, roofless factories, and long causeways laid over sheets of steaming water. Nothing green interrupts it. There is no moss in the cracks, no grass along the drains, no dark fringe of trees at the basin’s edge. The wetness has none of the softness ordinarily associated with rain. It shines on stone, beads on glass, gathers in metal gutters, and runs away through a city made entirely of hard surfaces.

The sun is severe at this elevation. It strikes through a thin, glaring sky and rebounds from every pool and bleached wall. Yet the air itself is heavy with moisture. Condensation continually forms beneath ledges and on the shaded faces of towers. Rocks weep from internal seams. Water is never far below the pavement; in some districts the stone has been cut into open channels, and the groundwater moves there in slow, lucid sheets, warm enough to blur the air above it.

Rain comes frequently but briefly. It arrives without much wind, descending in straight silver threads from compact clouds that form over the basin almost every afternoon. The drops vanish from exposed metal in seconds, but collect knee-deep in the sunken plazas. For an hour the city becomes a connected system of shallow lakes. Then sluices open, siphons begin, and the water is drawn down through the foundations.

Despite the machinery, this happens almost silently.

The oldest buildings are made from a porous volcanic stone glazed on the outside with fused silica. Their lower courses have been rounded by centuries of runoff. Steps dip gently at their centers. Doorways have softened corners. Salt blooms on shaded walls in branching white geometries, the nearest thing here to foliage. Newer structures are lighter and narrower: frameworks of black alloy, translucent kiln-glass, suspended pipes, and heat-stained ceramic plates. They stand far apart, leaving great intervals of brightness between them. The city is urban not because it is crowded, but because every part of the basin has been measured, drained, surfaced, and assigned a function.

There are streets broad enough for processions, though no procession uses them. There are squares capable of holding thousands, occupied by only a few low machines and clusters of resting animals. Conveyors cross the open districts on tall, widely spaced pylons. Pipelines descend from the mountain walls, pass through pressure houses, and divide into finer networks under the paving. At the center of the basin rise the evaporation towers: hollow, ribbed columns several hundred meters high, perpetually sweating. Sunlight enters them through slits and breaks into pale bands in the vapor.

The industries of the city are industries of separation. They refine what the water carries.

Deep below the plateau, water moves through hot mineral strata, dissolving metals and salts before rising into the basin. The city catches these springs and sorts their contents by temperature, charge, density, and crystallization. Black iron settles in one quarter. Copper compounds stain another in turquoise and blue. Vast shallow pans grow rafts of translucent salt. In covered chambers, rare elements accumulate as thin mirrored skins, peeled away by articulated tools.

There is little smoke. Combustion is considered wasteful where heat already presses upward from the ground and downward from the sun. Turbines turn inside sealed conduits. Pumps flex rather than hammer. Gears are submerged in oil or water. Bearings float magnetically. The result is an industrial city whose labor can be seen everywhere but barely heard: valves dilating, vanes changing angle, shutters slowly following the sun. At most there is the soft pulse of pressure through a pipe, the tick of contracting glass after rain, or the distant hiss where overflow meets a hot channel.

The silence also belongs to the animals.

They are abundant, but most have no voices that carry in air. The basin’s ecology is not founded on plants. Its primary producers are thin microbial films living inside wet stone, under translucent salt crusts, and along the dark walls of geothermal conduits. They harvest chemical gradients: hot against cool, iron-rich water against oxygenated rain, brine against condensation. Some are scarcely distinguishable from mineral tarnish. Others build brittle laminations, layer upon layer, producing low domes that look like poured wax or old enamel.

Everything larger feeds on these hidden skins, or on something that does.

Broad, flat grazers cling to wet masonry with hundreds of minute feet. Their backs resemble fragments of cracked porcelain, each plate reflecting enough sunlight to keep the body beneath from overheating. They move only when water covers them. After rain, entire walls seem to loosen and flow downward as the grazers migrate toward freshly flooded channels. When the stone dries, they seal themselves against it and become indistinguishable from repairs.

In the warm pools live glass-bodied filter animals, hand-sized and nearly invisible except for their pulsing internal fans. They gather around drain mouths in great numbers. Their feeding rhythm sends faint concentric tremors over otherwise still water. At the first change in pressure they collapse into clear knots and sink.

Long-legged basin walkers cross the plazas at dawn and after storms. Their limbs are black and jointed, lifting pale, heat-reflective bodies well above the paving. Instead of mouths, they carry damp brushes beneath the head, which they sweep through condensation on walls. They feed on airborne spores, mineral dust, microscopic swimmers, and the soft films forming where droplets remain. Their feet end in padded cups, and even a herd of several hundred makes no more sound than fingertips passing over glass.

Predators are equally quiet. Ribbon-shaped hunters inhabit the drainage system, reading the movement of prey through changes in electrical conductivity. They have no eyes. When floodwater spreads through the streets, they follow it out of the grates, dark lines uncoiling through water only centimeters deep. The grazers detect them through the stone and climb. The transparent filter animals extinguish their pulses. For a few minutes a plaza crowded with life appears utterly empty.

Above, in the bright thermal currents, float the most conspicuous creatures in the basin. They are buoyant, many-chambered animals with reflective membranes stretched over internal frames. Some are no larger than lanterns; the oldest span several meters. They drift between the evaporation towers, trailing fine conductive tendrils into the vapor. They feed partly on suspended microorganisms and partly on differences in electric potential between the wet city and the dry upper air. During storms they descend and fasten themselves to lightning masts. Their translucent chambers flash from within, but the discharge is spread through so many filaments that it produces only a dry whisper.

The inhabitants—if the city’s builders can still be called inhabitants—are sparse. At noon, one may look across five districts and see no upright figure at all. Those that do appear wear mirrored mantles and sealed masks, moving beneath arcades or along the shadow-lines of walls. They seldom cross open pavement directly. Heat governs the city’s schedule more strictly than clocks.

Most work is done before sunrise, during rain, or in the white hour immediately after a storm, when evaporation briefly cools the streets. At midday the population withdraws below the surface, into cistern halls and cooled chambers cut into the plateau. The city above continues without them. Gates adjust to water levels. Collector arms skim fresh mineral films from the pans. Herds of wall-grazers migrate across abandoned workshops. The floating animals gather in the towers’ shade.

Water here is not merely a resource. It is the city’s currency, archive, and principal law.

Every roof must surrender its rain to the common channels. No one may dam a natural seep completely. Hot water must pass through at least three uses—power, extraction, cooling—before it can be released to the lower terraces. The cleanest condensation is reserved for drinking and precision work. The most mineral-laden brines belong to the foundries. Waste is not defined by toxicity but by disorder: a substance is waste when its components have been mixed so thoroughly that the city cannot profitably separate them again.

Accordingly, nothing is buried. The soil, where any existed, was removed or vitrified generations ago. The dead are dissolved in closed mineral baths, their metals returned to the industrial cycle, their complex organic matter fed into the deep microbial chambers. Broken buildings are crushed, sorted by color and conductivity, and fired into new panels. Even corrosion is harvested.

The city has grown slowly into equilibrium with its own erosion. Drainage channels are allowed to deepen until they reach marked limits. Salt is permitted to accumulate on designated sacrificial walls, which swell and crumble over decades while the structures behind them remain intact. Some streets have been resurfaced so many times that their edges rise above the doors of old buildings. Elsewhere, abandoned lower stories sit beneath clear water, visible through grates and inspection wells. Pale animals move through former rooms. Pipes pass through old windows.

Beyond the urban terraces, the basin opens into a field of low mineral ridges and warm marshes without reeds. The marshes are mosaics of water, salt crust, ocher mud, and thin metallic scum. They look empty until the light shifts. Then thousands of small bodies can be seen: burrowers making bubbles under the mud; silver discs turning edgewise to the sun; jointed siphoners standing in the shallows; colonies of soft black beads rolling slowly toward fresher seepage.

The mountain slopes above are almost bare. Their stone is purple, brown, and vitreous, fractured by old heat and polished in places by rain. Narrow aqueducts contour around them, crossing gullies on delicate arches. At intervals stand condenser stations shaped like open combs. Moist air passes through their cooled stone teeth and emerges as strings of droplets. Animals gather beneath them, but never densely. The ecology has learned the danger of concentration: predators find crowds, mineral films are easily stripped, and a single hot day can turn a shallow refuge into brine.

From the highest pass, the whole basin is visible. The city occupies it not as a solid mass but as a network: bright roofs, black frameworks, silver channels, blue extraction ponds, white salt flats, and broad intervals of bare, wet stone. Vapor rises from a hundred points. Above it drift the reflective aerial creatures, catching sunlight like slow fragments of a broken mirror.

Nothing rustles. Nothing sings.

The loudest natural event is rain striking the evaporation pans, and even that becomes less a sound than a texture spread across the basin. Thunder rarely reaches the ground intact; the charged floating animals and the city’s towers unravel it into branching, muted discharges. The loudest mechanical event is the opening of the western spillway, a low vibration felt in the feet as excess stormwater leaves the high basin and begins its descent through tunnels toward the deserts below.

At evening, the glare weakens but the heat remains stored in every surface. Water condenses again on the shaded sides of towers. The wall-grazers detach. Drain hunters stir. The long-legged herds emerge from beneath bridges and spread through the plazas. Lamps come on only where necessary: narrow blue lines along channels, amber points over pressure doors, violet inspection beams moving inside the refining halls.

Most of the city stays dark under a luminous sky.

Its life becomes more visible then, though no less strange: a thriving world with no leaf, root, blossom, or branch; an industrial wetland raised high in hot air; a place where abundance hides in films and seams, where factories behave like geological organs, and where the difference between an animal, a machine, and a mineral deposit is often a matter of how patiently one watches.
