### The White Choir

The place is smaller than its first impression.

At first it seems to be a white plain extending beneath an equally white sky, crowded with towers, ribs, terraces, and low serrated ridges. Only after walking its perimeter—if walking can be imagined here—does its containment become apparent. The horizon is not distant. It curves upward. The “sky” is the underside of a shallow dome, and the whole landscape occupies a sealed hollow no wider than a large room, hidden inside some vastly greater mass.

There is no exposed earth. There never was.

Every surface is made: fitted, fired, pressed, scored, or laid in courses. The floor consists of millions of pale ceramic scales packed edge to edge, each one slightly cupped and finely cracked but never loose. The ridges are assembled from interlocking wedges. The towers are stacks of thin mineral plates arranged around empty shafts. Even the dust is manufactured—powder worn from corners and inscriptions, accumulated in seams so old that it has compacted into a second kind of mortar.

Nothing here has room around it. Architecture fills nearly the entire volume. Buttresses overlap screens; screens lean into colonnades; colonnades subdivide already narrow courts. There are passageways no wider than a hand, plazas the size of saucers, staircases climbing three or four steps before striking a ceiling and dividing sideways. Miniature walls stand upon larger walls. Bridges span cracks that are themselves roofed with lattices. Every available interval has been articulated.

Yet the place does not feel cluttered. Its density has passed beyond clutter into completeness. There are no accidental gaps. Emptiness survives only in assigned forms: keyholes, flutes, ducts, bell-mouths, narrow resonant chambers. Space itself appears to have been built.

The terrain is low and almost level. Its greatest heights are modest ivory fins clustered near the dome, while the lowest places are shallow polygonal basins. These basins have never held water. Their floors are coated in salts, dry waxes, and a dull gray sediment resembling powdered glass. Along their edges, delicate tide marks have been engraved rather than deposited: concentric lines cut with such patience that they imitate the memory of vanished pools.

The air is cool and utterly dry. It carries no scent of rain, rot, soil, or animals. Breathing it would taste faintly of chalk, hot stone that has long since cooled, and the sharp metallic cleanliness of a snapped mineral. Moisture brought into the hollow would not bead. It would disappear into the porous white surfaces almost at once, drawn away and fixed as another invisible layer in the walls.

Light is everywhere.

There is no lamp, flame, sun, or opening. The ceramic itself shines with a dense, shadowless whiteness. Not a glow exactly: more as if illumination were a material packed into the tiles when they were made. Recesses remain bright. The undersides of bridges are bright. Shafts descending into the floor retain the same unmodulated radiance at their bottoms. Objects cast only thin pearl-gray margins, enough to reveal their relief but never enough to form darkness.

This constant brilliance makes age easy to see. Edges have been rounded by immeasurable abrasion. Reliefs once sharp as lettering are now low, soft currents in the walls. Steps sag in their centers. Pillars have become asymmetrical from the passage of something that no longer passes. Whole rows of ornament have been worn down to pale blisters.

The erosion is ancient but finished. No grain falls now. No crack advances. The chamber has reached the final shape permitted by its materials, and that shape holds.

Across the made plain grow sparse colonies of something vegetable, though they have neither leaves nor apparent roots. They rise from selected joints as stiff black-green threads, each no taller than a pin. Several threads may join into a forked figure like a tiny antler or a written character. Their surfaces are matte amid all the shining ceramic. They do not sway. They do not spread. No specimen is young and none is dead.

These growths feed on brightness and on trace minerals diffusing through the masonry. They require no water. Their sap, if it can be called sap, is a slow-moving silicone oil sealed in capillary tubes. In each colony the oil has long since reached equilibrium. The plants are alive only in the broadest sense: maintaining form, exchanging almost nothing, persisting without season or generation.

There are no animals. No burrows, droppings, silk, shells, footprints, or gnawed edges. Nothing flies through the brilliance. Nothing watches from the holes.

Still, the White Choir is loud.

A continuous chord occupies it, powerful enough to be felt in the teeth and in the joints of the floor. It is not produced by machinery. There are no moving parts, no hidden engines, no vibrating membranes. The sound is simply one of the chamber’s conditions, as fixed as its dimensions and its coolness.

Each hollow architectural form holds a tone. The low basins contain a pressure like distant thunder. The plate towers sustain narrow, piercing notes. Tiny ducts whistle without breath passing through them. Grooved walls contribute a dry, crowded hiss. Together they form a vast stationary resonance: not music progressing through time, but a single acoustic structure into which the architecture has been fitted.

At certain positions one frequency cancels another, creating spots of near-silence no larger than a fingertip. Elsewhere the chord becomes so dense that it seems solid. Fine patterns can be felt along the walls—unchanging bands of pressure, knots and nodes arranged with the same precision as the masonry. The black-green growths occur mostly in these nodes, where the sound presses least.

The place has no weather. Its air does not circulate. Temperature is identical at floor and dome. Nothing warms under the omnipresent light. There is no morning, no evening, and no interval by which duration might be measured.

Its governing rule seems to be that all processes must eventually become structures.

Sound has become architecture. Light has become material. Erosion has become finish. Vegetation has become inscription. Whatever labor or intention made the chamber has vanished so thoroughly that it cannot be separated from nature—indeed, there is no nature beneath it to uncover. Removing one tile would reveal another fashioned layer. Beneath that would be fitted plates, then molded ribs, then compressed blocks bearing the impressions of still earlier walls. The construction continues downward without reaching rock.

Perhaps the White Choir is a decorative cavity in some enormous object. Perhaps it is a whole world whose scale has been misunderstood. Its narrow courts and inaccessible stairways suggest inhabitants, but no body could use them consistently: some doors are hair-thin, others broad and only a fraction as high; certain bridges meet walls; certain streets close into perfect loops. They are less like provisions for life than exhaustive variations on the idea of passage.

Nothing arrives. Nothing departs.

The chamber remains hidden, bright, cool, dry, and completely full, sustaining one changeless chord within its innumerable made hollows. Its permanence is not peaceful. The noise is too immense for that. But neither is it violent. It is the sound of a world that has already completed every event available to it and now exists only as the durable resonance of what was done.
