The city occupies the flooded space inside a single foundation stone.

From outside, the stone is unremarkable: a dark, waist-high block embedded among thousands of others in the breakwater of an abandoned coast. Its surface is young and angular, split only recently from some deeper geology. Rain and salt have not yet rounded its edges. One hairline crack crosses the upper face, narrow enough to hold a silver thread of water.

Inside that crack, distance unfolds.

The water enters vertically through a fault no wider than a fingernail and descends for what, within the stone, amounts to several kilometers. It falls as a continuous shining wall into a sealed interior sea. There is no air below the first few meters. Streets, towers, conduits, plazas, transit shafts, reservoirs, and machines are all submerged in cool, mineral-heavy water. Pressure should crush every cavity flat, yet the city maintains impossible intervals within the rock: rooms larger than the block that contains them, avenues bent through volumes with no exterior equivalent, neighborhoods folded beside themselves like pages.

There is scarcely any open water. Construction fills the interior almost completely.

Buildings rise wall to wall from fresh black shelves of basalt, packed so tightly that their facades become channels. Some are narrow stacks of ceramic cells, white and slightly luminous, with doorlike apertures opening directly into the water. Others are bundles of metallic ribs grown along stress lines in the stone. Bridges cross gaps only a handspan wide. Pipes braid around bridges; cables braid around pipes. Arrays of vanes, membranes, turbines, filters, and unknown instruments occupy every remaining recess. Even the water seems crowded, divided into lanes by gradients of salinity and pressure.

There are no inhabitants.

No fish cross the avenues. No shell adheres to a sill. No worm disturbs the pale sediment caught in corners. The city has never hosted animal life, and nothing in its proportions suggests that it was intended to. Stairs narrow to nothing. Corridors pass through walls without openings. Windows look into dense machinery or directly into rock. Certain plazas are only a few centimeters across but descend farther than sight. What resembles housing may instead be a method of calculation: millions of empty chambers changing volume in coordinated pulses.

Sparse vegetation persists near the upper fissure. Threads of green algae trail from mineral lips, all leaning with the inflow. Lower down, where the water has been stripped of ordinary nutrients, they give way to thin glassy films that might be plants or might be maintenance surfaces. They photosynthesize under the city’s illumination, but only reluctantly. Every few hours a membrane retracts, exposing a patch of vivid green; then shutters close and the patch disappears behind pearl-colored plates.

Despite its burial, the interior is brightly and evenly lit. There are few visible lamps. Light enters from surfaces rather than sources. Towers shine along their edges. Pavements release a clear, shadow-soft radiance close to daylight, adjusted to a cycle unrelated to the sun outside. At “noon,” the whole submerged city turns lucid blue-white, and the mineral particles in the water become visible as packed constellations. At “evening,” warmer light migrates down the vertical streets, not dimming but changing direction, so that shadows seem to sink.

The loudness is continuous.

Water thunders through the entrance fault and breaks into branching pressure canals. Pumps beat beneath the paving with slow concussions. Banks of resonators call to one another across solid rock, producing tones too deep to hear cleanly but strong enough to shake pale dust from ledges. Valves snap open in sequences, each report traveling through the liquid as a sharp metallic knock. Somewhere in the lower districts, a machine emits a rising chord that lasts for eleven minutes, stops abruptly, and begins again from a different quarter.

Because there is no air, every sound arrives through water, walls, and bone. Near the main conduits, noise becomes texture: railings tremble; tiled surfaces hum; suspended strands of algae lie flat under the pressure of passing vibrations. Silence exists only in engineered pockets where waves cancel one another. These quiet zones drift slowly through the city. When one crosses a street, the roar vanishes at a precise boundary. Beyond it, machinery can be seen hammering and churning in perfect muteness. Then the zone moves on, and all the sound returns at once.

The city is new. Not merely maintained, but unfinished.

Sharp excavated faces remain exposed between districts, dark and glossy where the stone has been cut without heat or abrasion. The tools are absent. At these active margins, the rock appears to make room by changing its internal rules. Crystalline grains rotate into alignment; fractures unzip in clean geometric planes; solid walls become temporarily permeable and allow whole structures to pass through before hardening again. Construction modules emerge from the stone already assembled, shedding clouds of excess mineral like smoke.

No rubble accumulates. Waste material is sorted grain by grain and carried upward in dense black currents. Along the ceiling of the interior sea, magnetic fields arrange these particles into temporary reefs of iron and olivine. They hang upside down, crowded with antennae, until they are drawn into the walls and vanish.

The water itself is part of the infrastructure. It flows through districts without always moving in the ordinary sense. One avenue contains a northbound current and a southbound current occupying the same volume, separable by temperature rather than by barriers. Elsewhere, pressure is tiled: crossing from one square meter to the next can feel like descending a hundred meters. Cylinders of unusually heavy water stand upright without containers, their surfaces quivering. Streams climb facades, turn corners, and enter sealed housings.

At regular intervals the whole sea contracts.

The buildings incline inward by a fraction. Water pressure rises. Every luminous seam turns violet. For several seconds the city becomes impossibly dense, its folded spaces compressed almost to coincidence. Streets overlap; towers occupy one another; distant machinery appears close enough to touch through intervening walls. Then a bass detonation rolls from the lowest chamber, and the interior expands again.

Each contraction seems to enlarge the city. New balconies appear where blank walls had been. Another transit loop opens. A stack of chambers unfolds beneath a plaza. Whatever intelligence directs this process does not distinguish between architecture and geology. The city is not being built inside the stone so much as teaching the stone to contain more city.

In the deepest accessible district, the water is cold enough to numb bare skin but never freezes. The streets there are broader—perhaps half a meter from wall to wall—and paved with black hexagons. Above them hang dense clusters of transparent objects shaped like bells, seeds, and inverted cups. They are not organisms. Fine inscriptions move through their material like schools of microscopic script, recombining whenever the pumps strike certain frequencies.

At the center stands a low circular basin filled with water clearer than the water around it. Its boundary is invisible except for refraction. Within the basin, a miniature version of the city can be seen: the same crowded towers, the same foundation stone, the same crack admitting rain from an exterior coast. Looking more closely reveals another basin in that city, and another within it.

The repetition is not exact. In some depths the upper world is dry. In others, the breakwater has not yet been built. In one, the stone lies under open ocean, and the hairline crack pours not water but a column of white radiance. The nested cities communicate by pressure pulses. Their pumps answer one another in staggered thunder, signals climbing and descending through scales that have no agreed-upon smallest level.

Nothing living appears to interpret these exchanges. The algae near the entrance bend, brighten, and fade. Mineral films spread over new surfaces. Beyond that, the ecology is one of currents, crystals, heat, and machinery: deposition competing with filtration, cracks forming and being sealed, chemical gradients blooming and collapsing. It is active without being alive, crowded without population.

Outside, rain continues to strike the breakwater. Each drop sends a disproportionate shock through the hidden sea. In the city below, bells of transparent material flare, shutters turn, and kilometers of submerged construction adjust themselves around the new arrival.

The stone grows no larger.

Inside it, another district opens.
