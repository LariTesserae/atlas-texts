The chamber lies not far beneath the surface, though there is no direct way down to it. It is a buried interval in the earth: a long, low nave of black stone, perhaps two hundred paces from end to end, hollowed by an ancient river and later intercepted by machinery. The river is mostly gone. Its work remains everywhere.

The walls have been worn into deep flutes and folded ledges, all rounded at their edges. Basalt ribs descend from the ceiling like melted beams. Between them, mineral skins have accumulated in successive eras—red iron, green copper salts, pale scales of lime—so that the chamber appears painted, stripped, repainted, and smoked. Nothing looks newly broken. Even the scars left by drills and cutting wheels have softened beneath deposits. Bolts protrude from the rock as blunt brown nodules. Cable channels have become little ravines.

The heat is immense and settled. It does not arrive in gusts. It occupies the chamber like a material.

Stone sweats continuously, but not enough to make a true stream. Condensation gathers on the ceiling, crawls along the mineral ribs, and hangs in swollen drops before falling. Each drop strikes foliage, metal, or one of the shallow black basins in the floor. There are hundreds of these basins, some natural and some machine-stamped, ranging from the breadth of a hand to the size of a dining table. Most hold only a thumb’s depth of warm water. Their rims are white with evaporated minerals.

Rain also comes, though the chamber has no sky. At long intervals the porous roof saturates after storms on the surface. Water then descends through invisible seams in a fine, warm downpour lasting perhaps an hour. Rust blooms. Leaves unfold. The old machinery darkens as if briefly restored to use. Then the rain ceases and the heat begins reclaiming everything.

Vegetation fills the middle levels of the chamber. It does not form a forest so much as a densely hung interior. Thick mats grow from the walls, fed by condensation and chemical seepage. Their leaves are broad, almost black, and corrugated like industrial filters. They overlap in tiers, leaving only narrow aisles between stone, leaf, and abandoned equipment. Pale stalks rise from cracks in the floor and terminate in fans of translucent green tissue. Rope-like growths descend from the ceiling, not roots and not vines, twisting slowly around pipes and chains.

Nothing flowers. Nothing fruits. There are no insects to visit blooms, no birds, no burrowing things, no pale cave fish in the basins. The ecology has narrowed itself to plants, fungi, mineral films, and microbial veils. Pollination, where it is necessary, is performed by vibration. Spores and dust lift whenever the machines sound, circulating through the chamber in ocher clouds. Some plants reproduce by touching: neighboring leaves fuse at their margins, exchange tissue, then separate again years later. Others bud directly from rust.

The absence of animals is profound. No tracks disturb the soft deposits. No nests occupy the ledges. No eyes reflect in the dark. Even carrion and bone are absent, as if animal matter cannot persist here.

Across the chamber stand the remains of an industrial installation built for a purpose no longer obvious. The structures are too scattered to be called a factory. A small brick control room leans against the eastern wall, its door missing and its windows sealed by green mineral glass. Three cylindrical tanks sit half submerged in stone at the chamber’s lowest point. A gantry crosses overhead, ending abruptly where the old river once removed its supports. Along the southern aisle, narrow pipes enter the rock and do not emerge again.

At the center is a great horizontal engine, no taller than a person but nearly thirty paces long. Its casing has split in several places, and dark leaves emerge through the cracks. Flywheels stand motionless around it. Every visible drive belt has rotted away.

Nevertheless, the chamber is loud.

A continuous mechanical roar comes through the stone, far below the foundations. It is not the sound of a nearby engine, but of an entire buried works operating at an immeasurable distance. The noise rises through bolts, pipes, water, and roots. Some surfaces hum; others hammer. The shallow basins wrinkle with concentric waves. Loose sheets of metal chatter against walls. At certain points the roar resolves into slow, distinct impacts, each separated from the next by almost a minute:

**THOOM.**

Silence never follows. The broader vibration continues, filling the ribs and teeth. Then:

**THOOM.**

The plants respond. Their black leaves incline fractionally toward each impact, not toward any source of light. Spores rise in measured pulses. Condensation drops loose from the ceiling all at once, producing a secondary rain. In this way the old industry has become the wind and weather of the chamber.

Light is sparse and has no single origin. Thin electric tubes still glow inside protective cages along portions of the wall, though no intact wire leads to them. Their light is amber and exhausted. Mineral films around the pipes produce a bruised blue radiance. Some leaves shine faintly along their veins, storing illumination during the rare moments when a surface fissure admits daylight. The brightest place is the control room, where a bank of dead gauges gives off a steady green glow behind fogged glass.

The gauges do not measure pressure, temperature, or flow. Their faces are marked with concentric maps of the chamber. Hair-thin needles advance so slowly that motion cannot be seen, yet their positions differ from year to year. One indicates the location of each pool. Another records the thickness of the ceiling. A third appears to show the arrangement of the vegetation, though its engraved map predates the oldest visible growth.

Nothing here changes quickly. Rust proceeds in polished layers. Water shifts from basin to basin over decades as mineral lips rise and spillways seal. A fallen pipe may take a century to reach the floor because the vegetation catches it, grows around it, lowers it by increments, and catches it again. The walls still erode, but so evenly that erosion resembles maintenance. Old channels deepen while new deposits reinforce their edges. The room has worn itself into a stable shape.

There are rules to the chamber, though no signs state them.

Standing water never reflects the ceiling. Each basin shows a different portion of the industrial complex as it once existed: bright lamps, clean railings, turning shafts, workers absent from their stations. The reflected machines move at the same slow cadence as the subterranean impacts. If a basin dries, its view does not disappear; it hardens into a dull metallic image across the bottom until the next rain.

Any object left in the chamber is gradually assigned a function. A dropped tool may acquire mineral teeth and begin to filter runoff. A length of cloth may stiffen into a valve. Glass fills with luminous green fluid. Nothing becomes animated, exactly, but the place does not permit uselessness. It incorporates.

The largest leaves have already begun to resemble access panels. Their veins branch in precise right angles. When pressed, some retract into the wall, revealing cavities lined with warm copper. Inside these cavities are smaller leaves, neatly stacked, each bearing embossed serial numbers that do not repeat.

Toward the western end, the floor slopes down into a narrow trench where the remnant river still passes. The water appears only intermittently, a warm black surge perhaps twice a month. It arrives soundlessly despite the chamber’s roar, fills the trench almost to its rim, and withdraws within a few hours. No one has found where it enters. No one has found where it goes. Afterward, the trench is left full of smooth stones, each worn into the same elongated shape. Some are stone all the way through. Others, when broken, contain tightly wound copper wire.

Beyond the trench stands the only intact door. It is set directly into unworked rock and secured by a wheel gone green with age. The door vibrates with every distant impact. Moisture beads around its frame, feeding a dense collar of translucent fronds.

The wheel can be turned, but only very slowly. Those who have tried say it takes several hours to make one revolution. No latch can be heard releasing. The door never appears to move.

Yet after each full turn, something in the chamber is different. A pipe has shifted to another wall. A basin contains water that was dry. The central engine has gained another narrow seam, and from that seam comes a breath of hotter air. Most subtly, the great underground rhythm grows louder, not by much, but permanently.

**THOOM.**

The leaves incline.

Warm rain loosens from the stone.

**THOOM.**

And somewhere beneath the chamber, machinery continues the work for which the vanished river, the ruined installation, and the patient black garden may all have been built.
