At the summit of an ancient plateau lies a shallow enclosure called the Black Sill.

It is not a valley so much as the last surviving hollow in a mountain worn almost level. The surrounding heights rise only a few dozen meters, broad-backed and rounded, their stone softened by an age of rain. No cliffs remain. No loose scree, no sharp fragments. Every exposed surface has been reduced to dark, close-grained curves: low shelves, kneeling ridges, basins polished deep enough to hold water for months. The whole place could be crossed in an hour, yet its boundaries feel complete, like the walls of a roofless room.

There is no light.

The darkness is not cloud-shadow or night. The sky above the Sill is permanently opaque, sealed by a warm, high-altitude stratum that absorbs starlight and returns almost nothing. Flame gutters blue and vanishes. Ordinary lamps lose their radiation within a hand’s breadth, their beams swallowed as if projected into black wool. Even the living things here make no visible glow. Distance is learned by echo, heat, pressure, and the changing smell of wet stone.

The air is warm despite the elevation. It rests close against the skin, saturated and faintly metallic. Water condenses continuously on the plateau’s cooled rock, gathering in films too thin to run until they suddenly join and pour down the rounded faces. These weeping surfaces feed a chain of shallow pools around the hollow’s edge. Between rains, water remains just beneath the soil, rising under a footstep and withdrawing slowly when the pressure lifts.

The ground is mostly open.

Across the central expanse there is only a broad pavement of ancient stone, interrupted by moss islands and occasional reed beds where fractures admit water. Nothing stands close to anything else. A person crossing the Sill passes through long intervals containing only warm air, bare rock, and the immense noise of unseen life. Each fern tower, each stone rib, each structure occupies its own silence of space, separated from its neighbors by tens or hundreds of paces.

Yet the margins are lush. Vegetation crowds every seam where the pavement meets the enclosing ridges. Thick, cool mosses lie in folded mats. Rope-rooted shrubs lean over pools without leaves, spreading membranes that drink condensation directly from the air. Ferns grow as isolated fountains, their fronds leathery and broad, opening only when humidity reaches a precise threshold. There are low groves of hollow-stemmed trees whose crowns cannot be seen but can be heard collecting water. Their trunks pulse subtly as internal bladders fill; every few hours they discharge through their roots with a deep wooden knock.

Growth is slow. A new frond may take three seasons to unroll. Moss advances the width of a fingernail in a year. Fallen material does not rot quickly, despite the warmth. It is dismantled with great care by fungi, mites, and pale colonial organisms that pass nutrients from body to body rather than releasing them into the soil. Nothing here wastes much.

The fauna are abundant but rarely encountered as separate shapes. They occupy cracks, flooded tubes, root hollows, and the underside of the stone pavement. Warm rain brings them close to the surface. Then the Sill becomes thunderous.

Millions of shell-backed grazers rasp mineral algae from the rocks, producing a dry, continuous roar like distant surf. Wingless insects drum their abdomens against hollow roots. Long-bodied amphibians call through water-filled fissures, each pool acting as a resonant chamber. Something heavier moves beneath the pavement at intervals, pressing air through the fractures in low organ notes. The sound has no obvious center. It rises from the entire enclosure and reverberates between the rounded ridges until even small motions seem enormous.

Animals navigate by adding their voices to this field. Silence would make them blind.

The largest are the basin walkers, warm-blooded creatures on six padded limbs. Their backs carry gardens of symbiotic fern and fungus, disguising their scent from smaller prey. They move with extraordinary patience, sometimes holding a foot above the ground for minutes before setting it down. Their low calls map the hollow in pulses too deep to hear clearly; one feels them in the jaw and sternum. There are perhaps nine in the Sill, though their overlapping echoes can suggest hundreds.

Scattered through the enclosure are slender structures made by no known inhabitant.

Most resemble narrow doorframes planted upright in the stone, each one taller than a person and thin enough to encircle with two hands. They are not metal, ceramic, or grown tissue, though they behave a little like all three. Water climbs their surfaces against gravity, divides into perfectly spaced beads, and disappears along the upper edge. Touching one produces no vibration, but nearby animals fall quiet for several heartbeats, as if a much louder signal has passed.

The frames stand far apart. No path joins them. Some have sunk until only a curved corner remains above the moss; others hover the thickness of a leaf over the bedrock, never settling even when roots grow beneath them. They repair scratches by borrowing matter from condensation. Given centuries, they also repair the stone around their bases, drawing eroded grains back into old fractures and placing them in their original crystalline alignments.

This may be why the Black Sill has reached such an unusual age. It has been worn down almost to its final form, but it is not permitted to vanish.

The rain erodes. The frames return a portion. Roots split the rock. Invisible processes close it again. Water carries minerals downslope, while something beneath the plateau raises them molecule by molecule. The result is not stasis but an exceedingly slow circulation of shape. Basins deepen and then become shallow. Ridges subside and are rebuilt from within. Over tens of thousands of years, the enclosure breathes without greatly changing its outline.

Near the center stands the only structure that could be called a building: a low circular shelter with no door and no interior wall. Its roof is a single concave plane suspended waist-high above the pavement. To enter, one crawls beneath it.

Inside, the sound of the animals vanishes.

The darkness remains absolute, but the air becomes cooler and strangely spacious. A whispered word returns several seconds later in one’s own voice, altered by age: sometimes hoarse, sometimes childish, sometimes spoken with an accent not yet learned. The stone floor is dry, the only dry surface in the Sill. Along its perimeter are shallow depressions shaped by knees, hands, tools, and appendages that fit no local animal.

The shelter does not appear abandoned. Nor does it appear occupied. It simply continues its function, whatever that function is, while epochs pass over it.

Local life treats it as a boundary. Moss stops at the roof’s edge. Basin walkers circle it but never cross beneath. During the heaviest rains, small creatures gather around it in such numbers that the surrounding pavement feels furred and shifting underfoot. They face inward and make no sound.

Then, from somewhere under the dry floor, a single drop falls.

It can be heard for kilometers through the plateau.

Afterward the whole Sill answers: roots knocking, shells rasping, amphibians calling through flooded stone, hidden wings beating in the warm black air. The noise builds slowly and may continue for days. Water rises a finger’s breadth in every basin. The upright frames bead with condensation. The ancient rock flexes almost imperceptibly beneath its living cover.

Nothing happens quickly here. Nothing needs to.

In the Black Sill, darkness is not an absence but a stable condition; erosion is not destruction but one half of a patient machine. Life fills the place without filling its space. The old structures tend a landscape already brought close to completion, and the landscape, in return, grows over them carefully—never enough to hide them, never enough to leave them bare.
