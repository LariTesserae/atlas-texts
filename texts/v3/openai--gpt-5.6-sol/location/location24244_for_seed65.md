At the top of the world lies a country of worn stone.

It is not a single summit but an immense, high-standing region: a succession of pale plateaus, shallow basins, long escarpments, and blunt mountains whose highest points have already been taken away by ages of weather. The land has no fresh edges. Even where a ravine drops abruptly for a thousand meters, its walls are scalloped, pitted, and rounded at the lip. Ribs of gray limestone pass beneath sheets of darker volcanic rock. Gravel has settled into every hollow according to size, the fine grains in the lee of ridges, the heavier pebbles arranged in broad, immobile fans. Nothing looks newly broken.

The altitude is extreme. The lower world is often hidden by a level white cover of cloud, so that the plateau appears to float above a second, softer ground. Peaks farther away rise from that brightness like islands. Distances are difficult to judge. A ridge that seems close may require a day to reach; a dark mark that resembles a boulder may resolve into an entire cliff.

The light is relentless.

The air is thin, dry in the mouth, and intensely clear. Sunlight falls almost without color, bleaching the stone and cutting short, dark shadows beneath every ledge. Snow survives only in protected seams, pressed into hard blue-white plates where the sun cannot reach. There are no broad glaciers and no generous rivers. Water occurs privately: a thumb-deep pool in a stone basin, a black thread under gravel, condensation beading along the cold interior of a fissure. At dawn, frost coats the shaded ground, and by midday much of it has vanished into the air without ever becoming flowing water.

Rain comes occasionally, but it is slight and remarkably regular. A narrow belt of cloud gathers against the same eastern wall, releases a brief silver fall, and dissolves. The drops darken the rock in overlapping circles. They fill old hollows, wake films of algae, and disappear. The channels they follow are ancient and settled. No flood seems capable of persuading them into a new course.

Yet the place is never quiet.

A permanent high wind crosses the plateau. It does not gust or wander. It comes from one quarter with such consistency that every exposed surface bears its signature: low ridges streamlined in the same direction, shrubs pressed flat, crystals frosted on their windward faces. Along the escarpments, the air enters thousands of cavities worn into the rock. Some are no wider than a finger; others descend as shafts through whole shelves of limestone. Together they turn the plateau into an immense instrument.

The sound is overwhelming.

Deep openings produce a continuous subterranean bellow. Narrow cracks scream above the range of comfort. Stone arches moan, hollows drum, and clusters of small perforations make a dense metallic roar like innumerable horns sounded at once. The notes do not form a melody and scarcely vary. They overlap until sound becomes part of the pressure of the air, something felt in the sternum and teeth. Loose speech is impossible near the cliffs. Even in sheltered basins there is always the distant, unwavering thunder of the rim.

There is almost nothing upright on the open ground.

Vegetation survives by remaining low. Black-green cushions cling to cracks, packed so tightly that they feel like wood under the hand. Tufts of silver grass occupy the damp margins of transient pools, each blade narrow and tough, all leaning west. Rust-colored lichens map the older boulders. In hollows where condensation collects, there are miniature meadows only a few paces across: moss, bitter leaves, tiny white flowers turned close to the ground. The flowers have little scent. Their petals do not flutter; they are thick and waxed against the wind.

From a distance, the land appears nearly sterile. Up close, it is crowded with animals.

The air above the escarpments is full of wings. Dark, long-bodied birds hang in the updraft without visibly moving, facing the wind and remaining fixed over a single point for hours. Smaller birds pour through the gaps beneath them, traveling in dense horizontal streams. They nest far back in the resonant holes, apparently undisturbed by the noise. Their calls cannot be heard except when one is held close, and many species have almost ceased calling altogether. They communicate through bright throat patches, wing angles, and abrupt changes in posture.

Insects rise from the cloud layer below. The constant updraft carries them onto the plateau in astonishing numbers: pale moths, hard-shelled beetles, translucent midges, and broad-winged forms that glide more than they fly. They gather in wind shadows behind stones, where lizards and small furred hunters wait. Thus the abundance of life does not depend entirely on the sparse plants. Much of the plateau’s food arrives through the air.

The larger animals follow fixed circuits between water pockets. Shaggy, narrow-hoofed grazers move in single file along paths worn deep but never widening. They crop the cushion plants carefully, taking only the outer growth before continuing. Gray hares shelter in the rubble. Colonies of blunt-snouted burrowers live under warm slabs, though their burrows are shallow and reinforced only by the natural fit of stones.

A few plateau birds use tools. At certain flat rocks, they drop hard-shelled insects onto naturally fractured flakes and retrieve the meat after the shells split. Some carry suitable stones from one feeding place to another. There are no piles, huts, walls, carvings, or fires—nothing that could be called a structure. Skill exists here, but construction has not begun. The land supplies edges, hammers, hollows, and shelter already formed. There is no need, or perhaps no impulse, to improve them.

Time seems to have reached equilibrium in this country.

The wind retains its direction. The pools empty and fill within the same old margins. Animals hatch, feed, migrate across the plateau, and die, but the flocks remain the same size in the sky. Bones bleach in sheltered niches until lichens cover them; eventually they become difficult to distinguish from stone. Rock still erodes, yet so slowly and uniformly that no transformation can be witnessed. Every fallen grain appears to take the exact place prepared for it.

Even the loudness contributes to the sense of stillness. Because it never ceases, it stops signifying weather or danger. It is not an event. It is the audible shape of the land.

Standing in the open, beneath the hard bright sky, one sees very little: stone, flattened growth, distant wings, a dark pool no larger than a shield. But the emptiness is deceptive. The cliffs are inhabited from lip to root. The wind is full of traveling life. Beneath nearly every stable slab, something breathes.

Nothing dominates. Nothing builds. Nothing changes enough to be remembered.

The plateau endures above the clouds, brilliant, cold, and roaring.
