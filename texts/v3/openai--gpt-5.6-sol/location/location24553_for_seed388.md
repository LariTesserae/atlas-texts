The place occupies a shallow limestone hollow no larger than a compact town ward. Its boundaries are clear: a ring of old retaining walls, blackened by damp, beyond which the ground rises into low, root-bound ridges. Within the ring, buildings stand close enough for their gutters to touch. Alleys narrow to wet seams. Workshops lean against cisterns; cisterns support bridges; bridges pass directly through the upper floors of mills. There is scarcely any open ground.

Everything has been here long enough to lose the sharpness of construction. Brick corners are rounded like river stones. Iron stairs sag in the middle of each flight, their treads polished and scalloped by water rather than feet. Concrete has separated into pale aggregate and dark paste. Copper roofs carry thick blue-green crusts, and their runoff stains the walls below in branching mineral fans. Wherever two materials meet, a third substance has formed between them: rust-lime, glassy salts, black moss, waxy deposits from the warm vapor.

The hollow is wet without being flooded. Water remains near the surface, pressing upward through cellar tiles and breathing through mortar joints. Every wall weeps. Roofs drip long after rain, though rain itself is difficult to distinguish from condensation. Warm air rises from grates and meets cooler masonry overhead, becoming a fine indoor weather that beads on cables, rafters, leaves, and pipes. Shallow channels run along every street, their current slow and full, carrying iron-red silt toward a central settling basin.

That basin is the lowest and oldest feature. It is nearly circular, surrounded by stepped stone aprons worn into shallow bowls. Water enters from dozens of carved mouths, none shaped as animals or faces—only simple slots, flutes, and rectangular openings. In the dimness, the surface looks black. At intervals, enormous bubbles dome upward and collapse without spray. Rings travel outward, strike the basin wall, and return as a complex trembling grid.

The town was built around this water and then rebuilt around its own rebuilding. Pipes cross older pipes in dense bundles. Some are ceramic, some riveted steel, some grown over with mineral shells so thick that their original material is unknowable. Valves sit behind barred niches. Flywheels turn in rooms without doors. Broad leatherless belts move from one building to another through slots in the walls, vanishing into darkness before reappearing two streets away. The machinery does not hurry. A piston may take half a minute to descend. A chain advances link by link with grave deliberation. Yet the accumulated sound is immense.

There is a continuous industrial thunder beneath the district: pumps striking their housings, deep shafts turning below the stone, water forcing itself through constrictions, sheets of roofing shivering under hidden pressure. Every few minutes a reservoir empties somewhere in the upper levels, and the whole town answers with a descending roar. Then comes the prolonged ringing of pipes, each one finding its own note. Smaller sounds lie inside the larger ones—the tick of condensation, the hiss of steam escaping mineral-crusted seams, the granular whisper of silt sliding through channels.

No birds cross the narrow sky. No insects gather at the lamps. There are no rats in the culverts, no fish in the basin, no worms in the soft earth beneath the courtyards. Nothing with breath, blood, shell, or wing lives here.

Plants nevertheless flourish. Ferns crowd the stairwells in overlapping green fans. Liverwort coats brickwork like damp velvet. Pale reeds emerge from cracked factory floors wherever groundwater reaches the light. Vines hang from gantries, but they grow without flowers and produce no fruit. Their leaves are broad, nearly black, and perpetually slick. In sealed rooms, root mats have lifted tiles and entered machinery, winding through gear teeth so gradually that the gears have adapted: they turn with roots compressed between them, never quite cutting through.

The vegetation follows the infrastructure rather than the sun. Moss brightens around warm pipes. Thin white stems gather near electrical conduits. Dense mats of glassy grass grow above buried cables, each blade faintly luminous at the tip. Where a line has failed, the associated growth slowly darkens and subsides. It is difficult to decide whether the plants are feeding on heat, leakage, vibration, or some slower property of the machines.

Light enters reluctantly. The sky above the hollow is usually a dull warm gray, reduced to strips between roofs and suspended walkways. Old lamps remain fixed to walls at regular intervals, their housings fogged from within. They glow amber behind condensation, making halos rather than illumination. In some workshops, rows of blue electrical arcs flicker behind wet windows, but the flicker is strangely slow, swelling and fading like distant summer lightning trapped in glass.

The air is warm enough that metal handrails never become cold. It smells of wet stone, oxidized iron, hot dust, algae, and the faint sweet sharpness of electrical insulation. Steam collects under awnings and inside roofed passages. At street level, visibility sometimes contracts to a few paces. Buildings then appear not as whole structures but as successive surfaces: a riveted tank, a curtain of roots, a sweating wall, a row of porcelain switches, another wall behind that.

Erosion is not destroying the place so much as completing it. Water has found permanent routes. Steps have worn into comfortable curves. Overflow has carved channels through thresholds, and builders long ago stopped trying to block them; later doorways were raised or bridged over the established streams. Foundations have settled toward the basin in a balanced inward tilt. The town seems to have reached an arrangement among gravity, water, machinery, and stone. Failures occur, but slowly, and are incorporated. A collapsed chimney becomes a culvert. A rusted boiler opens and fills with reeds. A cracked wall begins serving as a spillway.

There are signs that the settlement once accommodated inhabitants: narrow houses above the works, tiled washing rooms, lockers, counters, numbered doors. But the proportions are subtly inconsistent. Some stairs are too shallow, others too steep. Handrails pass directly into walls. Windows look onto sealed chambers only inches deep. A row of small dwellings has front doors but no interiors; behind each door is warm, flowing water and a vertical shaft descending beyond the reach of light.

The district appears to operate according to pressure rather than time. Clocks remain everywhere, though none has hands. Instead their faces fog and clear in slow sectors as subterranean reservoirs fill and empty. Doors unlock when nearby channels rise to particular levels. Bridges shift sideways by a few inches during periods of high condensation. Lamps brighten when the basin is lowest. The deepest engines pause only when rain begins, as though the added water allows them to rest.

Change is patient. Rust advances along a beam over years. Roots tighten around a pipe over decades. A whole building may rotate fractionally as its foundation dissolves, taking a century to turn toward the basin. The loudness gives an impression of furious activity, but almost nothing happens quickly. The town’s enormous effort is devoted to maintaining states: keeping water at a certain depth, pressure within a certain range, warmth moving from one wall to another.

At the center of the basin, just visible when the vapor thins, stands a low iron platform. It supports no statue, pump, or building—only a square opening covered by a grille. The grille exhales warm mist in measured pulses. With each pulse, every hanging drop in the district trembles at once. Leaves dip. cables hum, and the dark basin wrinkles from edge to edge.

Then the town resumes its slow, deafening equilibrium: a small, saturated world of water and manufacture, lush with unflowering growth, built for absent lives and worn smooth by a labor that has forgotten what it was meant to produce.
