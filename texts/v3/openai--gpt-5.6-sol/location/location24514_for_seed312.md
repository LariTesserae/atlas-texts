The country begins above the clouds.

It is not a single mountain range but an uplifted tableland vast enough to possess its own horizons, seasons, and weather. From the lower world its rim appears as a blue-white wall, broken by young ravines and long shelves of exposed stone. On top, the land spreads for weeks of travel: high plains, shallow basins, abrupt fault steps, and bare ridges whose edges remain sharp. The plateau rose recently in the reckoning of stone. Rivers have not yet learned how to dismantle it. They wander across its surface, vanish into cracks, reappear far away, and only at the margins begin to cut true valleys.

Cold governs everything. Even in the bright season the ground is hard before sunrise, and shadows preserve frost through the afternoon. The air is thin, dry in the mouth, yet moisture is seldom entirely absent. Clouds drag their bellies over the heights. They leave rime on walls, beads on rope, and dark stains beneath projecting stones. Rain comes infrequently and often sideways, in brief curtains that pass from district to district. Snow is more common, though usually light: powder caught in paving joints, wind-packed drifts behind parapets, a white tracing along every ledge.

Water exists in pockets rather than expanses. There are tarns in shallow volcanic hollows, black wells sunk into fractured rock, cistern courts beneath every neighborhood, and chains of marshy ground where meltwater gathers over clay. The streams are narrow, clear, and indecisive. Some run only at midday. Others freeze from the banks inward and continue whispering beneath a roof of ice.

Across this immense height stands a city so extensive that travelers disagree on where it begins.

There is no encompassing wall and no single center. Instead, thousands of close-built settlements have grown together along ridges, watercourses, sheltered depressions, and migration paths. From a distance they resemble natural outcrops: gray terraces crowded one above another, patched with pale plaster and roofs of sod, hide, slate, or tightly fitted timber. In the broad basins, districts merge into continuous stone fields. On exposed ridges they narrow into long, fortified spines. Between them lie pasture, frost-heaved ground, animal enclosures, quarries, refuse slopes, shrines, and strips of low cultivation.

The city is dense because cold makes distance expensive. Houses share walls. Courtyards are narrow and sunken. Alleys turn sharply to break the wind, descending beneath arches and climbing by uneven stairs. Roofs almost touch overhead in older districts, and many serve as streets. A person may cross an entire quarter without stepping down to ground level, passing over flat roofs, plank bridges, and stair towers crusted with ice.

Yet the place is not loud.

There are too few carts for a continual rumble, too little fuel for workshops to burn without pause, and the thin air seems to carry sound away rather than amplify it. Most labor is hand labor: the tap of stone against stone, the scrape of bone shovels, the thud of felt being beaten, the brief groan of a wooden axle. Voices remain low to conserve breath. Thick walls swallow domestic noise. At noon, when the wind drops, whole districts may seem deserted even while tens of thousands of people work behind their doors.

Then a flock rises, and the sky fills with wings.

Animals are everywhere. Small dark birds nest in masonry holes by the hundreds. Pale mountain pigeons crowd warm roof vents. Ravens patrol butcher lanes and funeral platforms. Long-haired goats climb steps, parapets, and quarry faces with equal ease. Sheep with heavy double coats move through the outer wards in compact rivers, guided by children and narrow-chested dogs. Pack animals—short-legged, broad-footed, patient under frost-stiff loads—kneel in caravan courts while condensation smokes from their nostrils.

Beyond the built districts, vast herds cross the plateau. Shaggy grazers follow the retreating snowline, feeding on cushion plants and hard sedges almost too low to cast shadows. Lean antelope-like animals occupy the open stone plains. Burrowing creatures honeycomb the softer banks, and foxes listen for them beneath the frost. On remote crags, heavy cliff birds wait on motionless wings. Their shadows move over the city more often than clouds do.

Vegetation is sparse but persistent. There are no forests on the open plateau. Trees survive only in protected clefts, temple yards, and warm springs: twisted junipers, dwarf willows, a few carefully guarded fruit trees spread against south-facing walls. Most growth stays close to the ground. Moss blackens around leaking cisterns. Orange and silver lichens mark old stone. Tough grasses occupy cracks where dust has collected. In wet basins, sedges form dull green mats that become grazing grounds in summer and hard yellow plains in winter.

The city depends on these small patches of life with an attention that borders on reverence. Dung is dried for fuel and kneaded into wall plaster. Straw is too valuable to waste beneath animals unless it can later be composted. Kitchen water is poured into root beds set behind sun-warmed masonry. Bones become awls, hinges, combs, toggles, sled runners, and reinforcing pins. Horn is steamed and shaped. Sinew is cordage. Hides are floors, doors, buckets, window coverings, and winter walls.

Metal exists, but it is scarce. A bronze knife may belong to a household rather than a person. Iron is known mostly as dark fragments traded up from the lowlands or collected from fallen stones, and a good iron edge can outlive several owners. Construction therefore remains a craft of weight, friction, balance, fiber, and patience. Stone is split by fire, water, wooden wedges, and repeated blows. Great blocks are moved on sledges over damp clay or winter ice. Towers rise without cranes more elaborate than timber tripods, rope, counterweights, and teams of animals walking downhill.

The results are not crude. Generations of builders understand stone as intimately as sailors understand wind. Walls lean inward by precise degrees. Doorways narrow toward the top. Roof beams are set where snow will brace rather than break them. Cisterns are lined with clay, ash, fat, and powdered limestone. Channels hidden inside walls capture condensation and carry it to covered jars. Whole neighborhoods are shaped to collect weather: a sloping roof here, a spillway there, a stair broad enough to become a temporary stream during rain.

Because the ground is young, it cannot always be trusted. Fresh fractures cross old streets. A house may stand for two centuries and then find itself half a handspan higher than its neighbor after a winter tremor. Cliffs occasionally shed enormous clean-faced slabs. New springs open; familiar ones cease. The people build in layers, expecting correction. Cracked walls are stitched with timber. Tilted rooms are filled and roofed over. Streets become tunnels, tunnels become drains, and drains, once dry, become dwellings.

Beneath the visible city lies another city made from all its earlier adjustments: buried courts, abandoned cisterns, livestock passages, tomb chambers, sealed storerooms, and old streets preserved under newer floors. Warmth accumulates below. So do animals. Mice, beetles, pale cave crickets, sleeping reptiles near hot springs, and colonies of bats occupy the masonry depths. In winter, people open vents between cellars and living rooms to borrow heat from the mass of bodies, dung, stored grain, and slowly cooling earth.

No ruler controls the whole plateau. Its scale defeats that kind of authority. Each water basin forms its own polity, usually governed by the households responsible for dams, cisterns, and snow fences. Boundaries follow watersheds rather than walls. A person belongs to the water they maintain. To poison a well, block a melt channel, or steal ice from a covered reservoir is a more serious offense than theft of livestock.

The oldest law is simple: water moves downhill, but ownership does not. A household may catch what falls on its roof, yet any overflow must be allowed to continue. Channels may be delayed, divided, filtered, and guided, but not ended. In dry years, inspectors walk the entire route of a stream from snowfield to marsh, carrying measuring cups and marked cords. Their judgments are enforced less by soldiers than by collective memory. Everyone downstream knows who closed a gate too long.

The city changes slowly. New districts appear not as sudden foundations but as thickening along old paths. A seasonal camp gains a shrine, then a cistern, then walls; a generation later it is joined to neighboring roofs. Abandoned quarters do not empty at once. They lose people room by room, becoming pens, workshops, bird colonies, and finally quarries for newer construction. The plateau’s apparent permanence is made from countless small acts of repair.

Weather also proceeds in long gestures. Clouds may gather for three days before releasing a few minutes of snow. A tarn may rise by the width of a finger over an entire season. Herds arrive as a distant brown stain and take half a day to pass. In winter, darkness does not become absolute; stars burn hard above the roofs, and reflected snow gives the alleys a dim blue shape. In summer the ordinary sun remains in the sky long enough to warm the southern walls, drawing people and animals into every patch of light.

At the far edge of the plateau, the urban fabric loosens. Roofs become isolated towers, then shepherd compounds, then low cairns marking roads across bare country. The ground lifts once more into raw ridges of pale stone. From these heights one can look back and understand, briefly, the scale of the inhabited land.

Smoke lies in thin horizontal bands over a thousand basins. Roads thread between them like cracks in fired clay. Reservoirs flash wherever the sun finds them. Herds pour through gaps in the buildings and divide around neighborhoods before rejoining on the other side. The city has no silhouette because it is too large to stand against anything. It is not an object placed upon the plateau. It is a texture of the plateau itself: stone rearranged into shelter, frost organized into water, animal paths made into streets, and human persistence spread almost to the horizon.
