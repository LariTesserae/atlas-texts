The city occupies a shallow coastal basin where the land never rises far enough to become dry. It spreads across many kilometers, but loosely: long causeways, pumping stations, cooling towers, warehouses, settling ponds, and blocks of low concrete housing separated by broad flats of wet mineral soil. From any elevated walkway, most of what can be seen is open ground—gray-brown silt, black aggregate, rust-streaked water, and the repeating geometry of industrial works fading into haze.

Nothing grows.

There are no weeds in the cracks, no algae greening the canals, no reeds along the drainage ditches. Heat, salinity, metal contamination, and constant disturbance have pared the surface ecology down to films of bacteria too thin to alter the color of the landscape. The absence of plants is not dramatic at first. It registers gradually, as a missing softness. Every horizon is hard-edged. Every patch of color belongs to paint, oxidation, mineral deposits, warning lights, or animals.

The air is hot even before dawn. It carries enough moisture to bead on cooler metal and gather beneath pipes in trembling drops. Low clouds remain over the basin for much of the year, held there by the sea and by the exhaust of cooling systems. Their cover dims the day to a pewter half-light. The sun appears only as a pale, painful disk, and when it does break through, the wet concrete flashes white and the heat becomes nearly unbearable.

Water is everywhere without making the place lush. It lies in wheel ruts and subsided foundations, threads through expansion joints, condenses on the undersides of roofs, and seeps up through the oldest pavements. Rain comes often in short, warm bursts. It darkens walls, rinses soot into gutters, and fills shallow depressions before vanishing through pumps or evaporating into the same heavy air. Along the basin’s lowest edge, tidal water moves through barred culverts and wide flood channels. It is brown with suspended silt and warm from industrial discharge.

The city was built in layers upon compressible ground. Older streets have sagged into smooth troughs. Steps descend to doorways that were once level with the road. Retaining walls bow outward under the patient pressure of wet sediment. Concrete corners have rounded; brickwork has lost its outer skin; iron rails are reduced to swollen, flaking forms the color of dried blood. Drainage has carved stable black streaks down every embankment. The erosion is old enough to look intentional. New construction accommodates it with hinged utility connections, floating platforms, and adjustable piers.

Despite the extent of the city, few people are visible. The inhabited buildings are sealed against heat and insects, joined where possible by enclosed corridors. Freight moves automatically along distant tracks. Pumps operate below ground. Electric motors replace the clatter of older machinery. The industrial district is active, but its activity is dispersed and muffled: a turbine felt through the soles, the soft cycling of sluice gates, a conveyor whispering inside a corrugated shed. Far off, a warning horn may sound once and leave the basin quiet again.

Into this quiet emptiness comes the animal life.

It is overwhelming not because the animals are always loud, but because they are everywhere one looks. The city stands on a migration coast and beside tidal feeding grounds of extraordinary productivity. Since almost no terrestrial vegetation competes for space, every sheltered ledge, warm pipe chase, culvert, roof hollow, and abandoned room has become den or nest.

Crabs crowd the drainage channels in layered masses. They shelter in broken masonry during the hottest hours, then emerge after rain until whole service roads seem to move sideways. Small pale crabs occupy the upper concrete; larger mud crabs rule the culverts and fight beneath the sluice gates. Their claws tick faintly against aggregate, a dry, localized sound easily mistaken for cooling metal.

Flies are present in quantities beyond counting, though they gather rather than fill the whole air. They mantle the shaded sides of buildings and lift in dark veils when disturbed. Mosquitoes breed in untended cisterns. Midges hover above settling ponds like columns of smoke. Cockroaches live within insulated walls and beneath warm electrical cabinets. Silverfish graze on paper, glue, and the microscopic residues of human occupation. None of this insect abundance depends on plants growing here. Food arrives through sewage, fisheries, freight, carcasses, guano, kitchen waste, and the organic richness of the adjacent sea.

The birds follow.

At low tide the basin’s outer flats whiten with gulls, terns, sandpipers, egrets, and ibis. Cormorants occupy the disused loading cranes, holding their wings open in the humid air. Pelicans wait beside outflow channels where stunned fish drift close to the surface. Pigeons fill the old housing blocks. Swifts nest in ventilation shafts and cross the dim sky so quickly that they seem to stitch it together.

Most remain quiet while feeding or resting. Their calls come in brief outbreaks—a quarrel beneath a warehouse roof, a sudden alarm rolling across a rooftop colony—then subside. The heat suppresses unnecessary movement. At midday even the gulls stand with open beaks, facing the faint wind.

Rats travel the cable galleries and flood tunnels. Feral dogs keep to the dry margins of freight yards, thin and mud-colored, sleeping beneath parked trailers through the day. Cats occupy upper floors where crabs cannot reach them. In the canals are mullet, eels, small sharks at high tide, and dense schools of hardy fish accustomed to warm, low-oxygen water. Their presence is often revealed only by a simultaneous dimpling of the surface or by the birds leaning forward as one.

The food web is built on what the city and sea deliver, not on what the land produces. Plankton blooms offshore. Tides bring larvae and carrion into the basin. Processing plants release scraps under regulation through screened channels, though the screens are imperfect. Human waste feeds bacteria; bacteria feed invertebrates; invertebrates feed fish and birds; eggs and chicks feed rats, cats, snakes, and larger birds. Heat accelerates decay. Rain redistributes it. Pumps determine which pools persist long enough to hatch another generation.

There are snakes in the storm drains, mostly water snakes and rat snakes. Monitor lizards bask on concrete aprons whenever the clouds thin. Their tracks appear after showers: long-toed marks and tail furrows crossing otherwise blank mud. At night, bats emerge from the hollow undersides of bridges. They fly in such numbers that radar operators account for them as weather.

Yet from a distance the city can look nearly lifeless.

Its openness dominates the first impression. Vast graded lots hold only a transformer cage or a row of silent tanks. Roads made for heavy traffic remain empty for minutes at a time. Windowless factories sit several hundred meters apart, surrounded by flood clearance and firebreaks. Between them, animals concentrate in seams—the wet edge, the shaded wall, the drain mouth—leaving the larger spaces bare.

The rules of the place are practical and old. Water must be moved or it will claim the lower floors. Metal must be painted or replaced. Food cannot remain exposed for even a few minutes. Doors must close fully. Every pipe crossing needs room to settle. Every roof needs nesting barriers, and every barrier eventually becomes a framework for another kind of nest. Workers shake out boots, inspect vents, and never put a bare hand into a dark recess. Vehicles slow after rain, not only for standing water but for the migration of crabs across the roads.

Evening does not cool the basin much. The cloud deck lowers, catching the amber glow of sodium lamps and the dull red pulse of aviation beacons. Condensation begins to run from handrails. Concrete releases the heat it stored during the day. The tidal gates open with almost no sound, and dark water enters the flood channels.

Then the animals rearrange themselves.

Shorebirds retreat from the drowned flats. Rats descend toward the market district. Bats fill the air above the ponds. Crabs climb out of drains and gather around the warm foundations of factories. Fish move inland on the tide, followed by cormorants and the occasional seal. The city’s machinery continues at a steady, subdued rate, less a collection of noises than a pressure beneath the silence.

In the dimness, the basin becomes difficult to divide into natural and constructed parts. The channels resemble creeks but run between numbered gates. The cliffs are warehouse walls. The caves are culverts. The warm springs are cooling-water outfalls. The rookeries occupy cranes, antennas, balconies, and rusting pipe racks.

It is an entirely ordinary place in the sense that nothing here violates nature. It is simply a landscape whose usual green layer is absent, leaving industry, weather, mineral surfaces, and animal appetite exposed. The city persists because it pumps, seals, repairs, and cleans. The animals persist because the city never does those things perfectly.

Between the two lies the wet, hot, weathered basin: mostly empty, almost quiet, and alive in every crack.
