### The Summit Seam

Near the crown of a young volcanic range, above the ordinary cloud deck and beyond the last trace of soil, a seam has opened in the mountain.

It is not a cave in the usual sense. It is scarcely large enough to enter. From outside, the opening resembles a black cut between two freshly split plates of obsidian, narrow enough that snowmelt should bridge it and dust should clog it. Yet warm daylight shines from within. Anyone placing an eye to the crack sees depth where the mountain has no room to contain it: a steep, wet country folded into a space no wider than a hand.

The seam lies at an extreme elevation, where the exposed ridges are flayed by wind and the air should be thin and cold. Inside, it is hot. Moisture condenses immediately on stone, skin, shell, and lens. Every surface sweats. Water threads through the black rock in silver capillaries, gathers in trembling beads, and descends into pools that seem deep despite being only thumbprints across.

The heat comes from the mountain’s recent making. Walls of volcanic glass retain a dull internal warmth. Pale veins of sulfur and salt cross the obsidian like nerves. In places the glass has not fully settled into solidity; it gives slightly under pressure and slowly restores itself, leaving no mark. Tiny fractures appear with razor edges, gleam for an hour, and seal again beneath mineral deposits. The landscape is young enough that erosion has barely softened it. Needles, fins, splinters, and conchoidal shelves crowd the interior. Water has begun its work, but has not yet learned patience.

There is no vegetation. No moss films the wet stone, no lichen stains it, no roots penetrate from the barren summit. Even the usual microbial haze seems absent. The pools remain clear except for minerals and animals.

The animals are everywhere.

At first they look like condensation moving incorrectly. Then the eye separates them from the water.

Translucent many-legged things graze mineral crusts with rings of glassy teeth. Flat, coin-sized swimmers circulate through pools in dense vertical stacks, never touching despite the lack of room. Hair-thin worms stretch between droplets, using surface tension as if it were solid ground. Pale shelled creatures crowd beneath overhangs, their bodies too large for their shells and folded through geometries that should injure them. Minute black hunters stand motionless on obsidian points, each holding its forelimbs apart like a pair of divining rods.

There may be hundreds of animals in a single bead of water. There may be one animal appearing hundreds of times.

This distinction is difficult to maintain inside the seam.

No creature is large. The greatest are no longer than a finger joint, though their reflections can fill entire pools. Their abundance is oppressive rather than spectacular: every crack occupied, every water film furrowed by passage, every warm current alive with suspended eggs or eyes or folded limbs. If a bare patch of stone appears, it is because something invisible is resting there.

Despite this density, the seam is nearly silent.

There is the faint tick of cooling glass, the intermittent release of a bubble, the whisper of water slipping through pores. Animal motion produces little more than a soft granular pressure, like fingertips moving over damp sand. Shells touch without clicking. Wings, where wings occur, beat without audible air. Even predation is quiet. A hunter opens; nearby motion ceases; afterward there is only one fewer pattern in the water.

The silence is partly physical. The hot, saturated atmosphere damps vibration, and the enclosing rock drinks sound. But it is also one of the seam’s rules: noise does not travel outward from its source. Each sound remains close to the object that made it. To hear the cracking of an egg, one must bring an ear almost against the egg. To hear running water, one must follow the stream until one’s face is wet with its spray.

Daylight enters from no consistent direction. It is neither dim nor glaring. The interior possesses the plain illumination of an overcast afternoon, revealing true colors and casting soft shadows. Closing the outer fissure does not darken it. At certain hours the water casts shadows upward, while solid rock becomes briefly transparent around the edges. The creatures respond to these changes with migrations: shells climb toward darker surfaces, swimmers descend into brightness, and the black hunters rotate to face whichever shadow has most recently appeared.

The ecology is founded not on plants but on transitions.

Some animals feed where hot water cools. Others feed where liquid becomes vapor, or where clear mineral solutions crystallize. They crop salt, sulfur, iron, and the brief electrical charges released when fresh glass fractures. A soft-bodied grazer can remain for days beside a weeping rock, its mouth fitted over the boundary between wet and dry. When the boundary shifts, it shifts with it.

Energy passes through the seam as heat, pressure, charge, and phase change. Flesh has evolved to catch these things directly. Blood, where it exists, contains suspended metallic grains. Many bodies are transparent because pigment is unnecessary; their internal organs shimmer with mineral plates and bubbles of compressed vapor. Death leaves almost nothing. A carcass loosens into clear gel, then water, then a brittle lace of salts consumed by the next procession.

The animals display a rudimentary use of tools, though they build nothing. Certain six-limbed hunters test fallen obsidian flakes, selecting them by edge and balance. They carry the flakes only long enough to cut open shelled prey, then return them to precisely the places where they were found. Others use sulfur grains to cloud a pool before crossing it, or set droplets vibrating to dislodge creatures from the underside of the water’s surface.

No nests remain. No walls are raised. Nothing accumulates into settlement or ruin. The seam tolerates action but resists construction. Objects deliberately stacked slide apart. Scratches close. Markers are moved by condensation. A shaped fragment becomes natural again once released, its worked edge indistinguishable from a fracture made by heat.

The creatures appear to understand this. Their craft is immediate, temporary, and almost ritualistically erased.

Water governs the seam’s cycles. Several times each day—though “day” is unreliable inside—the pools begin to rise without receiving any visible inflow. Droplets swell along the walls until they touch, merging into suspended sheets. Channels reverse. Steam condenses downward in slow, heavy pearls. The animals rearrange themselves in silence, predators and prey moving together toward higher glass shelves.

Then gravity changes its preference.

For several minutes, every drop pulls toward the largest nearby body of water rather than toward the mountain below. Pools climb walls to join one another. Rivulets arc through open air. Creatures caught between competing droplets stretch into threads, vanish, and reappear intact in whichever pool wins. The whole interior becomes a web of liquid lenses, each reflecting a slightly different version of the seam.

In some reflections the rock is older and rounded.

In others it is still molten.

In one, commonly seen but never entered, the fissure contains no animals at all.

Afterward, water falls normally again. Fresh glass surfaces are exposed. Eggs appear in cracks that were empty before. There is no storm, no flood damage, and no noise beyond a close, private pattering.

Distance behaves similarly. A ledge visible a few centimeters away may take hours to reach with a probe, while a pool deep in the seam can sometimes be touched immediately by reaching behind oneself. The landscape does not expand uniformly; it folds according to attention. The more closely one examines a feature, the farther its interior recedes. Under magnification, a droplet reveals shores, currents, and crowded transparent migrations. Look away, and it becomes a bead again.

This may be why the place remains hidden. It is not simply concealed by its small entrance. It is overlooked because it occupies the act of looking closely. Broad surveys pass across it. Maps reduce it to unbroken rock. Instruments report only heat, moisture, and a minor fracture near the summit.

A hand held near the opening feels warm rain.

An ear placed against it hears almost nothing.

But an eye held there long enough may see the black glass ridges continuing inward, wet and sharp beneath ordinary daylight, crowded beyond counting with quiet lives. Far down—or very near—a silver pool gathers itself against gravity. Around it, thousands of translucent creatures wait with found shards in their limbs, prepared for the next rearrangement of the world.
