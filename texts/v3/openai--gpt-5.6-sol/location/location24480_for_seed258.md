The country lies on a high, warm tableland where the ground is not ground.

From a distance it resembles a broad green plateau interrupted by shallow basins, low ridges, and widely spaced towers. There are no mountains sharp enough to break the horizon, no deep valleys, and almost no exposed stone. The land rolls gradually outward for many miles beneath a pale, ordinary sky. Sunlight falls cleanly here. Clouds pass. Shadows have familiar proportions. It is only when one steps onto the plateau that its construction becomes apparent.

Every surface is vegetal.

The apparent soil is a densely interlocked mat of living root-plates, black-green and slightly elastic. They overlap like enormous scales, each one broad enough to carry a grove. What looks like gravel consists of hard seed nodules set into the mat. The ridges are braided trunks laid horizontally and fused together. Cliffs are walls of pale cork, their faces ribbed by growth rings that run vertically rather than inward. Even the watercourses have been made: gutters lined with waxy leaves, cisterns grown as hollow bulbs, spillways formed from ranks of smooth, downward-pointing roots.

Nothing here has been cut, stacked, carved, fired, or fastened. There are no tools and no remains of tools. The place is nevertheless entirely built.

The plateau’s makers are the plants themselves—or, perhaps, a single plant society expressed through many specialized bodies. Buttressed stems serve as walls. Arched boughs make long, empty galleries. Curtains of pendant leaves divide one open tract from another. At intervals, immense column-trees rise alone from the root pavement, each separated from the next by hundreds of paces. Their crowns spread into thin horizontal disks, leaving the spaces beneath them open and warm. Vegetation fills everything without making the landscape crowded: it is substrate and boundary, roof and drainage, but seldom clutter.

There is nowhere to stand that is not on something alive.

Rain is frequent, though rarely violent. It gathers on the high canopy disks and runs down their fluted trunks in permanent silver threads. Moisture condenses along the cool undersides of broad leaves, so that even under clear skies the galleries drip. Shallow canals follow the plateau’s contours, meeting in round green pools whose walls contract almost imperceptibly around the water. The pools are clear at their edges and tea-dark at their centers. Beneath them, pale roots hang in parallel ranks, neither waving nor growing, each holding a bright bead of water at its tip.

The water is responsible for most of the sound.

It falls through hollow stems that amplify each drop. It drums on taut leaf membranes. It passes through reedlike ribs and produces deep, sustained notes. Every basin has its own pitch. Every spillway contributes a constant rush. Across the open country these voices overlap into a broad, steady roar, like a cataract heard from within a building. Yet there is no main waterfall to find. The noise belongs to the entire landscape. It is evenly distributed, without approach or recession, and after a while silence becomes difficult to imagine.

The air is warm but not oppressive. Heat rises from the dark root paving, while mist remains close to the canals and the bases of the towers. The result is a layered atmosphere: sun-warmed shoulders, cool ankles, droplets gathering on hair and skin. The smell is green and mineral, though there are no minerals in sight—crushed fern, wet bark, stagnant rain, and something faintly metallic released by the red sap that seals every damaged surface.

Damage is uncommon. Change is rarer.

At first the plateau seems youthful, as if it has only just begun to acquire terrain. Its channels are shallow. Its slopes are blunt and undecided. Along the margins of the basins, pale rootlets protrude like scaffolding awaiting a future layer. There are embryonic ravines no deeper than a handspan, complete with miniature terraces, undercuts, fans, and deltas. The place appears to be in the first instant of erosion.

But that instant does not pass.

Water has run over these surfaces longer than any witness could reckon, and still the gullies remain newly suggested. Droplets strike the same leaf hollows without enlarging them. Streams pour over soft-looking lips that never wear away. Fallen water returns through hidden vascular channels and emerges again above, in precisely the same volume. Nothing silts up. Nothing is carried downstream. The landscape possesses all the gestures of erosion without its consequence.

Here, process is architecture.

A crumbling bank is a kind of root grown in the shape of collapse. A fan of apparent debris is one continuous organism, patterned to resemble scattered fragments. Cracks open into darkness but never widen. Loose plates tilt from walls and remain forever on the verge of falling. Even wet streaks on the cork cliffs are fixed arrangements of translucent moss, though real water runs over them. The whole plateau has been constructed to look unfinished: a world permanently raw, permanently emerging, permanently one season away from becoming a recognizable landform.

No one knows whether it is ancient or newborn. Those categories may not apply.

Animal life is rare enough to feel accidental. Small white gliders sometimes cross the open basins, using the constant updraft from the warm root mat. They have no visible wings; thin petal-like membranes unfold from their sides only when airborne. In the canals live narrow, transparent creatures that resemble moving cracks in the water. One may pass under the surface without disturbing it. Far apart in the tower crowns, heavy-bodied pollinators produce a low knocking call, always in sets of four. They are more often heard than seen.

There are no grazing herds, no swarms, no rot, and almost no predation. Dead matter is absent. A leaf that loosens does not fall; its stem softens, folds, and is taken back into the branch. Spores descend only as far as the root pavement, where they vanish into receptive pores. The ecosystem does not consume itself in the usual way. It circulates.

Paths cross the plateau, although no travelers made them. They are strips of smooth, springy bark bordered by low fronds. Some run directly between the solitary towers. Others curve around empty spaces containing nothing obvious at all. At junctions, flower heads face outward like signs, but their markings have no stable meaning to an observer: rings within rings, branching stains, rows of seed sockets. There are stairways grown into gentle slopes, even where the incline requires no stairs. Bridges span canals narrow enough to step across. Vast roofed halls stand open on every side, sheltering only mist.

The made quality of the place is undeniable, but its purpose remains obscure.

Perhaps the empty halls were intended for bodies that never arrived. Perhaps the widely separated towers are houses, each occupied on a timescale too slow to observe. Perhaps the canals are writing, their bends forming sentences legible only from the sky. The plateau gives an impression not of abandonment but of readiness held indefinitely: water running, chambers aired, passages clear, surfaces washed by rain.

At the edge of the tableland, the living construction forms a long pale escarpment. Its woven roots descend into cloud. Water streams from thousands of openings in the wall, making the loudness there almost physical. Looking over, one sees lower terraces through the mist—more platforms, more isolated towers, more circular reservoirs—repeating at intervals until distance erases them.

The escarpment seems fragile. Fresh. Its layers are cleanly exposed, as though the whole high country rose only yesterday.

Nothing falls from it.

Nothing grows over it.

The rain continues, the roots hold, and the plateau remains forever at the moment of its own completion.
