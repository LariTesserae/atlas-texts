The place lies below the surrounding fields in a shallow, stone-walled hollow, no larger than a railway yard. From the rim it appears almost level: a dark floor crowded with low ridges, iron housings, sediment fans, and hundreds of narrow channels reflecting the ordinary gray of the sky. Nothing rises high enough to become a landmark. The whole basin seems pressed down, held close to the water table.

It is difficult to tell whether this was once a factory or whether the factory grew around something already happening here.

Water is everywhere, though seldom deep. It films the paving stones, gathers beneath warped plates, threads through seams in the clay, and hangs in beads from the undersides of pipes. Rain remains for days. Even in dry weather the walls weep steadily, each block darkened along its lower edge. There are sumps in which the water is clear enough to show bolt heads and pale mineral crusts several handspans below. Elsewhere it is opaque with iron, the color of weak tea or old blood.

The basin is densely occupied. There are flanges, valve wheels, collapsed ducts, ceramic insulators, lengths of rail too short to lead anywhere, and thick cables entering the ground at uncertain angles. Some pieces are recognizably industrial; others have been worn until their purpose has disappeared. Rows have slumped into arcs. Corners have rounded. Concrete has softened into aggregate, exposing pebbles polished by decades of runoff. Every surface has another surface beneath it, and beneath that, apparently, another: brick under steel, steel under silt, silt over old tiles marked with numbers.

Yet there are few plants. A skin of moss survives in protected joints, nearly black when wet. Wiregrass grows in isolated clumps where grit has accumulated behind obstructions. There are a few translucent reeds around the deepest sump, but they never spread. The water is too mineral, or the floor moves too often, or something below trims their roots. Lichen stains the upper walls in gray circles. From a distance these sparse growths look less like vegetation than residues of weather.

The machinery does not run in any conventional sense. No motor roars; no belts turn. Most of the housings are open to rain and packed with sediment. Nevertheless, the yard is never still.

Changes begin without warning. A channel abruptly reverses direction. Water climbs a shallow slope, hesitates, and drains through a crack that was dry moments before. A patch of gravel shivers and arranges itself into tight crescent ridges. Steel plates lift by the width of a finger as pressure moves below them, then settle with exquisite slowness. In one corner, a bank of reddish mud repeatedly builds itself against the wall and collapses, builds and collapses, as if worked by an invisible tide.

After rain, this activity becomes turbulent. The floor divides into dozens of small watersheds, each competing for the same few centimeters of descent. Runnels cut through silt, clog with washers and stone chips, burst sideways, and erase one another. Jets rise briefly from old inspection holes. Fine bubbles race beneath sheets of standing water. Heavy objects migrate: a wrench found beside the eastern wall may be embedded near the central drain by evening, though no continuous trail connects the two positions.

The basin contains constant motion but almost no noise. Water slips rather than splashes. Gravel rearranges with a dry, intimate ticking. Now and then a buried chamber gives a single low knock, felt through the feet more than heard. The loudest events are often the smallest: a drop striking an empty drum, a strained wire releasing its tension, the papery collapse of a mineral shelf.

This quiet makes the movement seem less mechanical and more geological. The yard behaves like a machine that has slowed until its cycles are mistaken for erosion—or like erosion that learned, long ago, to use valves and conduits.

Everything here has reached a weathered balance. Nothing is pristine, but little is simply ruined. Rust thickens to a certain depth and then flakes away. Channels wander within established territories. Walls lean but do not fall. Even the sudden upheavals leave the basin recognizably itself. It has practiced instability for so long that instability has become its settled form.

There are signs of former attendance. A narrow hut stands near the rim, built from green-painted panels and anchored by unusually deep footings. Inside are a desk, a rack of glass gauges, and a wall chart covered in vertical lines. The labels have faded, but the chart continues to mark itself. Moisture condenses along the paper in dark columns, each corresponding roughly—but never exactly—to a region of the yard below.

A metal walkway extends from the hut for twenty meters and ends over open mud. Its continuation may have been dismantled, or swallowed. Along its railing hang small numbered tags. They turn occasionally despite the absence of wind.

Near the western wall is the only structure still receiving electricity: a cabinet with a warm amber lamp above its door. The lamp burns steadily in daylight and darkness. Inside, relays click at long intervals. Their wires descend into water. No switch appears to control them, and opening the cabinet causes every moving channel in the basin to pause for several seconds. When the door is closed, the water resumes, but in a different arrangement.

Animal life is scarce. Small white flies gather under the walkway during the mildest weeks. Once or twice a year, a long-legged marsh bird lands on the rim, watches the ground shift, and leaves without feeding. There are no fish in the sumps. There are, however, thin trails in the sediment—paired grooves that emerge from one pipe and vanish into another. They may be made by cables dragging beneath the surface.

The air remains cool even in summer. It smells of wet stone, ozone, clay, and the faint sweetness of decaying insulation. Condensation forms on exposed metal before it forms on skin. In the afternoon, when the cloud cover thins, the basin is plainly lit: no dramatic gloom, no theatrical shafts of sun. The normality of the light makes every impossible adjustment easy to see.

Certain rules become apparent with time.

Water never leaves by the same route twice in succession.

Objects bearing numbers move more readily than unmarked objects.

Any straight trench begins to curve within a day.

Nothing placed at the exact center remains there overnight.

And although the basin fills during storms, it never overflows its wall.

Instead, at the height of heavy rain, when every channel is choked and the floor convulses with hidden pressure, the water level stops rising. The surface tightens. Reflections become unnaturally sharp. For a few minutes the entire crowded yard—rails, mudbanks, housings, broken gauges, drowned bolts—appears motionless.

Then some new cavity opens below.

The water draws down without a sound, carrying silt, fragments, and perhaps whole sections of the buried works into another layer. By morning the floor has changed again. New plates show through the mud. Old channels are gone. A stair may appear where no building stands, descending three steps into clear water and ending at a sealed iron door.

No one knows how deep the yard is.

Its boundaries are small and definite: four walls, one hut, one gate, a patch of low sky. But beneath that modest enclosure, the place seems to be manufacturing its own ruins without end.
