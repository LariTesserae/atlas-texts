## The Unfinished Escarpment

The city stands at an altitude the world has not yet fully agreed upon.

From the low country it appears to occupy the summit of a black mountain range: terraces, foundries, cable towers, and long rows of dim windows drawn across the upper atmosphere. From within the city, however, the ground seems nearly level. Streets run for miles through shallow grades. Freight locomotives move without strain. Rainwater, when there is rainwater, cannot decide which direction is downhill.

Surveyors continue to measure the discrepancy. Their brass instruments give elevations that vary not by weather or error but by where the numbers are written. A reading spoken aloud may place a platform eight thousand meters above the plains; the same reading entered in a ledger puts it below sea level. The survey office therefore employs two staffs: one to measure the city and another to measure the measurements. Neither has produced a reliable map.

The terrain itself is very young. Not young in the familiar sense of sharp volcanic rock gradually softening under wind and water, but visibly unfinished. Cliffs end in smooth, blank planes, as though their fractures have not been assigned. Slopes consist of tightly fitted mineral slabs with almost no scree beneath them. Canyons begin as hairline cracks and widen over decades, opening so slowly that warehouses are sometimes built across them before anyone realizes what they will become.

Erosion is less an old force here than an approaching idea.

There are places along the outer escarpment where future valleys can be heard inside the stone. Press an ear to the basalt and there comes a faint interior rushing: rivers that do not yet exist worrying at channels that have not yet opened. Engineers mark these murmuring seams with white paint. Buildings nearby are mounted on rails so they can be moved aside when the land eventually separates.

Nothing happens quickly. A crack may take a human lifetime to cross a street. A cornice of rock may lean farther out over the abyss by the width of a fingernail each winter. Yet the entire city is built in anticipation of these changes. Hinged bridges wait over solid ground. Drainpipes empty into shallow dents that will someday become gorges. Broad iron stairs descend directly into walls, ready for the moment the walls become slopes.

The city is spacious despite its size. It is made of districts separated by tracts of raw ground: gravel courts, black ledges, cool meadows, vacant switchyards, and fields of pale stone still warm at their centers. Clusters of habitation gather around factories and lift stations, then fall away abruptly. One may leave a crowded turbine hall and walk for an hour without seeing another person, though smokestacks remain visible on every horizon.

Most buildings are constructed from dark local materials: columnar basalt, blue-gray metal, cloudy glass, and concrete mixed with powdered mica. Their surfaces hold the weak light and give little back. Roofs are steep but asymmetrical, designed for winds that sometimes blow vertically. Many structures have no obvious ground floor. Entrances open from aerial walkways, cliff elevators, buried tram tunnels, or platforms reached by cage lifts.

There are towers whose upper stories are excavated rather than built. Workers enter them at the base, ascend through ordinary steel floors, and eventually emerge into rooms cut from solid underground rock. This is considered an engineering problem, not a philosophical one.

The industrial quarter stretches along the city’s highest apparent edge. Its mills refine ore extracted from seams that rise slowly through the mountain. The miners do not dig downward. They wait in low galleries for the deposits to arrive, shaving away a few centimeters each month as veins of copper, nickel, and an opalescent mineral called duskglass migrate upward through otherwise motionless stone.

Duskglass is used everywhere. It conducts electricity reluctantly and gravity extremely well. Set into lift machinery, it allows enormous freight platforms to descend without falling. In streetlamps it gives off a weak violet haze, no brighter than twilight reflected from snow. The city’s power grid hums at the edge of hearing, carried on heavy ceramic insulators and cables that sag between pylons like black vines.

The factories operate continuously but slowly. Vast wheels complete one revolution every few minutes. Presses descend with the solemnity of astronomical events. Conveyor belts carry single components through halls large enough to develop their own fog. A finished engine may take six years to pass from one end of an assembly works to the other. No one sees this as inefficiency. Fast-made objects behave unpredictably here: bolts forget their threads, gears arrive before their teeth, and sheets of metal retain the memory of ore.

Thus machinery is allowed time to become certain of itself.

The city is loud, but not frantic. The sound is immense and spaced out: a hammer blow from a distant forge, followed by ten seconds of echo; the groan of a funicular climbing through cloud; chains settling in deep lift shafts; warning horns rolling from district to district. Each noise travels too far in the cool thin air. A dropped wrench may be heard several valleys away, often before it is dropped.

Between these mechanical reports comes the animal life.

The escarpment is crowded with creatures even where it looks empty. Dark-winged gliders circle the towers in hundreds, almost invisible until they pass across a lamp. They resemble birds at a distance, though they have translucent ribs and no fixed number of wings. At rest they fold themselves into cracks in masonry, becoming flat enough to be mistaken for stains.

Long-legged cliff hares browse on lichen among the rail lines. Their feet divide into gripping fingers when they step onto vertical surfaces. They are common in warehouses, where they climb pillars to lick condensation from cold pipes. Freight crews tolerate them because they scream several minutes before a cable breaks.

There are also chimney stags, ash-colored animals with branching hollow antlers. They gather around foundries and stand with their heads in the exhaust, filtering spores, metallic dust, and small airborne insects from the smoke. At dusk their antlers begin to resonate, producing a low chord that mingles with the dynamos. Herds move slowly between industrial districts, obeying roads that no longer exist and stopping at intersections buried under newer construction. Traffic still yields to them.

In damp hollows live colonies of soft black amphibians called bellbacks. Drops of water striking their skin produce clear metallic notes. After rain, whole basins ring quietly as thousands of them emerge from the soil. The sound can last for days because rainfall does not always arrive in chronological order. A storm may wet one roof on Tuesday, the street beneath it on Thursday, and the air between them the following week.

Water is present everywhere, though seldom in abundance. It beads on steel. It gathers under stones. It runs briefly along gutters and disappears into faults before forming proper streams. High in the lift towers, condensation forms shallow interior ponds where blind fish graze on electrical algae. Some elevator operators keep nets beside their controls.

The city’s main river exists only intermittently. The channel, a broad unfinished trough called the Hesitant, crosses three districts and passes beneath seven monumental bridges. Most of the year its bed is occupied by markets, grazing animals, tram spurs, and temporary dwellings. When the river appears, it does not necessarily come from rain. Sometimes water simply begins accumulating in the channel, clear and bitterly cold, until stalls are dismantled and rails winched onto the banks.

The rise takes weeks. The river remains for a season or perhaps an afternoon, flowing with such composure that floating leaves barely turn. Then it thins from the bottom upward, leaving fish suspended for several minutes in empty air. Children wait with baskets to catch them. The largest are carried to roof cisterns, where they live until the river returns.

Vegetation grows in concentrated pockets. Most open ground is sparsely colonized by low moss, silver grass, and isolated shrubs bent into the prevailing ambiguity of the wind. But wherever moisture persists—beneath leaking mains, around cooling towers, inside abandoned stations—the plant life becomes sudden and extravagant.

Fern courts fill entire factory basements. Thick ropes of vine descend through ventilation shafts and blossom in the heat above boilers. Alpine trees grow from narrow ledges with roots extending into open air, branching downward until they touch another surface. Their needles are nearly black and gather dimness rather than light. In winter the trees shed shadows, leaving dark, leaf-shaped patches that drift across snow and feed small grazing insects.

The most fertile area is the Condenser Ward, where warm vapor from the power works meets the cold upper atmosphere. Here buildings vanish behind curtains of moss. Orchards occupy roof after roof, their trunks planted in beds of crushed brick. Pale fruit swells without direct sun and tastes faintly of iron. Walkways remain perpetually slick, and the air smells of wet stone, machine oil, mushrooms, and ozone.

Beyond this green district the city opens again into exposed platforms and enormous views.

On clear days the lower world can be seen, though never all at once. Plains appear through gaps in cloud like pieces of another planet. Far below, weather spreads in broad layers: rain falling upward toward the escarpment, lightning moving horizontally inside blue-gray banks, sunlight trapped beneath the overcast. At night the lowland towns seem to hang overhead. Their lights form unfamiliar constellations, and city residents use them for navigation.

The sun itself is rarely visible. Illumination arrives diluted through several strata of cloud, smoke, altitude, and geological uncertainty. Noon resembles late evening. Shadows are weak near the ground but become darker with height; a tower’s upper shadow may hang above it like a second, inverted structure.

Artificial light is similarly restrained. Brightness attracts gaps.

No one knows exactly what the gaps are. They appear as narrow vertical absences, usually in overlit rooms, where surfaces cease to connect. Looking into one reveals neither darkness nor distance, but a missing relation between nearby things. A hand passed through may emerge holding an object lost years before, or may become temporarily irrelevant to the arm.

For this reason, factories use rows of low amber lamps. Streets are lit by hooded duskglass beacons. Citizens carry small phosphorescent insects in wire lanterns. Darkness is not considered dangerous; clarity is.

The rules of space weaken toward the city’s rim. Alleys sometimes run beside themselves. A person walking east can meet their own westbound footprints while continuing forward. Two neighboring houses may share a wall without sharing a side of it. Delivery workers learn routes as sequences of smells, sounds, and textures rather than directions: coal smoke, three iron steps, dripping arch, red door, sound of bees.

Time is more stable, though not entirely. Slow processes remain dependable. Moss grows. Metal fatigues. Mountains separate. Animals migrate. But sudden events can become misplaced. Explosions are particularly troublesome; the noise may occur years before the damage. The municipal archive keeps a register of unexplained detonations so engineers can prepare for their eventual causes.

Births and deaths are also handled carefully. Every newborn is carried outdoors and introduced to the horizon, ensuring that the city includes them. The dead are taken to low stone houses beyond the last tram stop, where their names are engraved only after the letters stop rearranging. In rare cases a name migrates onto another marker. Families then negotiate kinship accordingly.

Despite these conditions, or because of them, the city is not a ruin and does not consider itself cursed. It is practical, patient, heavily administered. There are permits for constructing across prospective ravines, licenses for keeping backward-falling rain, inspectors of impossible staircases, and public notices announcing expected revisions to gravity. Tram conductors carry tuning forks to determine whether a station is physically present.

The inhabitants dress in layered wool, oilskin, and weighted boots. Many wear small bells at the ankle, not to announce themselves but to verify that the next step has occurred. They speak quietly under the immense industrial din. Their buildings are maintained. Their markets are crowded when the riverbed is dry. Schools teach geology as a future tense.

From the outermost platforms, the city seems both monumental and provisional. Cranes stand against the dim sky, their booms reaching over cliffs that have not finished becoming cliffs. Lift cages descend into cloud with ore, livestock, passengers, and bundled trees. Far below, hidden machinery gives a single deep concussion. Flocks rise from the roofs, turning slowly, their uncertain wings overlapping like scraps of night.

Then the echoes travel outward across the unfinished range.

They cross cool meadows and raw stone, condensation gardens and silent bridges awaiting water. They enter cracks no wider than threads. Deep in the mountain, where valleys are still only possibilities, something hears the noise and begins—almost imperceptibly—to open.
