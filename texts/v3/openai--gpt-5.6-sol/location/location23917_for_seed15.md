Beneath the public reservoir, behind a row of fitted limestone gates, there is a second, smaller basin known only to the masons and water-keepers. It is entirely submerged. Its ceiling lies several body-lengths above its floor, but the warm water fills every part of it, pressing into tool marks, hinge sockets, drainage grooves, and the narrow seams between blocks.

Nothing here is natural ground. The floor is made from broad slabs of pale limestone laid over brick vaulting. Low terraces descend through the chamber in deliberate steps, giving it the shape of a tiny drowned valley. Ridges, channels, pits, and ledges imitate terrain, but all were established by mallet, chisel, wooden straightedge, and wet cord. Even the loose material is manufactured: heaps of broken tile, ceramic beads, lime chips, and coarse fired-clay grit have been placed in shallow beds to catch sediment.

The work is new. Corners remain sharp. Chisel strokes are visible beneath the wavering light, repeated in orderly fans where the builders dressed the stone by hand. Mortar bulges from some joints, not yet worn flat by the current. Here and there, red construction marks persist under the water: lines brushed in iron pigment to guide the placement of blocks. The basin has not had time to acquire the softened, rounded character of old waterworks. It still looks recently opened, almost unfinished.

Sunlight enters through a grid of vertical shafts. Their upper mouths stand in the main reservoir’s open court, where polished bronze plates and white plaster collect the sun. Below, the shafts pour brightness into the hidden chamber. The water breaks the beams into moving nets, intensely blue-white over the limestone and green-gold over the brick. There are few true shadows. Even recesses hold a diluted glow reflected from pale surfaces. Air bubbles passing through the beams flash like chips of glass before vanishing against the ceiling.

The chamber is warm, fed by shallow sun-heated canals and by a mineral spring captured farther uphill. Heat gathers beneath the ceiling and along the western wall, where incoming water runs through narrow clay pipes. The masonry there is almost hot to the touch. Cooler water enters near the floor through square sluice mouths, so the basin is layered with subtle currents: warm water drifting upward, cooler threads slipping across the steps, small spirals forming around posts and corners.

It is never still. Wooden gates in the outer works are raised and lowered by rope, stone counterweight, and human effort. Each adjustment sends a pulse through the chamber. Water surges down the terraces, lifts the beds of ceramic grit, and carries pale clouds from one catchment to another. The clouds settle quickly, leaving thin crescents of lime dust behind ridges. In this way, the constructed landscape is already beginning to alter, though only slightly. Its erosion is young: a fresh notch at the lip of a channel, a bright scrape where a loose tile shard has worried the plaster, a few grains gathered in the lee of every raised edge.

The noise is disproportionate to the chamber’s size. Water entering through the clay conduits produces a constant hard rushing. Sluices thump in their frames. Wooden pegs knock against stone. When a gate opens above, the entire basin answers with a deep, enclosed roar, felt through the ribs as much as heard. Between surges comes the sharper hiss of bubbles escaping mortar pockets and the intermittent clatter of ceramic fragments rolling down a step. Sound travels cleanly through the water and returns from every wall, so no movement seems to come from a single direction.

There is no animal life. The water is too new to the basin, too warm, too mineral-rich, and too frequently drained and refilled. No fish have been introduced; no snails cling to the walls; no larvae tremble in sheltered corners. The absence is complete enough that the smallest drifting leaf appears conspicuous.

Plants survive only where the water-keepers intended them to. A few clay troughs are mortared into the upper terraces, each containing gravel and a knot of water grass. Their blades are long, pale, and sparse, streaming horizontally whenever the gates open. Thin green films have begun to form around the brightest shaft mouths, but they remain patchy, rubbed away during maintenance with bundles of reed fiber. In one protected recess, a tuft of soft algae covers several thumbprints left in wet plaster. It is the nearest thing here to wilderness.

The chamber’s purpose is practical. It is a settling room, a pressure-break, and an inspection space within a larger system of cisterns. Water entering the city must pass through this miniature terrain. Heavy grit drops into the first hollows; lighter sediment is carried over successive ledges; floating matter collects beneath removable wooden grates. Water-keepers descend through a dry access shaft when the basin is emptied, shovel the trapped sediment into baskets, replace cracked tiles, and reseal joints with lime paste. Their tools are simple, and their engineering is legible everywhere: wedges, pegs, plumb lines, ceramic pipe, gravity, leverage, patient fitting.

The rules of the place are those of weight and flow. Nothing moves without water moving it. Nothing remains hidden forever from the repeating surge. Loose fragments migrate toward the lowest square pit. Sediment records each opening of the gates in delicate bands. Plants point toward the latest current. Warmth rises. Bubbles seek the ceiling and collect in silver pockets beneath shallow vaults until disturbed, when they race along the stone and burst into the ventilation shafts.

Though fully constructed, the basin does not feel like a room. Underwater, its steps become escarpments, its drainage furrows become ravines, and its brick piers stand apart like sparse towers in a bright flooded plain. Yet the whole landscape is intimate enough to cross in a few strokes. One can see nearly all of it from any point: the pale terraces, the scattered troughs of grass, the dark sluice mouths, and the radiant columns descending from above.

It is a small world made entirely by hand, recently surrendered to water. Every day the machinery beyond its walls sends warmth, light, pressure, and noise through it. Every day the sharp artificial valley becomes, by a fraction, more like something water has always known.
