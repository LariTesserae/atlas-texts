High on the windless side of a black escarpment, above the level where ordinary clouds could form, there is a narrow cleft known only by the noise it makes through the stone.

The opening is scarcely wider than a doorway. It lies behind a fan of weathered slate, folded so neatly into the cliff that from any distance the mountain appears unbroken. Beyond it, the passage descends a few steps and then opens into a vertical chamber: a hidden garden suspended inside the summit.

The chamber is not large. A person could cross its floor in thirty paces, if there were any floor left to cross. Every surface is occupied. Growths crowd the walls, the ceiling, the old machinery, and one another until the interior resembles a room packed with dark foliage from which the room itself has almost disappeared.

There is no water here. The stone is cold and utterly dry. Dust, when disturbed, does not clump; it rises as individual silver grains and hangs in the electrically charged air. No dew forms on the leaves. No channels mark the walls, and no roots seek buried moisture. The plants belong to a chemistry that does not require it.

They are plants only by the broadest definition. Some unfold thin vanes of carbon-black tissue, pleated like radiators. Others rise in compact groves of coppery rods, each tipped with a velvet pad that drinks the chamber’s weak electrical field. Mineral vines cling to the rock with hundreds of ceramic hooks. Their leaves are overlapping scales of green-gray crystal, cool to the touch and harder than fingernails. There are pale cushions resembling moss, though each tuft consists of hollow glass filaments filled with slow-moving sulfur compounds. Here and there stand thick trunks with bark like braided wire. None bear flowers. Nothing needs to attract a pollinator, because there have never been animals in the cleft—not insects, not worms, not even the minute grazers that elsewhere turn gardens into ecologies of pursuit.

Reproduction occurs by contact. A mature growth presses against a neighboring surface and, over decades, persuades that surface to become more like itself.

The result is lushness without softness. Vegetation fills the chamber in tiers, dense enough to hide the underlying cliff, but it neither rustles nor bends. Its crowded canopies have been locked in the same positions for longer than the scattered structures among them have had names.

Those structures suggest an industrial purpose. At the rear of the cleft, half enclosed by mineral trunks, stands a squat converter housing made from riveted plates. A ladder climbs its face and ends abruptly in the foliage. Porcelain insulators project from the roof like blunt white antlers. Thick conduits pass from it into boreholes in the mountain, while narrower wires vanish beneath mats of crystalline leaves.

There are other remains: a pressure dial without numbers, a row of empty ceramic sockets, the corroded frame of a small lift, a cabinet whose door has been held open by roots. A metal walkway crosses the chamber at shoulder height, although only three sections remain visible. The rest has not collapsed; it has simply been enclosed, every rail and grid absorbed into the crowded growth.

Nothing appears recently abandoned. The steel has reached the final state of its corrosion and proceeds no further. Bolts are furred with stable oxide. Scratches in the paint have softened at the edges, not from continuing weather but from some ancient interval of wear that ended long ago. Dust rests everywhere in perfectly established quantities. Even loose fragments seem to have chosen permanent places.

The cleft is dim. A narrow seam in the roof admits a fixed gray illumination, too weak to cast clean shadows. The light never tracks across the chamber. The mountain’s upper ridges and the geometry of the opening hold it at the same angle throughout the day, while the high, pale sky above changes too little to matter. Much of the garden feeds not on light but on voltage, heat differentials too small for human skin to register, and the chemical generosity of the rock.

Yet the place is loud.

A single industrial note fills it: a deep, layered drone that seems at first to come from machinery still running somewhere below. It presses against the ribs and makes the teeth ache. Higher tones inhabit it like narrow ledges within a canyon—the whine of a transformer, the ringing of taut metal, a dry choral vibration from thousands of hollow stems.

Nothing moves to make these sounds.

The converter contains no turning shaft. The old fans are fixed in their housings. Belts have become rigid loops. The crystalline foliage stands motionless, even where the entrance should admit a draft. The noise is a standing condition of the chamber, as constant as its dimensions. The mountain, the conduits, and the densely rooted garden form one resonant body. Charge passes through it without measurable variation. Stone answers metal; metal answers leaf; leaf answers stone. The result is neither increasing nor fading. It has no pulse and no cycle. It is simply there.

Close to the walls, the drone becomes so exact that smaller sounds disappear. A footstep is swallowed before it finishes. Speech can be felt in the throat but barely heard. If a pebble is dropped, its impact enters the prevailing note and loses its individuality.

At the center of the chamber, where the growth is most compressed, rises a narrow column of pale vegetation around a black iron mast. The column reaches almost to the roof seam. Layer upon layer of translucent fins are fitted around it, each separated from the next by less than a finger’s width. They catch the dimness and return it as a faint green internal glow. There may be thousands of fins. None are young and none are old. Broken edges have sealed into smooth, dark rims. Their arrangement is so regular that it is difficult to tell whether the machine trained the plant or the plant constructed the machine.

The garden’s extreme elevation is perceptible only near the entrance. Beyond the slit lies an abrupt emptiness: ranges far below, flattened by distance; salt basins like dull mirrors that reflect nothing; a sky thinned almost to black. The cleft itself feels enclosed and intimate, but its threshold hangs over several vertical kilometers. A dropped object would vanish without an audible landing. No bird passes the opening. No airborne seed arrives. The outer cliff is sterile, scraped clean by ancient exposure, making the packed interior seem still more impossible.

The local rule appears to be that nothing enters and nothing leaves. Matter circulates only in place, though “circulates” implies more activity than can be observed. The plants do not visibly grow. The machinery does not visibly decay. Chemical exchanges occur in exact balance: one surface releasing what the next binds, one mineral darkening as another becomes pale. Even the air is held in a stable composition, sharp with ozone, hot-metal bitterness, and the dry scent of crushed stone, though the temperature remains cool.

Anyone squeezing into the chamber becomes its only changing element.

The garden does not react. No leaf turns. No mechanism wakes. There are no eyes to notice, no mouths to open, no defensive spores, no curious organisms creeping from beneath the plates. Still, the density of the place creates an impression of attention. Every possible niche has already been filled. Every exposed edge participates in the fixed resonance. A visitor’s warmth, breath, and moisture are foreign disturbances, small but profound.

Condensation will not remain here. A damp fingerprint on the converter vanishes quickly—not by evaporation, but by being separated and taken up. Hydrogen moves into the black vanes. Oxygen enters the mineral bark. The mark disappears, and the chamber returns to dryness.

Afterward, perhaps, there is no evidence that anyone came.

Only the uninterrupted note inside the summit, the industrial garden packed tightly around its dead or living works, maintaining a perfect equilibrium in the dark.
