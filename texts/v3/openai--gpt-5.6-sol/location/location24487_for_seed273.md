The mountains are manufactured.

They rise in long, bright ranges from one horizon to the other, not as imitations of mountains but as the accumulated consequence of construction: retaining walls built upon retaining walls, foundries roofed by reservoirs, housing terraces laid over obsolete mills, bridges thickened into districts, districts buried beneath newer districts. At a distance the slopes look geological. Close by, every cliff resolves into masonry, riveted plate, ceramic drainage tile, poured aggregate, glass block, and old structural concrete polished by generations of rain.

There is no exposed earth. Even the lowest ravines have floors of fitted stone or iron decking, and what appears to be soil is manufactured loam held in immense planter beds. Roots never enter anything that was not first designed to receive them.

The elevation is severe. Roads ascend by switchbacks cut into the faces of inhabited buttresses. Stairs climb until they become streets, then climb again as ladders bolted between neighborhoods. Freight elevators move through open shafts several kilometers deep, their counterweights passing slowly behind grilles. Inclined railways connect hot lower wards with the sunlit crowns. From the upper terraces, the built range falls away in green, rust-red, and pale concrete folds, vast enough that distant towers blur into the warm haze.

Yet the place is lush.

Vegetation occupies every horizontal surface that can retain moisture. Fig trees spread from courtyard cisterns. Bamboo fills old ventilation galleries. Ferns hang beneath aqueducts, and flowering vines soften the cables of suspension streets. Roof orchards crowd the stepped slopes in bands of citrus, guava, mulberry, and broad-leaved shade trees. Some industrial halls have lost their roofs and become deep gardens whose columns rise through the canopy like the trunks of a second, older forest.

This growth is neither wilderness nor ornament. It is part of the machinery of the range. Root beds filter workshop water. Groves cool intake air before it reaches the factories. Reed terraces clarify runoff, and thick mats of moss seal hairline cracks in cistern vaults. Gardeners work alongside mechanics. Pruning schedules are coordinated with turbine maintenance and monsoon forecasts.

Water is always present but seldom abundant. Rain comes in brief, warm bursts, hard enough to whiten the upper stairs and drum across a million metal roofs, then vanishes into the structure. Every surface is graded toward capture. Gutters pour into settling chambers; settling chambers feed buried tanks; overflow travels down tiled channels toward the lower reservoirs. Between rains, condensation does much of the work. At dawn the cold pipes sweat. The undersides of bridges bead with water. Nets stretched across wind gaps comb moisture from passing cloud, dripping patiently into narrow troughs.

The resulting landscape is damp in pockets rather than soaked throughout. A stair may descend from a dry, glaring plaza into a corridor green with liverworts. One side of a tower is powdery with dust, while the shaded side supports orchids and snails. Water collects in inspection sumps, fountain courts, abandoned boiler rooms, and long black tanks under translucent roofs. There are no true rivers, but several great drainage ways behave like them after rain, running openly through the valleys in broad ceramic channels. Most days they hold only braided trickles among sedges and maintenance walkways.

The climate is warm, especially below. Heat from the factories gathers in the lower chasms, where air shimmers above exhaust towers and the railings remain warm through the night. Higher districts receive strong wind and brilliant sun. Their whitewashed walls are almost painful to look at at midday. Mirrors, skylights, and pale paving carry that brightness deep into the range, illuminating courts hundreds of meters below the nearest open sky.

Shade is correspondingly dense. Under the upper bridges lie whole dim climates: humid neighborhoods of machine shops, mushroom galleries, animal shelters, laundries, and fern-covered conduits. Brightness enters these spaces in shafts and reflected bands, touching one balcony or one patch of leaves while leaving the surrounding structure in bronze gloom.

Everything is old enough to have settled into itself. The concrete is rounded at corners by feet and weather. Iron plates bear layered scales of paint, rust, and new paint. Drainage channels have acquired smooth, dark interiors. The oldest brick arches sag visibly but have been supported for centuries by additional arches, each reinforcement becoming a foundation for something else. Nothing is pristine, and almost nothing is ruinous. Decay here is anticipated, measured, patched, and incorporated.

Erosion occurs, though it is the erosion of a made world. Rain eats mortar. Wind scours paint from exposed towers. Roots lever apart parapets, and mineral deposits build pale curtains beneath leaking pipes. On the busiest stairs, millions of soles have hollowed metal treads into shallow bowls. Replacement is gradual. A district may spend fifty years exchanging its corroded beams one at a time without ever closing.

The industrial life of the range is equally continuous and unhurried. Large machines turn behind walls: pumps, cable drums, pressure fans, ore crushers, grain mills, refrigeration engines. Their purpose is often less dramatic than their scale. They lift water, circulate air, haul compost, grind salvaged masonry, and move freight between elevations. Workshops manufacture standardized parts for structures so old that their original standards have been forgotten. Foundries melt down railings, pipes, broken tools, and worn elevator teeth, returning the metal to the same landscape in altered form.

The sound is loud, but rarely abrupt. It is a layered, enduring loudness. Ventilation fans breathe through louvered towers. Funicular cables hum under tension. Pumps knock in deep, regular sequences. Freight trains grind around distant curves. Water ticks from leaf to leaf and rings in metal basins. In the inhabited terraces come voices, radios, market bells, hammering, dogs, and the rattle of wheeled carts over drainage grates.

No single source dominates. The whole range sounds occupied.

Animals thrive in the density. Swifts and pigeons fill the bright upper air, nesting in cornices and cable housings. Parakeets raid fruit terraces. Kites circle above warm exhaust columns. Geckos live behind sun-heated tiles; tree frogs gather near condenser outlets; snakes follow the frogs into reed beds and service ducts. Bats inhabit disused chimney stacks and emerge in streams at dusk. Monkeys travel the garden belts, using pipes and handrails as forest paths, watched with irritation by orchard keepers.

Below, in the water channels, small fish survive wherever flow remains steady. Dragonflies patrol the settling pools. Crabs occupy damp access chambers far from any open reservoir, feeding on algae and kitchen scraps washed down from above. The fauna has learned the operating rhythms of the place. Birds arrive when grain elevators vent. Frogs begin calling when condensation pumps shut off. Rodents know which freight lifts carry food and at what hour.

People live everywhere between these systems. Homes are fitted into bridge piers, along reservoir walls, above machine halls, and beneath orchard terraces. Some districts are spacious, arranged around sunlit courts with trees and public cisterns. Others are compressed into narrow vertical lanes, where doors open onto stairs and balconies nearly touch across the gap. Markets occupy level places wherever the architecture briefly permits them: old loading platforms, broad aqueduct roofs, the turntables of retired rail depots.

The inhabitants navigate by elevation as much as direction. Addresses include terrace, shaft, windward face, and distance above the nearest water line. Two buildings that stand side by side may be an hour apart if no bridge joins their levels. A journey across the landscape is a sequence of ascents, traverses, lifts, and descents, with entire climates encountered along the way.

The central rule of the range is that no surface is merely a surface. A roof catches water and supports trees. A wall bears a railway and contains ducts. A plaza is the lid of a reservoir. A garden is a filter bed. A stair is also a spillway. Even the monumental towers on the summits, visible for days of travel, are not symbolic structures: they are wind scoops, pressure regulators, and lightning collectors.

Another rule is that weight must always descend and water must always be persuaded to do the same. Much of civic life is organized around these facts. Inspectors mark load paths in colored paint. Children learn the meanings of drainage symbols before they learn distant geography. During rain, certain stairs become channels and certain doors must remain shut. When a warning bell sounds from the upper terraces, awnings fold, sluices open, and foot traffic shifts to dry routes with the ease of long practice.

Change comes slowly. A new bridge may take a generation to complete because it must attach to structures still in use. A grove advances along a terrace by a few planters each year. Neighborhoods migrate upward as lower levels grow hotter, then return when new cooling galleries are opened. The range is never finished, but neither does it feel under construction. Building is simply its form of weather.

At evening the brightness lingers on the uppermost faces while the ravines darken. Factory windows appear in vertical constellations. Garden lamps shine through leaves. Warm air rises from the depths carrying oil, wet masonry, yeast, flowers, hot metal, and the green smell released when rain strikes sun-heated vines.

From a high balcony, the landscape seems almost natural again—not because it resembles an untouched world, but because it has become too old, too extensive, and too thoroughly inhabited to feel like an object. Its ridges govern wind. Its valleys collect weather. Its mechanical forests make their own shade and humidity. Birds cross it by remembered routes. Water has worn paths through it. Generations have been born without ever seeing a stone that was not placed by someone’s hand.

Far off, another bank of warm rain moves across the manufactured peaks. One by one, the pale roofs disappear behind it. The pumps continue. The cables continue. The gardens receive what water they can.
