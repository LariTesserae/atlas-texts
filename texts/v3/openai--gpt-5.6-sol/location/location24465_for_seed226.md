The place occupies a high shelf on the shoulder of a young mountain. It is not much larger than a walled district or a monastery precinct, yet it has the organization of a city: lanes, cisterns, courtyards, stairways, public platforms, drainage channels, and rows of narrow houses. From the opposite ridge it resembles a dense settlement. Once inside, however, its emptiness becomes apparent. The buildings stand far apart behind walls and arcades. Broad courts contain only one tree. Long galleries serve three rooms. Streets meet in plazas large enough for crowds that have never gathered.

The mountain beneath it is still being made.

Its rock is pale, porous, and almost unworn. Rounded flows bulge from the ground like dough left beneath a cloth. Mineral water rises through these new formations and leaves skins of white, green, and rust-red stone wherever it passes. Runnels build their own banks grain by grain. Steps become taller rather than lower. Gutters slowly close. In some courtyards, entire thresholds have been swallowed by smooth deposits, so the inhabitants have cut new doors above the old ones.

Nothing here looks ancient in the ordinary way. There are no deep ravines, no polished boulders, no long talus slopes. Cracks are sharp and shallow. Cliffs have not yet acquired ledges. The few exposed faces are raw, as if split that morning, though some have remained unchanged for generations. Soil exists only where moss, root fiber, ash, and windblown dust have gathered in hollows. The land is not being ruined into a landscape; it is accumulating toward one.

Water is everywhere without forming a great lake or river. It beads on roof beams, clouds the undersides of stone arches, and slips from seams in the mountain. Rain comes frequently but seldom violently. More often, clouds settle directly over the shelf, and every surface begins to sweat. Walls darken from the bottom upward. Fine droplets appear along ropes and carved lintels. Roofs drip long after the air has cleared.

The largest spring emerges in the upper quarter of the settlement. It does not gush. It wells silently into a square basin lined with hammered clay, then divides into dozens of channels. These channels cross streets on low bridges, pass through kitchens and dye yards, feed garden beds, and finally disappear into holes along the outer wall. On cold mornings their water steams faintly, not because it is hot, but because it carries the interior warmth of the mountain into the chill air.

Around this network grows an improbable abundance of green.

Ferns crowd every damp stair. Moss thickens the backs of walls until their masonry is visible only at corners. Small trees with silver bark root in deliberately constructed pits, each surrounded by a low circular bench. Their crowns are dense and dark, pruned flat beneath the wind. Vines travel along rope trellises between houses, making green roofs over otherwise empty lanes. There are broad-leaved herbs in the drainage ditches, red-stemmed reeds in the lower courts, and cushions of tiny alpine flowers pressed close to the raw rock outside the walls.

The vegetation is lush but not wild in the lowland sense. It cannot spread far beyond the wet shelf. A few paces uphill, the mountain becomes gray and nearly barren. A few paces downhill, the ground breaks into fresh stone domes where roots cannot yet enter. The settlement is a bounded pocket of fertility, held together by waterworks and constant hand labor. If the channels were neglected for a season, one quarter would drown in moss while another dried to powder.

The houses are made with simple means. Their lower courses are rough blocks levered into place with poles. Upper walls are woven from willow, reed, and tough mountain grass, then coated with clay and mineral paste. Roofs rest on forked timbers and are weighted with flat stones. There are no great cranes, no wheeled engines, and little worked metal. Stone hammers, antler picks, wooden wedges, bronze knives, fired-clay pipes, and braided lifting slings are sufficient.

Yet the result is unmistakably urban. Construction is coordinated across the whole shelf. Streets follow measured grades. Water rights are marked by notches in channel stones. Shared ovens stand beneath vented towers. Storehouses occupy the dry southern wall. At the center is an open assembly court, ringed by tiered seats and sheltered alcoves. Clay tablets are kept in wall niches there, recording repairs, planting turns, and the changing heights of the streets.

Much of the apparent city is scaffold.

Some towers have stone only at their bases and lattices of timber above. Walls stop abruptly, their ends protected with bark caps against the rain. Doorways open onto unbuilt rooms. A stair climbs to a platform from which survey cords stretch across empty air. The inhabitants build in anticipation of ground that does not yet exist. They know where new mineral terraces will thicken, where roots will eventually stabilize wet sediment, and where the mountain’s slow swelling will lift a foundation into alignment.

Here, architecture may take longer than a human life. A family begins a wall knowing its grandchildren will place the roof. A public stair is constructed from the top down while the earth rises beneath it. Buildings are not finished so much as met by the land.

This makes the settlement seem deserted even when it is inhabited. There may be only a few dozen people within its many streets. They live in scattered clusters, moving quarters as channels clog and foundations rise. In the morning one sees smoke from perhaps five roofs. A figure in a reed cloak crosses a plaza, vanishes under an arcade, and leaves the whole district apparently empty again. Tools remain stacked beside worksites without guards. Doors are often tied open so damp rooms can breathe.

Fauna is scarce. The height, cold, and enclosing cloud keep most creatures away. A few black-winged birds nest in the vent towers, arriving singly rather than in flocks. Pale moths shelter beneath bridge stones. In the warmest channels live tiny eyeless crustaceans, translucent except for a red thread down the center of their bodies. They are regarded as evidence that the mountain’s water is still healthy. Larger animals are visitors: an occasional goat on the upper rocks, a long-legged fox at dusk, once or twice a year a migrating beast seen as a moving dot far below.

Because animal life is rare, movement attracts attention. The flutter of one bird can draw faces to windows. A moth striking a lamp is gently carried outside. Tracks in wet mineral silt are covered with an overturned bowl until everyone who wishes has inspected them.

The place is loud despite its emptiness.

Wind passes through the unfinished towers and makes them bellow in different tones. Clay pipes set into walls thrum when clouds press against the mountain. Water drops through vertical shafts into buried cisterns, producing deep, regular impacts that can be felt through the soles of the feet. Loose roof slates clack. Trellises groan. Channels hiss where they narrow around new deposits.

The loudest sound comes from the outer wall. Its stones contain many natural cavities, and when the prevailing wind crosses them the whole boundary hums like a vast, badly tuned instrument. The notes change slowly as mineral crust thickens inside the holes. People use this sound as a calendar. A certain low resonance means the western cistern will soon need opening. A thin whistle from the north wall warns that ice is forming in the shaded aqueducts. Children are taught the district’s tones before they are taught its paths.

There are few sudden events. Even the noise is patient.

Change arrives by accumulation: a basin shallower each year, a roof beam furred with moss, a lane lifted by the thickness of a fingernail each wet season. The mountain does not erupt or shudder. It presses upward almost imperceptibly. Survey pegs set into the ground lean by degrees. Water finds a different seam. A courtyard that once drained east begins, over several decades, to drain west.

The people accommodate this rather than resisting it. They do not speak of permanent foundations. Every structure includes sacrificial joints, removable wall panels, and courses of loose stone that can be taken apart when the ground changes. Furniture is low and portable. Shrines are built on sledlike wooden frames. Pipes are joined with waxed cloth rather than rigid fittings. The most respected craft is not building but adjustment.

By midday, when the cloud lifts, the light is clear and ordinary. Colors become surprisingly strong: copper-red channels, white mineral lips, blue-gray roofs, wet black timber, innumerable greens. The shelf looks less mysterious then, almost domestic. People spread blankets to dry. Gardeners clear moss from the water gates. Builders tap wedges into new stone. Steam disappears from the springs.

The view beyond the wall is immense, but the inhabited world remains small. Far below are old valleys carved into mature shapes, dark forests, and rivers that have had ages to choose their courses. They seem to belong to another phase of existence. Here, on the high shelf, no course is settled. The streets, plants, water, buildings, and rock are all provisional companions.

Toward evening the cloud closes again. Distance vanishes. The district contracts to the next archway, the next dripping tree, the next lamp in an unglazed window. Sounds enlarge in the white air. The wall begins its low song. Water strikes the cisterns. Somewhere, a stoneworker continues a measured tapping after the light is gone.

The settlement is neither ruin nor foundation. It is a city held in the long moment before completion, built on land that has not finished arriving.
