The land is low enough that the horizon rarely presents itself. There are no summits from which to see the whole, no clearings broad enough to reveal the sky. Yet the place is continental in extent. It continues for thousands of kilometers as a single, dark, vegetated plain, rising and falling by only a few meters, making its own pressure, its own warm weather, its own almost imperceptible circulation of water.

There is no bare ground.

What appears at first to be soil is a tightly braided felt of black roots. What looks like rock is older rootwood compressed until it rings faintly under pressure. The shallow scarps are not mineral cliffs but pale, cut faces of cork, cellulose, and glassy resin. Pools lie in depressions between enormous woody plates. Their water is warm, tannin-dark, and perfectly still, with fine green filaments stretched across the surface so densely that droplets can rest upon them without falling through.

Vegetation does not grow *on* the landscape. Vegetation is the landscape.

The uppermost layer forms a roof, although it has no trunks in the familiar sense. Vast laminae overlap like scales, each leaf many kilometers across and thin enough at its edges to tremble beneath the weight of condensed water. Their undersides are matte black. Their upper surfaces—almost never visible from below—are said only by their function: they receive whatever faint radiation filters down from the opaque atmosphere and convert it with extraordinary efficiency. No daylight penetrates to the interior. Beneath the leaf-roof, darkness is complete except where living tissues make their own illumination.

That illumination is sparse and functional. Along certain veins, rows of blue-white points ignite without flickering, marking flows of charge through the plant body. Deep in the root felt, amber seams disclose reservoirs of warm sap. Occasionally a broad wall of tissue becomes translucent, and behind it slow columns of luminous fluid stand like furnaces seen through smoked glass. These lights do not brighten or dim. They have held the same intensity for longer than any local process can measure.

The plain is warm, but not hot. Moisture gathers continuously. It condenses on every downward-facing surface, swells into beads, and hangs there. It gathers along leaf ribs in ruler-straight lines. It pearls on thorns sharp enough to seem newly formed. It weeps from vertical faces of pith and runs into narrow capillary trenches, yet the dripping is so regular that it scarcely constitutes change. For every drop that falls, another reaches the same size in the same place. Pools remain at fixed levels. Films of water creep through fixed routes. The atmosphere is neither drying nor raining; it exists in a permanent state just after rain and just before rain.

There are marshes, if a marsh can have no mud, reeds, insects, or open sky. Their floors are buoyant quilts of bladder tissue. Columns rise from them—hollow, jointed growths with broad flanges near their bases—and vanish into the overhead darkness. Warm water circulates inside these columns. Some carry mineral-rich fluid upward from buried strata. Others return cooled water downward. Around them, carpets of fine cilia comb dissolved metals from the seepage and pass them root to root.

Nothing here is constructed, but much is industrial.

The continent behaves as a factory without buildings, workers, or manufactured parts. Its refineries are glandular groves. Its pipelines are veins with walls thicker than fortress masonry. Its electrical grid is a mesh of conductive fiber extending beneath the entire plain. Its storage reservoirs are transparent fruits sunk into the substrate, each containing a precise separation of oils, acids, sugars, salts, and metallic colloids. Catalytic membranes perform processes that elsewhere would require blast furnaces and pressure vessels. Heat is exchanged through forests of black fins. Gases are sorted molecule by molecule in immense folded sacs. Dense nodules of iron and manganese grow inside root chambers in identical hexagonal arrays, not as artifacts but as organs.

There are no engines in the usual sense. There is, however, engine behavior.

Pressure travels through annular muscles around the largest sap channels. Electrical potential accumulates across layered membranes. Vast beds of starch are broken down and restored in a closed cycle. Somewhere far beneath the accessible surface, geothermal warmth enters the living mass. Somewhere above, the leaf-roof catches a thin and unwavering energy from the dark sky. Between those boundaries the whole continent maintains itself in equilibrium.

It produces nothing that leaves.

No waste heaps accumulate. No smoke rises. There are no roads, cranes, towers, vents, rails, cut blocks, or tool marks. Every output becomes an input before it can be called refuse. Carbon moves in sealed circuits. Water is lifted, filtered, condensed, and returned. Metals migrate from microscopic binding sites into structural ribs and back again. Even heat is detained in nested layers of wet fiber until it has done the maximum possible work.

The silence is correspondingly immense.

There are no wings, calls, feet, mandibles, lungs, or heartbeats. No animal has ever grazed these surfaces or bored into the wood. Nothing pollinates the plants; nothing eats them; nothing carries their seeds. There are no sudden alarms in the undergrowth because there is no undergrowth in which one organism hides from another. The dense mass contains many presences—columns, mats, blades, bulbs, threads, membranes—but they are not competing inhabitants. They are differentiations of one botanical continuum.

If one listens closely, there are quiet sounds. Water tightens around fibers with a minute creak. Sap under pressure gives the occasional glassy tick. Charged veins produce a low electrical murmur that may be felt more readily than heard. From very far away comes a soft, sustained tone, as if wind were passing over the mouth of a colossal bottle. There is no wind nearby. The air is warm and still. The tone is transmitted through the root body from some pressure organ beyond the local horizon.

It never changes pitch.

The surfaces look young. This is one of the landscape’s more troubling qualities. Edges are keen and unweathered. Fresh pale tissues show along every seam. Resin stands clear and glossy in angular fractures. Root ridges rise abruptly from the flats as if thrust upward yesterday. There are no rounded stones because there are no stones exposed, but neither are there softened forms. No surface bears the fatigue of age.

Yet nothing is growing into anything else.

A pale edge is not a wound healing; it is maintained as an edge. A split in the woody floor does not widen or close. The sharp hooks surrounding a water basin remain at the same angle, each holding the same burden of condensation. A suspended root tip, glossy and red-black, never quite reaches the mat beneath it. It seems to be in the act of descending, but it has occupied that unfinished gesture indefinitely.

The ecosystem possesses activity without development. Fluids move, charges pass, compounds transform, but the large arrangement remains exact. It is less like a forest persisting through time than like a single physiological instant extended across an era.

This constancy governs distance as well. A traveler—if animal travelers were possible here—might expect the plain eventually to transition into drier ground, upland, coast, or true swamp. It does not. There are variations, but no succession. A million-column marsh grades into a field of low overlapping plates; the plates gather into vaulted thickets; the thickets open around ink-dark reservoirs; and beyond the reservoirs the columns begin again. Each region is hundreds of kilometers wide. Together they form repeating organs on a planetary scale.

The largest reservoirs have no visible opposite shore. They resemble black seas, though their depth is often less than a person’s height. Their bottoms are lattices of living ribs. At intervals the water wells upward through circular pores, spreads in rings, and descends elsewhere without raising a wave. Islands float on them: thick green-black pads bearing forests of hairlike absorbers on their undersides. The islands do not drift. Their anchor fibers bend in the water but retain precisely the same arcs.

Above these seas, the roof lifts beyond perception. The darkness becomes spatial rather than merely dim. Condensation hangs in strata. There may be cloud inside the vegetation, though no movement reveals its boundaries. Occasionally the far distance is exposed by a silent transmission of bioluminescence: a blue line passes through the high canopy from horizon to horizon, illuminating the silhouettes of pendant roots longer than mountain ranges. For an instant the scale becomes comprehensible, and then the light settles back to its former level—not extinguished, but hidden again inside opaque tissue.

No stars are visible. There may be a sun, but locally it has no rising or setting. The upper leaves mediate it into constant power. There may be seasons outside, but they do not enter. The continent has removed itself from weather by becoming larger than weather. Its warm vapor makes a climate and then holds that climate still.

The absence of fauna is not felt as emptiness. On the contrary, the place is crowded almost to suffocation. Fine roots fill hollows in the greater roots. Membranes fill the gaps between stalks. Films fill the pores in membranes. Suspended threads divide droplets into smaller droplets, and still smaller organisms—if “organisms” is the right word—occupy the water as chlorophyllous dust. Every volume has been claimed by plant function. Even the darkness seems fibrous.

But there is no struggle for space. The shoots do not lean toward light; light is allocated internally. Roots do not race toward water; water is already everywhere and routed according to the larger body’s needs. No flower advertises itself. No fruit entices. There are blossoms, but they open inward, inside sealed chambers, exchanging genetic material through pulses of sap. There are seeds, but they are not cast away. They remain embedded as dormant archives: dense knots containing alternate plans for a world that has not altered enough to require them.

The rules here are quiet and absolute.

Nothing consumes without restoring.

Nothing grows unless something of equal volume withdraws elsewhere.

No process may leave an unbalanced gradient.

No shape is permitted to become obsolete.

Water may move, but shorelines may not.

Energy may pass, but the total visible state must remain unchanged.

The result is not death. It is an almost intolerable abundance held at perfect industrial readiness.

At the deepest accessible layer, beneath the marsh mats and root plates, the plant matter becomes pale and hot. Great vertical sheets stand close together, wet as gills, disappearing downward through cracks in young, sharp mineral crust. Here the botanical continent meets the newly formed world beneath it. Rock is still dark and angular, its fractures clean. Roots line each fracture without eroding it. They sleeve the stone, taste it, draw measured quantities from it, and then reinforce the edges with resin so that the fissure retains its original shape.

This may be why the whole place appears newly emerged: the land truly is young, but its youth has been arrested. The vegetation reached it at the first stable moment and enclosed that moment, preventing erosion, succession, and decay. It captured a fresh planet the way resin captures an insect—except there was never an insect, and the resin remained alive.

Whether the continent has a center is uncertain. The low electrical murmur suggests coordination, but not intention. Signals pass through the conductive mesh at steady intervals. They do not carry news; there is no new information to carry. They may simply affirm the existing state from one extremity to another: water level correct, pressure correct, temperature correct, mineral balance correct, darkness intact.

Across thousands of kilometers, the answer returns.

Nothing has happened.

Everything is operating.
