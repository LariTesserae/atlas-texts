### The Basin of Close Houses

The settlement occupies an entire shallow basin, from rim to rim, though the basin is small enough that a person standing on one edge can distinguish laundry cords, roof ladders, and the pale movement of hands on the opposite side. There is no countryside within it. The last natural ground disappeared generations ago beneath floors, cistern mouths, retaining walls, stairs, drains, and the foundations of houses pressed so closely together that their outer walls have become one continuous structure.

It is a city in the dimensions of a grove.

The basin itself is young. Its sides have not yet softened into hills, and rain exposes fresh planes of gray clay and blue-black stone beneath the buildings. Where construction has failed to cover them, these surfaces retain abrupt edges: little scarps, raw seams, and narrow cracks that seem to have opened only yesterday. The inhabitants say the ground is still deciding what shape to take.

It lies low, under a cool sky. Mist collects here even when the lands beyond the rim are clear. Water is seldom dramatic, but it is everywhere: beaded on door lintels, darkening plaster, whispering behind walls. The stone sweats before rain. Springs push upward through cellar floors. Roofs are made not to repel water but to delay and guide it, passing it from house to house through stepped ceramic gutters until it reaches one of the public settling jars.

There are hundreds of these jars. Some are taller than a person and built permanently into intersections; others are small bowls set beneath weeping cracks. Their rims are chipped smooth by ladles. In cold weather, vapor rises from them and hangs among the alleys, so the lower city appears filled with pale breath.

Nothing has room to stand alone.

Houses share walls, roofs, hearth flues, and sometimes rooms. A stair begun in one household may cross two roofs before descending into another family’s courtyard. Alleys narrow to shoulder-width and then become tunnels, passing under sleeping platforms or through the thickness of buttressed walls. Doorways face doorways at the distance of an outstretched hand. Public space survives only as a few irregular chambers in the urban mass: the Reed Court, the Potters’ Hollow, the Steps of Rain, and the cool central concavity called the Empty Place.

The Empty Place is not truly empty. It is packed belowground with cisterns.

Construction is mostly clay, stone, timber pegs, woven reed, rawhide, and fired ceramic. Metal is known but scarce enough to be ceremonial. A bronze chisel may belong to an entire street and be signed out by knot-cord. Ordinary cutting is done with flaked stone, shell blades, abrasive sand, and black glass traded from far away. The city’s astonishing density is not supported by complex machines but by habit, measurement, and communal labor. Every wall has a keeper. Every drain has a name. Children learn water routes before they learn distant geography.

The plaster gives the city its color. Freshly applied, it is nearly white; after weeks of damp it turns pearl gray, then greenish at the base, then black where mineral water seeps through. Repairs accumulate in ridges and handprints. Some walls have been patched so many times that their surfaces resemble flowing wax. On cool mornings, droplets gather along these ridges and travel predictable paths. People place cups beneath favored ones.

Vegetation is sparse and deliberate. There is no soil to waste on ornament. Moss forms in thin bruised patches around drain mouths, but it is scraped away and dried for packing wounds. Pale rushes grow in rooftop troughs, their roots filtering wash water. A few households cultivate bitter leaves in broken pots filled with dredged silt. The basin’s only tree stands against the northern rim, bent nearly horizontal by the weight of platforms built around it. Its leaves are small, dark, and few. No one remembers whether it was planted or survived the city’s founding.

Because green life is scarce, every appearance of it is noticed. A weed rising between two paving slabs may be marked with chalk and watched for days before the council decides whether it is medicinal, edible, structurally dangerous, or merely taking water.

Animals are rarer still. Small translucent frogs sometimes emerge from the oldest cisterns after prolonged rain. They make no call. Their presence is inferred from wet prints and the occasional trembling of the water’s skin. Gray insects live in plaster cracks, feeding on mold. Thin-bodied birds visit the roofs during migration but seldom descend, unsettled perhaps by the absence of open earth.

There are no draft animals in the basin. Loads move on backs, sledges, rollers, and hand-to-hand chains. Refuse, pots, roof tiles, water, and stone travel through the city with surprising speed, passed between neighbors who may never see the beginning or end of the route.

For such a crowded place, it is mostly quiet.

Stone hammers are wrapped in hide. Potters work with damp clay and bone tools. Speech in the tunnels is subdued because sound carries through shared walls and cistern chambers. The city’s ordinary music consists of drips, the soft scrape of sandals, gutter water ticking into jars, clay lids being lifted and replaced, and the occasional low instruction traveling along a work line.

Loud sound is treated almost as a material spill. A shout can cross half the basin by way of the water conduits. A struck jar may hum through adjoining walls. During heavy rain, when every channel fills, the city becomes briefly and overwhelmingly vocal: roofs hiss, drains gulp, overflow spouts knock against stone, and buried reservoirs answer one another with resonant booms. No one tries to speak then. Families sit beneath their strongest lintels and listen to the city measure the storm.

Water determines law.

No household owns the rain that falls on its roof. It belongs first to the roof below, then to the street cistern, then to the basin. Blocking a gutter is considered a form of theft. Allowing a wall to leak into a neighbor’s sleeping room is a debt; allowing it to leak into a public channel is maintenance. New construction is permitted only if its runoff can be traced with dyed water all the way to a settling jar.

The inhabitants believe that water has memory but no loyalty. It remembers every path it has taken and will return to old routes if neglected. This belief is practical as much as sacred. Beneath the present city lie earlier drains, sealed rooms, and abandoned channels. After unusual storms, water sometimes reopens them. A dry wall begins to weep. A stairwell fills from below. Clear water emerges from a plaster face several houses away, carrying fine red silt from foundations no living builder has seen.

This is how the city changes. Not by expansion—there is nowhere within the rim to expand—but by internal rearrangement. Rooms are subdivided, joined, raised, flooded, sealed, or surrendered to storage. Roofs become floors. Cisterns become shrines; shrines, when their walls crack, become drains. The basin is young stone supporting an old density, and the mismatch keeps everything in motion.

Small collapses are common. They rarely resemble disasters from a distance. A wall slumps quietly in the night. Three houses wake sharing a room. A lane closes; a new lane is cut through a pantry. Fresh clay is exposed, startlingly blue and sharp-edged, and people gather with baskets, wedges, cord, and lamps. By evening the breach has been reinforced. By the next wet season it has acquired shelves, hooks, soot, family marks, and a name.

At the lowest point of the basin stands the House Without a Roof, a circular enclosure open to the ordinary sky. Its walls are thick, inward-sloping, and covered in shallow channels. All condensation formed on the stone runs toward a central depression no wider than a handspan. The water there never overflows and never dries.

No one drinks it.

At dawn, when the light is plain and colorless, a faint trembling sometimes passes across that little surface. Soon afterward, somewhere in the city, a new leak begins.

The basin people do not regard this as prophecy. They call it notice. They send quiet crews through the close houses, palms pressed to walls, listening for water moving inside the stone.
