At first the country appears to be a high, empty basin surrounded by mountains. Then the eye adjusts and finds that the basin is itself part of a mountainside, and the surrounding peaks are not arranged around it so much as below, beside, and occasionally within it.

There is no clear horizon.

The land consists of broad shelves of dark stone, each several valleys wide, suspended at extreme altitude. Their surfaces are worn smooth without being flat. Long troughs cross them where vanished glaciers once pressed into the rock, and low ribs of harder mineral stand between the troughs like the backs of buried animals. Everything bears the finished look of old erosion: no fresh breaks, no loose scree, no raw scars. Cliffs have rounded lips. Cracks have softened edges. Even boulders seem to have reached their final shapes.

Yet nothing lies at the bottom of anything. Ravines descend until dimness closes over them, while the same ravines can sometimes be seen continuing upward on the opposite side of the sky.

The cold is severe but not violent. There is no cutting wind, no advancing weather, no daily thaw. It is simply cold everywhere, evenly and permanently, as though temperature were a property of the stone rather than the air. Breath hangs pale before the mouth and does not quite disperse. Frost covers the shaded ground in a grain too fine to sparkle. Where bare skin touches rock, the chill enters slowly and seems to remain after contact ends.

Light comes from a broad gray radiance without an identifiable source. It resembles the hour before dawn, but there has never been a dawn here. Shadows are shallow and uncertain. On some shelves they lean west; on others they collect directly beneath objects; in a few places they rise from the ground as faint vertical stains. Looking upward reveals layers of cloud, but looking down into deep chasms reveals the same clouds from above.

Moisture persists in pockets. Condensation gathers on the undersides of overhangs and along vertical seams, forming beads of perfectly clear water. These beads swell but rarely fall. In hollows protected from the cold, narrow pools lie under skins of black ice. Their water tastes faintly of iron and old snow. Thin rivers occupy the broad glacial grooves, no wider than a hand in places, though the channels around them are large enough to have carried inland seas.

There is occasional rain. It can be heard more often than seen.

A steady roar fills the entire region: the combined sound of waterfalls, avalanche, thunder, and great wind passing through stone corridors. It is loud enough to tremble in the sternum. Speech must be made close to another person’s ear, though the air itself is motionless. No source can be approached. The roar neither strengthens nor weakens, and echoes do not decay. A struck stone adds one sharp note to the noise, and that note can sometimes still be heard hours later, unchanged, occupying its own narrow place in the vast sound.

At certain cliffs, curtains of water appear to pour into the abyss. Close inspection shows that they do not move. Every droplet hangs in a continuous descending shape, each silver thread arrested in exactly the form of falling. The thunder of impact rises from far below, but no water ever arrives there. Beneath other cliffs, spray climbs soundlessly upward toward falls that cannot be seen.

This is not an event. It is the condition of the place.

Vegetation is sparse and low. Black lichens grow in circular colonies on the warmer faces of stones, their rings so regular that they resemble marks made by compasses. Gray moss occupies the rims of condensation basins. It is coarse, almost metallic to the touch, and its fronds all bend in the same direction despite the absence of wind.

Far apart, perhaps one plant in several square kilometers, stand pale stalks with no branches. They rise to the knee or waist and end in a closed, translucent bladder. A drop of water can be seen inside each bladder, suspended near its upper surface rather than its bottom. The plants neither flower nor visibly decay. Their roots enter hairline cracks and emerge from unrelated cracks many paces away. Cutting one root leaves no wound; both ends continue seamlessly into the stone.

The rare animals are quieter than the landscape but no less strange.

Small white moths cling to sheltered walls, each with wings thick as felt. They are never seen in flight. If disturbed, they vanish from one patch of rock and appear on another nearby, leaving the impression that the observer missed a movement too quick to register. Six-legged browsing animals, lean and slate-colored, sometimes stand on distant terraces. They lower their narrow heads to the lichens but leave no grazed marks. A herd may remain visible for days in precisely the same arrangement. Approach does not make them flee; instead, the distance between them and the approaching traveler ceases to diminish.

In the pools live transparent larvae with dark branching organs. They twitch continuously without changing position. Their movement may be the only genuine motion in the region, though even that becomes doubtful after prolonged watching.

There are structures, but few, and nothing about them suggests a developed craft.

On exposed saddles stand rings of upright slabs. The stones are uncut, selected only for their height and narrowness, and leaned together to make low chambers. No mortar joins them. Inside, the roar diminishes just enough for a visitor to hear another sound beneath it: slow breathing, apparently coming from the mountain. The chambers contain beds of dry moss and shallow depressions polished by bodies, but no tools, ash, pottery, cordage, or bones.

Elsewhere, single walls cross empty slopes. They begin abruptly and end after twenty or thirty paces. Some are built of stacked stone; others may simply be natural strata that have learned the shape of walls. There are steps cut into one cliff, though “cut” is perhaps incorrect. Each step is deeply weathered, its corners rounded by an age of passage or rain. They ascend for a hundred meters and stop at an unmarked face of rock.

A few cairns stand beside the narrow rivers. Their stones balance in ways gravity should not permit. They cannot be dismantled. Lift the top stone and the entire cairn rises with it as one object; release it and it occupies precisely its former place without falling.

These remains do not seem abandoned. Abandonment requires a before and an after.

Distance behaves according to local custom rather than measure. A nearby outcrop may take half a day to reach. A summit seen beyond three ranges may be touched by extending a hand into certain clefts. Two travelers walking side by side can find themselves on opposite rims of a valley without ever having separated. They can still hear each other at conversational distance beneath the perpetual roar, while seeing one another as tiny figures many kilometers away.

The few reliable rules are learned slowly.

Never follow a stream in the direction of its sound. The sound is not downstream.

Do not shelter beneath an overhang bearing more than seven drops of condensation. From beneath it, the open country may no longer include the place from which one entered.

A shadow that points upward belongs to an object not present on the visible shelf. Stepping across it produces the sensation of crossing a threshold, and one may return with frost on the inside of clothing.

The stone rings are safe, though not always from the same weather. One may enter under gray cloud and look out upon stars, or enter at night and see the same dim day. Leaving restores the usual light without any sense that time has passed.

Time itself has little purchase here. Hunger comes, sleep comes, wounds hurt and heal, but the landscape does not participate. Rain is always occasional without any particular shower beginning or ending. Ice is neither thickening nor melting. Erosion is everywhere complete yet not finished. Nothing accumulates at the bases of cliffs. Footprints appear in frost but remain neither newly made nor old; return years later and they may still be present, along with several sets belonging to the same traveler approaching from directions they never took.

From the highest accessible shelf, the country opens to its full, impossible extent. Dark plateaus float at differing inclinations beneath a ceiling of cloud. Waterfalls hold their pale columns between them. Ravines repeat at larger and smaller scales. Isolated stone chambers mark passes that no road connects. The animals are pinpricks of gray, each separated from the next by tremendous emptiness. Beyond all of it rise mountains whose bases cannot be located and whose summits curve inward until they seem to support the ground.

The roar is overwhelming there. It is the sound of every change that the landscape refuses to make: all the falling water that never falls, all the cliffs that will not collapse, every storm forever withheld, every echo preserved at full strength. Nothing happens, and the noise of that nothing is immense.

Remain long enough and the body begins to expect it. Silence becomes unimaginable. Motion starts to look like a flaw in perception. The traveler’s pulse seems reckless, their breath a disturbance, each step an act of erosion in a country that completed its weathering before sequence and consequence were fully established.

And still the dim shelves continue, empty and damp, through the folded altitude—an old equilibrium above the world, stable enough to contain contradictions, cold enough to preserve them, and vast enough that they never need to meet.
