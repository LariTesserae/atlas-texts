At the highest point of the range, where the atmosphere has thinned almost past weather, a black needle rises from the ice-colored stone. It is geologically new: an extrusion without talus, its edges still keen, its flanks unsoftened by frost or time. From a distance it appears solid. No windows catch the sun. No road approaches it. The summit is too narrow for a person to stand on and too hot for snow to remain.

The city is inside.

Its entrance is not a door but a hairline discontinuity beneath the summit—a seam visible only when condensation gathers along it. Every few hours, vapor from the lower valleys reaches this altitude in a thin, exhausted cloud. Moisture beads on the black surface, runs upward for several centimeters, and vanishes into the seam. For that brief interval the opening can be found: a dark incision no wider than a coin’s edge, leading into a cavity much larger than the mountain should contain.

Beyond it lies a vertical urban hollow.

The hollow resembles a deep well crossed by bridges, balconies, conduits, and vacant platforms. Its dimensions refuse stable measurement. From one ledge the opposite wall seems an arm’s length away; from another it recedes into dimness, occupied by entire districts of fine geometric construction. A dropped instrument may strike the floor in seconds, while its echo returns hours later. Survey beams curve. Parallel lines meet and continue through one another. The place is not enormous in the ordinary sense. It is embedded: volume folded into a minute defect in the summit’s crystal structure, street upon street nested in distances too narrow to exist.

Most of the city is empty space.

Structures cling sparingly to the walls or float at widely separated heights. A single transit mast may rise through hundreds of meters of apparent void before reaching one circular station. Narrow causeways end at unsupported plazas. Towers hang point-down from the ceiling, each separated from its neighbors by expanses of warm darkness. The architecture is urban in organization rather than crowding: sectors, utilities, transit lines, reservoirs, public chambers, maintenance corridors. Yet there are almost no furnishings and no signs that bodies ever inhabited it. Doorways vary from microscopic perforations to arches large enough for clouds. Some open only onto smooth stone. Others reveal rooms whose interiors slowly rotate while their thresholds remain still.

The materials are freshly made and difficult to distinguish from geology. Walls of vitreous basalt have been drawn into planes only a few molecules thick. Pale structural ribs pass through the rock without cracking it. There are ceramic surfaces with no pores, metallic surfaces that do not reflect, and translucent blocks holding faint depths of amber light. Everything is sharp. Corners have not chipped; stairs have not been worn; rail-like edges retain the intolerable precision of cutting tools. Even where slabs have ruptured, the breaks are clean and recent, exposing luminous lattices that seal themselves grain by grain.

Heat rises from no obvious source. The air near the entrance is merely warm, but deeper districts are furnace-hot. There are no flames. Instead, temperature pools in architectural forms: under arches, inside empty courtyards, along certain abandoned routes. One can cross a bridge and feel heat gather around the ankles, then climb toward the face, as though gravity acts separately upon warmth. In the lowest visible quarter, the atmosphere trembles. Black walls soften without melting, flexing slowly inward and recovering their shape.

The city uses this heat.

Fine channels carry it upward as a transparent current. Junctions pulse with dull red radiance. Vast machines, mostly hidden behind the inner walls, alter the pressure and geometry of the cavity. Their operation can be seen in consequences rather than moving parts: a district becoming briefly nearer; a staircase acquiring an extra landing; suspended towers turning a fraction of a degree; shadows arriving before the structures that cast them. Infrastructure here does not merely move matter or information. It edits adjacency.

Water is scarce but essential. During the infrequent cloud passages, the summit gathers moisture from an immense area of sky. Droplets climb its exterior through capillary grooves and enter the city, where they are divided into silver threads. Some feed shallow basins no larger than fingerprints. Others descend as isolated beads through the central void, never joining. Because gravity varies from corridor to corridor, water collects in unlikely places: beneath ceilings, around the rims of vertical shafts, in trembling spheres tethered between antennae.

There are no rivers, only itineraries of condensation.

At the beginning of each moisture cycle, the city changes tempo. Warm surfaces exhale mineral vapor. Cool partitions emerge from walls and comb the air. A brief internal weather forms—mist in the upper galleries, metallic drizzle under bridges, dew whitening the edges of vacant avenues. For several minutes, every hidden line becomes legible. The city gleams as if sketched in liquid. Then the basins drain, the droplets separate into precise measured volumes, and the wetness is carried inward toward processes that cannot be seen.

Nothing grows here.

No lichen clouds the stone, no root investigates the seams, no insect lies trapped in a basin. The moisture bears no spores. Chemical films begin to organize on warm ceramic and are immediately disassembled by the surfaces beneath them. In a few secluded corners, granular patterns spread across the floor like frost, but they are not colonies. They are computational residues: arrangements of salt and metal that solve some transient problem, then loosen and pour into drains.

There are no animals, either, not even the pale scavengers one might expect in a warm enclosed place. Movement belongs to the city itself.

Panels flex. Bridges migrate between anchor points. Threads of dark matter-like material—visible only by the way they bend the dim amber light—pass along conduits. Small apertures open and close with no accompanying mechanism. Occasionally, an entire block unfolds from a wall: streets extruding, rooms inflating, towers assembling from planes thin as shadows. The new quarter remains for an hour or a day, vacant and fully serviced, before folding flat again.

This activity is nearly silent.

The loudest regular sound is water touching hot stone: a sparse, delicate ticking from very far away. The structural systems produce a pressure more felt in the teeth than heard. At long intervals comes a single low note, pure and immense, after which dust rises from the floors and hangs motionless. Bridges tremble without rattling. Doors dilate without scraping. Even collapses are quiet; broken structures fall into regions where distance absorbs their impact.

The city appears to be maintained for an arrival that has not happened, or happened elsewhere.

Its transit stations illuminate in sequences. Empty platforms warm to habitable temperatures, although what would find them habitable is unclear. Directional marks form on the floors, composed not of symbols but of subtle inclinations in space: to read one is to feel gently pulled toward a destination. Some routes lead to reservoirs. Some lead to observation chambers overlooking the central void. Some turn back upon themselves and return the traveler subtly altered in height, temperature, or elapsed time.

At the hollow’s apparent bottom is a plaza only a few paces across. It is the most densely constructed part of the city and perhaps its center, though no two paths place it at the same depth. Around the plaza stand hundreds of needle-thin buildings, each smaller than a finger. Their facades contain streets finer than scratches, and within those streets are further empty structures, lit by intermittent blue sparks. The city repeats inward, not as a model but as additional territory.

Above the plaza floats a dark sphere of water.

It is perfectly still despite the surrounding heat. Its surface reflects neither chamber nor observer, but a high bright landscape under a thick atmosphere: broad wet plains, dense towers, motion everywhere. The image may be a record, a forecast, or the view through some impossible aperture. Whenever the mountain outside receives rain, the sphere enlarges by an immeasurable amount. Whenever the city unfolds a new district, it diminishes.

Thus the place remains active without seeming inhabited. It harvests thin clouds, redistributes heat, folds streets out of stone, lights stations, prepares routes, and continually makes room within a summit that has no room to spare. The mountain around it is young. Its glassy faces have barely begun to weather. Perhaps the city caused the peak to rise; perhaps the peak is waste material from the city’s construction.

In the long dry intervals, moisture disappears and the internal lights sink almost to black. The hollow becomes a warm, quiet absence crossed by a few amber points. From somewhere below comes the occasional tick of a final droplet. Then the spatial machinery shifts, very gently, and another empty neighborhood moves close enough to be seen.
