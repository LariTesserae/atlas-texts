The city lies only a little above the water table, though “above” is unreliable here.

It occupies a broad, shallow basin whose far limits are lost in warm darkness. There are no hills to contain it, only long swells of mineral ground rising by the height of a doorway and descending again into flooded districts. From any roof, one can see an irregular field of towers, arcades, courtyards, and black canals extending several miles before the atmosphere closes over them. Nothing shines at the horizon. There is no horizon in the ordinary sense—only a gradual thickening, as if distance itself condenses into vapor.

The heat is constant and intimate. Stone sweats. Walls are filmed with water even when it has not rained, and every ceiling carries trembling droplets. The air smells of wet limestone, iron, old salt, and something faintly sweet released from cracks in the paving. Breathing feels like drawing air from beneath a damp cloth.

Rain comes often, but rarely from the sky.

It gathers first inside the buildings. Beads form along dry-looking masonry, enlarge, and fall upward through stairwells. In some squares the paving darkens from the center outward, and a soft vertical rain rises from the ground for an hour, disappearing into the blackness overhead. Elsewhere, water slips horizontally across open streets, each drop keeping the height at which it emerged. These sideways showers pass through colonnades and ruined chambers without wind, leaving wet circles on one wall and untouched dust on the floor.

The city is unmistakably a city, yet nothing in it bears the mark of tools.

Its blocks are organized around avenues and drainage channels. Doorways repeat at roughly bodily intervals. Steps descend into plazas; bridges cross narrow cuts; tall, hollow structures cluster near the deepest water. But no stone has been cut. No brick was fired. No beam was joined to another. The surfaces are continuous, rounded, and deeply weathered, like a cave system that happened, over immense time, to erode into streets.

Columns rise directly from bedrock, their bases spreading like calcified roots. Vaults have the smooth inner curves of shells, though there are no shells here and never appear to have been. Windows are tall perforations worn through walls by persistent threads of moisture. Whole neighborhoods resemble sedimentary reefs from which the softer portions have dissolved, leaving behind rooms and passages. The resemblance to planning is too exact to dismiss, but there are no inscriptions, no tools, no refuse, no graves, and no evidence that anyone built or inhabited the place.

Some buildings continue below their foundations. A stair may descend through water for forty steps, emerge dry on the underside of the street, and then rise into the same courtyard from which it began. Certain doors open not into rooms but into weather: a warm drizzle, a bank of mineral fog, a darkness crossed by distant lightning that produces no illumination. Closing the door does not always put the wall back where it was.

The oldest quarters are the lowest and most settled in form. Their walls have been softened by ages of seepage into massive, patient curves. Gutters have deepened into canals; canals have undercut plazas until the remaining pavements form broad shelves over still water. The city does not look ruined so much as completed by erosion. Every collapse has long since found a stable resting place. Fallen arches have fused to the ground beneath them. Rubble has dissolved into terraces. Cracks have widened into purposeful-looking lanes.

Water stands everywhere but seldom in abundance enough to make the place lush. It occupies narrow channels, shallow courts, cellar-mouths, and long black pools under the streets. In some districts it lies just beneath a translucent mineral crust. The crust bows under its own weight, so slowly that the movement can be seen only by watching the reflected darkness change. Occasionally a whole square gives a low, almost inaudible sigh and sinks the width of a finger. Water wells around its edges, perfectly clear and almost hot.

There are plants, but they are widely separated and difficult to regard as a community.

Pale reeds emerge from flooded stairwells one stalk at a time, with several paces of empty water between them. Their stems are jointless and cold despite the heat. On certain walls grow flat black fans resembling scorched leaves. They drink condensation through their upper surfaces and retreat into hairline fissures when touched by rising rain. In open plazas, isolated ropes of red moss trace the seams between slabs. They grow only along cracks that do not lead anywhere.

Nothing flowers. Nothing fruits. There are no insects among the reeds, no fish in the canals, no birds under the vaults, no mites in the wet dust. The water contains no visible life. Even decay seems absent. Fallen plant matter does not rot; it slowly loses thickness until it becomes a stain, then a change in the direction of the stone’s grain.

The absence of animals is larger than silence. The city has countless niches, ledges, burrows, pools, and sheltered eaves, all empty. There are no droppings, bones, tracks, webs, nests, gnaw marks, or shed skins. Nothing startles and flees. Nothing watches from the windows. Rooms remain open without having been entered.

Sound does not travel normally.

Close noises are subdued: dripping water, the soft mineral tick of cooling walls, the whisper of condensation crawling down plaster-smooth stone. Footsteps, if there were footsteps, would seem absorbed after two or three paces. Yet events from very far away sometimes arrive with impossible clarity. A slab settling in a district beyond sight may be heard as a single deep knock directly beneath one’s hand. Rain falling miles away may murmur inside a sealed room. The loudest regular sound is made by the canals when their currents reverse: a long inward breath moving from quarter to quarter.

There is no sun and no moon. The sky, when visible between the towers, is a dense black plane without stars. Darkness does not vary with time, but it has local depth. Some streets are merely unlit; others seem filled with a darker material that pushes against the skin and rounds off the corners of buildings. In a few plazas, the darkness hangs several feet above the ground, leaving a low, dim clarity beneath it. Here the paving can be seen without any source of light: wet gray stone, ocher sediment, thin red moss, and pools like holes punched through the surface of the world.

Elsewhere, objects reveal themselves only through water. A dry wall is invisible, but condensation makes its outline appear. A tower cannot be seen until rain runs down it. The city is therefore repeatedly drawn and erased by moisture. During heavy seepage, entire districts emerge at once—bridges, facades, domes, ranks of empty windows—then vanish as their surfaces dry.

The principal avenue crosses the basin from nowhere to nowhere. It is broad enough for a procession, though no wheels have scored it and no feet have worn its center. Down its length stand shallow basins at regular intervals. Each holds a different water level regardless of elevation. The first may be empty, the next brimful, the next filled only along one vertical side, as if gravity has tilted inside the bowl. From time to time all the basins overflow simultaneously. Their water spreads in thin sheets across the avenue and climbs the nearest walls, carrying pale sediment upward.

At the city’s center is a low complex of courtyards surrounding a circular depression. There are no grand towers here. The structures crouch close to the wet ground, their roofs broad and eroded, their corridors narrowing toward the basin. The circular depression is filled not with water but with exposed depth. Looking into it reveals more of the city—streets, roofs, and flooded squares arranged far below—except that the lower city is larger than the basin that contains its view.

Rain can be seen falling there into a bright, colorless sky.

At intervals, one of the streets below moves. It bends slowly between buildings like a dark river, carrying its paving and colonnades with it. The motion produces no sound. Sometimes, hours later, a corresponding street in the upper city is found in a different district. Its buildings remain attached, its canals still aligned, but its doorways now open onto unfamiliar interiors.

This is one of the city’s stable laws: arrangement is temporary, form is not.

A courtyard may migrate, but it remains the same courtyard. A canal may cross itself, but its water never mixes at the crossing. A stair may invert, but always retains the number of its steps. Erosion alters every surface yet never destroys the underlying pattern. The city has been weathered for ages without becoming less complete. Indeed, weathering seems to be the process by which it maintains itself.

Another law concerns emptiness. Any enclosed space left unobserved becomes slightly larger. A closed room accumulates corners. A sealed cistern develops distant shores. Open places, by contrast, contract under sustained attention. If watched long enough, an avenue shortens: first the far arches appear closer, then entire intersections disappear between blinks. Looking away restores distance, though not necessarily the same distance.

Water moderates these changes. Reflections hold structures in place. A building mirrored in a canal cannot move until the surface is disturbed. For this reason, the most coherent districts are also the wettest. Their streets remain flooded ankle-deep, and their architecture stands doubled beneath them. Dry neighborhoods are less trustworthy. Doorways drift along walls. Towers exchange heights. Alleys narrow into seams and later reopen elsewhere.

The city is active without seeming alive. Water rises, condenses, reverses, and drains. Stone settles. Streets relocate. Darkness thickens in the upper stories and thins near the ground. Sparse plants withdraw and return. Mineral crusts spread over pools, fracture, and heal. Whole quarters become visible in rain and disappear after it.

None of these processes hurry. There is no violence in them, no struggle for survival, no sudden predation or bloom. The place changes with the calm certainty of tides, though no sea is nearby and no celestial body pulls upon it.

Or perhaps the sea is nearby in another direction.

In the western outskirts, beyond the last dense blocks, the land opens into a flat expanse of warm mud and shallow water. Towers continue there at wide intervals, each standing alone with its reflection. Between them run causeways that sink gently into the marsh and reappear miles away. The mud bears no tracks. Bubbles rise occasionally from below, each containing a moment of cold blue daylight. They burst without sound.

Farther out, where the architecture becomes sparse enough to resemble natural rock, the difference between city and landscape disappears. An arcade becomes a line of eroded cliffs. A plaza becomes a salt pan. A drainage tunnel widens into a canyon no deeper than a room yet impossibly long. The same rounded stone persists, wet and ancient, maintaining the suggestion that the whole basin—streets, marsh, towers, and buried levels—is one continuous formation.

In that outer darkness, the isolated structures seem less like ruins than punctuation marks in an unfinished sentence. Water gathers on them, revealing one wall at a time. Then the condensation shifts, and they are gone.

The basin remains: hot, saturated, almost lightless, empty of animals, scarcely green. A city without builders. A wetland without abundance. An old equilibrium whose parts will not stay where they are.

It is quiet enough to hear a drop moving up a wall.

And beneath that quiet, from everywhere at once, comes the patient pressure of more city attempting to condense.
