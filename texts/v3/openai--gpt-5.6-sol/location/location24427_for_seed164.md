At first it appears to be a mountain range under clearing rain: black ridges, pale runoff, cloud caught among the summits. Only after traveling toward it does the scale become apparent. The “range” is the raised rim of a single plateau, and the plateau reaches farther than weather can be seen. Its edge stands several kilometers above the lowlands in a sequence of fresh escarpments, each face angular and dark, as though the whole country rose yesterday and has not yet learned to crumble.

Water comes out of these cliffs everywhere. It beads on metallic stone, gathers beneath overhangs, and issues from hairline fractures in narrow silver threads. There are few rivers in the ordinary sense. The ground is too new for broad valleys, and the water too evenly distributed to choose a dominant course. Instead, the upland is covered by shallow runnels, flooded seams, chains of black pools, and marshes laid like panes of smoked glass across the high plain. Rain is not always falling, but everything has the condition of having just been rained upon. Moisture condenses continuously from the cold air. Handrails, leaves, roofs, exposed cables, even vertical walls acquire droplets until gravity pulls them down.

The light is clear and unremarkable: a cool white day beneath a high overcast, bright enough for color, too diffuse for hard shadows. This normality makes the country stranger. Nothing is hidden. Distances remain visible far beyond expectation, and what seems nearby may take days to reach.

Across the plateau stand cities.

They are not dense. A cluster of towers may occupy one horizon while the next is empty except for moss and wet stone. Elevated pipelines cross hundreds of kilometers without passing a building. Rail lines run in groups of twelve or twenty over enormous trestles, then separate and vanish into ravines. Cooling stacks rise alone from marshland. Reservoir walls stretch from ridge to ridge, retaining water whose level never varies. The settlements are urban in organization but continental in spacing, as if a city had been expanded until every street became a province and every vacant lot a wilderness.

Their architecture is recent and undecayed. Foundations bite into sharp basalt. Concrete remains pale, its aggregate still bright where surfaces have been cut. Steel is wet but not rusted. Ceramic conduits and insulated power trunks pass through the ground in ordered strata. In some places the plateau itself seems manufactured: hexagonal terraces, perfectly planar scarps, and cliffs with parallel grooves too regular to be geological. Elsewhere the construction follows the raw land with almost reverent precision, bridges stepping across cracks without touching their sides, towers placed on the only stable points among acres of saturated scree.

There are no inhabitants.

No birds cross the open sky. No insects collect in the marshes. No fish disturb the pools, and no hidden animals leave tracks in the mud. The absence is complete enough that the idea of an animal begins to feel local and improbable—a design belonging to some other world.

Vegetation nevertheless flourishes. Low forests of jointed green stalks fill sheltered basins. Thick mats of moss climb the weeping rocks and wrap abandoned loading platforms in luminous cushions. Dark, broad leaves lie flat against the ground, gathering condensation in central cups. Along warm industrial canals grow translucent reeds whose internal fibers carry faint green light. There are no flowers intended for pollination, no fruit, no burrs, no nectar. The plants propagate by division, spores, and slow mineral budding. They form closed, patient ecologies fed by water, light, dissolved stone, and the waste heat of machines.

Nothing grazes them. Nothing bites, nests, hunts, or decomposes in the familiar way. Dead plant matter is rare. A fallen frond does not rot; it pales, delaminates into thin mineral films, and becomes part of the ground. The lushness is not competitive. Every colony occupies its appointed moisture and temperature band, and its edge remains nearly fixed.

The machines are louder than storms.

The sound reaches through the soles before it becomes audible: a planetary industrial chord composed of turbines, pumps, compressors, relays, grinding mills, and the deep impacts of presses. Close to a city, individual sources emerge. Flywheels revolve behind walls. Ventilation shafts exhale without pause. Automatic cranes cross immense assembly halls, stop, lower empty hooks, raise them, and return. Trains of sealed silver wagons move along elevated tracks at exact intervals. In the refining districts, blue-white arcs burn behind thick glass, and banks of pistons descend together with a concussion that travels over the marshes.

Nothing is being depleted. Nothing accumulates.

Ore enters the factories, but the mines do not deepen. Finished beams leave the rolling plants, but no stockyard fills. Water pours through turbines while reservoirs remain level. Slag moves down incandescent channels and disappears beneath the ground; elsewhere, kilometers away, identical raw stone rises along a conveyor. The system is not merely recycling material. It is maintaining a distribution.

This is the first rule one learns here: change occurs locally, but the landscape does not change.

A raindrop forms, falls, strikes a leaf, runs into a gutter, enters a pump, crosses a cooling tower, and returns to the air. A railcar travels, yet the number and spacing of railcars across the plateau remain constant. A blade of moss unfolds while another, indistinguishable blade contracts into the mineral mat. Rock breaks beneath a cutter; rock crystallizes in a dark chamber below. Motion is everywhere, transformation is constant, and history is absent.

Even the noise never rises or fades. Walk toward a turbine hall and other engines recede in exact compensation. Enter a silent-looking valley and the ground conducts the same total force. The world maintains its volume.

The high country creates its own weather, but not storms. Cloud decks remain anchored over heat exchangers and marsh basins. Snow hangs on the upper scarps in permanent, thin shelves, neither advancing nor melting away. Cold air descends broad spillways between cities, meets warm exhaust, and becomes fog in stationary walls. From a distance these walls resemble pale architecture: corridors, domes, and columns of vapor that keep their shape while every droplet within them is replaced.

The terrain has the same impossible youth. Its fault edges remain sharp despite the running water. Talus slopes contain clean, newly broken facets, but no pile grows at their feet. Channels have begun to form and will always be beginning. There are embryonic canyons only a few meters deep yet thousands of kilometers long. In them, water roars over raw stone with the force of ancient rivers, accomplishing no visible erosion.

Beyond the main industrial belts, emptiness becomes the dominant structure. A single transmission mast may stand in a hundred square kilometers of wet tundra. Its cables cross the sky toward supports hidden beyond the curvature of the plateau. A maintenance road, perfectly drained and brightly marked, runs straight for weeks without reaching a door. At intervals there are plazas large enough for populations, paved in dark vitrified slabs and furnished with lights, drains, shelters, and signs. The signs bear diagrams rather than language: arrows returning to their beginnings, maps with no “you are here,” circles divided into equal phases.

At the plateau’s center is a depression so broad that its far rim lies below the horizon. It is called a basin only because the land tilts toward it. Inside, the cities become taller and more widely spaced. Needlelike foundries rise through permanent cloud. Their lower levels are drowned in cold marsh water; their upper platforms stand in dry, bright air. Between them hang bridges that appear impossibly thin until one sees freight convoys moving across them like dark filings.

Here the industrial chord is almost physical. Pools shiver without forming waves. Green stalks bend by a fixed degree and remain bent. Fine water leaps from surfaces in tiny crowns, each impact erased by the next.

At the very center, according to the diagrams, there is no capital, reactor, or controlling intelligence. There is only an open shaft several kilometers across, lined with black columns. Warm mist rises from it. Rain falls into it. Cables descend along its walls until distance and vapor erase them. The sound coming up is neither machinery nor geology, but something in exact balance between the two: a continuous note too deep to hear entirely.

No one built a viewing platform there, yet a viewing platform exists.

Its wet rail reflects the ordinary daylight. Moss fills the seams of its concrete. Indicator lamps glow green in unattended cabinets. Far below, brief lines of white light move through the shaft at regular intervals, revealing new rock, new steel, or surfaces for which there is no distinction.

The place gives no sense of ruin. Ruin requires a before and an after. Nor does it feel alive, despite all its greenery and labor. It is a world held at the instant of its own emergence: fresh mountains, new cities, first marshes, engines just started—an instant made vast enough to contain weather, infrastructure, and continents, and made stable enough to last forever.
