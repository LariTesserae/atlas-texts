At the top of a mountain too steep to carry a forest, there is a shallow hanging basin called the Green Nave.

It is not large. From the broken lip at one end, a person can see nearly all of it: a tilted floor perhaps half a mile across, enclosed on three sides by new, black ridges. The fourth side opens abruptly onto empty air. Beyond it are other summits, pale and remote, rising through a level plain of cloud.

The altitude makes distance difficult to judge. The far ridges look close enough to touch, yet crossing the basin takes hours. Every slope is steeper than it appears. Breath comes thin and cold. Voices lose their body almost as soon as they leave the mouth.

The mountain here is young. Its ribs have not yet been rounded into patient shapes. Slate stands in upright fans; basalt breaks into keen-edged slabs; pale veins of quartz project from the ground like buried antlers. There are almost no pebbles. Stones fall away as large pieces or remain where frost has split them, their fresh inner faces shining in the fierce light. The cliffs below the open lip are striped with recent scars, places where whole shelves have departed into the cloud.

Despite this severity, the basin is green.

Nothing grows tall. There is no room for height in the wind. Instead, the vegetation thickens close to the ground until it becomes a forest viewed from the scale of an insect. Cushion plants form dense green hummocks, some broad enough to lie upon. Their surfaces are packed with tiny leaves, each leaf furred in silver to hold warmth and catch moisture. Between them rise miniature thickets of red-stemmed heath, black sedge, and a creeping juniper whose oldest trunks are no thicker than a wrist, though they have been bending here for centuries.

The richest growth follows cracks in the stone. Moss fills them in vivid bands. Lichens spread in orange, white, and sulfur-yellow maps across the dark rock. In sheltered hollows, translucent ferns uncurl beneath overhangs of slate. They seem absurdly lush in such cold, but they are lush through closeness rather than stature: leaf upon leaf, root upon root, every surface occupied.

There is little open water. Rain comes only occasionally, driven sideways in brief gray curtains. Snow falls more often but seldom accumulates evenly. Most of the basin’s moisture arrives out of clear air.

At night, warmth stored in the black stone rises through the vegetation. The plants cool faster than the rock, and condensation gathers on them in astonishing quantities. By dawn, every moss filament carries a bead. The cushions darken and swell. Drops run inward through their tangled leaves, vanishing into peat pockets hidden among the stones. When the sun clears the eastern ridge, the whole floor flashes at once, as if strewn with crushed glass.

For an hour there is water everywhere, but nowhere enough to make a stream.

It trickles under mats of roots. It hangs beneath ledges. It gathers in hoofprints and in the chipped hollows of old stonework. Then the light strengthens, the wind descends, and the exposed droplets disappear. The basin passes each day from frost to glittering dampness to brittle dryness, often before noon.

The brightness is nearly merciless. Snowfields on neighboring peaks throw light upward, while the cloud plain reflects it from below. Shadows are sharp and blue. Even the undersides of stones seem illuminated. Travelers wrap strips of smoked hide across their eyes, pierced by narrow slits, and paint their cheeks with soot and animal fat. Without such protection, the basin leaves a person half-blind by evening.

Yet it is rarely warm. The sun heats one side of the body while the other remains numb. Touching shaded metal can tear skin. Water freezes beneath the moss even while insects move on its sunlit surface.

The animals are plentiful, though at first the place seems empty.

Most are small, low, and silent. Pale grasshoppers bask on black rock, folding themselves into cracks whenever a cloud crosses the sun. Beetles with heavy, sealed wing-cases crawl beneath the cushion plants, feeding on fungi and carrying spores in grooves along their backs. Dense colonies of mites live in the moss. On bright mornings, minute flies rise in shimmering columns no higher than a handspan, keeping close to the warmth trapped near the ground.

There are larger lives as well. Soft-footed cliff hares browse the heath, their coats gray in summer and white through most of the year. Long-tailed mountain voles tunnel beneath the vegetation, making warm chambers in the peat. Small herds of narrow-horned goats visit the basin by routes that appear impossible, stepping out of the cliffs at dawn and departing before the afternoon wind. Their passing is often known only by the slow movement of green backs and the sudden clatter of one dislodged stone.

Birds occupy the open air. Dark alpine swifts slice through the basin without calling. White-backed ravens patrol the rim in pairs, tipping their wings to remain almost motionless in the updraft. Small ground birds nest under slabs, lining their hollows with vole fur and dry lichen. Even the predators are quiet: a short-winged hawk that hunts along the ground, a snow-colored fox that waits beside burrow mouths, and, occasionally, a huge mountain cat visible only when the lesser animals freeze.

Much is happening at any moment, but nearly all of it occurs without spectacle. Roots tighten around shifting fragments. Beetles graze the underside of leaves. Hawks pass below the cliff edge. Frost opens hairline cracks. Meltwater enters them. A stone gives a dry click and moves the width of a fingernail.

The silence comes partly from the thin air. Sound has little richness here. The wind is more often felt as pressure than heard as a roar. Footsteps on moss vanish entirely. The loudest regular noise is the brief rattle of loosened shale, followed by a long pause before the falling pieces strike anything below. Thunder sometimes moves under the cloud layer, so distant and muffled that it seems to come from inside the mountain.

Scattered around the basin are signs that people once came more often.

Low walls of fitted slate stand among the cushions, never higher than the knee. They form small crescent shelters with their backs to the prevailing wind. No mortar was used; the builders selected flat pieces and locked them together by weight. In three places, shallow roofed chambers have been dug into the slope. Their ceilings are juniper poles covered with bark, peat, and stone. Smoke has blackened the interiors, though no trees grow within many days’ descent.

The largest structure occupies a notch near the open edge. It is only a platform of dark slabs surrounding a narrow upright stone. Chips around its base show that the pillar was shaped with harder stones and simple copper picks. Shallow cup marks cover one face. At dawn, condensation fills them. By midday they are empty.

Old mountain people say the cups were used to measure the season, though not by the amount of water they held. Each cup dries at a different rate. Their sequence gives warning of changes in the high winds, the arrival of migrating herds, and the rare weeks when storms climb above the clouds.

Those who still visit leave little behind: a braided cord, a bone needle, a wedge of copper hammered nearly flat, feathers tucked under the pillar, a packet of salt sealed in hide. Nothing remains undisturbed for long. Hares chew the cords. Ravens steal the bright pieces. Frost lifts the paving stones. Moss slowly closes over hearths.

There is one local peculiarity. Water beneath the vegetation does not always run downhill.

The cushion plants are interconnected by hair-thin fungal threads extending across much of the basin. During the coldest hours, moisture passes along this living mesh toward the eastern, sunward slopes. A cut in the moss may bead with water on its upper edge while the lower edge dries. The effect is slight, no more than a few drops moving through hidden tissues, but over generations it has shifted the richest soil uphill. Green terraces climb where bare scree ought to descend.

This gives the basin an appearance of resisting gravity. Stone goes down; life goes up.

The resistance is temporary. Every year, pieces of the rim break away. Every winter, frost widens the cracks. The Green Nave is becoming both deeper and smaller. Its lush floor thickens while its boundaries retreat. One day the open lip will fail, and much of the basin will slide into the white emptiness below.

For now, it hangs above the clouds: sharp, bright, cold, and briefly wet each morning. Almost nothing stands upright. Almost nothing speaks. Yet underfoot the place is crowded with roots, burrows, eggs, spores, tracks, and patient mouths—a small green room at the summit of a largely empty sky.
