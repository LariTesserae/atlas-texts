### The Woven Altitude

There is no ground here.

What passes for ground is the upper surface of an immeasurable construction: a continent of living fiber raised above an unseen depth. Its plains are mats of interlocked roots, layered until they can bear forests, pools, migrations, and weather. Its ridges are braided trunks. Its cliffs are cut ends of ancient bundles, each strand thicker than a tower, furred with moss and smaller vascular growths. Where the surface has split, one can look down through miles of spars, tendons, hanging gardens, and open chambers.

Nothing underneath is mineral. There is no bedrock, no native soil, no untouched foundation. Every surface has been grown, tied, folded, carved, grafted, pegged, or trained into place.

The whole world is made.

Its scale is difficult to recognize because it has no useful edge. An escarpment may continue beyond the curvature of sight, though that curvature is unreliable. Valleys are broad enough to possess separate climates. Bridges made from the hollow ribs of extinct trees take weeks to cross. The highest platforms rise into a permanent dimness where warm cloud presses against the weave and condenses on every hair, leaf, rope, and carved railing.

The light has no obvious source. It resembles late dusk beneath storm cover: plum-gray, green-black, and occasionally amber where translucent tissues have been stretched across openings. There may be a sun above, but it is never seen whole. Sometimes a pale disk appears through the cloud, too large and too close, and remains in the same place for several days. At other times light rises through cracks in the floor, filtered through the lower architecture as if dawn were occurring beneath the land.

Despite the altitude, the air is warm. It is close and richly scented, laden with vapor, pollen, spores, bruised sap, and the faint sweetness of fermentation. Moisture is frequent but rarely violent. Clouds comb themselves against the upper forests. Condensation gathers along the underside of leaves, travels through carved grooves, and drops into narrow basins. Rain usually falls in fine vertical threads, but near the great hollows it bends sideways, drawn toward thirsty walls. In some districts it rises from pools as a silver drizzle and vanishes into the canopy.

Open water is scattered rather than dominant. There are black cistern lakes sunk into the platform, their edges reinforced with root wicker and old lacquered bark. There are marshes where the woven floor has sagged under centuries of retained moisture. There are canals no wider than a hand and aqueducts wide enough to hold floating woods. Water seeps continually from the cut faces of cliffs, so that the rocklike braid appears to weep.

The water tastes of leaves and old tools.

Vegetation does not merely cover the landscape. Vegetation is the landscape’s material condition.

Roads are strips of flattened, self-repairing vine. Walls are pollarded hedges trained over lattices of bonewood. Terraces are giant shelves of fungus-hardened bark. Towers are hollow stalks bound together while young and still flexible, their chambers shaped by clay forms that have long since rotted away. Roofs overlap like scales, each one a waxy leaf broad enough to shelter a village. Doors close by curling. Bridges tighten in dry weather and loosen when rain makes them heavy.

Even the empty places are botanical. Great halls extend for miles inside dead trunks, their ceilings lost in dark vapor. Courtyards are stretched membranes of root, open except for a single tether tree or a dripping well. Shafts descend through sparse scaffolds into volumes so large that weather forms within them. The world is densely grown but spatially empty: matter is concentrated into skins, cords, columns, and distant planes, leaving enormous intervals between.

A traveler can stand on a narrow rootway and see almost nothing nearby—only the path beneath their feet, a few swaying guide poles, and far across the gulf, a green wall fading into cloud. Beneath is warm darkness. Above is more darkness, crossed occasionally by the pale belly of something flying.

Most construction was accomplished without machinery. Marks of handwork remain everywhere. Trunks bear adze scars. Joint holes were bored with stone drills and sand. Vast ropes were twisted from bast fiber by generations walking in circles. Structural limbs were persuaded into shape over centuries with wedges, weights, pruning knives, smoke, and patient grafting. The greatest works are not technologically complex. They are merely old, cumulative, and attended without interruption.

No one could build the entire world now. Perhaps no one ever intended to.

Maintenance is its governing principle. Small societies inhabit the surviving routes, carrying hooked knives, wooden mallets, shell scrapers, fire pots, and coils of cord. They do not distinguish clearly between gardening, masonry, medicine, and law. To repair a stair is to prune it. To open a window is to alter the light response of a wall. To destroy a room, one girdles its supporting stems and waits.

Fire is rare and deeply controlled. Open flame can awaken a draft within the hollow structure and send heat through passages hundreds of miles away. Most cooking is done with fermentation, heated stones, or the warmth of compost chambers. Metal exists only as scattered relics: green hooks, soft knives, bells that no longer ring. Stone is nearly mythical. Pebbles are heirlooms.

The land has settled into forms older than memory. Its ridges sag but do not collapse. Broken spans become hanging groves; hanging groves become rain collectors; rain collectors thicken into new pillars. Erosion here is not the reduction of stone but the loosening of weave. Water separates fibers. Boring animals open galleries. Fungi soften load-bearing walls. Wind worries at exposed cordage. Yet every act of decay invites occupation, and every occupied wound is eventually knitted over.

Thus ruin and repair are indistinguishable at a distance.

Animal life is abundant, though the world seldom sounds crowded. Most creatures are light-bodied and soft-footed. Velvet-backed browsers move along the vertical walls, cropping lichens with circular mouths. Long-limbed canopy animals suspend themselves between distant branches and may spend their lives without touching a horizontal surface. Mothlike things nest in dry architectural seams. Thin gliders, almost all membrane, drift across the hollows and turn by altering the tension of their ribs.

There are predators, but few roar or cry. Sound travels unpredictably through the woven structure, and a loud call may emerge days later in another valley. Hunters communicate by pressure: tapping roots, pulling on vine networks, changing the rhythm of dripping water. Prey animals become still when the floor trembles. Some have no ears at all.

In the marsh terraces live transparent amphibians whose organs carry colonies of luminous algae. They glow only when submerged, so ponds sometimes show moving constellations beneath their dark surfaces. Small beaked grazers follow the waterline, cutting new drainage channels as they feed. Their migrations keep the marshes from drowning the platform.

Far below, in the large interior voids, heavier things move. Their shapes are rarely seen. One knows them by the slow displacement of hanging bridges and by showers of dust rising upward through cracks.

Silence dominates because nearly every material absorbs sound. Bark, moss, wet fiber, vapor, and soft bodies swallow footsteps. Rain lands without patter. Wind passes through perforated walls rather than beating against them. The loudest common noise is the occasional tightening of the world: a long, low sequence of creaks traveling through the braids as humidity changes, each vast cable taking up or releasing its burden.

At such times, people stop walking and place their hands on the floor.

The world’s geometry has also aged.

Distances do not remain obedient. A corridor repeatedly repaired with grafts may become longer inside than outside. Two bridges tied from the same original vine sometimes lead to the same destination, even when they extend in opposite directions. A person descending through enough generations of flooring may arrive above the point from which they began.

These effects are strongest near old knots: places where hundreds of structural bundles converge. Within them, direction frays. Water divides and flows down every branch, though the branches curve toward one another. Shadows detach from objects and cling to nearby walls like stains. A door cut into a living partition may open onto whichever room most urgently requires an entrance.

Nothing here is entirely inert, and this is the closest thing the landscape has to a universal rule. A structure used as a bridge becomes more bridge-like. A vessel repeatedly filled with water begins to attract rain. A chamber where animals are born develops warmth, softness, and a pulse. An abandoned road forgets its destination and gradually leads somewhere else.

Memory is a physical force, but not a reliable one.

The inhabitants account for this by repetition. Paths are walked in groups. Names are spoken at junctions. Knots are renewed even when they appear sound. Maps are not drawings but cultivated mats: miniature networks of living veins that must be watered with liquid taken from the places they represent. When a mapped route dies, the corresponding vein browns and curls away.

Beyond maintained territory, the made world grows less certain. Forests revert into architecture or architecture flowers into forest. Stairs produce fruit. Windows close their lids. Vast suspended orchards drift a few handspans farther from their moorings each year. Entire districts migrate so slowly that only inherited measurements reveal the motion.

And everywhere there is the sense of height—not the cold, exposed height of a mountain, but a warm, breathless altitude inside an endless organism. Cloud presses close. The dimness has depth. Under every road is another road, far below; under that, a forest growing upside down; under that, a lake hanging in a net; under that, perhaps nothing for a hundred miles.

The woven continent continues over the void, old and damp, patiently remaking itself by hand, root, appetite, and error.
