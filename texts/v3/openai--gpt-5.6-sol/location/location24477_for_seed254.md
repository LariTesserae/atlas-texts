Below the surface lies a district no larger than a compact hamlet, enclosed within a chain of old brick cisterns. The chambers were built separately and joined over generations: settling tanks opened into pump rooms, pump rooms into service vaults, service vaults into narrow galleries carrying pipes and power cables. Walls remain everywhere, but there is almost no open wall left to see. The place has become a warm, wet compression of masonry, iron, water, and vegetation.

Its central chamber is high enough to feel civic. Brick arches rise into dimness, their upper courses blackened by condensation and old soot. Beneath them, former streets run between rectangular beds and machinery plinths. The streets are scarcely passable now. Roots have lifted the paving into shallow ridges; moss has softened every joint; mats of pennywort spread over the drains like green lids. Pipes cross overhead in crowded tiers, wrapped in climbing stems so thoroughly that they resemble branches trained into unnatural straightness.

Nothing here grows as an isolated specimen. Vegetation is the ground, the partition, the roofing, and much of the load-bearing mass. Fig roots descend through cracked vaulting and thicken around columns. Ferns occupy the fig roots. Liverworts occupy the ferns’ damp litter. Fine algae glaze the liverworts wherever water flows. Even the black water in the lowest tanks carries floating rafts of duckweed, several layers deep, their undersides tangled into pale submerged felt.

The district is warm without ever becoming bright. Heat comes from buried mains, old motors, and the slow decay of accumulated plant matter. It gathers under the vaults and keeps the air close to the temperature of skin. Moisture condenses on metal handrails and cable trays, swells into drops, and falls with heavy, regular taps. After rain on the city above, seepage appears along the ceilings in dark expanding patches. For several days every brick seems to weep. Then the flow diminishes, though it never stops entirely.

Light is infrastructural rather than celestial. Amber maintenance lamps burn behind cages of rusting wire. Some are hidden in leaves and turn whole thickets translucent. Narrow white strips mark stairs, valve platforms, and the lips of deep tanks. Here and there a shaft from the surface delivers a diluted gray column, but vines have entered most shafts and packed them with stems. At noon these columns become only slightly brighter than the lamps. There is no view of weather, only its pressure: increased dripping, cooler drafts, the tremor of runoff entering large pipes.

The loudness of the place is continuous. It is not the loudness of rapid activity but of massive systems persisting. Pumps labor behind tiled walls with a low rotary thunder. Water passes through constrictions and roars in the dark. Old ventilation fans beat unevenly, each revolution producing a soft metallic knock. Overflow channels hiss under curtains of roots. Every chamber has its own note, and in the connecting passages the notes overlap until the brickwork seems to hum.

Nothing answers these sounds.

There are no rats in the galleries, no insects in the lamps, no worms in the beds, no fish in the settling pools. No birds have descended through the shafts. The absence is complete enough to become one of the district’s physical qualities. Leaves are not bitten. Fruit, where it forms at all, hangs untouched until it softens and falls. Flowers open into air without bees or moths. Many old ornamental varieties have disappeared because they could not reproduce under these conditions. What remains is a flora capable of patience and repetition: ferns shedding spores into currents of warm air, mosses extending by fragments, reeds spreading through rhizomes, figs and willows rooting wherever a broken stem rests in wet sediment.

Pollination is performed only by moving air, vibration, water, or the occasional mechanical brush fixed to a rail. In several cultivation rooms, overhead carriages still travel once each week. They creep along corroded tracks, drawing soft fiber paddles across ranks of flowering plants. Their motors whine for an hour, then fall silent. The movement is so slow that leaves bend aside and return without shaking. Most machinery here works in this manner: rarely, heavily, according to buried schedules.

The old industrial order remains legible beneath the growth. Numbered doors divide the district into sectors. Green-painted pipes carry warm water; blue pipes bring cooler seepage from the upper drains; broad ceramic conduits take overflow toward deeper reservoirs. Control cabinets stand on raised concrete islands, their indicator bulbs glowing behind veils of condensation. Automatic valves turn by fractions. Sluice gates rise a hand’s breadth during storms. Pumps alternate to distribute wear. The system was made for cultivation, filtration, and municipal water management, and although no one tends it now, enough of it continues to function that the place has not flooded or dried.

Construction and plant growth have reached an old accommodation. Roots split mortar but brace weakened arches. Reeds clog channels, yet their dense beds catch silt before it reaches the pump intakes. Algal films slow the water and keep exposed concrete from crumbling. When a ceiling drips in a new place, plants colonize the wet line over years; when a pipe begins to leak, roots gather around it until the spray becomes a steady trickle. Damage is rarely sudden. It is absorbed, redirected, and made into habitat.

Water occupies every level. It shines in grooves worn into stair treads and lies warm in shallow depressions. In the lower passages it rises over the ankles of abandoned boots and the bases of signposts. In the tanks it is nearly black, not from depth alone but from tannins and shadow. Beneath the duckweed, root masses descend through still water to beds of fine mineral sludge. Overflow moves from tank to tank through notched stone weirs polished smooth by decades of passage.

The terrain is therefore difficult to separate from the built environment. There are slopes, but they are collapsed filter beds. There are cliffs, but they are the vertical faces of reservoirs. There are ravines between root-swollen foundations and broad shelves formed by old loading platforms. A low hill near the center is an anaerobic digester covered in moss, its rounded concrete roof warm enough to support thick colonies of maidenhair fern. One can walk around the entire hill in several minutes, though the path narrows to a sideways passage between leaves and sweating pipework.

At the district’s edge, the brick corridors taper into service ducts too tight for the larger plants. Here the vegetation changes from towering leaves to pressure-resistant mats. Moss fills bolt holes. Pale roots probe cable sleeves. The final gates are sealed, their lower halves buried in washed-in silt. Beyond them are other underground systems, but this particular place ends decisively at iron, brick, and packed roots.

Its cycles are long. Water rises after rain and subsides over a week. Leaves emerge, expand, and remain for months in the steady warmth. Mineral crusts accumulate around leaks one translucent layer at a time. Iron oxidizes beneath moss, becoming soft-edged and dark red. A fallen stem may take years to disappear because nothing chews it; it sinks gradually into wet litter, collapses under fungal decay, and becomes another ledge for new roots.

The district does not feel abandoned in the ordinary sense. Abandonment suggests vacancy, and there is no vacancy here. Every railing carries a vine. Every sill holds water or moss. Every cubic measure of warm air is crossed by stems, vapor, vibration, or falling drops. The city’s former purposes have not vanished so much as thickened into another arrangement—still organized, still supplied, still noisy with pumps and flow, but occupied entirely by plants and the patient infrastructure that keeps them wet.
