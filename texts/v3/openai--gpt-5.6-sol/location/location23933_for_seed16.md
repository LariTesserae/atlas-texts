### The Cinder Conservatory

From the salt field above, there is almost nothing to see.

The plain lies low beneath a white, obscured sky, hot enough to soften pitch and bleach fired clay. Its surface is a continuous pavement of pale mineral scales, fitted together by centuries of expansion and contraction. Here and there, a seam exhales dark vapor. There are no dunes, no bones, no tracks, no dry riverbeds. Water has never passed through this country. Even the shapes that resemble channels were cut by salt creep, thermal fracture, and the slow escape of gas from below.

Near the center of the plain, one of the scales is hollow.

A narrow crack descends through it, no broader than a doorway and nearly invisible beneath overlapping flakes. The crack opens into a buried chamber: a single enclosed city occupying the inside of an ancient gas blister. It is only a few hundred paces across, but its domed ceiling rises so far above the floor that the upper darkness feels unbounded.

This is the Cinder Conservatory.

The city stands at the bottom of the hollow, well below the surrounding plain. Its streets are circular and widely spaced, built around empty courtyards and open shafts. Houses of salt brick, black glass, and porous volcanic stone cling to the lower walls. Many are no larger than cupboards. Others are façades without rooms, made to shade passages or catch the descending mineral dust.

There is very little material here compared with the volume of the chamber. The buildings are thin-walled. Bridges cross distances they have no practical need to cross. Ladders rise into darkness and end at isolated platforms. A single pillar may support a neighborhood of small ovens and terraces, leaving acres of hot air untouched around it. Seen from above, the settlement resembles a handful of charcoal filings scattered in a bowl.

Nothing in it is metal except for rare greenish hooks hammered from native copper. Most construction is tied, wedged, fired, or grown. Doors turn on stone pins. Roofs are panels of woven fiber plastered with sulfurous clay. Pipes are hollow stems sealed with resin. The largest machines are woodless wheels made from ceramic ribs and mineral cord, driven by the breath of the chamber.

There is no water—not hidden in cisterns, not trapped as frost, not circulating in living tissue. The local life is founded on other solvents.

Thickets occupy the warmest cracks. Their trunks are hollow columns of black cellulose and silica, stiff as kiln-fired wicker. Instead of sap they carry a slow, amber mixture of sulfur compounds and oily carbon chains. Their leaves are broad, thin plates, dull violet on one side and silver on the other. They do not wilt. Under excessive heat they curl tightly, presenting their mirrored faces to the vents; when the chamber cools, they spread again with a dry, coordinated rustling.

These plants are lush only by the standards of this barren world. They crowd walls, roofs, and kiln mouths, but leave enormous spaces between groves. A garden may consist of one immense fan-leaf rooted in a crack, its canopy furnishing shade for an entire street. Filament mosses hang from ceramic trellises in dark red curtains. Pale bulbous growths swell beneath paving stones and split them apart over decades. Along the upper dome, colonies of soot lichen draw energy from hot gases and the faint radiance of the stone.

No animal lives among them. There are no insects inside the flowers, no worms in the beds, no birds in the rafters. Pollination is violent and mechanical. At intervals, heat tightens the pods until they burst, firing clouds of needle-fine spores across the chamber. Some strike walls with a hiss. Others fall for hours through the central void.

The flowers have no fragrance meant for noses. They release reactive vapors instead: sharp brimstone, hot resin, bitter alkali, scorched spice. A blossom is often a ceramic-looking cup surrounding a patch of catalytic crystal. When the right gas passes over it, the cup briefly glows.

The light is never bright. Thin cracks in the ceiling admit a tarnished gray radiance during the day, enough to reveal the descending veils of dust. Most illumination comes from the city itself. Beds of heat-loving lichen smolder dull orange. Sulfur lamps burn blue in stone niches. Certain walls retain daylight and give it back after dark as a weak green sheen. The result is less like night than the inside of a banked furnace: dim, close, and colorless until something moves.

And something is always moving.

The chamber breathes through several natural chimneys. As the salt plain above heats, gas is forced downward through one vent and expelled through another. By evening the current reverses. The entire city has been arranged around this cycle. Loose screens rise and fall. Ceramic vanes rotate on stone axles. Seed curtains snap open. Long fibers strung between towers tighten into resonant lines.

This makes the Conservatory astonishingly loud.

The dome amplifies every report. Wind pounds through the narrow vents with the voice of a distant foundry. Roof panels chatter. Hollow stalks boom and whistle. Mineral cords hum at different pitches according to temperature. Seedpods detonate in volleys. Thermal cracks travel across the ceiling like slow thunder. Somewhere in the dark, a counterweighted stone is always dropping, hauling open a shutter or lifting a basket of mineral dust to a higher terrace.

There are no voices beneath this noise.

Whoever laid out the city is gone, if they were ever animals at all. Their hand tools remain: chipped glass scrapers, grooved stones, fiber drills, clay molds, wedges polished by long use. The workmanship is patient rather than advanced. Nothing suggests engines, writing, or smelted iron. Yet the streets are deliberate, the vents tuned, the gardens sorted into chemical districts. Black-leaf plants occupy carbon-rich walls; silver fans grow near sulfur fissures; red threads descend where ammonia seeps from the deep stone.

It may be that the builders cultivated the vegetation. It may be that the vegetation built the city through them. Some of the oldest houses have root-patterns inside their bricks, and some younger groves imitate architectural forms: arches, stairs, clustered chimneys. A mature fan-tree will brace a failing wall with astonishing precision. Filament moss tightens around loose masonry. Resin bulbs seal cracks before the next pressure cycle. The settlement repairs itself, though without hurry and without apparent intention.

Its erosion has reached a kind of equilibrium. Wind has rounded every exposed corner, but the same wind deposits new mineral skin on the sheltered side. Steps wear down and are slowly raised again by salt crystallization from dry vapor. Walls lean, settle, glaze over, and become stronger. Nothing here is pristine, yet little is truly ruined. The city has spent so long inside its chamber that decay has become one of its construction methods.

There are rules to surviving objects here.

Anything wet, were wetness possible, would be destroyed by the heat and salts. Anything airtight is crushed or burst by the daily breathing of the hollow. Anything rigid eventually snaps. Thus all enduring things are porous, jointed, folded, or suspended. Buildings flex. Streets are paved loosely. Bridges sag by design. Plant tissues crack safely along predetermined seams.

Fire behaves strangely in the heavy atmosphere. It crawls downward along resin-coated fibers and forms low blue pools in depressions. Flames do not leap unless caught by a vent-current. The gardeners—if the absent builders deserve that name—made channels to guide these seasonal burns. Fire passes through the red moss beds, consumes old filaments, and leaves the silica skeletons standing. Within days, new threads climb them.

At the chamber’s center is the greatest open space, a circular pit lined with glassy blocks. It contains no monument and no well. It is simply an emptiness preserved within the larger emptiness. Hot gas rises from its depth in pulses. Around its rim, the oldest buildings face inward with narrow, sealed doorways.

Once each cycle, when the outer plain reaches its greatest heat, pressure beneath the city lifts a stone diaphragm at the bottom of the pit. The release is deafening. The hollow shakes. Every cord sings, every vane turns, every silver leaf folds at once. Dust spirals upward in a pillar and vanishes through the roof crack.

For several minutes afterward, the Conservatory becomes completely still.

In that rare silence, the scale of the place changes. The city no longer feels like a settlement inside a chamber. It feels like a tiny instrument built around a single held breath—an instrument weathered into balance, tended by plants with mineral blood, hidden under a plain where nothing else grows.
