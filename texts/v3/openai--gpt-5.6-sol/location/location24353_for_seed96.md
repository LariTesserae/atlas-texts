The high country is made entirely of vessels.

They begin at the horizon as a pale, crowded texture and continue beneath the feet: bowls set into bowls, troughs crossing terraces, cisterns sunk through vaults, steep ranks of tiled roofs with no chambers under them. There is no exposed soil and no bedrock. Where a cliff has opened, its face reveals courses of construction all the way down—brick, rammed chalk, pottery shards, fitted slabs, then older bricks beneath, each layer plainly shaped by hands. Even the mountains are masonry.

Nothing is large enough to dominate. The landscape’s scale comes from repetition. Ten thousand modest walls produce a ridge; innumerable thumb-sized gutters become a watershed. Seen from a height, the whole region resembles a densely laid-out town, yet there are no streets, houses, plazas, or doors. Every surface has been made for water.

Rain has recently passed. It remains everywhere.

It stands in bright saucers and black-rimmed jars. It moves through narrow channels incised into white ceramic. It gathers on the undersides of overhangs and descends drop by drop along hanging chains of fired clay. Dampness darkens the mortar between blocks, so that each joint appears freshly drawn. Some walls weep through their porous faces. Others have been burnished until the water cannot enter them; on these it lies in trembling lenses, brilliant enough to hurt the eye.

The elevation keeps the air cool. Breath would cloud here, if there were anyone to breathe. Mist forms close to the surfaces rather than filling the sky. It gathers as a low silver film inside trenches, under bridges, between packed rows of jars. Above it the light is severe and clean. The cloud cover is thin, and the wet construction reflects the sky many times over: pearl glaze, white chalk, mica grit, water, pale metal, more water. Shadows are sharp-edged but short-lived, repeatedly interrupted by glare.

The terrain is young, despite its overwhelming density. Corners remain crisp. Tool marks have not been rounded away. On some retaining walls, the impressions of woven mats survive in dried clay. Steps show no hollowing from passage. Fresh fractures expose orange, gray, and bone-colored interiors untouched by stain. The rain has begun its work but has not had time to soften anything. It has carried a little chalk down the faces of the terraces, leaving narrow white tails. It has loosened a few tiles. In the steepest channels, grains of crushed pottery click slowly downhill.

There are no plants—not even moss in the wet joints, no lichen on the upper stones, no green haze in the standing water. The basins are clear to their bottoms. Nothing swims there. Nothing has laid eggs along the margins. There is no smell of leaf mold, roots, fur, dung, pollen, or decay. The air smells only of rain, cold clay, wet ash, and the faint metallic odor released when water lies against copper.

The copper itself is scarce. Most of the place was made with simpler means: stone hammers, wooden forms, fiber cords, antler-like picks fashioned from ceramic because there were apparently no animals from which to take antler. The few metal components are thin and carefully conserved—staples folded around cracked lintels, green-edged sheets beneath spillways, narrow blades embedded in sluice gates. Many mechanisms depend instead on weight, balance, and swelling material. A porous plug becomes heavy when saturated and lowers a gate. A clay float rises in a cistern and tips a counterweighted trough. Water fills one cup until it bows, empties into the next, and returns upright.

These devices move slowly. Sometimes an entire hillside waits for a single basin to fill.

The land is packed almost beyond comprehension. Every gap contains another object fitted to the exact shape of the gap: a jar between buttresses, a rack of small cups under a leaking seam, a staircase inserted beneath a staircase, wedges driven into hairline cracks, channels braided around chimneys that vent nothing but cool vapor. Cavities that seem accidental reveal smooth interiors when approached. Even darkness has been constructed, sealed inside doubled walls and under false floors.

Yet the density does not feel chaotic. It is governed by gradients. High water must become low water. Clean water must be kept from water that has touched copper. Water that has seen direct sun is separated from water gathered in shadow. Rain from the east-facing surfaces enters square cisterns; rain from the west enters round ones. Condensation is granted its own tiny conduits, no wider than a fingernail. Overflow is never merely discarded. It is offered to a lower vessel, then another, then another, until it reaches the deep pressure vaults beneath the made mountains.

There may be a logic to these distinctions, though no inscription explains it. Marks are present everywhere, but they are practical rather than linguistic: notches counting volume, lines establishing level, stamped circles indicating which plugs fit which mouths. Handprints appear on some bricks. They are small, broad, incomplete, and all pressed to the same depth.

The nearest thing to an open plain is a valley of shallow evaporation pans. It stretches several kilometers between angular ridges, every span divided into low-walled polygons. After rain the valley becomes a field of mirrors. The bright sky is repeated so perfectly that the partition walls seem to float in whiteness. As the water thins over days, mineral lines form around the rims—first gray, then white, then a delicate blue where traces of copper have traveled too far. These deposits are the only growth in the landscape. They branch and feather like frost, but they are not alive.

At the valley’s upper end stand hundreds of towers. They are low, blunt structures assembled from stacked rings, each crowned with a wide clothless frame. The frames catch fog by means of dense lattices of knotted ceramic cord. Droplets condense on the cool strands, join, and descend into the towers. In still weather the towers appear inert. In a light wind, their lattices touch with occasional dry taps.

Farther up, the construction steepens into ridges. Here the made ground imitates geology without quite understanding it. Cliffs are too vertical, saddles too symmetrical, gullies too narrowly spaced. Retaining walls lean against other retaining walls until it is impossible to know what any single wall retains. There are fresh collapses where rain has found a flaw in the arrangement. A fallen mountainside resolves, on close inspection, into millions of intact handmade pieces: jars still sealed, arches detached whole, bricks bearing unfaded red stripes, lengths of pipe with cleanly broken ends.

No builders lie beneath. There are no bones.

The quiet makes every drop distinct. Water ticks into jars with different pitches according to their fullness. A hidden conduit gives a soft throatlike gulp, then is silent for an hour. Somewhere across the valley, a saturated counterweight releases and a stone gate settles with one low knock. The sound passes over the mirror pans, strikes the ridges, and returns diminished.

There is no wind-song in leaves, no wingbeat, no insect rasp. Even thunder is rare. Most weather arrives as condensation: a whitening of the air, an accretion on every cool surface, a gradual burden transferred downward through the system. Actual rain is a major event. During it, the landscape ceases to be quiet. Every lip spills. Every channel speaks. Thousands of resonant chambers fill and alter pitch, turning the high country into a single enormous instrument played by gravity.

Then the clouds pass, and the instrument slows.

The world here appears to obey one peculiar rule: an empty vessel does not remain empty. If left open beneath the bright sky, it will eventually gather moisture, even when the surrounding air seems dry. A sealed vessel, conversely, loses water through walls that show no pores. Thus water is always traveling from concealment toward exposure. The builders accommodated this, perhaps revered it. Their deepest reservoirs are not sealed; narrow shafts connect them to daylight. Their smallest cups are set on the highest points.

Another rule is visible in the distribution of damage. Water erodes only what was made without care. A roughly shaped brick softens quickly, while a carefully smoothed clay edge remains sharp under years of dripping. Precision has the durability of stone. Neglect becomes mud. As a result, the entire country is slowly judging its own construction. Poorly fitted terraces sag and dissolve; exact ones endure, directing the released material into molds below. There, washed clay settles in prepared forms and dries into new blocks.

No hand lifts those blocks. They accumulate in ranks beneath the molds, newly made and unused.

At noon, brightness strips the landscape of age. The wet surfaces blaze; the high pools become pieces of sky; the towers cast compact blue shadows. It could be the morning after completion. Only the water’s patient movement suggests duration: cistern to cup, cup to channel, channel to buried vault, vapor back toward the light.

The place is not ruined, exactly. Its mechanisms still perform their small obligations. Its mountains still catch the weather. Its valleys still sort one kind of water from another. But everything remains crowded with readiness, every ledge occupied, every hollow assigned, as if the makers expected a final substance to arrive and had prepared a vessel for each possible portion of it.

Nothing has arrived except water.

Water, and the brightness to reveal it.
