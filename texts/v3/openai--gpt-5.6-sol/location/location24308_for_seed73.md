The country begins above the clouds, though the clouds do not lie below it in a clean white floor. They climb the mountainsides in long, wet migrations, snagging on forests and spilling through passes. Peaks rise another four or five kilometers beyond the first inhabited ledges, green almost to their summits. The air should be cold at this altitude. Instead it is hot: a steady, close heat held against the stone as if the whole range has been wrapped in invisible glass.

Water is everywhere without becoming a single dominant body. It beads on leaves, gathers under bark, and condenses in the open air. Threads of it descend the cliffs, not as waterfalls but as weeping seams, darkening the rock beneath mats of fern and orchid. Every hollow contains a pool. Every level shelf has a marshy center. After the daily clouds pass through, the ground shines for hours.

The mountains are the remains of an immense stepped uplift, weathered long enough to have lost all violence of outline. Their cliffs are tall but settled: blue-black walls rounded at their crowns, buttressed with ancient talus now bound by roots. Ravines have found their permanent courses. Rivers no longer appear to be cutting downward so much as polishing what is already there. They move through channels worn smooth as vessels, dividing and rejoining around stone islands covered in cycads.

Across the broadest high basin, ranges overlap in humid blue layers. Nearer slopes are crowded with vegetation; farther ones soften into silhouettes, their immense height disguised by haze. Sunlight is ordinary and almost reassuring—white at noon, gold toward evening—but the moisture thickens it. Distant rain can be seen as gray columns standing between mountains. Shadows have soft edges. Bright leaves flash briefly when the canopy stirs, then vanish back into green.

The forest is lush without being impenetrable. Great pillar trees stand widely spaced, each supporting a neighborhood of smaller life. Their trunks are ribbed and pale, warm to the touch, and continually damp. High branches carry gardens of bromeliads, hanging moss, waxy flowers, and shallow basins of rainwater where insects and translucent tadpoles live. Beneath them grow fans of blue-green fronds, thornless rattans, broad herbs, and low tangles of plants whose leaves fold into cups at dusk.

Roots do not always enter the ground. Some spread over stone in flat, interlocking webs; others descend from branches and stop a finger’s width above the soil. These suspended roots drink from the wet air. They sway slightly but never make contact, even during storms. Fallen leaves gather beneath them in rust-red drifts, decomposing slowly in the oxygen-thin heat.

The altitude reveals itself in the body rather than the vegetation. Breath comes short. Sound travels strangely far. The heart works hard while sweat refuses to evaporate. A person climbing only a few terraces may feel exhausted, though the air is full of insects and birds moving with effortless vigor. The native animals have broad lungs, latticed blood vessels, or thin auxiliary breathing membranes along the throat and ribs. Many rest for long periods and move in sudden, economical bursts.

The place is loud, but not hurried.

From dawn until after dark, the basin resonates with calls. Tree-dwelling grazers produce hollow bell notes that roll from ridge to ridge. Flocks of small, metallic-winged birds click as they turn in formation, thousands of wings striking the humid air with the sound of rain on shutters. In marshes, amphibians sustain chords so low that pools tremble around their bodies. Water mutters in stone channels. Cicadas pulse for minutes at a time, stop together, and leave behind an immense temporary silence—then begin again farther up the valley.

Nothing changes quickly. Herds follow the same contour paths for generations, wearing narrow grooves through moss without widening them. Flowering moves upslope at a rate of a few meters each year. Pools fill with sediment, become reed beds, then reopen centuries later when subterranean water quietly alters course. Even storms are slow. Clouds thicken over the western escarpment through most of an afternoon before releasing warm, heavy rain that seems to hang in the air before reaching the leaves.

Scattered through this old equilibrium are structures that do not belong to any visible settlement.

One stands on a promontory above the central marsh: three black arches, each thin as a blade and nearly a hundred meters tall. No road approaches them. Vines climb their lower surfaces but cannot cross certain hairline bands in the material. At noon, when the sun passes between the arches, the air inside their span becomes clear and dry. Through that clear region, stars are sometimes visible against the blue sky.

Elsewhere there are smaller objects: a hexagonal plate sunk into a streambed; pale filaments braided through a cliff; a door-shaped recess in a boulder that is always several degrees cooler than the surrounding shade. Most have been absorbed into the ecology. Birds nest on the black arches. Algae form precise green spirals around the stream plate. A colony of soft-shelled animals sleeps inside the cool recess and emerges at night to graze lichen from nearby stones.

The devices appear inactive only on human timescales. Measurements taken over decades would show that the arches move apart by a fraction of a millimeter each wet season. The buried plate removes traces of one mineral from the river and replaces them with another that should not be stable in warm water. The cliff filaments conduct no detectable current, yet lightning bends toward them from kilometers away and enters the mountain without thunder.

They may be responsible for the heat. They may be responsible for the air remaining thick enough to sustain a forest at such elevation. The oldest trees have grown around them, and the oldest exposed roots treat their surfaces as familiar terrain. Whatever purpose the machines once had, they now behave less like ruins than like geological laws.

Above the forest rise the crownlands: open, saturated meadows draped across ridges so high that, beyond the anomalous warmth, they should be ice. Grasses there grow waist-deep around warm tarns. Their seed heads carry globes of clear gel that magnify the landscape upside down. Herds of six-legged, slate-colored browsers cross these meadows in loose lines, their backs furred with symbiotic moss. Small white birds walk among them, harvesting insects and strands of moss. When danger approaches, the herd does not run. Every animal lowers itself into the grass and releases a long, flute-like alarm. Neighboring herds answer across the heights until the entire range seems to breathe through stone pipes.

The predators are patient. Long-bodied cats sleep in the buttresses of trees for days, digesting a single meal. Marsh serpents suspend themselves beneath floating vegetation, taking air through tubes grown along the upper jaw. In the highest cliffs live immense gliding animals that launch only during the evening updraft. Their membranes are nearly transparent. From below, they resemble slow distortions crossing the sky, patches where clouds and blue are gently bent.

The central marsh is shallow and warm, divided into thousands of irregular mirrors by ridges of peat and pale mineral crust. It extends farther than it first appears, continuing around wooded knolls and beneath hanging gardens. Bright fish move through grass flooded to the stem-tips. Some are visible only by the shadows they cast; their bodies reflect the water above so precisely that they disappear when viewed from the side.

At intervals, circular clearings open in the marsh. They are perfectly still, untouched by wind, rain, or animals. Leaves falling toward them stop just above the surface and remain there, slowly circling. Over decades, these suspended leaves form dark horizontal discs. Over centuries they decay in place, creating layers of soil that rest on nothing. Shrubs eventually germinate on these floating sediments, producing islands held a handspan above the water.

These clearings reveal one of the landscape’s quieter rules: gravity is not entirely consistent here. It remains strong enough to make the climbs punishing and the cliffs dangerous, but in certain narrow volumes it has direction without urgency. Water may descend a rock face and then travel sideways for several meters. Seeds collect beneath overhangs. Mist forms vertical sheets that persist after clouds have passed. The anomalies are stable, mapped unknowingly by animals and incorporated into their trails.

There are signs that people once did the same. Stone steps appear in the forest, never more than six or seven together. A roof of fused ceramic shelters an empty shelf beside one of the upper paths. On a distant ridge, a line of narrow towers stands among trees, each no taller than a hut and pierced with round windows. They contain no rooms. Their interiors are filled by a continuous material resembling pearl, veined with slow points of light.

No village surrounds them. No fields interrupt the forest. Whatever builders came here either lived lightly or lived elsewhere, approaching by means that required no roads. A few artifacts suggest habitation: a basin that fills with cool water each morning despite having no inlet, a bench grown from transparent stone, a cluster of shallow marks arranged at hand height on the wall of a cave. Yet the marks alter when no one observes them. Returning after a night, one finds the same number in a different order.

Near sunset, heat lifts from the marsh in visible wavering columns. Condensation forms along the black arches and runs upward, silver against their surfaces. The day’s loud chorus changes register. Birds settle. Frogs begin. Far across the basin, the bell-voiced grazers call one by one, each note arriving seconds after the movement that produced it.

Darkness comes gradually because the wet air retains the light. Pools remain gray-blue after the ridges have gone black. Flowers open along the cliff seams, their pale interiors reflecting starlight rather than producing any glow of their own. The advanced devices do not illuminate themselves. They become less visible than the trees and rocks, detectable only by absences: a patch without mist, a silence in the insect chorus, a set of stars briefly sharpened above an arch.

The mountains endure in their hot, wet suspension. Water keeps polishing the established channels. Forests breathe against impossible altitude. Animals sound across valleys too large for them to see across. The scattered machines continue their work at a pace nearly indistinguishable from erosion, maintaining conditions that have become natural through age.

Nothing here feels newly broken or newly invented. The strange has weathered into the ordinary. The landscape has had time to accept its impossibilities, root through them, nest on them, drink from them, and make them part of the mountain.
