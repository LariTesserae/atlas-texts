### The City in the Antler

The place occupies a shallow bowl high on the shoulder of a new mountain. It is not a large bowl: from one rim to the other is perhaps half an hour’s careful walk, less if the ground were level and there were no walls to negotiate. Beyond its edge, the mountain drops into cloud.

The bowl looks at first like a grove interrupted by ruins. Then the ruins resolve into streets, cisterns, courtyards, and low houses. It is a city, though a very small one—a city compressed to the scale of a neighborhood, with all its institutions fitted into a few steep acres. There are gates, public stairs, market ledges, gardens, shrines, workshops, and a council hollow. Yet the buildings stand far apart. Between them lie tracts of bare dark stone, as though emptiness itself has been zoned and protected.

The mountain is geologically young. Its rock has not had time to soften into familiar shapes. Ridges end in sharp lips. Slabs lie where they first broke, their undersides unworn and glittering with pale crystals. Narrow cracks run ruler-straight for several paces and then turn abruptly at angles too clean to seem natural. The earth moves often—not violently, but with small settling knocks that can be felt through the soles. In the mornings, fresh chips sometimes lie beneath the cliffs, blue-black inside, untouched by lichen.

There is little flowing water. Rain comes irregularly, usually as a fine cold mist that never becomes a storm. The stone drinks most of it. What remains gathers in hollows, roof basins, leaf cups, and deep ceramic jars sunk into the ground. Water is therefore everywhere in small quantities and nowhere in abundance. A wall may sweat steadily while the courtyard beneath it stays dusty. A dry stair may descend to a chamber whose ceiling drips once every seven breaths. Each drop falls into a different bowl.

The inhabitants built around these punctual waters.

Their houses are made without mortar, from fitted volcanic plates, split timber, woven root, and packed moss. No building rises more than two stories. Many have roofs of overlapping bark weighted with stones, although some are roofed in living leaves trained over ribbed frames. Doors are low and narrow to keep out the cold. Windows are round and covered with translucent animal membrane, giving the interior light the color of old honey.

Metal is rare. A few copper hooks and knives are passed between families and resharpened until their blades are thin as grass. Most work is done with stone adzes, horn wedges, bone awls, wooden levers, and abrasive sand. The lack of sophisticated tools has not made the city crude. Its complexity is one of patience rather than machinery. Gutters are carved one finger-width at a time. Doors pivot on polished stones. Vast nets of twisted hair and nettle fiber hang beneath the eaves to gather condensation.

The city’s name, if translated directly, means something like **The Interior of the Antler**.

This is not metaphorical.

Across the basin grows a sparse stand of enormous white structures. From a distance they resemble winter trees stripped of twigs: smooth trunks branching into blunt forks, ivory-colored and faintly porous. They are warm to the touch even in frost. Their bases disappear into cracks in the mountain, while their crowns reach above the roofs.

The people insist they are antlers.

No animal large enough to bear them has ever been seen.

The city has been built among these pale branches, using them as towers, bridges, boundary stones, and public squares. One house is tucked into the fork of an antler just above ground level. A spiral stair has been chipped around another. Water condenses most reliably on their surfaces, forming clear beads that migrate slowly downward against small irregularities in the grain. The oldest cistern sits beneath the lowest point of the largest branch.

The antlers are still growing. Not visibly, but measurably. Marks cut into them are found higher each year. Sometimes, in the deep cold before dawn, there is a pressure in the ground and one of the white forks pushes another finger’s breadth out of the rock. Buildings are never attached too firmly to them. Bridges rest in cradles. Stairs have sacrificial joints of soft wood. Every household keeps wedges nearby for when the city must loosen itself.

Vegetation is abundant but concentrated. Nothing forms a continuous field. Instead, lushness occurs in islands so dense they appear almost solid.

Between expanses of naked stone rise circular thickets of fern, moss, liverwort, and broad black-green leaves. Some are no wider than a table; others fill whole courtyards. Their margins are abrupt. A leaf may hang over barren gravel where no seed will sprout. The plants do not seem to spread gradually. New groves appear after misty nights, already tangled and knee-high, while old ones collapse together into gray pulp.

The city maps these migrations. Streets are not permanent lines but agreements revised each season. A lane may be abandoned when a fern island forms across it. A former garden may reopen as a plaza after its vegetation sinks away. Stepping stones are stacked at intersections so they can be moved. Property belongs not to plots of land but to doors, hearthstones, jars, and roof frames. The ground is understood to have its own intentions.

The plants grow cool. Their leaves are often colder than the air, and in dim weather they gather necklaces of condensation. Pale orchids open beneath them without direct sunlight. Mosses swell into soft mounds, luminous at the edges. There are tiny trees bearing blue cones, each tree twisted into the size and posture of a seated person. Their wood is too wet to burn, but their roots can be boiled into a bitter paste.

Fire is difficult here. Fuel must be dried for months in attic racks, and even then flames remain small and blue. Cooking is done in lidded stone pots nestled among heated pebbles. Smoke is precious: it deters some of the city’s animals, attracts others, and is never allowed to escape without use. Chimneys pass through hanging gardens where soot-loving fungi comb the air.

The animals are the true population.

They are everywhere, in impossible numbers, yet the city is nearly silent.

Moths crowd the shaded sides of walls, overlapping like scales. Hairless mice sleep in braided ropes beneath floorboards. Glass-backed beetles fill cracks in the new rock, turning slowly when the mountain shifts. Snails cling to the antlers in pale constellations. Thumb-sized frogs occupy the mouths of water jars and must be gently removed before drinking. Long-legged, ash-colored birds stand on roofs with their wings held open, not to warm themselves but to collect mist.

Nothing here calls loudly. Predators hunt by sensing vibration, so the fauna has evolved toward quietness. Birds open their beaks and produce only warm breath. Frogs pulse their throats without croaking. Beetles communicate through changes in reflected light. Even the larger creatures move on soft pads or fringed feet.

At dusk the city seems to fill with motion while making no more sound than falling dust.

Hundreds of moths lift from the walls simultaneously. Pale lizards emerge to take them, leaping from roof to roof. The ash birds step after the lizards. Narrow, six-legged foxes come down from the rim to stalk the birds. Each species hunts another while being watched in turn. The sequence passes through the settlement like a tide, perceptible in shifting silhouettes and the trembling of condensation nets.

The people have adapted to this hush. They speak closely, mouth to ear, or use hand signs in public. Tools are wrapped in hide. Stonecutters strike only during designated hours, and then all other work stops. Children learn never to shout across an open space. A dropped bowl can empty an entire district: moths burst upward, lizards scatter, birds launch from the roofs, and foxes appear at the edges, drawn by the chain of alarm.

Silence is not sacred. It is structural.

Sound has weight here.

A loud noise does not echo normally. It enters the antlers. The white branches vibrate, though often too slowly to see, and hours later the sound emerges elsewhere altered. A hammer blow in the western lane may return at midnight as three soft knocks beneath a bed. A cry made near the council hollow may seep from a cistern days later. Old people claim that no sound is ever lost in the basin; it is merely stored in the antler and released when the growing bone needs room.

For this reason, certain noises are forbidden. No one whistles. No two drums may be struck together. The names of the dead are spoken only outside the bowl, facing the clouded drop. The city’s few songs are almost entirely breath, tongue clicks, and fingers drawn over cloth.

The highest point in the settlement is not a tower but a flat stone roof built against the basin’s northern rim. From there, the boundaries of the place become clear. Below lies a maze of cloud. Above rise black pinnacles, raw and angular, with snow caught only in their cracks. The bowl is a small sheltered anomaly, held between altitude and emptiness.

The air stays cold even in the growing season. Frost forms in shadows at midday. Rain may pass across the surrounding peaks while never reaching the city, leaving only a sheen on the antlers and a smell of wet mineral. At other times cloud pours over the rim and fills the streets to waist height. Buildings become islands. The pale branches vanish upward. Animals swim through the vapor in silent swarms.

During these cloudfalls, the city changes fastest. Groves appear. Old ones dissolve. Snails descend from places no one can reach. Water bowls fill without rain. Fresh cracks open in the stone, exhaling warm, animal-scented air.

Sometimes a crack contains fur.

Sometimes it contains leaves from no plant growing in the bowl.

Once, according to the marks kept on the council wall, a fissure opened beneath a house and revealed an eyelid larger than the house itself. It remained closed for eleven days. The family continued to live above it, entering by ladder. On the twelfth morning the fissure had sealed, leaving only fresh black rock and a line of pale lashes embedded in it.

The city’s scholars—keepers of scratches, knots, and stacked tablets—disagree about whether the mountain is alive. They agree only that it is unfinished.

Their calendar measures not years but emergences: the First White Fork, the Season of Warm Gravel, the Three New Springs, the Long Moth Dusk. Time is tracked by changes in the terrain because the terrain changes more reliably than the sky. A house may be older than a ridge. A person may remember when a cliff was level ground.

Despite this instability, the settlement is not fearful. Its people treat impermanence as ordinary civic maintenance. Walls are rebuilt, cisterns shifted, paths renegotiated. When the antlers grow, the city loosens its joints. When a grove appears, they harvest its margins and let its center remain untouched. When the animals flood a district, the residents move uphill for a few nights and leave their doors open.

Nothing is exterminated. Attempts at clearing pests only bring stranger replacements. Kill the moths and the lizards eat roof membranes. Kill the lizards and the ash birds begin drinking from sleeping mouths. Kill the birds and the foxes settle beneath the houses. Balance here is not peaceful; it is crowded, hungry, and continuously correcting itself.

The quietest time comes just before morning.

The nocturnal hunters have withdrawn, and the day creatures have not yet unfolded. Condensation creeps down the antlers in bright beads. A woman crosses an empty plaza carrying two stone jars. Fern islands breathe out threads of mist. Somewhere below the paving, thousands of small bodies turn together in sleep.

Then one drop falls into the oldest cistern.

The note is clear and tiny.

Across the basin, every animal pauses.

Far inside the white branches, something answers.
