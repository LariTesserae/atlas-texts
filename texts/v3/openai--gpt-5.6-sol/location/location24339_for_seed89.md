### The Warm Throat

Near the summit of a very old volcanic massif, where the air outside is thin enough to make every breath deliberate, a narrow cleft opens in the stone. It is not a cave so much as the exposed throat of one: a steep-sided chamber perhaps two hundred paces long, roofed by shelves of basalt that nearly meet overhead. The mountain has been weathered into equilibrium. Nothing looks freshly broken. Every surface has already found the shape it will keep for another age.

The cleft is dark even at noon. Sunlight reaches it only as a pale seam high above, caught in mist and divided by interlocking ribs of rock. The floor receives no direct beam. Its illumination comes from wet stone, from faint mineral sheen, and from the occasional silver turning of thousands of wings.

Outside, the summit slopes are cold and glaring. Inside, warmth rises continuously through fractures too narrow to see. It is not furnace heat, but the enclosed warmth of breath: humid, mineral, almost animal. Condensation gathers under the overhangs and along the black columns. Drops swell slowly, hang for minutes, then fall. In the dry season these drops are the chamber’s principal rain.

The walls descend in close ranks of weathered fins. Some are thin as blades; others are broad-backed, rounded by ages of dripping water and the touch of bodies. Between them lie slots, hollows, tubes, and ledges, so densely nested that the chamber seems to contain far more interior than its boundaries should allow. Every handspan conceals another pocket. Every pocket is occupied.

The stone is predominantly basalt, dull brown-black beneath its coatings. Pale salts feather from the drier cracks. Rust-red films mark places where warm seepage has carried iron to the air. Lower down, the rock is lacquered by moisture and centuries of contact: claws, bellies, folded wings, and the soft passage of fur. The floor is deep, yielding, and uneven. It is composed of old guano, shed skins, powdered bone, insect husks, fruit stones carried from distant valleys, and mineral dust blown down through the opening. Where condensate reaches it, this material becomes a dark paste with a faint internal heat. Where it remains dry, it forms friable terraces that crumble soundlessly under weight.

There is little vegetation. A fringe of wiry grass clings to the upper lip, visible only as black threads against the sky. Deeper in, a few colorless fungal fans emerge from the richer banks. Thin moss survives around persistent drips, not green but nearly charcoal, its tiny leaves spread to catch moisture from the air. In one protected fissure grows a rootless vine with three or four waxy leaves. It flowers perhaps once in several years, when rainwater penetrates far enough from above. Most of the chamber’s life does not depend on leaves.

It depends on arrival.

At dusk, creatures return from every side of the mountain. Cave swifts pour through the upper seam in twisting files, navigating by sharp clicks that rebound between the basalt ribs. Small warm-climate bats rise from lower cavities at the same hour, crossing the incoming birds in opposite streams. Neither flock collides. Their routes have been rehearsed for generations: invisible roads laid through darkness, curving around projections by distances no wider than a finger.

They are only the most noticeable inhabitants.

The high walls are furred with roosting bodies. Bats hang in layers, the lower animals clinging partly to the backs and folded limbs of those above. Pale cliff-mice move through the guano banks in narrow, inherited tunnels. Long-legged crickets crowd the ceilings of recesses. Beetles graze on fungal films; mites graze on the beetles; tiny translucent scorpions wait at the edges of dripping pools. Flat-bodied salamanders occupy cracks warmed by the mountain, emerging when condensation is heavy enough to gloss their skins.

Around the few pockets of standing water, life becomes almost continuous matter. Midge larvae flex in the sediment. Blind, threadlike worms knot around drowned insects. Minute crustaceans pulse beneath the surface. Frogs no larger than a thumbnail press themselves into wet seams, their bodies the same color as the rock. Their calls are disproportionately deep: a series of resonant knocks that seem to come from inside the mountain rather than from living throats.

Larger animals are present mainly in fragments and patience. Tawny cliff-serpents inhabit warm shelves where the bats pass close. They may remain in one coil for days, their scales beaded with condensation, striking only when an exhausted animal brushes them. A broad, nearly featherless owl nests alone in a high alcove. Its hunting method is mostly stillness. It waits for the living density of the chamber to produce an error.

Near the entrance, a small troop of dark-furred mountain primates visits during the wetter months. They build nothing and carry no possessions for long. They know, however, which loose stones split hard-shelled beetle cases and which hollow reeds can draw larvae from crevices. The useful objects are selected, handled, and abandoned. A favored stone may pass through many hands before vanishing into the yielding floor. Their craft never accumulates into a settlement. It remains a momentary relationship between hand, appetite, and available shape.

The chamber is seldom quiet.

Wings make the loudest continuous sound: a soft roar like distant surf, changing in pressure as the colonies shift. Beneath it run countless finer noises—the dry rasp of insects, the ticking echolocation of swifts, bat squeals too sharp to follow, mouse feet in loose crust, frogs knocking from the wet cracks. Drops strike stone with clear, solitary notes. Drops striking guano make almost no sound. From somewhere below comes a low breath, periodic but irregular, produced by warm air moving through the mountain’s buried passages.

The acoustics are crowded as well. A single cry returns from several directions, altered each time. Close sounds may seem remote; a rustle at one’s feet may arrive from above. The animals distinguish these reflections effortlessly. An unfamiliar creature does not. In the Warm Throat, hearing gives no reliable sense of distance until the place has taught the listener its particular grammar.

Rain transforms the chamber without making it bright. Water travels down from the summit through stone and old ash, emerging hours or days after the weather above has cleared. For a while the walls sweat heavily. Shallow basins fill. Mineral curtains darken. The guano terraces soften and slump by fractions of an inch. Insects hatch in abundance, and the frogs answer one another until the rock itself seems to throb.

Then the seepage diminishes. Pools shrink inward, ringed by salts and tiny tracks. Dampness retreats to the deepest slots, carrying much of the smaller life with it. Nothing truly leaves; it compresses. Eggs wait in silt. Salamanders seal themselves behind skins of mucus and dust. Fungi withdraw into the floor. The chamber passes from wet density to dry density, from movement to suspension.

Its changes are measured less by days than by accumulated coatings. A basalt knob is polished over centuries by the landing of birds. A drip hollows a cup slowly enough that generations of frogs inhabit each stage of its making. Guano rises around fallen bones and buries them, then subsides elsewhere as warm gases consume the deepest layers. Ledges become narrower. Cracks widen. Colonies shift a few body-lengths along the walls in response to gradients of heat too slight for an unadapted visitor to notice.

The mountain itself appears still, but it is breathing moisture upward and life inward.

There are rules here, though none are inscribed. Open water is shared until it is nearly gone. A warm ledge belongs to whatever can remain upon it. Paths through the air are older than individual memory and are not crossed carelessly. Anything dead becomes habitat before it becomes absence. Nothing useful stays unused, yet almost nothing is deliberately changed. The place permits tools but not monuments, occupation but not ownership.

Above, the summit may be empty rock under stars, cloud, or a violent white sun. None of that enters fully. The cleft keeps its darkness, its damp breath, and its packed interior. It is a small place at an immense height, crowded beyond counting—a pocket of warmth held in the weathered body of the mountain, loud with lives that have learned to fit between drops.
