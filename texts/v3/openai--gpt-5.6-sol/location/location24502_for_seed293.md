The city occupies a shallow basin no more than a few kilometers across, though distance behaves poorly inside it. From the rim, it resembles a loose arrangement of green towers standing in flooded clearings. There are broad gaps between them—open water, low meadows, warm mudflats—and yet there is scarcely a handspan anywhere that is not alive. Sparseness here is a matter of shape, not biomass. The city’s vegetation has withdrawn from clutter and concentrated itself into immense, deliberate forms.

Its ground is an ancient surface, eroded almost past the point of further erosion. Hills have been reduced to long-backed swells scarcely higher than a person. Stone remains only as slick, dark islands under the roots, rounded beyond recognition, with no corners left to tell what strata made them. Rainwater does not run downhill so much as hesitate, gather, and move sideways through the soil. Every hollow is a temporary pool. Every pool has a slow subterranean connection to every other.

The oldest streets are channels worn into this nearly level foundation. They are shallow enough to wade, lined with a resilient bronze-green skin that tightens beneath the foot and glows faintly where pressed. Warm water moves along them in alternating directions, obeying no visible slope. At intervals the current reverses, and petals, seed-cases, small boats, and schools of translucent animals all pause as if considering the change before being carried back the way they came.

Nothing in the city was built from dead material.

The towers are grown from columnar plants whose trunks divide around interior chambers. Their walls are braided layers of living fiber: pale load-bearing wood, capillary meshes carrying water upward, and outer curtains of broad leaves that fold over one another during storms. Windows open when the leaf tissue becomes transparent. Doors appear as seams that relax in response to particular body heat, scent, or electric pattern. Bridges are tensioned vines. Roofs are concave crowns that collect rain, digest debris, and distribute filtered water into the rooms below.

These structures are advanced beyond any simple division between organism and machine. Each tower is both architecture and instrument. Its cells contain engineered spaces larger within than their membranes should allow. Fine rootlets exchange information through effects that resemble entanglement but persist in warm soil and living tissue. A damaged wall does not merely heal; it consults the pattern of the whole district and decides whether the old shape is still useful.

Consequently, the city is never quite finished. It is not even committed to remaining a city.

During calm periods, plazas spread as floating mats across the larger pools. Stalls rise from them in orderly spirals, bearing fruit, medicines, tools, symbiotic garments, stored memories, or temporary senses. Then pressure shifts in the warm, condensing air. The market detects a storm before the clouds exist. Within minutes, walkways retract, towers lower their crowns, plazas break into thousands of lily-like vessels, and entire neighborhoods redistribute themselves along the waterways. When the rain arrives, what had been an urban square becomes a flowering marsh.

The turbulence is not only weather. Growth comes in pulses.

A bare clearing may remain open for decades, occupied by nothing taller than silver moss and scattered reed fans. Then one humid afternoon the moss darkens. Bladders swell under its surface. Roots erupt in coordinated loops, lifting new halls from the mud while insects arrive in black, revolving clouds. By nightfall there may be a district there: six low pavilions, a reservoir, a humming transit arbor, and a grove of signal masts shedding blue pollen. A week later the construction may be dismantled and absorbed because the city has changed its mind.

These changes are loud. The basin never produces the muffled quiet expected of so much vegetation. Water drums on leaves large enough to roof houses. Trunks groan and boom as their internal pressures alter. Seedpods crack like distant gunfire. Pollinating wings make dense metallic chords above the canals. Frogs call from resonance chambers shaped by the plants specifically to amplify them, and the sound rolls between towers until a single animal seems vast enough to occupy the sky.

Beneath all this is the steady hydraulic labor of the city: sucking, pumping, dripping, sluicing. On warm evenings the roots release surplus water in synchronized bursts. Millions of droplets strike the pools at once, producing a broad applause that can last an hour.

The fauna is abundant but rarely monumental. Space is dominated by plants; animals inhabit intervals, surfaces, and moments of transition. Long-legged grazers move singly across the shallow water, clipping only leaves that display a particular ultraviolet mark. Thumb-sized fish swim through flooded rooms and clean parasites from the walls. Ribbon-bodied creatures travel inside the city’s vascular canals, carrying spores and diagnostic microbes from tower to tower. There are gliding reptiles that sleep under roof leaves, pale crabs that prune root junctions, and warm-blooded moths whose fur condenses drinking water from the air.

The largest animals are the ferry beasts. They look, at first, like low islands of sedge drifting along the wider channels. Under the vegetation lies a broad, patient body with many soft feet. The sedge is not camouflage but civic equipment: benches, shade, navigation vanes, and fruiting kiosks grow directly from the animal’s back. Ferry beasts follow routes encoded into the water’s chemistry. When streets change, their routes change with them.

Predation exists, but the city regulates it strangely. Carnivores are permitted in some districts and excluded from others by altering the flavor of the air. No wall prevents entry. Instead, a predator crossing a forbidden boundary becomes intensely aware of every nearby heartbeat, including its own, and usually retreats in distress. In designated hunting gardens, the same sensory field is narrowed and sharpened. There, violence is sudden and exact, almost ceremonial.

The atmosphere is warm without being oppressive. Moisture hangs close to every surface. Even between rains, water condenses on shaded leaves and runs down the towers in bright threads. Rock outcrops weep continuously, fed by the saturated ground. Mist forms near the soil in the hours before dawn, but above waist height the air often remains clear, showing an ordinary blue or overcast sky. The light is not theatrical. It is simply daylight: softened by vapor, broken by moving foliage, reflected from water. This normality makes the city’s stranger motions more convincing.

At the basin’s center lies its oldest feature, a circular depression perhaps three hundred meters wide. It is known, insofar as the city names places, as the Last Slope.

The surrounding world has been eroding for an incomprehensible duration, and the Last Slope is the shallowest gradient that still exists. Its descent is almost imperceptible. Water placed at its edge may take nine days to reach the center. Yet the entire city organizes itself around this negligible fall. Canals align with it. Towers angle their roots toward it. Migratory animals circle it. Storms appear to hesitate above it.

At the center is no monument, only a patch of black water and a single low plant with widely spaced leaves. It is one of the emptiest places in the basin. Nothing grows within several meters of it. No insects land there. The plant’s roots pass into the final uneroded seam of the old stone and emerge elsewhere—not elsewhere in the basin, but in other versions of the basin, adjacent possibilities separated by fractions of unrealized history.

This is the principle on which the city’s technology rests. It does not manufacture impossible things so much as cultivate neighboring outcomes. A bridge grows by borrowing structural success from a version of itself that did not collapse. A medicine works by teaching the body the memory of having recovered. A tower survives lightning because, for several seconds, it exchanges places with one that was not struck.

Such exchanges have consequences. After violent weather, unfamiliar buildings may stand among the known ones. Residents may find a canal occupied by crimson fish never bred here, or a grove bearing fruit associated with a season that has not yet arrived. Occasionally an entire rainstorm falls upward, droplets lifting from pools and vanishing into a cloudless sky. The city accepts these events as weather.

There are rules, though none are written.

No structure may remain unchanged so long that its inhabitants mistake it for inert matter.

No quantity of water may belong entirely to one place.

Every open space must be preserved until the reason for its emptiness becomes known.

Dead tissue must be eaten, remembered, or transformed before the third rain.

And nothing may be built taller than the depth of its roots in possible worlds.

At night, ordinary darkness settles over the basin. The advanced systems do not blaze with artificial light. Towers close their transparent membranes and show only dim green and amber veins. Beetles mark the canal edges. Amphibians pulse blue in the reeds. Heat lightning flickers beyond the low rim, and the next storm can be heard assembling itself: leaves turning their pale undersides upward, roots tightening beneath the mud, ferry beasts sounding their deep warning notes.

Then the whole small city begins to rearrange.

Bridges unhook and crawl toward safer trunks. Rooms seal like buds. Market platforms loosen into flotillas. Animals climb, dive, burrow, or take wing. The nearly flat land disappears under moving water, yet nothing seems surprised. This turbulence is not a disaster imposed upon the place. It is the metabolism by which the place remains ancient without becoming still.

By morning, the basin will be recognizable but not identical. A street may have become a pond. A tower may have laid itself horizontally and opened as a public garden. New clearings may interrupt the total green, spacious and unexplained. Rain will drip from every edge. The warm air will fill again with wings and hammering calls.

And beneath the city, under the roots and rounded stone, the water will continue moving sideways through the oldest ground, carrying instructions toward forms that have not yet decided to exist.
