The city stands on the summit of a mountain no one has ever seen whole.

Its lower slopes descend through miles of warm black water, broadening into an abyssal plain where the pressure hardens flesh and timber alike. Only the crown is inhabited: a drowned highland of ridges, shallow basins, spires, and terraces suspended far above the true sea floor. There is no open air. Even at the highest point, water continues upward into dimness.

The summit is young. It has only lately pushed through the older crust, and its geology still appears unfinished. Ridges end bluntly, without gullies or scree. Domes of dark stone bulge from pale sediment like bones not yet given joints. Long cracks breathe mineral warmth. In places, basalt is still smooth and faintly glassy, marked by wrinkles made when it cooled rather than by any later weathering. Nothing has had time to wear down. The mountain has not acquired ruins of itself.

Sediment scarcely settles here. Gentle upwellings keep it hovering in veils, so every ledge has a blurred edge and every distant tower seems to be in the act of forming. The water is full of suspended mineral flour, spores, pollen-like gametes, and minute flakes of translucent bark. Looking across the city is like looking through dark green rain.

It is warmest near the fissures. Heat comes out softly, without boiling, spreading along the terraces in slow, invisible rivers. The upper waters are cooler but never cold. There are no storms and almost no currents—only broad circulations that may take days to cross a courtyard. A strand of loosened weed can remain between two doorways for an entire season before drifting away.

The settlement occupies a saddle between three newly risen peaks. It is roughly the size of a valley town, though its boundaries are hard to define because the gardens, retaining walls, bridges, and wild growth pass gradually into one another. There are streets, but they do not lie flat. They climb cliff faces, pass under shelves, cross open water, and branch vertically. Some are paved with fitted slabs. Others are marked only by ropes stretched between stone posts, each rope braided from tough plant fiber and hung with carved shells that can be felt by hand in darkness.

Buildings gather densely wherever the rock offers an anchor. They stand beside one another, above one another, and occasionally upside down beneath overhangs. Buoyancy bears part of their weight, so walls need not be massive and roofs need not shelter against rain. A house may be a ring of stone ribs enclosing a garden of membranes. A warehouse may hang like a basket under an arch. Public halls are built around chimneys of warm water, their chambers stacked along the rising flow.

There is little metal. The mountain contains ores, but there is no fire in which to smelt them. What metal exists is native copper hammered cold into hooks, pins, narrow blades, and ceremonial mirrors that tarnish almost at once. Most construction uses basalt split along natural seams, pumice drilled with stone points, shell, bone from exceedingly rare large animals, and the cultivated stems of water-plants.

The finest masonry is not cut so much as persuaded. Builders wedge young roots into cracks and guide their growth through perforated slabs. Over years the roots swell, levering stone apart or binding separate pieces into one. Mineral-secreting moss is painted along joints. It lays down pale ridges of carbonate, slowly sealing walls and cisterns. Scaffolds are made from buoyant wood tethered beneath the work rather than erected below it. A careless tool does not fall; depending on its composition, it may rise and vanish into the upper dark.

The city therefore seems both primitive and impossibly intricate. Everything has been made by hand, tooth, pressure, abrasion, growth, and patience. There are no engines. There is no wheel useful in the ordinary sense. Yet generations of tiny adjustments have created sluices that separate warm water from cool, seed nurseries that turn toward faint light, and resonant chambers through which a struck stone can send a message across several districts.

Vegetation occupies almost every unworked surface.

Broad, dark fronds grow from the terraces, their upper faces covered in velvet cilia that comb nourishment from the water. Some rise as slender trunks and open into crowns of ribbon leaves twenty or thirty paces overhead. Others creep close to the stone, making thick mats of violet, soot-green, and deep bronze. In the dimness, color reveals itself only nearby. At a distance, the whole city appears black-green and silver.

Light comes weakly from above, dispersed through an immeasurable ceiling of water. It has no visible source and makes no sharp shadows. Near the warm fissures grow nodules that shine by chemistry: dull blue pustules clustered around vents, strings of green lamps beneath leaves, pale fungal fans glowing along buried timber. The inhabitants cultivate these organisms in ceramic bowls. A doorway may be marked by a single living bead of blue light, pulsing once every few breaths.

The gardens are lush but slow. Nothing flutters. Nothing springs open in a morning. A leaf may take a month to uncurl. Flowering bodies inflate gradually into translucent bells, release a cloud of dust-fine spores, and collapse over the course of a year. Groves are inherited rather than planted for oneself. Gardeners work with hooked shell knives, stone combs, and weighted cords, thinning one thicket by inches while encouraging another to colonize a new wall.

There are few animals.

Now and then a small white grazer can be found beneath a leaf, no larger than a thumb joint, scraping the surface with a ring of teeth. Nearly transparent eels inhabit the warm cracks, but they emerge so seldom that their appearance is taken as a measure of geological change. Higher above the streets, solitary balloon-creatures hang in the water, each trailing one long filament. They may remain in the same district for decades, shifting only when the city’s slow currents turn.

No schools of fish pass through. No reefs chatter with claws. The dense life here is chiefly vegetable, fungal, and microbial: rooted, drifting, layered, patient. Empty ecological roles give the place an unsettled feeling. There are flowers with elaborate traps for pollinators that do not seem to exist. There are fruiting chambers large enough to admit an animal no one living has seen. Sometimes a garden gate is built unusually wide, as if the city still expects something enormous to return.

The inhabitants themselves are rarely visible in the open streets. Their number is small for the size of the settlement, and they move slowly to conserve breath and warmth. Houses are crowded with objects, cords, cultivated membranes, stone vessels, sleeping niches, and ancestral bundles, but the avenues may remain empty for hours.

When someone does cross a plaza, the motion is deliberate: a push from a wall, a long glide, fingers closing around the next guide-rope. Bodies are wrapped in woven fronds, not for warmth but to keep loose skin and hair from snagging. Polished stones are worn as ballast. Children learn the city first as a sequence of handholds. Directions are given not only as north and south, but as heavier and lighter, warmer and dimmer, rooted and open.

Sound behaves strangely here. Water carries it far, but the city produces very little.

There is the occasional knock of stone on stone, low and immense after traveling through walls. There is the rasp of a shell blade, the minute fizz of gas escaping a fissure, the leathery creak of stems bending in current. Voices are subdued because a murmur travels. Conversations in enclosed chambers can be felt through the ribs as much as heard. Loud noises are reserved for warning, ritual, and surveying.

At long intervals, someone strikes one of the city stones.

These are tall pillars sunk directly into the mountain. Each has been chosen for a particular tone. The blow runs through the young rock and returns as a series of softened pulses. Listeners press their palms and jaws to nearby walls. From the changing echo they judge whether a crack has widened, whether a chamber has filled, whether the mountain has risen another finger’s width.

For the summit is still rising.

The change is almost imperceptible, but it governs everything. Over decades, old courtyards tilt. Warm springs migrate between districts. A stair cut carefully along one ridge may no longer meet the doorway it served. Walls split not because they are decaying, but because the land beneath them has not finished becoming land. The city is continually adjusted around this growth. Bridges are lengthened. Root-bindings are redirected. Whole houses are detached and towed to new anchors.

There is almost no erosion, only birth and rearrangement. Broken surfaces remain sharp. New stone enters the settlement before old stone can soften. The people do not speak of permanence as stillness. To them, a permanent structure is one capable of being retied.

Above the highest peak lies a peculiar region called the False Shore. Here the mountain approaches a layer where the water changes density. Leaves, sediment, and bubbles collect against it as though beneath an invisible ceiling. A swimmer passing upward feels resistance, then enters warmer, thinner water in which buoyancy abruptly weakens. No air waits there—only another ocean with different rules.

From below, this boundary reflects the city faintly. Towers appear overhead as inverted shadows. Gardens seem to continue upward into a second drowned valley. At certain seasons, when mineral blooms spread across the layer, the reflection becomes almost solid. People gather on the upper terraces and watch another city form above them: dim, wavering, uninhabited, matching every bridge and spire but never perfectly.

The oldest belief says that the mountain is growing toward that reflected settlement. When the summit finally touches it, stone and image will join, and the water between them will become sky.

Nothing suggests this will happen soon. The ascent is measured in generations. The city waits without impatience, repairing its ropes, guiding roots into new fractures, listening to the mountain’s slow internal knocks.

All around it, the warm water presses evenly against walls, skin, leaf, and unfinished stone. The darkness is neither empty nor hostile. It is dense with growth and suspended beginnings. The place has the silence of something still being assembled.
