The country begins above the weather.

Its lowest ground is already higher than the cloud decks, a broad upland of pale stone thrust between summits that seem close enough to touch but may be days apart. Distance behaves poorly here. A ridge can remain the same size throughout an afternoon’s approach; a cairn seen across a gorge may prove small enough to cover with one hand. Paths lengthen under direct attention and shorten when followed by sound.

There is almost no soil. The surface is an old accumulation of mineral layers: chalk-white terraces, iron-dark shelves, beds of blue-gray shale, and masses of translucent stone crowded together like frozen waves. Erosion has worked on them for ages. Everything is rounded, fluted, undercut, pierced, and polished. Yet the forms are not gentle. Long exposure has refined the plateau into innumerable blades, bowls, channels, arches, chimneys, and leaning pillars. The landscape is open in extent but dense in detail; a person can see fifty kilometers and still struggle to cross fifty meters.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming abundant.

It lies in skins over the rock, sometimes only a few molecules deep, turning the entire plateau into a broken mirror. It gathers in hollows and narrow cisterns, beads beneath overhangs, and seeps from apparently solid cliff faces. After the daily condensation, every vertical surface weeps. Runnels braid across the stone and vanish into cracks with the sound of whispered syllables. There are no true rivers, only temporary agreements among droplets.

In deeper basins stand black pools, cold and perfectly clear. Their bottoms appear close, usually a handspan below the surface, though stones dropped into them may take several minutes to strike. In some pools the splash comes first and the falling stone afterward. Others reflect not the sky above but neighboring valleys, rooms of bare masonry, or stars invisible in daylight.

The air is cool and thin, though breathing is not uniformly difficult. Breathability collects in pockets. Some ledges hold rich, heavy air that spills over their edges like water; other stretches are nearly empty, and travelers learn to watch loose dust to see where air is flowing. Exhaled vapor condenses immediately, becoming bright threads that drift toward the nearest cliff. By noon, enough breath and mist have accumulated on shaded walls to feed the seeping springs.

The light is overwhelming.

A white sun occupies the eastern sky for most of the day, but its illumination also arrives from cracks in the ground and from certain wet surfaces. Shadows divide. A pillar may cast one shadow downhill, another toward the nearest pool, and a third that remains attached to the previous hour. The wet stone flashes so intensely that details disappear into brilliance. Colored cloth, when present, fades first to silver and then to a hue that has no corresponding pigment.

Storms pass below the plateau, filling the chasms with flickering cloud. Lightning climbs upward from them. It strikes the undersides of arches and makes the entire high country ring.

That ringing is the dominant sound: stone transmitting impacts across impossible distances. A fall of gravel in one ravine may thunder from a mountain on the opposite horizon. Water adds its own constant noise—dripping, rushing in hidden channels, hissing over warm mineral seams. Wind combs the rock fins and draws from them long tones, some low enough to be felt in the teeth. Where many fins stand close together, the plateau becomes an organ played by weather.

There are also knocks.

They come in sequences of three or five from inside the cliffs. No hollow has been found behind them. If answered by striking stone against stone, they repeat at another location, usually farther ahead. The knocks do not appear to be made by anything alive. Nothing here appears to be alive at all.

There are no grasses in the cracks, no lichens on the walls, no insects trapped in the pools. No wings cross the brightness. Even decay is absent. Objects weather, fracture, dissolve, and become sediment, but nothing rots. The sterility is so complete that spilled food remains unchanged until the water leaches it into colorless paste. Fire burns reluctantly, as though combustion were an imported custom.

Yet the plateau has something like an ecology.

Minerals migrate through it. At night, when the temperature falls, white salts emerge from the stone in branching patterns. They resemble frost, coral, or leafless forests, but they are simple crystal growths, advancing with minute crackles. Dawn dissolves them. Their residue runs into seams where copper-green crusts develop, only to be scraped away by windborne grit. Iron nodules roll slowly toward magnetic outcrops. Quartz needles turn over years to face the brightest source of light. Pools exchange their dissolved contents through passages that are not spatially connected.

This cycle supports nothing, consumes nothing, and nevertheless has territories, successions, invasions, and collapses. A basin may be dominated for decades by red precipitate before a single night of reversed gravity strips it clean. Delicate towers of calcite grow beneath dripping ledges, are broken by thunder, and begin again. The land is active without being alive.

Scattered across it are signs of deliberate hands.

The most common are low shelters built from fitted slabs, each just large enough for one or two people to crouch inside. No mortar was used. The stones have been chipped with hammerstones and wedged with bone-colored mineral splinters. Their entrances face neither away from the wind nor toward the sun, but toward particular gaps in the distant ridges. Many contain shallow basins carved into the floor and grooves for collecting condensation.

Around the shelters lie primitive implements: black-glass scrapers, stone-headed picks, braided cords made from hammered metal foil, clay cups fired unevenly in small kilns. The clay bears fingerprints, sometimes six to a hand, sometimes three. Copper appears rarely, beaten cold into hooks, mirrors, and thin bells. The bells have no clappers. They ring when nearby distances change.

There are no bodies, graves, or images of makers. Nor are the structures clearly ruins. Some walls have stood long enough to be rounded by erosion, while a roof slab atop them shows a fresh fracture. In one shelter a clay vessel may be wet inside despite centuries of dust around it. In another, footprints lead out across a sheet of water and continue vertically up the reflection of a cliff.

The largest construction occupies a saddle between two peaks: a field of waist-high stone frames, hundreds of them, arranged in dense, irregular rows. Each frame encloses empty air. Viewed from one side, they are simple doorways. Viewed from the other, some open onto places not present nearby—a dark coast, a red cavern, a room where rain hangs motionless. Most show only intensified brightness. Walking through them usually leads to the expected side, but not always, and never if one carries iron.

The local rules are learned by repetition rather than reason.

Water runs downhill unless it can see the sun.

Echoes return to the place where a sound was intended, not necessarily where it was made.

A covered object keeps its weight. An uncovered one may become lighter as the sky brightens.

Cracks widen when counted.

No summit can be reached by climbing its visible face. The successful routes approach from behind, even when the mountain has no rear side.

At dusk, every pool becomes level with every other pool. For a few minutes, water can be poured into one basin and rise from another kilometers away. The old shelters seem designed around this interval. Their floor channels fill, their bells sound, and cold vapor rises from their doorways. Then the sun slips—not below the horizon but briefly behind the land’s own reflection—and the connections break.

Night is short and crystalline. The plateau releases the warmth stored in its bright stone. Frost forms in geometric patches that avoid footprints and structures. The wind stops, but the landscape does not become quiet. Deep below, water shifts through the mountain in vast, pressurized movements. Pillars groan. Loose slabs settle. Somewhere, always, a rockfall begins and continues too long.

Before morning, the stars appear close enough to illuminate the bottoms of cracks. Some are reflected in dry stone. Some move when watched. One or two are revealed, with changes in perspective, to be small lights sitting on nearby ridges.

Then condensation sweeps across the heights like invisible rain. The stone darkens. Channels fill. The salt growths collapse in soft cascades. The upward lightning ceases, the white sun returns, and the entire wet, empty country becomes painfully radiant again—ancient, crowded with forms, full of motion, and inhabited only by its own unreliable laws.
