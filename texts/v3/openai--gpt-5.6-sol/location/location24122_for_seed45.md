The land is still rising.

It extends as a dark province of ridges, tilted shelves, and unfinished peaks, all lifted to an altitude where the sky has thinned to a black-violet emptiness. There is no horizon in the ordinary sense. Nearby scarps conceal farther scarps; each summit reveals another field of summits beyond it, their profiles sharp enough to seem newly broken from a mold. The deepest valleys are not lowlands but gaps between immense elevations, hanging miles above whatever foundation the world may possess.

No rain has ever rounded these heights. No river has selected a course. The stone retains every fracture made during its birth.

The prevailing material is a charcoal-colored mineral with the dull sheen of fired clay. In places it opens into seams of red glass, warm enough to soften at the edges but rarely fluid. Elsewhere, pale salts push upward through cracks and assemble themselves into ribs, fans, and hollow columns. These salts do not come from vanished seas. They precipitate from the hot, dry breath of the interior: mineral vapors rising through the range, cooling in the thin air, and becoming solid without ever passing through water.

Nothing here is wet. There are no buried aquifers, no frost in the shadows, not even dew. Loose dust behaves accordingly. It does not clump or darken. It pours like smoke around the ankles of cliffs and collects in weightless-looking drifts that may be hundreds of meters deep.

The warmth is surprising at such elevation. Heat comes from below and from within. The ledges exhale it. Long seams glow dimly under translucent crusts, and broad slopes sometimes become hot in waves, as if a furnace door had opened beneath the mountains. Yet there is almost no visible fire. Combustion is rare in this oxygen-poor country. Heat travels through stone, through charged dust, through slow changes in crystalline structure.

Above, a permanent canopy of airborne mineral obscures the sun. It is neither cloud nor smoke, but a high suspension of carbon grains and metallic flakes. Day is only a faint dilution of darkness. At its brightest, the sky resembles tarnished iron. Most of the landscape is revealed indirectly: by dull red seams, by occasional sheets of green electrical light passing through the upper atmosphere, and by the weak phosphorescence of growing salt.

The range changes constantly, but it does not yet erode.

Its turbulence is constructive. Mountains do not crumble so much as interrupt one another. A ridge may thrust upward over the course of an hour, exposing warm black planes untouched by air. A basin may fold shut. Needles of stone emerge from level ground, pause at impossible angles, and acquire branching fins as fresh mineral condenses upon them. Entire escarpments travel laterally by increments, opening long fissures behind them and closing others ahead. The land is less a finished terrain than a crowded moment in the act of becoming terrain.

From a distance, these events appear violent. Close by, many occur almost silently. The atmosphere is too thin to carry their full force. A cliff several kilometers long may split with no more than a dry click, followed minutes later by a tremor felt through the feet. Dust fountains rise without a roar. Slabs collide in near silence, and only the stone itself transmits a deep, slow pressure—less heard than recognized in the chest and teeth.

Between upheavals, the quiet is immense.

There is no animal life to break it. Nothing flies in the warm updrafts or crosses the ash plains. No tracks appear except the temporary lines made by rolling stones. There are no plants. In a few sheltered fractures, however, one finds thin black skins laid directly over the mineral. They have no leaves, roots, or apparent cells. They spread only where a particular salt is forming, feeding perhaps on electrical differences between adjacent crystals. During strong tremors they contract into glossy beads; afterward they flatten again. Whether they are alive or merely a self-repeating chemistry is unresolved by the landscape itself.

Scattered across the high shelves stand structures.

They are sparse and far apart: one on a saddle, three along the lip of a chasm, another half enclosed by a ridge that rose after it was placed. They contain no machines, hinges, glass, metalwork, or inscriptions. Each is assembled from upright stone slabs leaned together to form a narrow, roofless chamber. Some are only waist-high circles. Others are tall enough to be mistaken for natural pillars until approached.

They seem built, but only just. Their stones are selected rather than shaped. No tool marks remain, and no road connects one enclosure to another. Perhaps they are the work of a vanished, pre-metal people. Perhaps the ground itself sorts loose slabs into these arrangements while convulsing. A few structures have been caught mid-formation: stones balancing apart, their intended walls still separated by strips of untouched dust. Others have been folded into the mountains and appear now as rectangular cavities deep within new cliff faces.

Inside them, the darkness has a peculiar density. Heat does not enter. Dust avoids the thresholds even during storms. If a loose crystal is placed within one of the chambers, it slowly turns until its longest axis points toward the nearest actively rising peak. This is the only consistent behavior the structures exhibit, and it may be no more mysterious than magnetism.

The principal weather is not wind but charge.

At irregular intervals, the suspended minerals overhead begin to rotate. The sky develops broad, invisible vortices revealed by spiraling specks of pale dust. Static crawls across exposed ridges in branching violet threads. These discharges make little sound, but they alter the terrain. Salt columns bloom from bare rock. Glass seams flash and become opaque. The black skins in the fissures tighten. For several minutes every sharp edge shines with a dim corona, and the whole mountain system can be seen: hundreds of ridgelines, isolated towers, raw basins, and immense gaps opening toward the dark.

Then the charge passes. The light collapses. A few newly raised peaks remain incandescent at their roots.

Navigation is difficult because direction has not yet become reliable. The peaks shift; the valleys close; the stars, when visible through the canopy, seem to slide because the ground itself is turning by tiny degrees. Old landmarks may remain in sight while becoming unreachable. A passage crossed at dusk may stand vertically by the next dim morning. Distance is therefore measured not in straight lines but in intervals of stability: three quiet shelves to the east, one trembling basin downward, a salt bloom beyond.

The place has one overriding rule: nothing is being worn away yet. Every event adds, lifts, folds, seals, or transforms. Fallen stone becomes the nucleus of a new spur. Dust becomes glass. Cracks fill with crystalline ribs stronger than the rock around them. Even the scattered chambers, whether artifacts or accidents, are gradually incorporated rather than ruined.

Someday, perhaps, erosion will begin. An atmosphere may thicken. A solvent may arrive. Winds may learn to carry weight, and the first grain may scrape against another grain until an edge grows smooth.

For now, every edge is the first edge. Every hollow is a provisional hollow. The warm mountains rise under their mineral night, vast and unfinished, changing with nearly no voice.
