The place is a narrow hanging cleft near the upper limit of vegetation, enclosed on three sides by black, newly fractured rock. It is scarcely larger than a cathedral nave, though its walls rise far beyond sight. Their upper edges disappear into cloud, giving the cleft the contradictory feeling of being both cramped and bottomless.

There is almost no true daylight. A cold gray seepage comes through the cloud ceiling, enough to distinguish surfaces but not distances. At times even this vanishes, and the vegetation becomes a mass of wet textures: fern fronds brushing fern fronds, mats of moss piled over angular stone, pale grasses flattened and lifted by gusts. Water caught on every tip trembles without falling. Condensation is more constant than rain.

The floor is young. Splinters of basalt and slate still retain their sharp edges; broken faces show no lichen beneath the fresh black sheen. The cleft seems to have opened recently, perhaps when part of the mountain slumped away or when pressure from within split the ridge. Narrow seams run through the floor like cracks in cooling metal. Pebbles shift in them whenever the mountain vibrates.

Despite the altitude, the hollow is lush. Moisture gathers here while the exposed slopes around it remain austere. Dense cushions of moss swallow the lower stones. Liverworts overlap like scales. Crowded stands of alpine fern occupy every sheltered angle, and dark dwarf shrubs press their roots into fractures barely wide enough to admit soil. There are sedges with nearly black blades, translucent fungal threads under decaying leaves, and low plants whose red stems are the only warm color in the cleft. Nothing grows tall. Growth is horizontal, compressed by wind, layered so closely that bare ground appears only where rock has fallen too recently to be colonized.

There are pockets of water but no open stream. Rain collects in deep drill-like cavities in the stone, in leaf cups, and in small basins dammed by roots. During heavier weather these reservoirs connect for a few minutes. Silver threads race across the floor, plunge into cracks, and are gone. The hollow then returns to dampness rather than wetness, its water hidden in moss, soil, cloud, and rock.

Nothing animal lives here. There are no insects beneath the leaves, no birds passing overhead, no small tracks in the mud. Flowers, where they occur, are wind-pollinated and inconspicuous. Seed heads rattle against one another but are never eaten. Decay proceeds slowly through fungi, cold, and water. The absence is not uncanny; the cleft is simply too isolated, too unstable, and perhaps too close to the mountain’s mineral breath. Some cracks exhale air with a faint sulfurous and metallic smell. The plants tolerate it in ways larger life does not.

The loudness comes from below.

Deep inside the ridge, beyond any visible entrance, industrial workings drive through the mountain. Nothing made has been placed in the cleft—no road, cable, railing, pipe, or discarded tool—but machinery travels through the stone as sound. Ventilation fans produce a continuous low pressure, more felt in the ribs than heard. At intervals an engine climbs in pitch, labors, and drops away. Distant cutting heads send a fine metallic vibration through the walls. Sometimes there is a sequence of blunt impacts, evenly spaced and unmistakably mechanical. Sometimes a detonation arrives as a single enormous compression: moss shudders, trapped water jumps from leaves, and fresh grains of rock hiss down the cliff faces.

Because the machinery cannot be seen, the mountain itself seems industrial. Cracks act as ducts. Stone slabs resonate like plates in a factory floor. Fumaroles and ventilation pulses mingle until it is difficult to say which breath is geological and which has been forced through kilometers of tunnel. Warm vapor occasionally rises from a fissure, encounters the cool air, and condenses into a dense ground fog that pours among the ferns. The fog carries a trace of iron, oil, and pulverized rock, though no stain or artifact marks its source.

The cleft is never still. Cloud enters in sudden folds. Wind reverses without warning, drawn by pressure changes in unseen shafts. Plants bow one way, then another. Condensation thickens into rain and stops within minutes. Small rockfalls continually revise the walls. Watercourses appear, braid, and vanish. Each underground blast creates new hairline fractures and closes old ones. A slab may detach from the heights and strike the floor with a report louder than the machinery, crushing a patch of vegetation beneath fresh stone. Around older falls, moss advances in bright green margins, reclaiming the black surfaces almost as soon as they settle.

There is no stable center to the place. The safest-looking ground may begin to tremble; a dry ledge may suddenly receive a sheet of runoff from the cloud; a dense bank of ferns may conceal a fissure descending into warm, roaring darkness. The cleft’s only rule is provisional balance. Root mats hold loose slopes until they become too heavy with water. Sharp stone channels moisture until frost splits it. The machinery opens space below while vegetation closes space above.

In the darkest weather, the place reduces itself to sound and touch: engines laboring under the mountain, rock ticking in the cold, gusts thrashing wet leaves together, unseen water striking lower ledges. Then a muted industrial glow may briefly enter through the cloud—not from anything within the hollow, but reflected from work lights somewhere on the far side of the ridge. Every drop shines dull amber. The slick cliff faces resemble forged iron, and the crowded vegetation becomes a black, heaving mass.

The glow passes. Darkness returns. The machines continue, the cloud presses close, and the small green cleft clings to the newly opened mountain.
