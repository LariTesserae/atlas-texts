High on the mountain, where the outer slopes have already fallen away into cloud, there is a small basin sunk deeply into the rock. It is no wider than a grove, though its boundaries are difficult to see. Every surface is occupied. Ferns overlap mosses; mosses cover roots; roots enter old cracks crowded with pale insects and black water. The enclosing walls lean inward beneath curtains of vegetation until the basin feels less like an opening in the mountain than a chamber retained inside it.

No direct sun reaches the floor. Above, a narrow aperture shows only a dim dilution of the sky, usually sealed by mist. The light below is green-black and nearly without direction. Water shines before stone does. It collects in leaf-cups, beads along the hairs of stems, and makes fine silver seams down the walls. The air continually condenses, so that even when rain is absent the basin remains in the condition of having just been rained upon.

Its central pool is shallow, cold, and perfectly dark. It has no bare shore. Low sedges enter it from every side, growing among cushions of saturated moss and the exposed knees of dwarf trees. Their trunks have been bent by centuries of mountain weather, though no wind seems to enter now. Bark lies in softened plates beneath lichens the color of ash, old cream, and dull blue. Fungal shelves emerge from the wood in close tiers, each holding a narrow rim of water. Nothing has room to stand alone.

The rock is ancient and thoroughly weathered. It does not appear broken so much as completed by erosion. Each ledge has been rounded to the shape required for catching water; every runnel has found its final course. The walls are hollowed into shallow niches, fluted channels, and smooth basins no larger than a palm. Pebbles lie tightly nested in the streambed, their arrangement so settled that the water passes over them without shifting one. There are no raw scars, no recent falls, no fresh angles. Even the fallen slabs have long since sunk into humus and become islands for liverworts.

Water enters from innumerable sources but never visibly rises. It seeps from weeping stone, descends in threads through hanging roots, and appears beneath mats of moss as if generated there. At the far end of the pool it vanishes under a low shelf, maintaining an unvarying depth. The same droplets seem always to occupy the same tips of the same leaves. A skin of moisture coats the basin so evenly that wetness is not weather here but material—one of the substances from which the place is made.

The cold is moderate but penetrating. It belongs to elevation rather than winter: the chill of cloud, soaked stone, and earth that has never been warmed through. Breath would show faintly. Skin would soon feel slick with condensation. Yet there is no frost, and the vegetation remains full. Small glossy leaves crowd the lower levels; above them stand ferns with nearly black fronds; above those twist the compact crowns of mountain trees. Every layer shelters another. Seeds germinate in crevices on living trunks. Moss grows over moss until the oldest growth has become peat without ever touching the ground.

Animal life is everywhere, but the basin is almost silent.

Frogs sit along the pool in such numbers that at first they resemble wet stones. Some are dark brown, some green as submerged leaves, some almost translucent along the limbs. None calls. Their throats expand and contract without sound. Salamanders lie in the water channels, their blunt heads facing the current. Snails cover the undersides of leaves and crowd the stone hollows in pale spirals. Fine-legged insects hang beneath fern blades. Moths rest wing to wing on the inner walls, making patches that look like bark until one sees the repeated pattern of bodies.

In the branches are small birds—dozens, perhaps hundreds—each tucked into the dense foliage, each awake. Their dark eyes reflect what little light exists. They do not sing or quarrel. Now and then there is the faintest adjustment of claws on bark, yet even this seems less an event than a permanent component of the hush. Higher in the canopy, furred animals cling along horizontal limbs: mountain mice, small gliding creatures, and slow, round-bodied browsers with dexterous hands. They harvest moss tips and fungus flesh with flakes of stone held between their fingers.

These stone flakes are not made into structures and are never carried far. The animals select thin shards from a single exposed seam, use them to split tough stems or prise grubs from rotten wood, then fit them back into cracks in the rock. Certain birds use short splinters of bone to lift the lids of seedpods. An old amphibious creature at the pool’s edge taps snail shells with a smooth pebble, always against the same hollow in a root. There are no paths, no shelters, no caches, no marks of ownership. Tool use has emerged here only as another feeding behavior, as local and unobtrusive as a beak probing bark. Nothing accumulates. Nothing becomes architecture.

Predators are present as densely as prey. Narrow snakes loop among the roots, motionless in the cool. Spiders occupy every interval between stems, though their webs are so wet and fine that they can be seen only when a droplet outlines them. A small pale owl rests in a cavity above the pool, feathers swollen against the damp. Beneath the water, dark fish hang between submerged roots. Leeches lie along the mud in ordered curves.

Yet the basin never appears disturbed by appetite. Hunting, feeding, decay, and birth have reached an exact balance. No population swells; none declines. A frog is taken where another frog leaves the water. A moth disappears from the wall as a new moth unfolds from its casing behind the moss. Fallen leaves are consumed before they can form a visible layer. Death does not empty a space here. It transfers occupancy.

This equilibrium gives the place its unusual quality. It is not frozen: water slips, lungs breathe, roots absorb, eyes blink. But these movements do not amount to change. They repeat without progress or season. The pool has neither flood mark nor drought line. The trees carry buds, mature leaves, and soft decay at once. Nests contain eggs beside fledged young, though no shell ever seems newly broken. Mushrooms remain at the same stage of opening. The basin possesses activity without history.

Even its sounds are held in balance. Water does not splash; it makes a continuous, close whisper against moss. Wings occasionally produce a soft, papery pressure in the air. There is the minute rasp of mandibles, the touch of rainwater leaving one leaf for another, the muted click of stone used against shell. Together these should form a great noise, given the abundance of life, but vegetation and wetness absorb almost all of it. Sound travels no farther than the body that makes it.

The smell is dark soil, cold bark, mineral water, mushroom flesh, and the faint animal warmth of many hidden bodies. Underneath is the clean iron scent of the mountain’s interior.

There is no open place to stand. A visitor entering the basin would have to put a foot among frogs, roots, and nests, and would discover that every apparently empty stone is itself a shell, a sleeping animal, a fungal crust, or the roof of a water-filled hollow. The landscape offers no position outside its ecology. It has filled itself completely.

From above, if the cloud ever thinned enough to permit a view, the basin would look like a small dark knot in the high country: a dense patch of green enclosed by pale, rounded rock. Nothing would suggest the multitude within. Nothing would mark it as a destination.

It has no ruins and no promise of ruins. No hand has rearranged its stones. Its only tools are still held by claws, beaks, and damp little fingers; its only chambers are erosion hollows and spaces under roots. The mountain has worn this place into its settled form, and life has occupied that form down to the smallest usable darkness.

Here, abundance has ceased to mean expansion. Water has ceased to mean weather. Motion has ceased to mean time. The basin persists—wet, cool, crowded, and almost entirely quiet—holding the same countless lives in the same deep breath.
