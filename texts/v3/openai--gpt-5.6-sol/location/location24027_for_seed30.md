The basin occupies a high ledge on the side of a mountain that does not yet appear on any map. It is not hidden so much as unfinished.

From rim to rim, the place is scarcely wider than a hamlet. Dark cliffs enclose it on three sides; the fourth opens abruptly onto cloud and a great drop, though the edge is softened by curtains of warm mist. The elevation should make the air cold and thin. Instead it is close, damp, and almost feverish. Breath gathers on the skin. Metal sweats. Every exposed stone carries a transparent film of water, and the air tastes faintly of salt, hot glass, and the mineral bitterness released when rain first strikes dust.

There has been rain, or something similar to rain. Water rests everywhere without forming a single proper stream. It beads in hollows, hangs beneath ledges, and threads upward through cracks in defiance of slope. Along the basin floor are shallow, irregular pools, each only a few steps across. Their surfaces are dark enough to seem bottomless, but most are no deeper than an ankle. When disturbed, they brighten from below with a dull amber radiance, as if the water remembers sunlight that never reached this place.

The ground is raw geology: smooth swells of gray-black material rising through a skin of pale sediment. There are no gullies yet, no rounded river stones, no deep-cut channels. Nothing has had time to weather. Edges are blunt because they were never broken; slopes look poured rather than worn. In places, the rock is still deciding what kind of rock it is. A slab that rings like ceramic in the morning may have become fibrous and faintly flexible by evening. Some surfaces harden under footsteps. Others relax after being crossed, slowly lifting the prints until the ground is seamless again.

This is not erosion but its predecessor. The basin is accumulating the weaknesses that future water will exploit.

Fine lines appear in the stone during the warmest part of the day. They branch, pause, and sometimes withdraw. Moisture gathers along them. Grains align. Mineral crystals bloom in narrow seams, only to dissolve when the cloud cover thickens. Here and there a crack has survived long enough to earn a trickle, and the trickle produces a loud, disproportionate hiss, as though cutting through centuries rather than millimeters.

Above, the sky is dim and near. Clouds drag their undersides across the upper cliffs. Light filters through in muted violet-gray, never quite becoming daylight. There are occasional pale flashes with no visible lightning. Each flash reveals details absent a moment before: terraces beneath the pools, towers in the mist, enormous moving silhouettes beyond the open edge. Most disappear when the light settles. A few remain.

The vegetation is sparse and recently arrived. Low copper-colored mats cling to warm seams in the rock. They are neither moss nor lichen, though from a distance they resemble both. Each mat consists of thousands of flat scales that open when droplets pass over them, exposing a deep green underside. There are also solitary reedlike plants around the largest pools. They grow no higher than the knee and stand far apart, each surrounded by bare stone. Their hollow stems hum continuously. When fog condenses on their tips, they bend and deposit perfect spheres of water into small clay cups formed around their roots.

Nothing here makes a thicket. Nothing casts much of a shadow.

Yet animal life is everywhere.

The pools are crowded with translucent swimmers no longer than a finger. They have too many fins, arranged in rotating bands around their bodies, and they move through water with the smoothness of screws entering wood. At intervals an entire pool empties upward: hundreds of the creatures leap at once, unfold wet membranes, and circle beneath the cloud ceiling before dropping into another pool. Their migrations are brief and deafening, a storm of wings, splashes, and tiny clicking calls.

Between the pools walk pale, many-jointed grazers. Their bodies are light enough to stand on surface tension, even where water is only a sheen over stone. They scrape invisible films from the ground with black mouthparts. Every few minutes they freeze together, regardless of distance, all facing the same direction. Nothing obvious commands them. After a silence of one or two heartbeats they resume, often faster than before.

Predators hunt among them: flat animals like folded leather, each supported on three narrow legs. They do not chase. They place themselves in likely paths and alter the color and apparent depth of the ground around them. A grazer approaching one may believe it is crossing an empty stretch of wet rock until the rock closes.

The air belongs to larger creatures. Long-bodied fliers circle the basin in restless loops, never landing. Their wings are small, almost inadequate, and beat with a bass concussion that can be felt through the chest. They seem to remain aloft by fastening themselves to something above the clouds and swinging from it. On tight turns, an invisible line briefly shows as a distortion in the mist. The fliers call to one another in descending metallic notes. Their voices overlap with the humming reeds, the hissing cracks, the clatter of airborne swimmers, and the continuous roar of water condensing along the cliff.

The place is loud even when it appears still.

Scattered through the basin are structures. There are only seven, though this count is unreliable.

The most visible is a narrow arch planted near the open edge. It is made from a material resembling wet basalt, flawlessly black and warm to the touch. It supports nothing. Mist passes through it and emerges as heavy drops, which fall upward for several meters before vanishing. Small animals gather near the arch during the dimmest hours, drawn by a vibration too low for human hearing. Their bodies tremble in unison. Occasionally one disappears from the group and reappears days later with mineral growths along its joints.

Farther in stands a three-sided shelter, no larger than a roadside shrine. Its panels are thin, pearly, and perforated with moving holes. Inside, the air is dry and several degrees cooler. There is a bench shaped for no familiar anatomy and a shallow recess holding a persistent image of the basin as it may look in the future: deeply eroded, forested, split by a silver river. The image changes when anyone looks away. Sometimes the future basin is a lake. Sometimes a city fills it. Sometimes the mountain has opened and the entire ledge is gone.

Near the central pool, a row of waist-high pylons emerges from the ground at uneven intervals. Most are tilted. Rain avoids them. Their surfaces carry fine grooves that rearrange themselves around any hand placed upon them, briefly matching the lines of the palm before continuing into patterns the hand does not contain. The pylons produce no obvious energy, yet the stone between them remains soft and uncommitted. New species are most often found there.

The other structures are less certain: a ladder descending into solid rock; a door visible only in reflections; a cluster of silver pins that migrate several centimeters each night; something like a collapsed antenna under the sediment; and, after heavy condensation, a small white building on the far side of the basin. The building has no entrance and is never present when approached directly.

The technology does not seem abandoned. Nor does it seem operated.

It behaves more like climate.

The arch regulates the direction in which water is allowed to fall. The pylons edit the ground’s readiness to become terrain. The shrine samples possible ages of the basin. Tiny mechanisms, if mechanisms they are, drift in every droplet and circulate through the bodies of the animals. Under magnification they resemble knots in space rather than objects. They assemble no visible machines. Instead they alter probabilities by fractions: a crystal is slightly more likely to form at one angle, a mutation slightly more likely to survive, a crack slightly less likely to close.

Through these small permissions, the landscape is being constructed.

The basin has rules, though they are local and not entirely stable. Water seeks living heat before it seeks the lowest point. Shadows tend to lean toward recently born things. A sound repeated exactly seven times acquires weight; insects can land on it, and droplets sometimes collect along its fading edge. Any object left overnight will be incorporated a little into the geology. A boot may be found rooted by a flange of new stone. A dropped tool may develop a mineral duplicate beside it. Bones vanish quickly, but their empty shapes persist in the air, outlined by condensation.

Nothing decays normally. There has not yet been enough history for rot to become established.

Instead, dead matter is revised. Fallen fliers soften into ribbonlike organisms that enter the pools. Shed shells become pale nodules in the rock. The flat predators, when killed, lose their depth and remain as stains that can still trap unwary grazers. Life here does not form a clean cycle. It branches, doubles back, and borrows from its own possible futures.

Toward evening—though the dimness changes only subtly—the basin grows more active. Warmth rises from beneath the stone. The copper mats unfold. New pools appear where dry ground had been, filling from the bottom without springs. The flying creatures descend until their wingbeats drive ripples across every surface. Calls multiply along the cliffs. Something massive moves inside the mountain, producing a sequence of impacts like distant doors closing.

Then the rain begins.

It does not fall from the clouds. It condenses suddenly in open air, millions of droplets appearing at once throughout the basin. For a moment every creature and structure is suspended inside a glittering volume of water. The droplets hesitate, choosing directions. Some fall. Some rise. Some stream sideways toward the reeds or curve around the pylons. The loudness becomes immense: patter, hiss, wing-thunder, ringing stone, and the deep harmonic of the black arch.

Under that brief deluge, the basin advances.

A channel deepens by the width of a hair. A new seam chooses not to dissolve. One reed divides at the root. A pool learns an outlet. The mountain gains another minute of age.

By morning, if morning comes, the place will look almost unchanged. Yet it will be more certain than before—less a proposal, more a landscape.
