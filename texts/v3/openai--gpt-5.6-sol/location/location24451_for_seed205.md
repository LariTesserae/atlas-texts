## The Green Altitude

From a distance the plateau appears empty.

It rises several thousand meters above the surrounding lowlands, a broad green-brown table extending beyond any visible horizon. Heat trembles over it. Widely separated pillars stand against the sky, too irregular to be towers and too large to be ordinary trees. Between them lies an immense open plain, sparsely marked by pale ridges, shallow basins, and wandering bands of shadow.

Only upon landing does it become clear that there is no soil.

The plateau is a plant.

Its upper surface is a continuous living rind, old enough to have acquired geography. The “rock” underfoot is compressed vascular tissue: layered grain, mineralized sap channels, knots harder than stone. Thin coppery bark plates shift beneath a softer skin of mosslike cilia. Cracks open and close over seasons. Valleys are wrinkles. Escarpments are the healed margins of ancient tears. What looks like erosion is the slow compromise between weather and growth—wind sanding the plateau down while the organism thickens from below.

The land does not recover quickly enough to seem alive. A footprint may remain for years before its edges swell shut.

Across this substrate, vegetation is almost total yet visually sparse. There are no crowded forests. Instead, enormous growths stand kilometers apart, each commanding its own territory. Some are black-barked columns with crowns lost in heat haze. Some resemble fans of bone-white leaves, spread flat to catch the brief afternoon winds. Others are low radial organisms hundreds of meters wide, their leaves pressed against the plain like green tiles around a central breathing pit.

Between these giants, the plateau’s living surface is exposed to the sky. It is photosynthetic in places, armored in others, and nearly dormant where the sun is fiercest. Thus the emptiness is deceptive: every open interval is occupied by life, but life turned horizontal, sealed, and economical.

The heat is severe. At midday, exposed bark becomes too hot to touch. Resin bubbles through hairline fractures and hardens into amber mounds around trapped wings and seeds. The air smells of pepper, warm fruit, dust, and the sharp medicinal scent released whenever the plateau flexes. Even at this elevation the atmosphere feels heavy, as though the heat has learned to climb.

Water is scarce in quantity but persistent in behavior.

Rain comes irregularly, often in narrow moving curtains that wet a strip of country while leaving either side untouched. Most of it never forms streams. The living ground drinks through millions of pores, darkening as the moisture passes inward. What escapes gathers in old wounds: long clefts lined with slick red tissue, cup-shaped scars, collapsed breathing wells, and hollows where sections of the rind have died and curled upward.

These pockets form entire wet ecologies. A pool no wider than a hut may descend through chambers for hundreds of meters, following an abandoned sap conduit into the plateau’s body. Its water is warm, tannin-dark, and faintly sweet. Translucent roots hang in it. Small lights move below the surface, though not all of them belong to animals.

At night, condensation is more important than rain. The great pillars cool faster than the plain, and mist forms around their upper branches. Grooved leaves conduct droplets downward in steady strings. By dawn each pillar has made its own temporary wetland: a ring of dripping shade surrounded by mud-colored tissue, flowering films, and drinking creatures. By noon the ring contracts. By evening it is gone.

The fauna arrive before sunrise.

They come in such numbers that the apparent emptiness becomes impossible to reconcile with them. Vast flocks rise from slots and pores in the plateau, where they have sheltered from the night wind. Herds unfold from camouflage, revealing that many of the distant “stones” were sleeping bodies. Winged grazers skim low over the plain, shaving the green skin with rows of glassy mouthparts. Six-legged runners follow them to eat the exposed sap. Predators follow the runners. Parasites ride nearly everything.

Their abundance does not fill the landscape evenly. It concentrates into moving fronts: rivers of feet, clouds of wings, migrating knots dense enough to darken the ground. Behind each front, kilometers of open country fall silent for a few minutes before another population emerges.

For most of the day, however, the plateau is deafening.

The sound is not a chorus so much as weather. Wingbeats make a continuous dry thunder. Hollow-backed animals boom to maintain contact across the distances. Burrowing colonies strike the underside of the rind in synchronized pulses, producing bass notes felt through the soles before they are heard. The pillar-plants answer changes in pressure by opening valves in their trunks; whole groves exhale with the noise of distant surf.

There are shrieks, clacks, whistles, shell-rattles, and the metallic hiss of millions of small bodies rubbing their heat vanes together. Conversation at midday is possible only face-to-face. During the great seasonal emergences, even shouting becomes useless, and hunters communicate by touching cords tied between their wrists.

Silence has meaning here. When it spreads suddenly, something immense is passing overhead.

The larger animals seem less like inhabitants than local events. Long-bodied browsers walk on widely spaced legs, straddling ravines and feeding from several pillar crowns at once. Their hides carry gardens of their own. Blind, plated creatures move beneath the living plain, visible only as traveling ridges kilometers long. Once or twice in a lifetime, a community may witness one breach the surface: an explosion of bark plates, pale soil-flesh, steam, and frightened swarms. The wound left behind becomes a lake, then a marsh, then a scar.

Nothing is merely plant or animal in a stable way. Many creatures begin life rooted and end it mobile. Some pillar-plants shed branches that walk away on jointed twigs, carrying seed clusters in their hollow bodies. Certain grazing herds stop during the dry season, bury their limbs, and grow leaves until the rains return. Predators mimic fruiting structures, attracting both pollinators and the animals that feed on them. The plateau itself occasionally flowers through its wounds, raising slow red towers that smell like blood and ripe melons.

This confusion is not exceptional here. It is one of the world’s governing rules: movement is a phase, not a kingdom.

Another rule concerns distance. Life needs space because nearly every large organism alters the climate around itself. Each pillar controls a basin of shade, condensation, scent, and subterranean chemistry. If two mature pillars grow too close, their root pressures interfere. One poisons the other, or both tear free of the plateau and topple. Their spacing gives the land its sparse monumental character—an orchard planted by conflict rather than design.

The old fallen pillars do not rot quickly. Heat hollows them from within while their mineralized skins remain intact. People have made use of these shells.

Settlements are few and difficult to see. Most consist of a single ribbed hall within a dead trunk, or several shade shelters tucked against the cool side of a scar ridge. No settlement can stay large for long; concentrated waste attracts burrowers, and too many cooking fires trigger defensive secretions from the ground. Communities divide when they reach a few dozen households, following separate animal migrations to distant water pockets.

Their buildings are grown, cut, and persuaded more often than assembled. Curved bark plates are sewn together with tendon. Roofs are broad living leaves pinned without severing their veins. Floors are laid on small bone or wood feet so that the ground can breathe beneath them. A structure that blocks too many pores will be lifted, tilted, or slowly engulfed.

Stone is rare because the plateau covers it. Metal is rarer still. Tools are made from heat-hardened thorn, shell, tooth, obsidian carried up from the escarpment, and the exceptionally dense heartwood found around lightning wounds. Short copper blades exist, green with corrosion and passed down through generations, but they are too valuable for ordinary cutting. Most harvesting is done with serrated jawbones set into wooden handles.

Fire is treated with care bordering on ritual. The air is rich in volatile oils, and a spark can run beneath bark plates faster than a person can flee. Hearths are built in old water cups and surrounded by wet clay made from digested plant dust. Open flame is forbidden during the plateau’s molting season, when thin sheets of dry surface tissue peel loose and cartwheel for kilometers in the wind.

Paths are not roads. They are pale lines rubbed into the plateau by migration. Some have been used for thousands of years, yet they never become deeply worn; the ground rises behind each passing herd, healing at nearly the same rate that feet abrade it. Travelers navigate by sound as much as sight. A certain low drumming marks a hollow region where the rind is dangerously thin. A chorus of descending whistles promises water. The clicking of fan-leaves indicates wind direction even when the air at ground level is still.

Near the plateau’s margins, the terrain becomes more visibly weathered. Great shelves curl upward from ancient drying, creating canyons between overlapping layers of bark. Wind has carved their dead edges into fluted walls and perforated arches. Roots cross these canyons like bridges, some broad enough to carry a migrating herd. Beneath them hangs open sky, and far below lies a hot blue haze hiding the low country.

The plateau does not end cleanly. It frays.

Massive tendrils descend the escarpments, attaching to neighboring mountains and drawing minerals from exposed stone. Dead tendrils become ridges. Living ones contract over centuries, pulling portions of the plateau higher or splitting off green islands that drift downslope on mats of root. Earthquakes here may be geological, but they may also be muscular.

Weather forms across the organism’s breadth. Warm vapor released from millions of pores rises into broad cloud systems. Shadow fronts move for days. Dry lightning travels along the crowns of the tallest pillars, and thunderstorms sometimes remain fixed over one region because the plateau beneath them is actively exhaling. Rain then falls upward in places: droplets caught in columns of heated air, suspended and flung back into the clouds until cooling finally releases them.

Evening brings no true quiet. The diurnal thunder diminishes, but the ground begins to murmur. Heat leaves the upper rind. Plates contract. Sap shifts through channels deep below, each surge announced by a far-reaching creak. Pools brighten with soft green organisms. Hunters with sail-shaped ears move between the sparse pillars, navigating by the internal sounds of the land.

Above them the sky is ordinary—clear where it is clear, clouded where weather gathers, full of familiar distances.

Below, the whole horizon breathes.
