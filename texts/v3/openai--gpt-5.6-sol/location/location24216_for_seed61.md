Beneath a shelf of black sedimentary stone, behind a crack too narrow to admit a shoulder, there is a city no larger than a burial chamber.

It occupies the flattened hollow between two strata. The ceiling hangs low enough to touch in many places, and the streets are not laid side by side but fitted into every available surface: floorways, ledges, wall galleries, crawl-tunnels, niches, bridges no longer than a forearm. Buildings crowd one another like mineral accretions. Their doors open onto staircases; their roofs serve as plazas; their foundations are older rooms packed with rubble and sealed in clay. The whole settlement has the density of an intricately carved box.

Nothing grows here. There are no roots, mosses, or pale underground gardens. The materials are stone, fired mud, shell, bone, and a little hammered copper turned green-black by the wet. Walls were assembled from shale flakes and mortared with silty clay. Arches are corbelled rather than cut true. Channels have been grooved with hand tools whose marks remain visible beneath centuries of mineral skin. On certain lintels, thumbprints survive in the hardened mortar, shallow and exact.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming a river. It beads on the ceiling, gathers at the points of stone teeth, and falls into a system of cups. From the cups it passes through channels, from channels into cisterns, from cisterns through porous walls. Every surface sweats. The floors are damp but rarely flooded; the city was built at the boundary between saturation and drainage, in the thin zone where the rock continually gives up what it cannot hold.

This water makes the place thunder.

A drop strikes a bronze basin and produces a note far larger than itself. A hidden fall, perhaps only the height of a hand, roars through a resonant shaft. Clay pipes mutter and knock. Stone diaphragms hum where pressure moves through cracks behind them. The low ceiling catches every sound and sends it back multiplied, until the chamber seems crowded with mills, rainstorms, distant crowds, and enormous breathing animals. Conversation here, if there were anyone to converse, would have to be conducted by touch.

Yet nothing visibly moves except the water, and even that movement feels fixed. Each drop falls into the same hollow. Each overflow divides along the same polished lip. The cistern levels do not rise or fall. The roar has no crescendos and no pauses. It is less an event than a property of the stone, as permanent as its color. Dust cannot settle, but neither is anything washed away quickly. The city has reached an exact equilibrium with its seepage.

Long erosion has rounded every corner that water can reach. Steps are dished in their centers. Gargoyle mouths have lost their carved expressions and become smooth holes. Whole inscriptions have been reduced to rows of shallow dimples. Elsewhere, protected beneath eaves only centimeters deep, incised diagrams remain sharp: plans of channels, measures of pressure, symbols for different qualities of water. The builders seem to have understood the chamber not as a place beneath the world but as a machine made by the world, one that they could inhabit only by fitting themselves into its tolerances.

There is no central square. The nearest thing is a shallow basin surrounded by hundreds of tiny recesses. Each recess contains a cup, weight, stopper, mallet, or wedge. These are not offerings. They are civic tools, arranged so any resident could alter the flow by hand. A plug inserted in one wall would silence a pipe and awaken another three rooms away. A pebble shifted in a dividing channel would send condensation toward the drinking cistern instead of the cooling vault. Governance must have consisted largely of listening: judging from the pitch of the city whether its water was correctly apportioned.

The air is cool and close, tasting of slate, old clay, copper salts, and the faint metallic sweetness that follows rain. Light enters from no natural opening. A few bowls contain a dense mineral paste that holds a weak bluish luminescence, not flame but stored radiance released so slowly it appears motionless. Most passages are lit only by reflection from wet surfaces. A single dim bowl can repeat itself in thousands of droplets, making the city seem full of distant lamps.

Its inhabitants are gone, or nearly so. In the lowest sumps, rare white creatures cling beneath submerged tiles. They have jointed legs, transparent shells, and no visible eyes. They remain still for days, feeding perhaps on mineral films or on matter too fine to recognize as living. Occasionally one detaches and is carried through a grille into the pipework. Its passage alters no sound anyone could distinguish.

Higher up, there are signs of former bodies: sleeping hollows worn into clay platforms; bone needles stored in wall cracks; palm-sized doors polished around their edges; narrow chimneys darkened by ancient soot. The scale of these things is uncertain. Some rooms would suit a child, some only a hand, yet the wear suggests deliberate use rather than ornament. It may be that the city was built by people who lived folded close to the rock, or that each household occupied several tiny rooms and moved among them on elbows and knees. No chamber allows a person to stand fully upright. Grandeur was expressed through intricacy, not height.

At the city’s deepest point is a sealed gate of layered shale. Water weeps around its edges but does not pass through it. The gate bears no lock, only a pattern of concentric grooves worn almost smooth. Pressing an ear to it reveals the same immense roar heard everywhere else, though deeper and steadier. Perhaps another chamber lies beyond, another compressed district with its own cisterns, bridges, and innumerable damp rooms. Perhaps the sound is only the rock carrying the city back to itself.

Nothing indicates that the gate has ever opened.

Nothing indicates that it needs to.
