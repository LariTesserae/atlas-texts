The place occupies a shallow basin no wider than a small village, though from within it is difficult to judge distance. There are no clear sightlines. Every interval has been taken by growth.

The ground is not soil in any ordinary sense. It is a compressed, springing thickness of roots, old leaves, living rhizomes, seed husks, and pale fungal cords. Beneath that mat lies warm black water, near enough that a heel pressed down firmly will summon it. It wells through the footprint, clear at first and then tea-colored, carrying bubbles with a faint mineral smell. Nothing here is quite dry. Even high surfaces sweat. The broad leaves hold a permanent polish, and the bark of the larger plants is darkened as if rain ended only moments ago.

The basin’s walls are low shelves of stone, weathered into rounded steps and almost completely dressed in vegetation. Water weeps through them without forming proper streams. It threads down moss, travels along root hairs, gathers beneath hanging leaves, and drops at slow, irregular intervals. The stone has been doing this for so long that each drip has grown its own cup, each cup its own green lining. In places the rock is visible only as a firmness under the moss.

Growth does not merely cover the landscape. It constitutes it.

Great strap-leaved plants arch together overhead, making aisles and chambers. Their oldest leaves are tough enough to walk upon where they overlap, and narrow paths have been cut across them, reinforced with planks whose grain has softened beneath lichens. Thick vines run horizontally from trunk to trunk, some natural, some trained, some impossible to distinguish from old insulated cable. Curtains of small round leaves fill the gaps. Ferns grow on the ferns; moss grows on the bark of parasitic shrubs; pale seedlings germinate in rainwater pooled in the cups of flowers. Even the open water is hidden under floating rosettes locked edge to edge.

The air is warm, but not oppressively so. Its warmth is held close by the foliage. A person moving through it soon becomes damp at the cuffs and hairline, less from exertion than from contact with the place. Leaves brush shoulders. Tendrils test sleeves. Seed filaments cling to skin. There is a constant sense that the vegetation is closing again behind every passage, not quickly enough to be seen, but thoroughly enough that yesterday’s route may be padded over by morning.

Near the basin’s center stand the scattered remnants of an industrial installation.

At first they resemble unusually straight tree trunks: six iron columns, red-brown beneath skins of liverwort, supporting portions of a roof. The roof has mostly become a garden. Its corrugated panels sag under root mass and shallow ponds. Small trees have risen through the holes, their crowns filling the absent walls. Where gutters once ran, there are troughs of sedge and flowering creepers. Water descends from them in ropes rather than drops.

Beneath this canopy sits a low engine house, though “house” is generous. Three walls remain. The fourth is an interlocked barricade of roots. Inside, a flywheel turns with enormous patience.

It makes one revolution every several minutes.

No visible motor drives it. A belt disappears through a wall of leaves, broad and black and glossy with moisture. The belt advances almost imperceptibly, then gives a deep report as some distant tension passes through the system. Each report travels around the basin. Sheet metal answers. Pipes buried under the root mat groan. Hollow stems tremble and release small avalanches of collected water. Thus the place is loud without seeming active: a landscape of long pauses punctuated by immense mechanical knocks, low resonances, and drawn-out metallic complaints.

Between those impacts there is the continuous noise of water. It ticks, patters, drains, and whispers. Condensation slides down old tanks. Hidden sumps gulp slowly. Somewhere under the vegetation a pump labors in a cycle so prolonged that its intake and exhaust seem unrelated: first a distant shudder, then, much later, a heavy exhalation of water into the marsh.

The surviving machinery has been claimed without being silenced. Ferns sprout from bolt holes. White roots thread through control boxes and emerge from cracked gauges. A bank of ceramic switches remains mounted to one wall, each lever held in place by the growth around it. Their labels—FEED, RETURN, PRESSURE, SOUTH BED—can still be read beneath transparent films of algae. Copper wires vanish into trunks. Porcelain insulators support vines with red-veined leaves.

In one chamber, a row of shallow steel vats extends into the green. They are full of dense, dark vegetation whose leaves resemble folded hands. Every few hours, valves beneath the vats open in sequence. Warm water rises through the plants, lifting the whole mass by a finger’s width. A muted chime travels down the row as floats touch corroded bells. Then the water recedes. The leaves settle. Their pale undersides briefly show and disappear.

It is unclear whether the machinery irrigates the plants or the plants operate the machinery.

There are signs that this was once a place of deliberate production. Narrow rails emerge from the root floor and run for ten or twelve meters before vanishing beneath bromeliads. A handcart remains on them, loaded with cylinders of compacted fiber. The cylinders have rooted where they lie. An enamel sign depicts a leaf divided into numbered sections, beside instructions for grading moisture and tensile strength. In a shed no larger than a bedroom, spools of green plant-filament hang from rusted racks. Some are old and brittle. Others are fresh, smoothly wound, and wet.

No workers are seen.

There are work marks, however. Certain vines have been neatly braided around pipes. Drainage slots remain free of debris. The slow wheel’s bearings shine where the rust should be. Once in a while, a lamp under a hood of leaves comes on: an ordinary amber industrial bulb, weak in daylight but unmistakable. It burns for an hour or more, illuminating nothing except nearby stems and the silver backs of leaves, then fades without a click.

Animal life is scarce. The basin possesses too much plant matter and strangely little appetite moving through it. There are no choruses of frogs, no clouds of insects above the standing water, no birds nesting in the dense canopy. Occasionally a single long-winged gnat passes through a shaft of light. Tiny snails leave clean arcs on the condensation of the tanks. A pale, soft-bodied lizard has been seen lying along the warm rim of a motor housing, but never twice in the same season.

The absence of animals gives every rustle an exaggerated importance. Yet most motion belongs to plants.

Some leaves turn throughout the day, not toward the sun but toward the engine house. Clusters of translucent flowers open when the flywheel completes a revolution. Their petals pulse once with greenish light at the moment of the mechanical knock, then close by degrees. The largest vines tighten and relax in cycles lasting weeks. Their pressure has bent railings into smooth arcs and drawn two of the old columns closer together, so slowly that the distortion appears intentional.

Nothing here grows wildly, despite the saturation. Growth is packed, layered, absolute—but established. The plants have divided the available space among themselves with ancient precision. Each species occupies a thickness: slick groundcovers at the waterline, hollow-stemmed stands above them, mats of epiphytes along every trunk, high fans under the roof of the basin. New shoots do not burst into empty ground, because there is none. They emerge by replacing old structures, digesting them, inheriting their position.

Decay is equally measured. Fallen leaves do not lie loose. They are caught, wrapped, softened, and incorporated. Rust flakes are held in resin and carried along roots, giving some tissues an orange metallic edge. Broken concrete is split along patient botanical seams. The place does not erase its industrial remnants; it metabolizes them while preserving their functions.

There are boundaries. At the rim, where the weeping stone rises slightly toward drier land, the vegetation ends in a dense vertical braid. It forms a hedge taller than a person and several meters thick. Passages through it resemble doorways, but none remain open for long. Beyond is rumored to be ordinary scrub, wind, and open sky. From inside, that outer world feels implausibly vacant.

The basin’s sky is visible only in pieces. Daylight descends green and silver through the canopy, sufficient to make colors clear: black water, rust-red beams, waxy emerald leaves, white root tips, yellow paint surviving on a valve wheel. After rain, the light brightens as every surface reflects it. The entire basin seems briefly glazed.

At night, it does not become quiet. Water continues. Pipes settle. The underground pump draws its immense breath. Plants rub against the corrugated roof with a sound like heavy fabric being dragged across a floor. Along the old rails, faint electrical sparks sometimes travel under the moss, each one illuminating the outline of a buried sleeper.

Then the flywheel completes another turn.

The impact comes from everywhere at once—metal, water, root, and stone answering together. For several seconds the basin hums as a single vessel. The floating plants tremble but do not separate. Drops fall from every level. A few flowers open in the darkness.

Afterward, the place resumes its slow, crowded work.
