The plateau begins above the breathable weather.

It is not a summit but a country lifted whole: an immense table of dark mineral, gently warped, extending from one white horizon to another. Its surface has been exposed for so long that everything sharp has been worn down—not by rain, for no rain has ever fallen here, nor by rivers, nor by ice, but by heat, pressure, abrasive wind, and the passage of animals in numbers too large to comprehend. Ridges are rounded like old tools. Basins are shallow and polished. Long parallel grooves cross the ground, some broad enough to walk within for days, others finer than fingerprints.

There is no water. Even the idea of moisture seems physically unwelcome. The air strips it from anything entering the plateau, pulling vapor through skin, seals, membranes, and stone. Salt exists only in dry metallic forms: pale blooms of chlorides around vents, brittle blue crusts, yellow fans of sulfur compounds. Underfoot, the surface alternates between black glass, iron-red clinker, and expanses of a gray ceramic mineral that rings when struck.

The heat is high and constant. Daylight is ordinary in color—a hard, honest sun in a washed blue sky—but the ground stores centuries of warmth. At noon, distance trembles. At night, the plateau remains hot enough to glow faintly through fractures, not red but a deep plum color at the edge of vision. Shadows cool by only a few degrees and therefore offer concealment rather than relief.

Nothing grows.

There are no grasses in the cracks, no lichen on the shaded faces, no buried seed awaiting an impossible season. Yet the place is not barren. It is overcrowded with life.

The fauna comes in belts.

Across the open flats move herds of towering furnace-bodied animals, each supported on six or eight articulated legs. Their hides are layered mineral plates, dull from age and scoured pale at the edges. They take in powdered ore through broad mouths near the ground. Deep inside them, rotating organs grind the mineral; symbiotic chambers separate metals by heat and magnetic pulse. Waste pours from vents along their flanks as sparks, slag beads, and threads of brittle glass.

They are not machines. Nothing here has been built. No hand designed a piston, no intelligence assembled a gear. Industrial form is simply the direction evolution took.

Some animals possess crankshafts instead of spines. Some circulate charge rather than blood. Bearings grow in cartilage sockets and replace themselves layer by layer. Teeth emerge as continuous cutting chains. Reproduction may involve stamping, casting, extrusion, or the exchange of coded electrical arcs. Their young are soft and almost silent, wrapped in pale insulating fiber; with age they harden, blacken, and begin to thunder.

The plateau’s emptiness makes their abundance more disturbing. For hours there may be nothing in sight but grooves, heat, and sky. Then the horizon darkens. A migration front arrives, hundreds of thousands abreast, spaced widely but extending farther than vision. Their footfalls do not form a quick rhythm. Each step may take a minute: limb lifted, joints locking one after another, weight traveling through the body, foot descending with the inevitability of a geological event.

The sound never stops.

It is a slow, enormous sound—a layered factory roar without urgency. Grinding herds moan across the plains. Pressure animals discharge through chimney-like throats. Buried colonies hammer at intervals deep beneath the crust. Winged things high overhead beat the air with metal-edged vanes, each stroke separated by several heartbeats. The pauses are filled by resonance traveling through the plateau. A noise made a hundred kilometers away may arrive through the stone hours before it arrives through the air.

There are predators, though predation here is difficult to distinguish from dismantling.

Long, low creatures follow the migrations inside the ancient grooves. Their front limbs end in arrays of shears; their mouths emit narrow induction fields that soften armor without touching it. They rarely chase. They choose an individual and walk behind it for months, matching its pace, waiting for fatigue or mechanical error. When they feed, they sort the body with exquisite care. Iron organs go one way, conductive nerves another. Silicate armor is left in neat mounds—not from intention, but because the predator’s digestion obeys a rigid internal sequence.

Scavengers descend afterward. They are small only by comparison: wheel-shaped organisms, cable worms, clicking assemblies of legs around hollow cores. They fill the carcass until it appears to move again. At dusk, static grazers drift over the remains like translucent sheets, feeding on residual voltage.

All of this occurs slowly. A herd crossing the central basin takes thirty years. A feeding event lasts a season. Territorial disputes can remain unresolved for generations, two colossal animals facing each other across a ridge while their horns increase pressure grain by grain. Even catastrophe has patience. A beast may begin to explode in one century and finish in the next, its armor opening plate by plate while incandescent dust hangs above it in a stationary column.

The plateau is old enough to have reached an equilibrium with this violence. The animals erode it, and the eroded stone feeds them. Their slag fills depressions, only to be ground back into powder by later migrations. Their heat widens fractures; thermal contraction closes others. The land is neither being created nor destroyed in any meaningful sense. It is being processed.

Near the plateau’s center, reality becomes unreliable.

At first the failures resemble mirage. A distant herd appears inverted above itself. Shadows point in slightly different directions. An animal passes behind a ridge and emerges before it has vanished.

Farther in, the errors become structural. Distances repeat. A traveler crossing ten meters of ground may arrive ten meters to the left but several hours older. Certain grooves have two bottoms, both equally solid. Stones cast into them strike one floor, then the other, producing separate sounds. The sky occasionally displays the underside of the plateau: a dark, grooved plain hanging overhead, crowded with upside-down migrations.

The fauna has adapted to these defects as readily as it adapted to heat.

Some creatures feed on duplicated matter, placing a mouth on either side of a spatial error and grinding the same stone indefinitely. Others migrate through loops of terrain, aging while their bodies remain in place. There are immense stationary animals that exist at several distances at once. From afar they resemble black towers; closer, they resolve into populations of identical limbs sharing a single torso that cannot be located.

Causality itself behaves like a resource. Certain hunters leave wounds before arriving. Their prey begins shedding armor, then hears the predator weeks later. Herds avoid regions where their footprints appear ahead of them. Mating displays sometimes produce ancestors rather than offspring: old, fully armored forms emerging from casting chambers, already scarred by events that have not occurred.

No species seems alarmed by this. Alarm may be impossible in organisms whose thoughts propagate through slowly turning magnetic organs. Their awareness is broad, methodical, and perhaps distributed over centuries. A herd may constitute one mind. A migration route may be a sentence that takes five hundred years to complete.

There are no ruins here because there was never a civilization separate from the landscape. The foundries walk. The mines digest. The power stations mate beneath the crust. Transmission lines rise briefly as airborne chains of living conductors, stretching from beast to beast during storms of dry electricity, then break apart and crawl away.

The closest thing to architecture is accidental: slag reefs built by generations of excretion, molting grounds paved in discarded plates, circular depressions where ancient herds slept standing. These forms have no doors, no rooms, no makers. They are traces of appetite and duration.

Toward evening the ordinary sunlight turns amber. The heat haze settles somewhat, revealing more of the plateau rather than less. Ridges appear behind ridges. Migratory columns become visible at impossible distances. The open land is suddenly understood to be crowded all the way to the horizon and perhaps beyond it—every speck a living engine, every dark seam a procession, every intermittent flash an organism signaling through metal.

Then the nearest herd releases pressure.

One after another, from horizon to horizon, the animals open their dorsal vents. The discharge moves across the plateau over several hours: a wall of white vapor that contains no water, only hot fluorine compounds and powdered glass. Each plume catches the level light. For a while the whole high country resembles a city of smokestacks, though there is no city and there never was.

The roar deepens. The ground answers. Shadows detach from their owners and advance a few steps into the coming night.

Nothing hurries. Nothing rests. The plateau continues its ancient production, manufacturing no product except more of itself.
