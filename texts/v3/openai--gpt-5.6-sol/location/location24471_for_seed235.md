At first it seems to be a high, wet city abandoned in the instant after a storm.

It occupies the broad crown of a mountain so tall that the country below is not visible. There is only a level field of white vapor surrounding the heights, bright under an ordinary sun. Above, the sky is a washed blue. The light has no supernatural color and casts familiar shadows. This normality makes the rest of the place difficult to accept.

The mountain’s summit is not a peak but a succession of immense terraces. Each terrace extends for miles before ending in a cliff, and each cliff rises again beyond itself. From one plaza, looking outward, a traveler can see lower districts spread across descending shelves: dark roofs, green courts, walls streaked with mineral white, and narrow waterways shining between them. Looking uphill reveals districts that should be higher than the summit, standing on ledges that appear to overhang the sky. The whole region has the openness of a valley and the compressed intricacy of a city. There is always a farther square, another escarpment, another line of towers softened by steam.

Heat lies heavily over everything. Stone is warm even before noon and remains warm in shadow. Moisture condenses on walls, leaves, stairways, and exposed skin. The air tastes faintly of salt, iron, and crushed green stems. There is no breeze, yet veils of mist hang across streets as if being continually drawn through them. They do not advance. Their folds remain unchanged.

Water is everywhere, though little of it can be seen to move.

It stands ankle-deep in some avenues, perfectly clear over fitted black slabs. It fills long rectangular basins under colonnades and gathers in the hollows of stair treads. It beads on lintels, trembles beneath leaves, and emerges from porous rock in continuous silver skins. Along the cliffs, vast waterfalls descend into the cloud layer—but the falls have no visible motion. Each is an unbroken white architecture of water, every strand and branching droplet held in the same position. Touching one is like putting a hand into a hard rain: the water strikes, wets, and exerts pressure, yet no particular drop can be watched falling.

The city is deafening.

Its noise is the uninterrupted concussion of those suspended cataracts. The sound has no attack, rhythm, or fading. It is simply present, occupying the air like temperature. In lower streets it becomes a broad white roar. Near the cliffs it compresses into a force that shakes water in the lungs and blurs the edges of sight. Stone chambers hum at fixed pitches. Wells produce bass notes too low to hear cleanly, perceptible instead in the jaw and sternum. Narrow passages between buildings whistle with tones that never waver, despite the absence of wind.

Nothing answers the noise.

There are no birds, insects, fish, reptiles, grazing animals, or people. No spoor marks the mud. No shells lie in the pools. No webs bridge the corners. Even the usual small lives of wet heat—midges, larvae, beetles, mites—are absent. Flowers open without pollinators. Fruit swells without being eaten and does not drop. Rot has no flies and almost no smell.

The vegetation is abundant but unnervingly composed. Moss upholsters walls in deep emerald planes. Ferns fill courtyards from paving to balcony. Broad-leaved plants rise through roofless halls, their waxy blades overlapping like green tiles. Vines bind towers together and descend in heavy cables into flooded streets. Some districts are almost entirely buried beneath foliage, but the plants do not invade farther. Every root, tendril, and frond has reached a permanent frontier.

Leaves do not stir. They do not yellow or unfold. Water hangs from their tips in polished drops that can be brushed away; when the hand is withdrawn, the drops are present again in precisely the same places.

The buildings have no sign of tools.

Their organization is unmistakably urban: streets meet at deliberate angles, drainage channels follow contours, stairs connect terraces, and plazas gather around reservoirs. There are arcades, retaining walls, bridges, towers, courtyards, cisterns, and long roofed galleries. Yet nowhere is there a chisel mark, saw cut, hinge, nail, wheel rut, shard, inscription, or worked metal fitting. Doorways have no doors. Windows are simple apertures. Bridges seem to have grown across gaps by the slow meeting of mineral deposits. Columns resemble basalt trunks or the calcified stems of enormous plants. Their spacing is regular, but not perfectly so—regular in the way ribs are regular.

The city may not have been built. It may be the mature form of the mountain.

Its oldest walls are composed of volcanic stone banded with limestone and fibrous green material. In places the layers pass gradually from masonry into bedrock, making it impossible to say where construction ends and geology begins. A house may have three square chambers on one side and dissolve into a cave on the other. Staircases continue beneath pools, descend through stone, and emerge upside down in ceilings elsewhere. Aqueducts arch between precipices without supports, carrying channels of water whose surfaces remain level even when the channels turn vertically.

Erosion has softened every edge without destroying the larger order. Steps are bowed by passage, though nothing passes. Corners have rounded under rain. Facades bear pale runnels, old lichen crusts, and shallow cavities where water has patiently tested the stone. The weathering feels complete rather than ongoing. Each surface has arrived at the exact degree of age proper to it. No wall is freshly broken; no wall is close to collapse. Fallen blocks rest below parapets, embedded in moss, but none ever fall.

This equilibrium extends to the climate. It is always hot, always wet, always shortly after rain. Puddles remain full but do not overflow. Clouds rest below the terraces and never rise into the streets. The sun moves enough to define morning and afternoon, yet shadows return too soon to their earlier positions. A person who watches the shadow of a tower will see it lengthen eastward, then find—without witnessing a reversal—that it is once again short and westward.

Distance behaves even less reliably.

Two towers visible across a plaza may take an hour to reach. A district seen on a horizon can appear behind the next wall. Streets that descend steadily return to their starting elevation without climbing. From certain balconies, the observer can look down upon the roof of the balcony itself. A flight of ninety steps always has ninety steps when counted upward and eighty-nine when counted downward. No step is missing. The discrepancy belongs not to the staircase but to counting.

The farther districts are crowded with repetitions that are not copies. Reservoir follows reservoir; garden follows garden; each has different stone, different leaves, different arrangements of columns, but all occupy what feels like the same location. Sometimes an avenue opens onto several versions of one square superimposed in clear daylight. In one, the central pool is round. In another, it is dry and planted with reeds. In a third, the pool stands upright like a pane of water. All are equally solid. Walking toward one does not make the others vanish; instead, the traveler gradually acquires several shadows, each choosing a different route.

There is no evident danger in the ordinary sense. No predators wait in the foliage. No storm approaches. No structure gives way. The heat never becomes hotter, and the floodwater never rises. Yet the place resists alteration.

A stone lifted from a courtyard can be carried away, but the courtyard continues to contain it. The carried stone remains real and heavy. Returning it creates no duplicate; both stones become the same stone as soon as they touch. A vine can be cut with a sharp fragment of rock, though there are no tools here. Pale sap appears, the severed ends separate, and an instant later the vine is whole—not healed, but as though the cutting remains an event that never gained permission to become history.

Footprints are possible in mud. They do not fade. Neither do they accumulate. After a hundred steps, only the latest few are visible, but the earlier prints are not erased; looking back from a different angle reveals them all occupying the same shallow depressions.

The city has rules, though they are not rules of change.

Water is always arriving but never more abundant. Plants are always alive but never growing. Stone is ancient but never older. The cataracts always fall but never descend. Sound fills every space but has no source that can be approached. Paths lead somewhere, but arrival does not increase the distance from departure.

At the highest accessible terrace stands no palace or temple. There is only a vast open court cut into reddish rock and crowded with pillar-like trees. Their crowns interlace hundreds of feet overhead, forming a green vault wet with condensation. The trunks are square on one side, fluted on another, and smooth as river stone on the third. They have no fourth side; walking around one returns a person to where they began before completing the circuit.

In the center is a shallow pool.

Its surface reflects not the sky above but the entire terraced city as seen from far beyond the mountain: a green-black crown surrounded by clouds, its cliffs pouring white rivers into a world hidden below. No observer is visible in the reflection. Neither is there any route down.

The roar is strongest here, although the cataracts are distant. It presses from every direction with perfect, immovable force. The pool remains smooth beneath it. The leaves remain still. Sunlight lies calmly across the paving.

The place gives no impression of waiting. Waiting would imply that something might happen. It is not dead, ruined, sleeping, or preserved. It is complete—an immense hot city of water, stone, and vegetation caught not at one moment, but in a condition for which moments are unnecessary.

Nothing lives here except the green things.

Nothing changes except the traveler.

And even that may be true only while the traveler is looking.
