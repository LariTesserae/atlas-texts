The country is not built upon stone. Stone occurs here and there—black splinters caught in roots, grains in the stomachs of animals—but it is an impurity, an inclusion. The mountains themselves are vegetable.

They rise in long, cold arcs above the cloud deck: immense braided trunks laid one over another, each ridge a bundle of pale fibers under dark bark. Some stand upright as towers; others lean across valleys wide enough to contain weather. Their highest crowns are lost in a permanent night of needles and interwoven fronds. Between them hang open chambers miles deep.

Seen from a distance, the range appears densely forested. Seen from within, it is almost empty.

The living mass is gathered into walls, columns, cables, and ceilings. Between these structures stretch enormous volumes of black air. A traveler may walk for days along the back of a single root while, on either side, the land falls away into hollows without visible bottoms. A few filaments cross those abysses, trembling like harp strings. Far across the void, another trunk-face rises under faint veils of condensation, so remote that its growths resemble frost on a window.

There is no true daylight beneath the upper canopy. Light enters as a mineral-blue diffusion during the middle hours, too weak to cast more than a suggestion of shadow. Most illumination is made by life: green pinpricks among the bark scales, slow amber pulses in fungal bladders, and the cold white flashing of animals disturbed in flight. Along fresh wounds in the mountains, sap shines turquoise for a few minutes before crusting over.

The cold preserves every scent. Crushed leaves smell of pepper and snow. Wet bark carries a faint sweetness. Old growth chambers smell dry and papery, while the low condensation basins have the sour, almost animal odor of fermenting pollen.

Water is never plentiful enough to form a continuous world. It arrives in episodes.

Moisture gathers first on the high, hairlike foliage. Droplets form for hours, swelling in darkness until the canopy can no longer hold them. Then the forest rains inward. Water falls through tier after tier, sometimes for several miles, breaking into mist before reaching anything below. On broad leaves it gathers again, runs through veins converted into open channels, and spills from pointed tips in bright, intermittent ropes.

Pools occupy folds in the living terrain. They are narrow and deep, their margins soft with moss and drinking fibers. Some last through the cold season; others vanish overnight, absorbed by the mountain. Streams are stranger. They rarely run downhill for long. The plant’s vessels seize them, carrying the water sideways or upward through transparent tubes. A rivulet may disappear into bark at one end of a valley and emerge centuries of walking distance away as rain from a branch ceiling.

During dry intervals the air becomes sharp and thin. Moisture retreats into sealed bulbs and buried tissues. The great organism contracts. Cracks open along the ridges with reports like splitting ice.

This is a young country, though its dimensions suggest antiquity. Growth is recent and still violent. New ridges thrust out pale and unweathered, armored in overlapping scales with edges keen enough to cut hide. Root-spurs spear across older chambers. Buds the size of hills force apart ceilings and unfold over the course of a season, each leaf emerging wrinkled, tender, and almost colorless before darkening to blue-black.

There has been little time for wear to soften anything. Paths end at raw clefts. Broken stalks remain jagged. Whole slopes are covered in stiff hooks and unfinished thorns. Where a limb has torn free, the exposed fibers form cliffs of wet white grain, already crowded by tiny scavenging plants.

The landscape’s loudness comes partly from this ceaseless construction.

Fibers strain. Hollow trunks boom under changes in pressure. Seedpods burst with cannon-like cracks, flinging winged grains across the open chambers. Wind enters the upper vents and makes the mountain pipes sing—deep notes, sustained for hours, that can be felt through the soles before they are heard. Rain rattles on the canopy in sheets. Far below, unseen waterfalls strike leaves with a continuous surf-like roar.

Animals add their own layers. Most are small in relation to the place, and therefore seem fleeting: swarms, flocks, processions, brief illuminations in the dark.

Long-bodied grazers move along the underside of branches, gripping with rings of hooked feet. They scrape films from the bark and call to one another with wooden knocks. Clouds of warm insects gather around new blossoms, their combined wingbeat sounding like a distant engine. Pale gliding creatures leap between root cables and unfold membranes ribbed like leaves. They may remain aloft for days, feeding on suspended spores, navigating by the mountain’s low vibrations.

The condensation pools hold eyeless swimmers. When their water begins to be absorbed, they bury themselves in wet tissue or climb out on delicate jointed fins, following the smell of another basin. Predators wait along these migrations: flat things that resemble patches of lichen until their mouths open, and narrow, furred hunters that can run vertically down bark. The furred hunters hunt in complete silence, but after a kill the whole pack sings—a shrill cascading chorus answered from distant walls.

The largest animals are seldom seen whole. They move through the void between the mountain-trunks, grazing on hanging foliage. A passing one is first known by the failure of local winds. Then luminous parasites appear, arranged in constellations along a moving surface. Branches bend. Leaves tear loose and spin downward. At last comes a slow exhalation, warm enough to produce clouds.

Nothing here is still for long. Flowering fronts travel across the range like weather. One valley opens its blossoms, and millions of animals arrive; within days the nectar is gone, the petals collapse, and the migration pours onward. Sudden freezes seal the pools and drive burrowing species deeper into the warm sapwood. Growth-quakes reroute trails. Windstorms strip old foliage and reveal chambers that have not been open for generations. Sometimes an entire suspended forest detaches from the ceiling and falls into the dark, its animals flying, clinging, or calling as it goes.

Despite the total dominion of plant life, settlements are rare. They occupy places where several structural ribs meet, or where an old wound has dried hard enough to bear weight.

A settlement may consist of six shelters woven into the curled edge of a leaf. Their walls are felted fiber, stitched with gut and sealed with resin. Ladders made from knotted vine descend toward water pockets. Narrow bridges cross local gaps, each one renewed constantly as the anchoring bark grows around it.

The people of these heights possess bone knives, chipped stone brought up from rare inclusions, wooden wedges hardened in cold smoke, and small amounts of soft metal hammered from nodules found in ancient roots. Fire is difficult and dangerous. It is kept in clay-lined pods or in the shells of enormous nuts, never allowed to touch the living floor. More often they use heat-beetles bred in ceramic baskets, or fermenting pulp packed beneath sleeping platforms.

Their marks upon the country are slight: footholds cut into a trunk-face; resin arrows indicating a safe vessel crossing; a whistle hung where wind announces a change in pressure. Some abandoned structures remain, but the mountain slowly consumes them. Floors buckle as veins swell underneath. Doorways close. Posts sprout shoots. A house left empty for a decade becomes a knot in the bark.

Travelers carry long sounding staffs. A surface that appears solid may be only a thin rind over a chamber thousands of feet deep. They test each step, listening not merely for hollowness but for the pitch. A high clear note means young tissue: strong, tense, and liable to split without warning. A soft note means rot. A deep resonance means a great open vessel beneath, perhaps safe, perhaps carrying a surge of water or sap.

There are rules understood by everyone who lives here.

Never sleep below a newly opened flower; its night visitors are too large.

Never drink from a pool whose surface trembles against the wind.

When the bark begins to tick, move toward a branch junction and lash yourself down.

Do not follow blue lights in a dry chamber. Those lights belong to seed-lures, and by morning the chamber will have closed.

Most important: when the mountain draws breath, become still.

The breath comes irregularly, perhaps several times in a lifetime. Vents open across the high canopy. Cold air pours inward, rushing through the voids with enough force to strip animals from the walls. The trunks flex. Suspended bridges swing into darkness. Then the vents close, and the air is driven elsewhere through passages too large to map. Communities calculate their calendars from these events, yet no one agrees whether the entire range is one organism breathing, a colony communicating through pressure, or merely a forest responding in unison to distant weather.

On the clearest nights, the uppermost ridges break through the clouds. There the cold is severe enough to silver every hair and thorn. The sky above is almost black. Stars can be seen between towering fans of foliage, but the view is never broad: the living architecture intervenes, always climbing, always branching.

At those heights the few dwellers build small observation nests from dead fiber. They listen to the booming interior and watch the crowns grow against the stars. Occasionally one crown divides. A pale spear rises with astonishing speed, sheds plates of ice, and opens into a fan wider than a city. For a short while its underside reflects the starlight, and the dark world below becomes faintly visible: ridge beyond ridge, chasm beyond chasm, an immeasurable scaffold of life suspended over depths that no falling object has yet been heard to reach.

Then the new leaves darken.

The country disappears again, leaving only its noises—the groan of growth, the rain traveling inward, the wings of numberless animals, and somewhere far off, passing through miles of hollow wood, the slow knock of one inhabited place answering another.
