At first it resembles a high tropical plateau after a storm: a broad green country under ordinary white daylight, steaming where the sun finds it, with shallow water flashing across open ground. The horizon is far away. Ridges rise in long, abrupt folds, and isolated towers stand several kilometers apart. Clouds travel below some of the outer escarpments. Warm rain remains suspended in the air as mist, gathering on every edge and dripping without cease.

But there is no ground.

The entire plateau is constructed from living plants.

Its plains are immense rafts of interlocked root, layered so deeply that they bear their own hills. What seems to be soil is a dark, crumbly tissue produced by the upper surface of those roots: soft bark continually grown, waterlogged, split, and reabsorbed. Beneath it are pale load-bearing ribs, hollow chambers, sap conduits broad enough to resemble tunnels, and tightly braided fibers descending into cloud. There is no stone buried below, no mineral foundation waiting at depth. If the plateau were cut from top to bottom, every stratum would bleed clear or amber fluid.

Nothing here has been left unmade. The slopes are buttressed. Valleys have raised rims. Pools sit in smooth, bowl-shaped calyces with petaled spillways. Even the apparent accidents—the cracks, terraces, ravines, and scattered sharp peaks—are consequences of growth, rejection, grafting, and structural repair. The landscape is architecture, though it has no architects distinct from itself and no tools other than pressure, thirst, sunlight, adhesion, and time.

Vegetation fills everything, yet the place feels sparse.

There are few separate plants in the familiar sense. A low, seamless hide covers the open expanses, glossy after rain and quilted into irregular cells. Here and there, kilometers apart, enormous vertical forms interrupt it: fluted trunks without crowns, fan-shaped walls, cages of green spars, or solitary flowering pylons that open to the sky. The distances between these structures are exposed and sweeping. Nothing clutters the view. No underbrush crowds the path because the path itself is alive, and the plain has no need to colonize itself.

Across the largest reaches, the surface is pale green and almost featureless except for seams filled with rainwater. These seams divide the plateau into plates. They are not cracks caused by age, but expansion joints: flexible channels that widen when the living substrate swells. Water races through them after each storm, turning the plain into a vast geometric marsh. In dry intervals the channels narrow, their lips curling together until only shining threads remain.

The country is young. Its edges have not yet softened.

New ridges rise with knife-thin crests, formed when deep tissues inflate faster than the upper skin can stretch. Fresh escarpments are clean and brilliant, striped with ivory fibers and red sapwood. They do not crumble. They unzip. A cliff face may split along a vertical seam with a report like torn canvas magnified across a valley. The exposed inner wall immediately beads with moisture. Within hours it greens; within days it begins weaving a new skin over itself.

This constant making gives the plateau its turbulence. The land does not move quickly enough to look animal, but it moves too quickly to be mistaken for geology. Horizons alter over the course of an afternoon. A basin that held a warm lake at noon may tilt by sunset, pouring its water through a newly opened gorge. A row of buttresses may emerge from a flat plain in sequence, each lifting with a deep wooden detonation. Elsewhere, overloaded roots collapse into the chambers beneath them, leaving a sudden circular gulf fringed by tightening fibers.

There is no permanent map. There are only recent shapes.

The water is almost as restless as the vegetation. Warm rain falls frequently, often from low clouds generated by the plateau itself. The living surface exhales enormous quantities of vapor. It beads on upright membranes, runs down grooves, collects in hanging cisterns, and overflows through trumpet-shaped mouths. Some cliffs weep over their full height, not because water has seeped through rock but because internal vessels have opened deliberately to relieve pressure. The resulting falls are thin, numerous, and oddly regular, like curtains of glass thread.

In the broad depressions lie hot, shallow lakes. Their floors pulse faintly as submerged tissues exchange gases. Clear blisters rise and burst, carrying bitter green scents into the air. Around the margins, mats fold themselves into floating baffles that slow the water and trap whatever the rain has brought—mostly dust, dissolved salts, and airborne spores. There are no reeds in these marshes, no frogs, no insects, no tracks in the soft surface. The absence of animals is complete.

Flowers still appear, though not to summon pollinators. They are hydraulic organs.

Some are wide as courtyards and hold rain in waxy interiors. Some open only during the hottest part of the day, exposing dark surfaces that absorb heat and drive sap upward through the plateau. Others snap in the wind, dispersing powder from one living district to another. Their colors are not especially vivid. Most are cream, green, rust-red, or the dim violet of a storm cloud. Without animal eyes to attract, the vegetation spends little effort on display. Its beauty comes from scale, translucence, repetition, and function.

There are no nests. No burrows. No grazing. No pollination by wing or mouth. Rot itself is different here, conducted internally by the landscape’s own soft tissues and microbial films so deeply incorporated into the greater plant body that they seem less like inhabitants than organs. Fallen structures do not become habitat. They are sealed, liquefied, and drawn back into the rootwork.

The air is hot despite the elevation. Moisture makes the heat intimate and persistent, wrapping exposed surfaces in condensation. Where the wind fails, the atmosphere feels almost chewable: rich with resin, wet cellulose, green tannin, and the faint sweetness of ruptured storage bulbs. Yet strong winds often sweep the plateau. They come up the escarpments from the cloud-filled depths, strike the sparse towers, and break into long turbulent streams.

This is when the landscape becomes loud.

The vertical walls are shaped to flex and resonate. Wind passes through rows of slits, producing bass notes that carry for tens of kilometers. Hollow trunks answer in uneven chords. Tension fibers creak beneath the plains. Water drums on stretched leaf-roofs, roars in spillways, and hisses where it strikes hot outer bark. Seedless pods clatter together, not as dead husks but as valves regulating the pressure in aerial chambers. Every so often, beneath all these sounds, a deep concussion travels through the ground as another structural layer locks into place.

There is no birdsong to scale the noise, no insect hum to soften it into wilderness. The sounds are architectural and meteorological: groaning spans, flushing reservoirs, thunder, wind organs, tearing membranes. During storms they overlap until the whole plateau seems to be speaking through strain.

The tallest constructions rise at the margins of the region. They resemble unfinished towers assembled from latticed ribs, open to the weather and constantly adding new arches to their upper edges. Water is lifted through them by evaporation and released inland as warm mist. From a distance they look ruined, but ruin is not quite possible here. Missing sections are often intentional. A gap may admit wind, shed excess weight, or prevent a resonance from becoming destructive.

Their sparseness makes them monumental. One may stand alone in a hundred square kilometers of low green plain, visible through steam like the frame of a cathedral. Near its base, the living ground is drawn upward into sweeping braces. Pools occupy the spaces between them. Veils of root hair hang in the shade, drinking mist before it can touch the surface.

Nothing suggests hands, wheels, blades, fire, or measurement. Nevertheless, proportion recurs. Arches share the same curve. Channels branch at consistent angles. Reservoirs appear at regular heights. The plateau has arrived at engineering without invention. Growth is its only craft, and selection its memory.

Its central rule is that no surface remains merely exposed. An opening becomes an intake, a wound becomes a seam, a pool becomes a cistern. Every stress is answered by form.

Its second rule is that form does not imply permanence.

A valley exists because water is needed there now. A wall stands because this season’s wind arrives from the east. If the rains shift, the wall will soften at its base and be lowered strand by strand. If too much water gathers, the plateau will grow a spillway before the basin ruptures—or attempt to. It does not always succeed. Great failures occur: raft sections peel away from the escarpment and descend intact into the clouds, trailing roots that whip and snap behind them. The remaining edge seals itself in a fever of pale growth.

Its third rule is that growth has no obligation to be gentle.

At rare intervals, pressure passes through the entire region. The open plain rises in a slow wave, lifting lakes and ridges together. Water spills in shining sheets from one basin to the next. Tower walls bow. Seams open. For several minutes the plateau reveals that its apparent geography is only the upper expression of something deeper: a woven, tensile body adjusting its weight over emptiness.

Then the wave moves on.

Behind it, the land is rearranged. New marshes glitter where dry flats had been. Sharp green fins stand in rows. A tower has folded itself shut. Another has begun opening. Steam pours from fresh rents, and warm rain starts again under a sky that remains, strangely, neither alien nor dramatic—just bright cloud, blue intervals, and normal sun falling on a world where nature has built everything and finished nothing.
