The land lies in a broad depression only slightly above the reach of an unseen sea. It is not a valley so much as a settling of the world: a region where the crust sagged, cracked, and was worn for ages without ever being opened fully to daylight. Low ridges surround it, but they are distant and almost always lost behind mist. Within them extends a dark plain of stone, mud, and innumerable moving bodies.

There are no trees, no grasses, no moss-bright slopes. Nothing holds the horizon upright. The mineral ground is exposed wherever the animals have not covered it: blue-black shale, pale ribs of limestone, flats of compacted gray silt. The rocks are old in their shapes. Corners have gone from them. Low shelves have been rounded by condensation, abrasive hides, and the passage of millions of feet. Water gathers in hollows polished smooth as sockets, remains for several days, then drains through cracks with a long subterranean gargle. The larger pools are shallow and cold. Their surfaces are seldom still.

The darkness is not complete, though at first it seems so. A permanent ceiling of low cloud fills the basin, dense enough to extinguish sun and stars. It gives back only a diluted iron-colored glow, varying from charcoal gray to the faint violet of a bruise. At intervals, remote lightning moves silently within the upper cloud. Only later does its thunder arrive, folded into the greater noise of the plain.

Everything below that ceiling is occupied.

Animals stand flank to flank over much of the basin. They cluster on ridges, pack the ravines, mantle the shelves, and crowd the rims of every water pocket. Some are low, broad-backed grazers without anything recognizable to graze upon. They rasp mineral crusts from the stone with layered mouthplates, feeding on thin bacterial veils and salts lifted from below by seepage. Their hides are white and softly folded, marked with streaks of black mud. Where thousands have passed, the rock has been planed into shallow lanes.

Among them move tall, jointed creatures whose bodies are borne above the crowd on six narrow legs. Their heads hang beneath their chests. They feed by lowering combs of translucent cartilage into the warm breath rising from fissures, filtering spores, microbes, shed skin, eggs, and airborne tissue from the vapor. They remain motionless for hours, then relocate all at once, stepping delicately over the backs of smaller animals.

The smallest forms are beyond counting. Soft, hand-sized runners spill through crevices in continuous streams. Thumb-sized shell animals cling in layers to every damp surface, including the larger beasts. Black ribbon things coil in pools and knot themselves around each other when the rain begins. Clouds of pale flyers occupy the air from ground level to the cloud base, though many have no wings in the ordinary sense. They inflate lateral membranes with warm gas from their own bodies and drift until cooling brings them down. In places the sky appears granular with them.

There is no quiet interval.

The basin roars with wings, throats, shells, and impacts. Deep calls pass through the ground more strongly than through the air, making loose stones jump. Flocks produce a continuous dry rattling, like gravel poured without end. The broad-backed herds pulse in low unison, each animal matching the vibration of its neighbors until several square miles become a single resonant organ. Predators announce themselves with abrupt concussions rather than cries. When one strikes, the nearest thousands surge away, and the retreat travels outward as a visible wave. Bodies collide. Mud sucks. Plates clap. Wings beat hard enough to raise sheets of silt.

Even the rain is loud. It comes irregularly, usually as a fine condensation that forms beneath the cloud and falls only a short distance. At other times the cloud descends to the ground and coats every surface without drops. Then hides shine, limestone sweats, and water runs along the old animal tracks. Once or twice in a cycle, a true storm crosses the basin. Heavy drops hammer shells and exposed stone; hollows fill in minutes; dry channels become braided, ankle-deep floods. The animals drink as they move, or open pores and let the water enter through their skins.

Within hours the flood is gone. Much of it drains downward. The remainder is swallowed, carried away in bladders, trapped under overlapping shells, or churned into mud. Moisture is not rare here, but it is never abundant enough to spread freely. Water exists as an event, a possession, or a hiding place.

The absence of vegetation gives the ecology a stripped, unsettling directness. Life does not rise from the land in stalks and crowns. It lies over the land as appetite. The primary abundance is microscopic: dark chemosynthetic films inside cracks, colonies around cold mineral seeps, and airborne cells nourished by sulfurous vapor. Almost every visible animal either scrapes this hidden production from stone, filters it from air and water, or eats something that does.

Death feeds the basin more openly than growth. A fallen giant rarely touches bare ground. Scavengers arrive before its last movements end, climbing across it in layers. The outer hide is opened, the warm interior becomes shelter, and eggs are pressed into tissues that will remain moist. For a few days the carcass is a hill crowded with life. Then the bones separate, are gnawed for salts, and vanish into the traffic. Only an oval of darker mud remains.

Predators are numerous but difficult to distinguish. Camouflage here does not imitate foliage or shadowed branches; it imitates the crowd. A hunter may resemble several harmless animals pressed together. What appears to be a cluster of shell-backs can unfold into one radial body with many gripping limbs. Long pale forms travel beneath the densely packed herds, moving through mud or loose sediment. They surface only enough to pull prey downward. In the pools, the apparent stones sometimes breathe.

Because sight is of little use, most creatures orient by pressure, heat, and communal sound. Calls are less messages than changes imposed upon the medium. A low note can open a corridor through a herd. A rapid clicking can make flyers descend. Certain predators reproduce the vibration of rain, drawing thirsty animals toward dry pits. Some harmless species survive by carrying the acoustic signature of something enormous.

Sound also maps the landscape. Every bluff, fissure, and hollow alters the basin’s roar. Animals know where they are by the echoes inside the noise. A collapse or newly flooded channel can confuse migrations for days. Crowds circle misplaced landmarks, wearing rings into the shale. Over centuries these mistakes become part of the terrain.

There are stranger regions farther inward.

Near the basin’s center, the ground divides into thousands of narrow stone fins, all rounded to the same height by erosion. Animals fill the spaces between them, and pale flyers roost along their edges until the fins appear furred. Warm mist rises there continuously. It condenses on bodies before it reaches rock, so the place is wet despite having almost no open water. The density becomes so great that many creatures never stand on the ground at all. They hatch, feed, mate, and die while moving across the backs of others.

Beyond the fins lies a shallow expanse known only by its different sound: a wide mud flat where the crowd thins but never disappears. The mud is underlain by a flexible mineral crust. Weight makes it bow and release trapped gas through distant vents. Thus every migration plays the flat like a vast, slow instrument. Notes rise miles away from the feet that caused them. Herds answer these notes instinctively, and the resulting resonance can continue long after the original animals have passed.

Nothing here has been built. The lanes are not roads, though generations follow them. The circular hollows are not pens, though creatures gather there. Tall cones along the seep fields resemble towers in the darkness, but they are mineral deposits grown around vents and polished by rubbing hides. The landscape repeatedly approaches the appearance of intention, then dissolves back into erosion, instinct, and accumulation.

Its governing rule is not simply that life is plentiful. It is that no creature encounters the world directly. Stone is reached through shell and hide. Water is found beneath mouths, wings, and eggs. Wind arrives already used, warmed by lungs and thickened with scent. Even darkness is crowded with signals.

There may be open ground somewhere under the moving mass, but it is rarely exposed for more than a breath. When it appears—a patch of wet shale revealed by a sudden stampede—it looks startlingly empty, almost unnatural. Then the streams of small animals close over it, larger bodies press in behind them, and the old, deafening surface of life restores itself.
