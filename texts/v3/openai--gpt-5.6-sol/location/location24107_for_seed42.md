At the summit of a long-eroded mountain lies a lake with no visible shore.

Its basin is small—perhaps the size of a walled town—but steep-sided and extraordinarily deep. From the valleys below it appears as a dark oval set into the mountain’s crown. Clouds drag across its surface, and cold rain keeps it perpetually full. The landscape proper begins beneath that surface, where the whole basin is occupied by a submerged city grown almost entirely from plants.

There is very little bare ground. The ancient crater walls have been covered for so long that stone survives only as an unseen armature. Root-felt lies over it in heavy terraces: generations of moss, waterwood, fibrous tubers, and pale runners compressed into a substrate several body-lengths thick. The lake floor is not mud but old vegetation, soft in its upper layers and increasingly dark and woody below. Even the slopes seem botanical rather than geological. Ribs of root trace the contours where ridges once were, rounding every fracture and catching whatever the water carries.

The city rises from these slopes in widely spaced towers.

Each tower began as a planted stake or a living trunk. Over centuries it was bent, grafted, hollowed, and braided with neighboring growth. Their bases are massive, but their upper stories divide into narrow lattices bearing translucent leaves. Some resemble cypresses enlarged to the scale of bell towers; others are bulbous and chambered, with rows of openings sealed by membranes. Rope-vines run between them in measured arcs. Suspended paths of woven reed sway above empty plazas, though here “above” is simply nearer the light.

It is urban without being crowded. Buildings stand apart, separated by generous volumes of blue-green water. A civic district may consist of only seven living structures arranged around a clear central void. Gates are indicated by paired trunks rather than walls. Roads are vertical as often as horizontal: buoyant cords, notched climbing stems, chains of air-filled gourds, and guide-lines threaded through the open water. The plan is deliberate and old, but the city has the spacing of a grove.

Nothing was assembled quickly. Construction here is horticulture practiced over generations. A room is formed by persuading broad roots to enclose a volume. A doorway is maintained by pruning. A bridge grows from opposite banks for years before the tips are bound together with twisted bark. Repairs are made with wedges of shell, stone blades, bone needles, and careful grafts. Metal, if it exists at all, appears only in a few green-black hooks inherited from an earlier age. Fire is absent beneath the lake; heat is kept in sealed bladders of fermenting plant matter, and light comes from the water overhead, from polished pale leaves, and from colonies of luminous insects cultivated in wicker cages.

The shallow upper reaches are bright enough to feel almost terrestrial. Daylight passes through the cold surface in broad, moving bands. The city’s highest terraces, though submerged, are lit with ordinary cloud-filtered morning: gray, silver, fern-green. Farther down, color narrows. Red vanishes first, then yellow. The oldest streets lie in a blue twilight beneath canopies of leaf and root, their windows marked by soft points of animal light.

Vegetation occupies nearly everything, yet the place does not feel lush in the manner of a tangled swamp. Growth has been disciplined by cold, altitude, and long tending. Leaves are broad but few. Stems are thick, slow, and widely separated. A single frond may take decades to unfurl and then endure for a century, collecting a faint nap of algae along its upper surface. The emptiness between plants is important. It preserves corridors of light and water, and it allows the architecture to be read from a distance.

Above the towers floats a sparse ceiling of enormous pads. Their roots descend like columns through the city, some wrapped with ladders and small hanging gardens. The pads press close beneath the lake surface, trapping flat mirrors of air. These are the city’s breathing courts. Inside them, the underside of a leaf becomes a roof, silvered by the boundary between air and water. Condensation beads on its veins. The chambers smell of sap, wet fiber, fish oil, and cold stone, though no stone is visible.

The inhabitants—when they appear—move between water and these air pockets. Their forms are easy to mistake for bundled reeds: narrow bodies wrapped in woven insulation, hands webbed by fitted paddles, faces hidden behind bark-and-bladder breathing hoods. They travel slowly, pulling themselves along fixed lines rather than swimming freely. Tools hang from their belts on short cords: shell adzes, obsidian scrapers, bone awls, baskets with closing throats. Every dropped object is expected to sink into a lower district, so nothing is left unsecured.

Much of the city’s movement belongs to animals instead.

Long-bodied fish browse on the tower walls in silver herds. Round, dark grazers crawl across the root terraces, trimming algae with rasping mouths. Small crustaceans inhabit the woven bridges by the thousands, turning the fibers clean. Eel-like pollinators pass through tubular flowers, emerging with their heads dusted in pale yellow clouds. Between the upper pads, amphibious birds dive from the mountain weather into the lake, folding their wings and continuing through the streets like thrown knives.

Larger creatures arrive rarely but dominate the city when they do. Broad-backed lake rays drift through the plaza voids, slow as weather. They must tilt to pass between towers, stirring curtains of loose seed. A blind, pale animal lives in the deepest root vaults and announces itself by knocking its armored forehead against the buried crater wall. The sound may come from any direction. The city’s residents count the intervals but do not descend to investigate.

Although almost everything changes slowly, the place is loud.

Water carries every impact. The braided towers groan under their own buoyancy. Seedpods snap open with concussions like distant axes. Rain striking the lake roof becomes a continuous granular thunder, strongest in the upper streets. Guide-lines hum in the currents, each tuned by length and tension. Animals click, scrape, drum, and bark. From deep below comes the periodic cracking of ancient root layers settling under the city’s weight.

The loudest structures are deliberate. Hollow trunks have been grown across the prevailing currents, their side branches pierced with carefully sized openings. Water passes through them and produces low, sustained notes. Each district has its own chord. The tones are less music than infrastructure: they mark location in murky water, warn of changes in flow, and reveal when a bridge has broken or a channel has clogged. On storm days the entire settlement sounds at once. The water trembles with overlapping drones, woody knocks, cable-hum, rain roar, and the dry internal rattle of seed chambers.

There are few sudden currents. The lake has no strong river entering or leaving it; water seeps through the mountain by hidden routes. Circulation is patient. Cold surface water descends along one wall while slightly warmer water rises through porous root beds on the other. A fallen leaf may spend months spiraling from the upper gardens to the lower terraces. Silt settles in fine, undisturbed bands. Streets change through accumulation rather than catastrophe.

Erosion is equally slow and thoroughly incorporated. The crater has long since lost its sharpness. Wherever the underlying mountain sheds a fragment, roots catch it and build around it. Wherever a current hollows a passage, gardeners line the new channel with woven stems until it becomes a recognized lane. Old damage is not repaired back to an original form; it is absorbed into the plan. A collapsed tower becomes a horizontal neighborhood. A drowned air court becomes a fish nursery. An abandoned corridor fills with clear-stemmed plants and is eventually designated a garden.

The city’s boundaries are distinct even though it has no enclosing wall. Beyond the outermost towers, the tended root-felt thins. Guide-lines stop. The sounding trunks can no longer be heard as separate notes. The crater walls descend into darker, less organized growth, and the mountain’s original shape begins to assert itself under the vegetation. Farther still is the central depth: an open shaft of cold water where almost nothing grows.

This shaft is the one true emptiness in the basin.

The city encircles it but does not bridge it. Towers lean toward its blue-black volume, and roads terminate at balconies of living wood. Fish enter and return hours later. Seeds fall into it and do not rise. The deepest civic chambers face the shaft as if it were a square, a temple, or a well.

On clear afternoons, when the mountain clouds part, sunlight reaches down through the center. For a short time the shaft becomes a pale column, and the entire city is visible around it: spaced towers, hanging roads, air mirrors, browsing herds, patient gardeners, and the immense layered floor of roots beneath them. Then the clouds close. The beam withdraws, district by district, and the old botanical city resumes its cool green dusk, sounding steadily inside the mountain’s crown.
