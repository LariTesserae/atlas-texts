### The High Condenser

The city occupies the upper lip of a mountain that is still being made.

It stands at an elevation where distance loses its softness. The horizon is hard and close-looking, though it may be hundreds of kilometers away: tiers of black plateaus, pale mineral flats, and ranks of volcanic teeth rising through a copper-colored atmospheric haze. There are no forests below, no alpine meadows, no lichen whitening the stones. Nothing green moderates the scale. Every surface belongs either to geology or construction, and from afar it is often impossible to tell which is which.

The mountain is not an old peak lifted and weathered into shape. It is raw extrusion—a broad, slow blister in the crust. Its flanks consist of basaltic shelves, vitrified spills, and immense folds of stone that have cooled only at their surfaces. Cracks open over decades. Ridges rise by fractions of a meter each year. Whole districts tilt so gradually that the change is first noticed in cistern levels, doorframes, and the behavior of hanging chains.

Erosion has scarcely begun. Edges remain sharp. Scree is rare because most cliffs have not yet had time to crumble. Where the ground has split, the exposed interiors are smooth and dark, like snapped ceramic. Dust gathers only in sheltered machinery courts, a fine gray powder produced less by weather than by drilling, traffic, and the patient abrasion of metal soles.

Across this unfinished geology spreads the city.

It is dense without being tall in the ordinary sense. Buildings crowd one another in terraces, pressure-sealed halls, retaining chambers, stacked foundries, and narrow residential blocks sunk partly into the hot rock. Roofs serve as roads; roads become aqueducts; aqueducts enter cooling towers and emerge as walls. There is little unused space. Every crevice contains a pipe, cable, ceramic conduit, inspection stair, or heat exchanger. From above, the settlement resembles a mineral deposit organized by intelligence: veins of dull steel, pale refractory brick, black glass, red oxide, and white salt.

Its industrial districts are enormous but seldom loud.

The engines here are built for duration rather than speed. Great pistons descend once every several minutes. Flywheels turn too slowly for their motion to be obvious at first glance. Kilns hold a single temperature for months. Deep beneath the streets, pressure machines exploit the mountain’s gradual swelling, converting tectonic strain into mechanical work. Their movement is measured in the flexing of walls and the periodic tightening of cables.

The city’s loudest events are therefore isolated: the remote report of rock parting, the brief metallic scream of a brake, a hammer striking three times in a maintenance vault. Afterward, quiet closes again. One hears the faint tick of contracting plates, condensate moving inside hidden pipes, and the almost subsonic pulse of pumps. In the narrow streets, footsteps travel farther than voices.

Heat is the governing condition. Daylight is ordinary—clear, white, and often pitiless—but the stone stores it with unusual efficiency. Even at this altitude, exposed surfaces become too hot to touch. Air rises from the lower valleys already warmed, then passes over fissures and industrial vents. Streets shimmer. Metal railings are wrapped in ceramic sleeves. Doors have double handles: one for night, one for day.

Yet the place is damp in pockets.

Moisture arrives intermittently from immense river gorges far below. Warm air ascends the mountain’s flank carrying invisible water, then meets the chilled upper surfaces of condenser towers. The towers stand throughout the city like blunt, hollow monuments. Their shaded interiors are lined with ribbed metal and porous stone. At certain hours, water appears on them without rain: first as a darkening, then as beads, then as thin streams descending into covered channels.

Actual rain is rare and abrupt. Clouds catch on the summit and release a few heavy drops, widely spaced, each one striking dust with a small explosion. The shower may last four minutes. Streets steam afterward. Water that reaches exposed ground vanishes quickly, but the city is shaped to intercept nearly all of it. Roofs incline toward slots. Courtyards are concave. Statues, where they exist, hold their arms in positions designed to direct runoff.

Beneath the oldest quarter lies the Reservoir of Minutes, so called because its water level is marked not by depth but by how long the city could continue if condensation ceased. Its chambers are narrow and immensely deep. The water is warm, mineral-rich, and dark as polished iron. Pipes dip into it from many eras of construction, some still functional, some sealed, some belonging to systems no current engineer can identify.

There are no plants. No weeds enter cracks, no moss softens shaded walls, no roots contest the foundations. Organic decay is almost absent. Waste dries, carbonizes, or is fed into thermal digesters. Soil, where imported material has accumulated, remains merely powder. Gardens are known as an architectural idea: rooms in which branching sculptures of copper and glazed stone imitate forms described in old records. Their basins contain water but nothing grows in it.

Life persists only at the margins of classification.

In certain condensation shafts, translucent films form on the coldest surfaces. Engineers call them scale and remove them with scrapers. Under magnification the films reorganize after damage, slowly aligning their crystalline threads with the flow of heat. Whether they are organisms, self-ordering minerals, or residues from the city’s chemical processes remains disputed.

The few unquestioned animals are small, deliberate, and rarely seen. Pale six-legged crawlers live near reservoir vents, feeding on mineral films and the residues of human habitation. Their shells are thin enough to show the movement of fluid inside. They remain still for days, then migrate in single file before a pressure change. High above the city, long-winged black creatures sometimes cross the sun. No nests have been found. They never descend within the walls, and some surveyors insist they are not animals at all but sheets of airborne carbon folded by thermal currents.

The city’s inhabitants obey the mountain’s slowness. Construction is planned in generations. No wall is considered fixed; every foundation includes joints for future uplift. Streets carry two sets of names, one for their present direction and one for the direction they are expected to face after the next major tilt. Property boundaries are marked by buried plumb lines rather than surface monuments.

Nothing truly level is trusted.

The central civic structure, called the Datum, contains a chamber suspended independently from the moving crust. Inside hangs a needle over a basin of mercury. Officials gather there to determine how the city has shifted—not only vertically, but in relation to its own previous shape. When the needle passes one of the engraved rings, old maps are retired and new ones issued. This may happen only once in a lifetime.

Beyond the dense districts, the city thins into infrastructure rather than wilderness. Rail lines cross naked shelves of black stone. Pump stations squat beside temporary pools formed where new fractures interrupt drainage. Quarry terraces expose layers that were molten within living memory. Towers stand at intervals to collect condensation and relay pressure readings. Their shadows are often the only shade for kilometers.

Farther still, the mountain’s summit breaks into a field of embryonic ridges. Heat leaks through hairline seams. Glassy domes rise almost imperceptibly from the ground, pushing aside survey markers without toppling them. Around these domes the air smells faintly of hot metal and rain, though there may be no cloud in the sky.

At dusk the city does not blaze with light. Energy is plentiful, but illumination is kept moderate so that workers can see the faint red of overheated structures and the silver trace of leaking water. Windows glow in scattered grids. Furnaces show through guarded apertures. Navigation lamps pulse slowly from the condenser towers, each flash separated by a long interval.

Then the whole high settlement seems less built upon the mountain than embedded in its thought: crowded, hot, nearly silent, gathering droplets from empty air while the ground beneath it continues, patiently, to become.
