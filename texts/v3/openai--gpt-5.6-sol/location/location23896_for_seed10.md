### The Last Plateau

At first it appears to be a high, empty moor under bad weather.

The horizon lies far off and almost level, a charcoal line beneath a low ceiling of cloud. Nothing rises into a tree. Nothing resembles a house, tower, road, or ruin. The land has been worn past grandeur: no sharp summits remain, no cliffs fresh enough to show their strata. This is the back of an ancient mountain range after ages of rain, frost, and wind have reduced it to a broad, cold tableland. Its slopes are so old that they seem less carved than remembered.

Yet there is almost no exposed stone.

The ground is plant.

Underfoot, the plateau consists of interlocked root-sheets, compressed moss, pale fungal cords, and centuries of living peat. The mat is many meters deep in places. It lies over the last crumbs of the mountains, digesting them grain by grain. What looks like gray shale breaks open into green vascular threads. What looks like gravel twitches when rain touches it. Even the sparse black outcrops are not rock but the hardened, mineral-rich knuckles of colossal root systems.

Vegetation fills everything, though it seldom grows tall. From a distance the country looks bare because its life has chosen breadth, depth, and repetition instead of height. Silver moss runs to the horizon in immense low fields. Dark sedges stand far apart, each clump surrounded by several body-lengths of open water or smooth root-felt. Flat-leaved plants spread flush against the wind, their leaves overlapping like dull scales. Here and there rises a single stalk the thickness of a mast, leaning under clusters of translucent bladders. Such stalks may be kilometers apart.

The emptiness is therefore deceptive. There is no unoccupied soil, only living surfaces waiting in different states.

Water lies close beneath all of it. Each step brings a dark seep around the foot. Pools appear without basins, held in shallow tensions between root fibers. Rainwater moves sideways through the mat, sometimes for days, then wells up far from where it fell. Along the plateau’s margins, the land weeps continuously. Droplets gather on hanging root-beards, swell, and fall into the cloud below. Streams exist, but they are indecisive things. They divide into hundreds of threads, vanish into moss, emerge uphill, and occasionally flow in rings.

The air is cold enough to stiffen wet cloth but rarely cold enough to seal the water. Ice forms as skins, membranes, and delicate internal skeletons. It grows beneath leaves rather than over them. On dim mornings, every blade holds a clear spine of ice inside its own tissues. By afternoon those spines melt, and the entire plateau settles by a few centimeters with a long, saturated sigh.

There is little direct light. Clouds drag across the vegetation, frequently touching it. The world is illuminated from everywhere and nowhere, in subdued grays, bruised blues, deep greens, and the occasional sulfurous glow of reproductive chambers beneath the moss. Shadows are weak except during electrical storms, when they become sharp and multiply in opposing directions.

The storms are nearly constant.

Some are weather. Others are produced by the plateau itself.

Across the root-mat stand scattered growths known only by the function their shapes suggest: turbine flowers, piston reeds, dynamo pods. They are not artifacts, though from a distance they resemble the remains of industry. No hand assembled them. The turbine flowers open their radial vanes into the wind and spin with a low metallic howl, driving fluids down through buried vascular shafts. Piston reeds contract in coordinated fields, pumping water from one watershed into another. Dynamo pods accumulate charge along copper-rich veins and discharge it into the cloud deck in branching green flashes.

Nothing here was built, but much of it operates.

The plateau’s botany long ago crossed into mechanism without ceasing to be botanical. Root joints rotate on lignin bearings. Sap valves chatter. Hollow stems compress air and release it through rows of tonal vents. Some colonies refine mineral slurry in warm subterranean chambers; others produce waxes, solvents, antifreezes, and conductive resins. There are assembly lines, but they are trails of specialized insects passing components from mandible to mandible. There are factories, but each factory germinated from a seed.

They do not manufacture objects. They manufacture conditions: pressure, heat, charge, fog, current, migration.

The result is loud. Wind alone would be enough, scraping continuously across the high open country, but beneath it runs the plateau’s deeper machinery. Pumps thud underfoot. Root cables groan as distant areas swell and contract. Seed chambers burst with concussive cracks. Vast submerged leaves slap against the undersides of pools. At irregular intervals a whole district of turbine flowers turns into alignment, and their combined note rises from a murmur to a sirenlike chord that can be heard across the valley systems.

The animals add their own clamor.

They are abundant without always being visible. The open spaces between stalks and hummocks swarm at ground level. Long-legged grazers, no larger than hands, move in dense processions over the moss, clipping only the pale tips. Glossy black amphibians occupy every seep, their throats flashing blue when the atmospheric charge rises. Flocks of transparent-winged creatures pour close to the surface like windblown grit, then vanish all at once into pores in the root-mat.

Larger animals keep to the watercourses. There are shaggy, six-limbed browsers whose feet spread into floating pads. They walk across bog pools, feeding on the undersides of leaves by lowering their narrow heads through the surface. Pale eel-like things travel through saturated peat as though it were open water; one sees only the moving ridge they raise, sometimes winding against gravity up a slope. Predators resemble loose knots of quills until they unfold and run.

Nothing here is truly warm-blooded. Animals borrow heat from chemical vents, from fermenting root chambers, or from electrical organs embedded in the vegetation. At dusk they crowd around these sources in such numbers that the ground seems to boil. When the source is exhausted, the gathering disperses instantly, leaving an eerie span of empty moss.

Ecological boundaries are uncertain. Many animals begin as fruit. Certain reeds uproot themselves during their juvenile phase and migrate in herds before settling again. Insects enter flowers and emerge weeks later with vertebrae. The oldest grazers acquire chlorophyll and finally stop moving, becoming low green islands around which their descendants feed.

Death is similarly incomplete. Bodies sink into the mat, but identity often travels onward through the roots. A call uttered by an animal in one valley may be repeated years later by a seedpod in another. Footprints sometimes open mouths. A line of old tracks may begin migrating by itself, pressing one after another into the wet ground long after the creature that made them has vanished.

This is where reality begins to fail—not dramatically, but persistently, like water finding faults in stone.

Distances vary with moisture. A landmark seen across dry moss may take an hour to reach; after rain, the same crossing may require days. Pools can be wider beneath the surface than above it. Some contain reflections of weather that has not yet arrived. If one watches too long, clouds in the reflected sky descend upward and disappear into the dark.

Gravity is mostly reliable, except around the great drainage wounds where the plateau sheds its water into the lowlands. There, rain falls sideways toward the exposed root walls. Small animals leap from the edge and orbit hanging curtains of moss, grazing as they circle. Streams may leave the land, hesitate in midair, and break into beads that climb back into the cloud.

The plateau’s extreme age has not made it quiet or settled. Rather, it has eroded away everything that resisted change. Stone once imposed shape here: ridges, ravines, fixed watersheds. Almost all of it is gone. What remains is a landscape with no rigid skeleton, a living surface free to revise itself.

Valleys close overnight. Wetlands migrate against the prevailing slope. A field of moss can peel loose during a storm and travel several kilometers like a slow green tide, carrying pools, animals, and weather with it. Root pressure lifts broad domes from the plateau; they swell for hours, rupture into steaming fissures, then collapse and are absorbed. Whole forests of short glassy stems germinate, mature, and shatter between one dusk and the next.

At times the activity becomes regional. The buried network begins a pumping cycle, signaled by three deep pulses felt through the feet. Water drains from millions of small pools simultaneously. Animals flee toward higher hummocks. The moss pales as fluid races through vessels below. Then the plateau arches in immense traveling waves, not fast, but fast enough to see: a rolling deformation of the horizon, accompanied by thunder from underground.

These events expose the deeper layers. Beneath the green surface are red pressure tissues, black braided conduits, caverns lined with reflective membranes, and ancient root trunks broader than river valleys. Some descend farther than measurement or imagination permits. They may reach the buried remnants of the mountains. They may be the remnants.

After the pumping cycle, warm mist erupts from vents across the moor. Rain forms without clouds. New pools fill with water that carries unfamiliar stars in its reflection. For a few hours, unfamiliar animals emerge—forms adapted to climates that do not exist here, feathered things steaming in the cold, soft-bodied grazers with flowers growing inward from their mouths. Most vanish when the water cools. A few remain and are folded into the ecology.

There is no clear center to this place. No governing organism can be found, no heart, queen, control chamber, or original tree. Coordination passes through weather, chemical gradients, migrating animals, electrical storms, and the remembered pressure of vanished mountains. The landscape behaves as one body only at certain scales. At others, it is an argument among billions of colonies.

Its oldest rule is simple: nothing may remain merely inert.

Stone is eaten. Water is recruited. Air is filtered and charged. Cold becomes storage. Distance becomes a response to rain. Even emptiness has a biological purpose, keeping the scattered vertical growths from interfering with one another’s winds.

From the plateau’s highest worn rise, one can see far across it: dim green reaches, leaden pools, isolated stalks turning in the gale, herds moving like spilled ink, sheets of rain dragged sideways beneath the cloud. Beyond them, the land continues in broad folds until it dissolves into mist.

It looks untouched because no one has ever built there.

It sounds like a continent-sized engine trying to remember whether it is a forest.
