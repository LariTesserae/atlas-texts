The place is hidden in a seam near the summit of a black mountain.

From outside, the entrance is nothing more than a horizontal flaw in the rock, scarcely wide enough for a hand. It lies above the last ordinary clouds, where the atmosphere has thinned to a dark, metallic clarity and the slopes hold no snow. Snow cannot persist here. Neither can ice. The mountain’s upper stone is so dry that even hydrated minerals have been stripped from it, leaving basalt, old impact glass, and pale veins of anhydrous salts. The cold is severe but immaculate: no frost softens an edge, no meltwater enters a crack and widens it. All weathering is accomplished by pressure, charged dust, and time.

The seam opens inward.

Not by becoming wider, exactly. Its dimensions lose agreement with one another. A person passing through would feel the ceiling remain a finger’s breadth above their back while seeing it recede into a dim vault. The floor occupies perhaps thirty square meters if measured by footsteps, yet optical instruments report a surface area varying between that of a small room and that of a continent. Distance is not concealed here; it is undecided.

This interior is called, in the few surviving references to it, the Dry Altitude.

It resembles a miniature basin at first. A shallow depression of grey powder lies between low ridges of polished mineral. The ridges rise no higher than a boot, but their profiles have the authority of ancient ranges: folded escarpments, scree fans, fault faces, and long shadowed cirques. Their material is not native to the mountain. Some are made of dense black ceramic whose crystal structure repeats only after several billion atoms. Others consist of translucent sheets stacked edgewise, each sheet thinner than a molecular bond should permit. Here and there stand columns of a porous white substance, cold enough to absorb the heat from nearby matter without noticeably warming.

Nothing is wet. Nothing has ever flowed in the familiar sense.

The basin was shaped instead by a slow migration of absence. At intervals too long to observe directly, regions of slightly reduced physical probability move across the floor. Dust is less likely to remain where they pass. Atomic bonds fail a little more often on their leading edge and reform imperfectly behind them. Over ages, these weak fronts have cut channels, undercut shelves, and rounded exposed corners. The results look like erosion by water remembered inaccurately: deltas without tributaries, potholes opening upward, terraces that cross one another without intersecting.

Most of the floor is bare. Grains lie far apart, each surrounded by a delicate zone in which its shadow points toward a different light. There is no vegetation, no lichen, no microbial film. Sterility here is not merely an absence of organisms. The local rules resist repetition with variation, the basic habit on which life depends. Molecules that begin to copy themselves find that the second copy occupies the same causal position as the first. One vanishes. Complex chemical cycles close too perfectly and cease.

If the place has an ecology, it is an ecology of errors.

Sparse patterns travel through the mineral substrate. A defect appears in one crystal, remains for centuries, then disappears as an identical defect forms in another ridge several meters—or several impossible kilometers—away. The white columns exchange no matter, but their internal vacancies drift in coordinated seasons. On rare occasions, every grain in the basin rotates by the same fraction of a degree. These events are not alive, though they respond to one another, compete for stable configurations, and leave descendants in the form of altered constants.

The light is low and has no obvious source. It is a bruised violet near the floor, greyer above, with narrow bands of darkness suspended like stationary smoke. Objects are visible chiefly along their edges. Broad surfaces remain obscure no matter how directly they are viewed. A ridge may present a bright outline around a center so black that it seems cut out of the world.

Illumination arrives before its cause. If a reflective object is brought into the basin, a faint glimmer may appear on it several minutes before the object crosses the entrance. When it is removed, its reflection sometimes remains, gradually flattening into the mineral surface. The oldest reflections have become part of the terrain: tiny pale figures, tool shapes, a gloved hand, the curve of an instrument case. They are not carvings. They are images that weathered into relief.

There are constructions, though they are scattered and difficult to distinguish from geology.

At the basin’s apparent center stands a frame composed of five dull rods. From one direction it resembles an open doorway no taller than a chair. From another it is a closed, many-sided solid. The rods do not meet at their corners; small gaps remain, but anything passed toward a gap emerges from a different one. Dust occasionally threads these discontinuities, producing brief geometric loops before settling.

Farther along the eastern ridge—“east” being a convention imposed by the entrance—three discs are embedded in the rock. Each is the size of a coin and engraved with no markings. Their surfaces display depth rather than reflection. Looking into one reveals the same basin under a brighter sky, with its ridges sharper and less eroded. The second shows the basin empty of all built structures. The third shows no basin at all, only a high, dry plain extending beneath a black sun. None behave as windows. They radiate no energy and permit no passage. They may be records, alternative states, or components of a machine whose operation is the distinction between those possibilities.

Near the wall is the remnant of a shelter. It consists of a low arch and one triangular panel, both made from a material that weighs less when supported. Beneath the arch, gravity points toward the nearest enclosed angle rather than downward. A few objects remain there: a spindle with no axis, a cracked lens that magnifies duration, and six grains of red matter arranged at unequal intervals. The grains cannot be moved closer together. An increasing resistance develops in empty space between them, as if separation itself had been engineered into a rigid substance.

No bodies remain. No footprints remain except one.

The print is shallow and narrow, impressed in a patch of black dust. It has no tread and no anatomical shape, only a sequence of five parallel depressions. Each depression is older than the one before it by roughly twelve thousand years. Taken spatially, they form a single mark; taken temporally, they record five visits. The dust around them has been undisturbed for so long that individual grains have worn smooth under impacts from particles passing through the mountain.

The air, if it can be called air, is exceptionally thin. It contains argon, neon, carbon compounds with unfamiliar bonding geometries, and a population of electrically neutral particles that interact mainly with angles. Breathing apparatus functions poorly, not because the atmosphere is corrosive, but because pressure is distributed unevenly across shape. Gas gathers inside acute corners. Rounded vessels empty themselves. A sealed cylinder may retain its contents for years, while a sealed sphere loses them in moments.

The cold also follows geometry. Flat surfaces fall to temperatures near interstellar darkness. Edges remain somewhat warmer. Points may be warm enough to emit a dim red radiance, though they contain no heat source. For this reason the smallest ridges seem faintly embered at their tips, while the basin floor drinks warmth from anything placed upon it. There is no wind. Motion in the atmosphere occurs as slow rearrangements around changing shapes.

Silence dominates.

Now and then there is a tiny ceramic click from somewhere beyond apparent reach. It may repeat after an hour, or a century, from much closer by. The rods of the central frame emit a nearly inaudible tone when no one is listening directly. Instruments record it as a pure frequency below hearing, yet visitors have described the sensation of remembering a bell.

Loose grains make no sound when they fall. Sound requires them to arrive, and arrival is unreliable here.

The entire pocket is high in more than the geographic sense. The summit outside rises above the clouds, but the basin lies higher still along another axis: farther from common causality, nearer whatever boundary separates physical law from the conditions that select it. Clocks brought inside run normally relative to themselves and disagree violently with one another. Plumb lines angle toward events rather than masses. A compass points toward the last decisive action taken nearby. If no decision is made for long enough, its needle slowly turns toward the entrance.

Change is extremely slow. The probability fronts cross the basin perhaps once every nine centuries. The violet light brightens and dims on a cycle longer than the mountain’s exposed history. The five-rod frame rotates, although no single observation has detected movement; old surveys simply disagree with newer ones by a fraction of a degree. The white columns are gradually hollowing, their missing interiors appearing as additional space elsewhere in the chamber.

Eventually, perhaps, the interior will become larger than the rock containing it in every measurable sense. Perhaps that has already happened.

The mountain shows no strain. The seam remains narrow. Dust gathers at its lip but never crosses. Dawn comes to the summit, casting hard gold across the barren slopes, and none of that light enters. Inside, the small ranges continue their patient erosion beneath the source-less violet dusk.

The place waits without expectation. Its machines may still be operating, but operation here is indistinguishable from landscape. Its ruins may not be ruins; they may be instruments designed to require geological time before producing a result. The basin may be a laboratory, a discarded component, a shelter, or the smallest surviving fragment of a world whose dimensions no longer align with ours.

Whatever its purpose, it is almost complete.

Only one thing continues to accumulate: the empty distance between its scattered objects, growing by an immeasurable amount each cold and silent year.
