The plain is so broad that its horizon is softened not by distance alone but by its own weather. It lies low beneath a warm, pale sky: a continent of compacted clay, chalk, salt, and wind-laid dust, scarcely folded, scarcely cut. There are no rivers. There are no lakes, not even the polished scars of former ones. Water exists almost entirely out of sight—sealed in waxy leaves, buried in tubers, carried in blood, trapped as minute films between grains of clay. Rain is rare enough to be remembered across generations of animals.

Yet the plain is crowded.

From horizon to horizon rise earthen structures: blunt towers, low domes, ribbed walls, clustered chimneys, vaulted mounds, and long causeways only a hand’s breadth above the ground. They are built without tools. Termites raise the tallest spires from saliva and mineral dust. Burrowing mammals push up long, inhabited berms. Small communal birds pack clay beneath overhangs until nesting walls become streets of round entrances. Resinous shrubs grow into one another and are continually reinforced by insects, so that living hedges form courtyards, corridors, and shaded chambers.

Seen from above, the place resembles an immense city assembled by instinct. Districts merge without empty land between them. One colony’s buttressed mound touches the root-hall of a thorn thicket; the thicket adjoins a field of sealed burrows; the burrows open beneath banks of basket grass and dry succulents. Every surface is occupied. Pale lichens glaze the exposed clay. Beetles live under the lichens. Mites live on the beetles. Fine roots comb the minute cracks. Seeds wait in cavities no larger than pinholes.

Nothing here is old enough to be deeply worn. The ground has the unfinished quality of material recently laid down. Its crust is thin and sharply edged. Mounds stand with fresh corners. New mineral blisters push upward overnight as the warm subsoil expands, and animals immediately colonize them. Where a wall collapses, it does not become a ruin for long: its fragments are carried away grain by grain, plastered into nearby galleries, packed around roots, or swallowed and recast as pellets. The landscape is not being eroded so much as assembled and reassembled.

The vegetation is dense, though rarely tall. It spreads laterally, conserving shade. Cushion plants overlap in dark green and dull silver mats. Thick-leaved vines crawl across roofs of hardened earth, fastening themselves with fibrous pads. Thorn trees grow in compressed groves, their crowns flattened and interlocked, making continuous canopies only a few times the height of a person. Beneath them the air is warm, still, and scented with dust, resin, crushed herbs, and the faint animal smell of occupied chambers.

Most plants keep their living moisture hidden. Leaves are narrow, felted, or coated in wax. Some fold tightly through the brightest hours, giving whole neighborhoods a gray, dormant appearance, then open toward evening. Their flowers are small but numerous: cream, ocher, faded violet, dusty red. There is little extravagance because there is little to spare. Even fallen petals are eaten almost as soon as they touch the ground.

The light is ordinary, clear daylight, but the packed surface breaks it into countless small contrasts. Sunlit roofs are white-hot to the eye; entrances remain perfectly black. Every mound casts a precise wedge of shade, and those wedges are valuable territory. Lizards move with the shadows. Beetles wait along their cool edges. Small antelope and harelike grazers travel from one strip of shade to the next, feeding quickly on succulent tips before withdrawing beneath the thorn canopy.

Life is abundant without being loud. The city-plain murmurs at close range but falls silent a short distance away. One hears the sift of dry soil from a burrow mouth, the faint clicking of mandibles inside a wall, claws brushing clay, wings opening and closing. A flock may pass overhead with only the soft friction of feathers. Even the larger animals have broad, padded feet and move gently, because brittle roofs and hollow ground punish heavy steps.

Predators hunt by patience rather than pursuit. Slender cats lie along warm parapets colored exactly like the clay. Snakes enter ventilation shafts and wait beside interior crossings. Owls watch the evening openings from the dead forks of thorn trees. In response, prey species coordinate through motion: a sudden freezing of grazers, the folding of wings, a ripple of insects withdrawing into cracks. Alarm spreads visibly across the land while scarcely producing a sound.

The atmosphere is active despite the quiet. Heat rises through millions of chimneys. Cool air is drawn through shaded tunnels. The exchange is vast enough to alter the weather immediately above the plain. By afternoon, wavering columns of warm air gather dust into high, diffuse veils. They do not become rain clouds. Instead they moderate the sun and drift in broad bands, so that the continent seems to breathe—brightening, dimming, warming, releasing.

At night the stored heat of the earthen city escapes slowly. Leaves unfold. Burrow mouths widen. Moths, bats, rodents, and pale insects emerge in numbers that make the ground appear to move. The activity remains subdued: feeding, pollinating, mating, rebuilding. Fresh walls rise in darkness from grains carried one at a time. Roots press into unset clay. Dung is buried, seeds are shifted, and old chambers are sealed. By dawn, whole pathways may have changed.

There are no avenues in the human sense, no monuments, no machinery, and no central design. Still, the place has infrastructure. Ventilation corridors align with prevailing winds. Broad animal tracks connect feeding grounds. Thorn belts shelter nursery warrens. Waste chambers enrich the deepest roots. Abandoned nests become seed vaults; collapsed towers become elevated gardens. Each builder follows local needs, but across continental distance those needs accumulate into order.

The governing rule of the plain is that nothing may remain vacant. An opening becomes shelter. Shade becomes pasture. A crack becomes a root channel. A carcass disappears into mouths and soil before its bones can bleach. Even the air is partitioned by hunting height, flight hour, pollen season, and temperature.

Its second rule is that water must never be exposed. Any drop released by breath, flesh, or fruit is absorbed almost immediately—licked from fur, condensed in a tunnel, taken up by dry fibers, or sealed under clay. The land’s lushness is therefore not soft or wet. It is hoarded lushness: green matter packed tightly into a dry world, life maintaining life through thrift, proximity, and ceaseless exchange.

From the horizon, the plain looks almost featureless, a low brown-green roughness beneath a warm sky. Only within it does its immensity become apparent. There is no true wilderness between settlements because the settlements are the wilderness. There is no boundary between architecture and ecology. The continent is one enormous, unfinished habitation, growing outward and inward at once, quiet as dust and busy in every grain.
