The land has almost no height left.

Whatever mountains stood here were dismantled before the present sky formed. Their substance lies spread across the continent in thin, compacted layers: red silt, black clay, powdered quartz, salt, and sheets of stone worn so level that rain rests on them without knowing which way to run. From a distance the whole region appears to be a single dark plain beneath a low ceiling of cloud. Only after entering it does one understand how crowded it is.

Every surface is occupied.

The mud is packed with pebbles, the pebbles with fossils, the fossils with hairline cavities full of warm water. Plates of ancient bedrock overlap like scales. Between them are basins no wider than a footprint and basins larger than countries, yet their edges resemble one another so closely that size becomes difficult to judge. Water lies in all of them: shallow, brown, metallic, sometimes perfectly clear. There are no open gaps, no blank horizons. Even the air is dense with mist, mineral dust, vapor, spores, and the fine spray thrown upward by impacts too distant to see.

The plain is low enough that the sea enters it without a shoreline. Saltwater advances beneath the ground through old porous strata, while fresh rain presses down from above. The two waters meet just below the surface and refuse to mix. Underfoot they slide over each other in opposite directions, creating tremors in the mud and long traveling bulges in the stone. A pool may suddenly rise into a glassy dome, hold for several minutes, then collapse with a deep report. Elsewhere the ground opens in a line and exhales brine.

It is warm, but not brightly so. Heat accumulates under the cloud cover and in the saturated earth. The atmosphere feels breathed already. Condensation beads on bare rock, on skin, on the hard stems of the few plants. Rain is frequent but rarely falls in recognizable drops. Sometimes it descends as a continuous gray fabric. Sometimes it forms directly on the ground, as if the plain were sweating upward. At other times a river appears in the air a few meters above the surface, flows horizontally through the mist, and disperses before touching anything.

The light has no evident source. Day is a diluted pewter glow; night is a deeper charcoal. The clouds are layered so thickly that lightning travels inside them for hours without breaking through. Its buried flicker reveals immense structures moving overhead—folds, chambers, suspended cataracts—but these may be shadows cast upward from the land. Direction is unreliable here. Reflections often brighten before the sky does.

Vegetation survives in scattered, stubborn forms. There are low black reeds with no leaves, each growing alone yet connected by roots that may extend hundreds of kilometers through the clay. Their stems bend toward pressure rather than light. When storms approach, whole districts of reeds incline in different directions, tracing the hidden geometry of the weather.

Pale mats cling to weeping rocks, neither moss nor lichen, feeding on dissolved minerals. They grow for centuries into thin concentric skins and then peel away all at once, sliding across the flooded flats like translucent hides. In the deeper marshes stand occasional groves of hollow trunks, widely spaced and scarcely taller than a person. They are smooth, branchless, and open at the top. Rain striking their interiors produces low organ notes that travel through the root network. A grove separated by a thousand leagues may answer.

There are few animals, or few that can be recognized as separate from the terrain. Flat, many-limbed things move beneath the shallow water, matching the color and grain of the stone below them. Their passage is visible only as a seam traveling against the current. Small white fliers emerge after rain, circle once, and vanish into the cloud ceiling; no one sees them descend. Along brackish margins, immense soft-bodied grazers leave broad compressed paths through the mineral films. Their tracks remain for thousands of years, although the animals themselves are encountered perhaps once in a lifetime.

Life here tends not to rise. It spreads, infiltrates, and becomes surface.

The sound is overwhelming.

Rain on stone supplies the smallest part of it: a universal hiss, present even when no rain is visible. Beneath that are the bass detonations of water chambers collapsing underground. Mudflats crack like artillery as buried salt swells and contracts. The air-rivers roar overhead. Thunder moves laterally through the plain, sometimes circling a region for weeks. When pressure shifts, millions of shallow pools begin resonating at once, each at a slightly different pitch. The result is not music but a physical density, a wall of vibration that shakes ripples into standing patterns and makes loose teeth ache.

Silence occurs only in narrow moving corridors. These pass across the land like invisible animals. Within one, every noise stops at its source: splashes have no sound, thunder becomes light alone, reeds flex mutely. The borders of such corridors are sharp. A person can stand with one ear in silence and the other in a continental storm. Nothing living willingly remains inside them. Even roots turn aside.

Though the plain is the final product of erosion—ground down almost to the theoretical limit of flatness—it is never still. Change has ceased to be vertical and become lateral. Channels migrate faster than floods. Marshes exchange locations overnight. A field of stone plates may flow slowly south while the water upon it streams north. Distances expand in heavy rain and contract during the brief dry intervals. Two landmarks visible beside each other may require months of travel between them; two places separated by the horizon may share the same pool.

This is the oldest law of the plain: adjacency is temporary.

The land does not break dramatically. It revises itself. A traveler may sleep beside a warm tarn and wake beside the same tarn under a different weather, with unfamiliar stone beneath it and a coastline of reeds that should lie far away. Tracks do not merely wash out. They are redistributed, appearing in other basins, sometimes enlarged, sometimes older than the feet that made them. Fossils surface before the creatures whose bodies will create them. In certain clay beds, raindrops leave impressions seconds before they fall.

There are scattered signs of habitation, though no craft survives and perhaps none ever existed. On rare stone rises stand small enclosures made from slabs leaned together. They are too low for comfort and too porous for shelter. Many have been rounded almost back into natural outcrops. Inside each is a patch of dry ground, even during flood, and a shallow depression polished by repeated contact. No cut marks, bindings, tools, or hearth remains are found.

Elsewhere, solitary walls cross the flats. They extend for a few dozen paces and end without corner or purpose. Some are built from stones that occur nowhere nearby. Others may be natural seams mistaken for masonry until one notices that they always contain an opening facing the loudest part of the sky.

The most persistent structures are rings of upright bone-white material embedded in the marsh. They could be ribs, roots, or mineral deposits. Their scale varies from hand-sized arcs to circles enclosing whole weather systems. Water inside the rings falls upward during storms. Sparse reeds around them grow with their roots exposed to the air and their stems buried in mud.

Nothing here demonstrates technology. Whatever intelligence touched the plain, if any did, worked by waiting, arranging weight, diverting seepage, or persuading organisms to harden in particular shapes. The few structures seem less constructed than encouraged.

Beyond any given horizon lies more plain. Its vastness is not empty extension but accumulation. There are so many pools, stones, storms, buried channels, pressure fronts, and nearly identical marsh edges that the continent seems packed beyond capacity. Features overlap without displacing one another. Several climates may occupy the same tract. Rain can strike one pool from three directions. In places, four horizons are visible, nested at incompatible distances.

Near the center—if the plain has a center—the water becomes only a finger’s depth everywhere. It covers an expanse larger than an inland sea, smooth enough to reflect the hidden sky. Storms race across it continuously, raising no waves, only patterns: enormous concentric rings, branching veins, spirals with straight sides. The designs form and erase themselves faster than they can be understood.

Sometimes the entire flooded surface lifts.

It rises very slowly, still level, carrying stones, reeds, ruins, and wandering creatures with it. Beneath is not soil but another sky: dim, warm, and storming downward into immeasurable depth. For a few moments the plain is revealed as a thin skin between two atmospheres. Thunder from above and below meets in the water, producing a sound so great that the reeds lie flat and the stone shelters shed dust.

Then the surface settles. Mud closes over the view. The old flatness returns, complete and ancient, as though nothing has happened.

Rain resumes—if it ever stopped—and the land begins rearranging what has always been there.
