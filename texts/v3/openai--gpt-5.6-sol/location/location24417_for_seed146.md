Far beneath the surface lies a space so large that it cannot be understood as a cavern. Its roof is a buried horizon. Its floor rolls away in black mineral provinces, descending by imperceptible degrees toward an interior too distant for sound or scent to return. Between them stands a dense wilderness of stone: blades, columns, splintered shelves, fields of hollow crystals and tightly packed ridges sharp enough to cut cloth at a touch.

The place appears newly made.

Every fracture is clean. Basalt fans retain edges like chipped glass. Slabs lie where they first separated, their matching faces still close enough to recognize. There are no rounded stones, no channels, no silt, no soft deposits in the hollows. Nothing has been carried from one place to another. The usual handwriting of water is entirely absent.

It is warm—not furnace-hot, but persistently, bodily warm. The rock gives off a deep heat that never seems to rise or dissipate. There are no drafts. The atmosphere, where it gathers, is a heavy mixture of argon, sulfur vapor, metallic dust and larger molecules with resinous, almost spicy odors. Breathing it without adaptation would coat the lungs. Exposed iron does not rust here. It slowly darkens beneath films of sulfide and carbon.

The darkness is nearly complete. Light belongs to injuries: a crystal split open, a charged plate scraped with stone, the shell of an animal disturbed. Then there may be a pale green seam, a blue static fork, or a dull red glow fading over several minutes. From far away these brief emissions look like signals, though most are no more intentional than sparks from flint.

There is no weather in the familiar sense. Yet the cavern is large enough to possess regional atmospheres. In the lower basins the gases lie thick and perfectly stratified. Fine black particles hang in them without falling, held between weak electrical fields. Higher terraces are clearer, their surfaces lacquered by deposits of sulfur. In one direction the air smells bitter and carries charge; in another, it is dense enough to blur the edges of nearby stone. These conditions have persisted so long that travelers use them as geography. The Bitter Layer is not moving east. The Iron Haze will not lift tomorrow. A bank of ocher vapor resting against a ridge has rested there beyond recorded memory.

The landscape does not visibly change.

Its apparent youth comes from preservation rather than recent violence. Perhaps the great chamber opened abruptly in the planet’s early cooling; perhaps it was never open at all, but grew as the surrounding mineral contracted. Whatever made it has ceased. With no rain, frost, roots, rivers or meaningful wind, every sharp feature remains in the first tense of its creation.

And yet the dark is crowded with animals.

At first they are easy to mistake for mineral clutter. Their abundance reveals itself slowly: an arrangement of shells too regular to be scree, a field of low domes all turned toward the same buried fault, a column whose plates close one by one when touched. Nearly every stable surface carries some form of animal presence. Some cling flat beneath ledges. Some stand on rigid legs between crystal spires. Others occupy cracks so narrow that only their feeding filaments emerge.

They do not depend on plants, fungi, or anything recognizable as a producer. The foundation of life here is the stone’s trapped disequilibrium: radioactive decay, pressure held in crystal lattices, minute charge separations between unlike minerals, sulfur compounds that can surrender a little energy when pressed against metal. It is a poor nourishment, but it is dependable and ancient.

Consequently, the fauna live slowly.

The most common creatures are shield-shaped grazers called, by those who have tried to name them, **palms**. They are not soft. Their bodies consist of overlapping ceramic scales surrounding a thin web of conductive tissue. A palm fastens itself across the boundary between two minerals and spends decades drawing charge through its body. It may shift the width of a finger in a year. Whole valleys are paved with them, thousands near-touching, each apparently inert until a sudden light reveals the slight difference between one arrangement and the next.

Above them stand **wire beasts**, angular animals with six or twelve jointed limbs and bodies no larger than a clenched fist. Their legs may extend several meters, allowing them to bridge from column to column without touching the crowded floor. They wait with their limbs locked. When a crystal somewhere in their reach develops enough strain, they fold upon it with astonishing speed, pierce it, and drink the electrical release. The act takes less than a second. The beast then remains motionless for months.

Large hollows contain the **choirs**, though they make almost no sound. Each choir is a colony of translucent sacs suspended from a communal framework of silica rods. They exchange pressure through their connected bodies. A disturbance at one end passes through the colony as a slow tightening, and faint violet light appears in sequence across hundreds of sacs. A choir can occupy an entire ravine. Its patterns take hours to cross from one side to the other, turning the ravine briefly into a dim constellation.

Predators are equally patient. Long ribbon-bodied things inhabit cracks beside the densest grazing grounds. Their mouths are rings of stone-hard hooks. They may wait for generations for a wire beast to place one leg within reach. Scavengers gather around a carcass before it is dead, not from prescience but because dying here can last a century.

There is reproduction, migration, hunger and violence, but all are stretched over scales of time that make the ecosystem appear fixed. A traveler returning after thirty years might notice that a patch of palms had rotated slightly around a seam of nickel, or that one choir had dimmed while another had brightened. No season commands these changes. There are no blooms or swarms. Population is regulated by the tiny, constant ration of energy emerging from the rock. Life fills nearly every usable place, yet almost nothing is in motion at any given moment.

The quiet has weight. Most of the time there is only the pressure of blood in one’s ears. Then comes a dry tick from somewhere beyond sight. Much later, another answers—not communication, necessarily, but a crystal settling, a shell closing, a feeding spike entering stone. Rarely, a fracture crosses a mineral field. Its report is sharp and enormous. Blue fire races along the crack, thousands of animals contract at once, and echoes travel beneath the roof for hours. Afterward the silence seems newly constructed.

Scattered through this vastness are structures.

They are small compared with the cavern and often difficult to distinguish from natural formations. Low walls of fitted plates shelter narrow clearings. Stone ribs have been wedged between columns to make bridges. At intervals, shallow bowls have been hammered into conductive rock and lined with hammered sheets of native copper. These bowls collect charge over many years. When full, they produce a weak, steady glow and enough warmth to wake certain dormant animals.

The builders are still present, though seldom seen.

They are upright only by local standards: squat, many-jointed beings covered in dark overlapping armor, with four grasping limbs surrounding a hard sensory crown. They have no fire. Open flame is difficult in the heavy, oxygen-poor atmosphere, and combustible material is almost nonexistent. Their technology begins with fracture.

They know how each mineral prefers to break. They tap listening rods against the stone and feel the returning vibration through their limbs. With wedges of harder crystal, horn taken from the wire beasts, and braided animal filaments, they split plates from the cavern floor. Native metals are beaten cold. Sulfur is warmed against the rock and used as cement. Static charge is stored in layered discs. A tool may take a year to make and remain in use for a thousand.

Their habitations are not villages. A single roofed niche, a wall against falling splinters, three charge bowls and a rack of stone implements may be the only constructed place for many leagues. The builders travel between them along routes marked by paired scratches. Because the terrain does not alter, a mark need never be renewed. A path established ten thousand years ago remains perfectly legible, provided no animal has settled over it.

They count distance in rests and history in tool edges.

There are signs that they once attempted larger works. On the shore of an empty depression stands a line of pillars, each crudely squared, extending into darkness with severe regularity. Nothing is supported by them. No road joins them. Many are unfinished, still attached to the bedrock by thin ridges. Elsewhere a wall circles a patch of ordinary black stone while leaving several deliberate openings for slow-moving fauna. Perhaps these are traps, sanctuaries, calendars without cycles, or declarations of ownership in a world where ownership can outlast species.

The deepest regions are stranger.

There the floor breaks into hexagonal shafts that descend beyond measurement. Warm gas fills them, perfectly still. Around their rims cluster shell animals unlike those of the upper provinces: tall, folded forms whose inner surfaces shine faintly without interruption. Their light is not metabolism in the usual sense. It seems to be a standing electrical pattern passing through generations of bodies. When one animal dies, its neighbors extend filaments into the abandoned shell, preserving the circuit.

Seen from above, the shaft rims form immense luminous diagrams. The diagrams are too large to have been made by the builders and too regular to dismiss as ecology. Some repeat across hundreds of kilometers. Others terminate abruptly at ancient faults. No one knows whether the animals gathered around a preexisting planetary pattern or whether, over millions of years, their collective feeding inscribed that pattern into the crust.

Nothing here offers an easy distinction between organism, machine and mineral.

A creature may have a basalt skeleton older than its living tissue. A tool may incorporate an animal that continues to feed. A stone can store a response to touch for centuries before releasing it. Dead shells become circuits; circuits attract grazers; grazers become walls as sulfur deposits around them. The place is full of presences, but few possess the urgency associated with life near a sun.

To cross it is to move through a crowded world holding its breath.

One passes between forests that are not plants, beneath a sky that is only more buried stone, over ground sharp with its first and only breaking. The warmth never varies. The dark does not deepen or lift. Far-off blue sparks reveal, for an instant, impossible distances packed with columns, shells, bridges and waiting limbs.

Then the light closes.

The vast interior remains as it was: warm, dry, densely inhabited, and almost perfectly still.
