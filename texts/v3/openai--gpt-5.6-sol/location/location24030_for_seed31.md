The city occupies a broad, high basin where the mountains have only recently finished rising. Their ridges remain angular and dark, with raw faces of basalt showing through the green. There are few deep gullies. Water has not yet had time to soften the land or carry much of it away. Instead, rain lies close to where it falls: in shallow tarns, in seams between rocks, beneath moss, and in long, level marshes gathered along the basin floor.

The elevation brings the clouds down without making the air cold. It is warm and dimly humid, a high-country warmth without glare. Mist condenses on stone walls and under the lips of roofs. Every exposed surface is either damp or just beginning to dry. From the cliffs, water emerges in clear threads, not as violent waterfalls but as continuous weeping. It beads along fresh fractures, joins into narrow runnels, and disappears beneath shelves of black rock.

The settlement is large enough to be a city, though it does not crowd itself. Its districts are separated by wet meadows, garden terraces, bare plazas, and tracts of exposed stone. Buildings stand in low clusters along raised ground. From a distance they resemble additional outcrops: broad walls of fitted basalt, pale clay plaster, roofs made from timber poles beneath thick layers of reed and turf. Most structures rise only one or two stories. A few stepped towers mark cisterns, storehouses, and ceremonial courts, their upper platforms open to the weather.

There is no metal sheen anywhere. Doors turn on stone sockets. Roof beams are pinned with carved hardwood pegs. Channels are lined with fired clay, and bridges are made from flat slabs dragged into place over narrow cuts. Along the main streets, gutters have been shaped with considerable precision using hammerstones, wedges, fire, and water. The workmanship is patient rather than advanced. Individual blocks do not match perfectly, but they settle together under their own weight, and the walls lean inward slightly for strength.

Water governs the city’s plan. Streets run along the crests of low ridges, while canals occupy the hollows. Broad stairways descend to public pools with polished rims. Smaller conduits pass through courtyards and beneath houses, feeding rectangular beds of taro-like leaves, rushes, gourds, and low fruiting shrubs. At the outer edge, the channels loosen into a marsh of knee-high sedges and bright green floating plants. Causeways cross it in straight, deliberate lines.

Despite all this water, little visibly moves. The pools are unruffled. The channels flow so slowly that their direction can be judged only by the faint bend of submerged grasses. Drops collect on roof edges, swell, and fall at long intervals. Nothing floods, and nothing dries. The basin seems held at the point just after rain, when the ground is saturated but the sky has already begun to lift.

Vegetation is abundant without becoming dense. Moss covers shaded masonry, but does not engulf it. Ferns gather beneath stairs and beside cistern mouths. Small trees stand widely spaced in courts, each with a round crown and exposed, moisture-darkened roots. On the slopes beyond the city, thickets occupy cracks and drainage lines while broad areas remain open: green turf, slick rock, shallow water, and pale mineral soil. The effect is lush in color but spare in arrangement. There is always room around each tree, each building, each clump of reeds.

No animals inhabit the basin. There are no birds over the marshes, no insects above the pools, no fish beneath the clear water. Leaves show neither bite marks nor nests. Flowers open without bees visiting them. Mud preserves the imprints of rain and fallen stems, but no tracks. Even within the city there are no people, though the structures suggest a population that once understood the place intimately. Grinding stones remain set into worktables. Clay jars stand beneath wall spouts. Woven mats have decayed into dark rectangular stains, while stone bowls endure beside cold hearths.

The silence is therefore unusually complete, though not uncanny. Water provides nearly all the sound: a drop into a cistern, a faint seep along a channel, the soft overflow of one terrace into the next. Occasionally a saturated bank releases a thread of water with a whisper like sand being poured. There is no wind strong enough to stir the trees. Reeds stand upright. The warm air rests over the basin in transparent layers.

Fresh geology is visible beneath the ordered city. In several districts, builders incorporated sharp lava ridges directly into walls and platforms rather than leveling them. One avenue divides around a glassy black spine and rejoins beyond it. Houses at the eastern margin are founded on pale stone that still breaks into hard-edged plates. The surrounding peaks have little scree at their feet; their faces are too young to have shed much. Soil exists mostly where it was carried and placed—in garden beds, terraces, courtyards, and shallow pockets trapped behind retaining walls.

The city has altered the plateau, but not mastered it. Its greatest works are accommodations: steps aligned to natural ledges, reservoirs fitted into old vents, roads raised a handspan above permanent wet ground. The central plaza is paved with broad porous slabs that drink in rain. At its center stands no statue, only a square well whose water lies almost level with the paving. The basin’s hidden water table is everywhere close, making depth unnecessary.

Beyond the last houses, the land opens for many miles. Green shelves rise toward serrated ridges. Marshes shine among them like pieces of dull silver. Dark lines of masonry continue outward—field walls, aqueducts, boundary markers—until distance reduces them to threads. There are isolated hamlets and storehouses on higher knobs, all built in the same heavy, simple manner, all separated by generous reaches of open wet ground.

Nothing here appears ruined by violence. Walls have not fallen far. Canals are not choked. Nor does the place seem newly deserted in any dramatic sense. It is simply unoccupied and enduring, preserved by a climate without extremes and by the absence of creatures that would burrow, graze, nest, gnaw, or overturn. Plants advance slowly and stop where conditions cease to suit them. Water follows channels cut long ago. Stone remains sharp. The same mild brightness hangs beneath the cloud cover from morning into afternoon.

At dusk, the wet rock darkens before the sky does. Cisterns and pools retain the last plain light, each one a quiet geometric opening among green terraces and black walls. The city becomes difficult to distinguish from the young mountain beneath it: both are angular, water-bearing, sparsely clothed, and almost perfectly still.
