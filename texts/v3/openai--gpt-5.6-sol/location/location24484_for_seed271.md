The place lies in a shallow basin no larger than a small hamlet, enclosed by low shelves of weathered limestone. Nothing rises sharply. The basin has been worn into rounded steps, shallow channels, and broad-backed stones whose edges have long since lost any suggestion of fracture. Even the fallen pieces seem settled. They rest half-sunk in silt, pale gray beneath a thin brown skin of mineral stain.

It is warm here, but not bright. A high veil of cloud often covers the sky, turning daylight the color of old linen. The enclosing shelves also keep much of the floor in shade. Heat remains in the stone without becoming fierce; walls and ledges feel warm to the palm, while hollows retain a mild, cellar-like dampness.

Water is present without ever filling the basin. It arrives as condensation on the limestone at night, as brief rain, and as a slow seep from a dark seam along the northern wall. The seep gathers in several thumb-deep basins before passing through a narrow channel and disappearing beneath gravel. There is no open stream beyond that. Only a darker trail in the dust shows where water has gone.

The pools are clear and still. Their rims have been polished by centuries of slight overflow, though they may stand unchanged for weeks. Pale mineral crusts mark former waterlines. In the deepest pocket, the stone is stained a muted green, but there are no insects on the surface, no tadpoles, no small tracks coming to drink.

Vegetation appears in widely separated patches. A few clumps of wiry grass grow where silt has collected against the shelves. Low gray-green herbs occupy cracks near the seep, their leaves thick and blunt, conserving what moisture they find. Farther from the water stand several thornless shrubs, spare as bundles of twigs, with foliage only at their tips. There are no trees. The open spaces between plants are broad and deliberate-looking, made of compacted earth, loose chips of limestone, and patches of fine dust.

At the basin’s western edge are the remains of three small dry-stone enclosures. They might once have been shelters, storage places, or simply walls raised against wind. No roof timbers remain, and no worked object lies inside them. Their stones are hardly distinguishable from the surrounding outcrops. One wall reaches shoulder height; another survives only as a curved line across the ground. The third has become a shallow terrace where two grass clumps grow. Lichen has softened the joints, and rain has carried soil into every gap. The ruins do not interrupt the landscape so much as submit to it.

There is almost no sound. When rain comes, it taps briefly on stone and gathers into a subdued trickle along the channels. Afterward, droplets fall at long intervals from an overhang into one of the pools. In dry weather the quiet is deeper: no wingbeat, no chirring, no call from beyond the rim. Occasionally a faint movement of warm air passes across the basin, brushing the stiff grasses together with a papery whisper. Then the stillness returns.

Nothing here suggests struggle or recent disturbance. Erosion continues only in increments too small to witness: a grain loosened by moisture, a trace of mineral added to a rim, dust settling into an old footprint-shaped depression that was never a footprint at all. The channels have found their courses. The stones have found their resting places. Even the sparse plants seem permanent in their spacing, neither advancing across the basin nor retreating from it.

The place has simple rules. Rain falls, but does not stay long. Water gathers only where stone permits it. Plants grow only within reach of hidden damp. Nothing feeds on them, nests among them, or carries their seeds; what propagation occurs is slow, by roots extending through cracks or seeds dropping almost directly beneath their parent stems. The broken walls continue to lower, but so gradually that each year resembles the last.

From the center, the whole basin can be taken in at once: the dim shelves, the few old enclosures, the separate clumps of vegetation, the clear pockets of water, and the broad intervals of bare earth between them. It is not desolate in the sense of having been emptied. Emptiness is its settled condition. The basin is complete with very little in it.
