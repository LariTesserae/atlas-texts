At first it appears to be a city drowned in vegetation. From a distance there are towers, avenues, districts, bridges, plazas, walls. Only when the distance closes does it become clear that nothing has been overgrown. There was never any stone beneath the green.

The towers are trunks gathered into columns, each column composed of thousands of stems pressed so tightly together that they have fused into a single load-bearing organism. Their surfaces are tiled with bark scales, leaf shutters, and translucent membranes full of amber sap. Buttress roots spread from their bases in orderly radial streets. Long boughs meet overhead and graft themselves together, forming elevated walkways broad enough to resemble boulevards. What look like windows open and close with the sun. What look like doors are vertical mouths in the wood, sealed at night by pleated petals.

There are no builders. There have never been tools.

The city grows because growth here has acquired the habits of planning.

It occupies a warm lowland extending farther than can be seen, an almost level basin whose minor rises are wholly made from accumulated roots. There is little exposed mineral earth. Plant lies beneath plant to an unknown depth: fresh foliage over woven branches, living cambium over compressed peat, root vaults over older root vaults. Even the mud is threaded so densely with hair-roots that it flexes like muscle. Digging—if anything existed that could dig—would reveal no decisive ground, only successively darker architectures of living fiber.

Moisture comes intermittently. Rain crosses the basin in narrow moving curtains, drenching one district while another steams beneath clear light. Water gathers in cistern flowers the size of courtyards, in the hollow joints of giant vines, and in shallow canals enclosed by root lattices. These canals do not flow reliably downhill. They are pulled from quarter to quarter by thirst. A neighborhood of pale trees may drink an entire lagoon overnight, transferring the water through underground tissues and exhaling it at dawn as silver mist several kilometers away.

The air is warm enough that every wet surface begins to breathe.

Condensation pearls on the undersides of leaves and runs upward along their veins. Dew gathers in suspended sheets between branches, trembling like transparent cloth. Now and then a whole sheet loses cohesion and collapses, producing a brief local rain beneath a perfectly blue sky. In the drier stretches, leaves fold into narrow blades and the city turns bronze; minutes after moisture returns, the same streets open into green chambers dense with fernlike fans.

There is no empty space in the ordinary sense. Every interval has been occupied by tendrils, aerial roots, floating seed-floss, pollen veils, or thin leaves held edge-on to the light. The great avenues are not clear corridors but rivers of flexible grass, all blades leaning in coordinated directions. Plazas are mats of interlocked rosettes that lift and settle in slow waves. Arcades of looped wood grow continuously, closing behind themselves and opening elsewhere. A route remains a route only because the vegetation agrees, temporarily, to bend.

Yet the lowland feels open. Its structures are immense and separated by long perspectives. Through successive green arches one can see distant towers leaning against the bright, ordinary-looking sky. The canopy is broken into districts rather than sealed into one roof. Sunlight reaches down in broad columns, warm and nearly white, illuminating drifting spores and the glossy backs of leaves. There is none of the twilight expected beneath such abundance. Plants have learned to pass light inward. Some are clear as glass; some redirect it through chambers of water; some hold the day inside pale tissues and release it into shaded root halls.

The sound is overwhelming.

Without animals, without machinery, without thunder, the city is still louder than a storm coast.

Seed chambers detonate constantly in the heat. Each rupture is a flat wooden concussion, followed by the hiss of seeds cutting through foliage. Trunks twist as they grow and release their strain with reports like splitting cliffs. Millions of leaves clap against one another in coordinated gusts. Hollow vines breathe through rows of apertures, producing organ notes too low to locate and too powerful to ignore. The canals boom inside resonant root tunnels. When rain touches certain broad membranes, entire districts become drums.

Most deafening are the flowering fronts. They move across the basin as visible walls of color. A bud begins to open, its petals dragging across one another with a shriek; nearby buds answer; the opening propagates outward until tens of thousands of blossoms are unfolding at once. The resulting sound is architectural—a grinding, exultant roar that shakes droplets from every surface. Then the flowers collapse, and their closing makes a softer but still enormous noise, like a city inhaling through clenched teeth.

There is no animal life. No insects enter the flowers. No birds cross the sky. No worms turn the deep plant-soil, and no microscopic grazers trouble the pools. Pollination occurs by impact, pressure, heat, and proximity. Towers bow together until their crowns strike. Pollen is fired across streets in yellow blasts. Roots exchange knots of living tissue. Seeds crawl without limbs by swelling on one side and drying on the other, advancing over the leaf-paved ground with the patience of spilled resin.

Nothing eats. Nothing is eaten.

Death is not absent, but it is difficult to distinguish from construction. A tower may suddenly bleach, soften, and fall across an avenue. Before it reaches the ground, vines erupt from its interior and suspend it horizontally. Its dead mass becomes a bridge; the bridge sprouts chambers; the chambers fill with water; the water gives rise to floating disks that knit themselves into a new district. Collapse is one of the city's principal methods of expansion.

The landscape is young, although it contains apparent ruins. Its edges are sharp and wetly torn. New ridges of root have burst through the basin floor and not yet weathered smooth. Fresh towers remain pale where their bark has not hardened. Walls split daily, exposing luminous green interiors. What resembles age—the leaning facades, buried halls, fallen colonnades—is often only yesterday's growth superseded by this morning's.

The place does not preserve its own history. It revises it.

A courtyard remembered at noon may have become a vertical shaft by evening, its floor lifted overhead on swelling roots. An avenue can turn inside out: the trees bordering it bend beneath the ground while the former road rises between them as a ridged wall. Distances are unstable because the vegetation adds and removes space. Two towers may be several kilometers apart when approached from the east and share a common chamber when entered from the west. A root passage can descend continuously and emerge higher than the canopy without ever turning upward.

Perspective itself sometimes germinates. A distant grove, seen through the fork of a branch, may take root in that fork. It remains far away and becomes nearby at once. Its tiny trees enlarge only if touched, unfolding from the image into full trunks with explosive cracks. Elsewhere, reflections in water continue growing after the plants above them have closed. In some pools the reflected canopy flowers first; hours later, the real canopy reluctantly imitates it.

The laws governing form are local and botanical. Whatever is enclosed becomes an interior, regardless of size. Whatever is grafted becomes adjacent, regardless of distance. Anything receiving sunlight acquires an upper surface. Anything thoroughly shaded begins to behave as a root.

These rules overlap badly.

Where several apply at once, the city convulses. Streets branch like stems and then branch again, producing entire neighborhoods in minutes. Buildings bud from their own shadows. Balconies curl inward and become underground vaults suspended in the air. A plaza folds along its veins, closes into a pod, and is carried away by a vine thick as a river. When the pod opens elsewhere, the plaza unfolds with all its surrounding avenues attached, though those avenues still remain in their original district too.

Such duplications do not last. The two versions pull on each other through the ground. Leaves stream toward the contradiction. Roots tighten. The air fills with a rising wooden howl until one district bursts into a million green ribbons, or both merge violently along a seam of flowers.

There is no stable center. There are only temporary concentrations of intention.

One day, the tallest growth may be a cluster of smooth red spires pumping vapor into the sky. The next, those spires have bent into a vast circular colonnade around a newly emerged wetland. The wetland itself might drain upward into suspended bladders, leaving a floor of moist, blinking leaf tissue. By afternoon, the bladders rupture and rainfall begins beneath them, confined to the exact geometry of the former pool.

The turbulence never becomes mere disorder. Patterns form everywhere, insist upon themselves, and are immediately challenged by other patterns. Spirals meet grids. Radial gardens invade branching streets. Towers arrange themselves into sentences that cannot be read from the ground. Fields of grass lean in arrows toward places that cease to exist before the arrows finish forming.

In the hottest part of the day, the whole basin accelerates. Sap visibly races through transparent stems. Buds swell and burst in seconds. Root bridges rear from the ground, clash overhead, and rain splinters. Canals reverse. Leaves revolve to follow several suns, though only one is visible. The noise becomes a continuous physical pressure, flattening soft growth and stippling every pool.

Then a rain curtain arrives.

Where it passes, the city quiets—not into silence, but into a deep wet murmur. Explosive pods soften. Leaf clapping becomes dripping. Towers drink and straighten. Steam rises from miles of foliage, blurring the distinctions between streets and structures. For a few minutes the place seems almost comprehensible: a warm green metropolis in a shallow basin, newly grown, empty of creatures.

Within the mist, however, immense shapes are already rearranging themselves.

They are not inhabitants moving through the city.

They are the city, deciding what an inhabitant might have been.
