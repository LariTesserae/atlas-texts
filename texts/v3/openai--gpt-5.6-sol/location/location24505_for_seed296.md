The city lies higher than weather.

From the low country it appears to occupy a range of blunt mountains, but at its own level the land is almost flat. This is not a contradiction so much as a matter of age. The plateau was raised long ago, then worn for an interval so vast that its summits became plains. Valleys broadened, escarpments retreated, and the last sharp ridges softened into long-backed swells of gray stone. Only afterward did the country rise again, lifting that exhausted surface into the cold upper air.

Now the edges fall away for thousands of meters.

There are few dramatic peaks. Instead there are immense pavements, low domes, shallow basins, and isolated tors with the rounded shapes of old tools. Near the brink, whole districts stand above cloud. Beyond them the land ends in pale cliffs streaked with mineral seepage and vertical gardens. On clear days, distant plains can be seen through blue depth, so far below that rivers look like scratches in metal. Such days are uncommon. More often the city is bounded by mist, and the plateau seems to be the only remaining country in the world.

Water is never abundant, yet nearly everything is damp.

Rain comes in fine, brief episodes: cold needles slanting across roofs, a soft tapping that ends before gutters begin to run. The greater source of moisture is condensation. Clouds drag themselves over the plateau lip and comb through towers, cable forests, chimneys, trellises, and stands of high grass. Every surface is made to catch them. Roofs wear ranks of ceramic fins. Railings are threaded with coarse fiber. Black chains hang beneath bridges, gathering beads that descend link by link into stone cisterns.

The native rock assists. It is a dark, porous material that draws moisture upward at night. Before dawn, exposed walls bloom with a skin of droplets, even where no rain has fallen. The people call this the stone’s breathing. Engineers describe capillary pressure and thermal gradients, though the oldest buildings seem to draw more water than their structure should permit.

By morning the city glistens.

It is not a city of open squares or clean skylines. Construction has accumulated in the plateau’s shallow valleys until buildings, gardens, pipeways, and retaining walls nearly touch. Streets thread between them like cracks in old masonry. Houses are built of fitted gray slabs below and riveted iron above, with additions in green-painted steel, copper mesh, fog cloth, and translucent panes clouded by internal moisture. One generation builds over the roofline of another. Upper doors open onto bridges; old ground floors have become cellars; cellars have become cisterns or fern rooms.

The densest districts fill the ancient drainage channels. Here factories and dwellings are interlocked. A turbine hall may share a wall with a monastery of seed-keepers. Conveyor galleries cross over kitchens. Narrow-gauge rails vanish through markets and emerge in moss-dark courtyards. Tall exhaust stacks are common, but they seldom smoke heavily. Fuel is scarce at this elevation. Most machinery runs on stored water pressure, counterweights, compressed air, and the steady pull of loads descending the cliffs.

The cliff-lifts are the city’s largest engines. Their cables pass over wheelhouses built into the plateau rim and drop out of sight toward quarries, forests, and settlements below. Freight rises slowly: grain, timber, coal, clay, sometimes livestock trembling in covered cages. In return, baskets of finished metalwork and dense machine parts descend. The motion is nearly continuous, but quiet. Great drums turn behind thick stone walls. Greased cables move with a low fibrous murmur. From the streets one may notice only that a row of iron hooks is advancing overhead, or that the weights in a public shaft have sunk several meters since morning.

Everything seems muffled. Moss pads the walls. Fog softens distance. The industrial quarter does not roar so much as breathe: a valve sighing, a piston striking at long intervals, belts brushing through darkness, water ticking into tanks. Farther away, a warning hammer sounds from the rim—three slow blows to announce high wind—and each blow enters the mist without returning an echo.

Vegetation occupies every pause in the construction.

There are orchards in roof hollows, dense with low, twisted trees whose branches are trained beneath netting. Their leaves are nearly black on top and silver underneath, turning in the wind so that whole terraces flash and dim. Ferns grow in stairwells. Moss accumulates in thick cushions around warm pipes. Pale reeds crowd the seep basins, and tough flowering vines climb disused cranes, producing small blue bells that close when touched by fog.

The city cultivates this growth rather than merely tolerating it. Vegetation catches water, binds masonry, insulates pipes, filters industrial dust, and marks subtle changes in air chemistry. Different mosses are planted around different workshops. Rust-red mats near the foundries absorb metal salts. White, branching lichens on the outer walls blacken several hours before an electrical storm. A glossy liverwort that grows inside reservoirs turns its leaves edgewise when the water becomes unsafe.

Below street level, former mines have become warm gardens. Waste heat passes through brick channels under beds of mushrooms, root vegetables, medicinal molds, and soft-stemmed greens. These caverns are among the lushest parts of the plateau: wet, close, layered with leaves, cables, steam lines, and racks of dark soil. Condensation rains steadily from the ceilings. The city’s little true running water is mostly here, circulating through filters and descending from garden to garden before it becomes industrial wash.

Animals are uncommon. The height, cold, and fragmentation of habitat exclude most of them. There are no large wild grazers and few birds. Occasionally a flock of narrow-winged black swifts rises from the cliff face and wheels silently through the streets, feeding on minute insects suspended in the fog. Their arrival brings people to windows because it may happen only a few times in a season.

Small pale salamanders live in the oldest cisterns. They have external red gills and almost transparent bones. Workers will halt a pump rather than disturb one, not entirely from reverence: reservoirs inhabited by salamanders are said never to sour. In the outer gardens, one may find the tracks of a six-toed cliff fox, though sightings are so rare that some consider it an old civic emblem mistaken for an animal.

The apparent stillness of the plateau conceals constant activity.

Mist forms, thins, and returns. Water migrates through walls. Plants open and close according to pressure and electric charge. Elevators rise and sink. Counterweights pass one another inside the rock. Frost enters cracks at night and leaves them at noon. Retaining walls flex almost imperceptibly with the saturated ground. Soil creeps toward the escarpment. Engineers walk established routes placing chalk marks across fractures, then return after storms to see which marks have broken.

Erosion here is not violent in appearance. It has gone beyond violence. The land has already surrendered its towers and teeth; what remains is the enduring residue, rounded and dense. Yet the plateau is not immutable. Its destruction proceeds by patience. A drop gathers, freezes, widens a grain-sized opening. Moss enters. Roots follow. A stone loosens after three hundred winters and falls into cloud without ever being heard below.

At the margins, abandoned neighborhoods have been taken apart by this slow process. Window frames lean over emptiness. Stairways descend to rooms that no longer exist. Gardens spill from broken floors and hang against the cliff in mats of root and wire. Some ruins have been netted to harvest fog; others are left as wind gauges. Their doors open and shut with changes in pressure, giving distant districts the appearance of being inhabited.

The weather is the city’s principal clock. There are shifts for clear air, shifts for cloud immersion, shifts for rim ice, and shifts for the heavy condensation that comes when warmer currents rise from below. Work does not cease when conditions change; it changes form. Factory vents close and garden louvers open. Tram cables are tightened. Roof collectors unfold. Children carry cups to overflow spouts. Crews in waxed coats cross the bridges to scrape ice from load-bearing lines.

During the deepest cloud, visibility contracts to a few steps. Lamps glow in recessed amber boxes, not bright enough to illuminate the fog, only to mark walls, doors, and sudden drops. The city becomes a chain of local places: one wet stair, one iron balcony, one room full of turning wheels. The broader plateau disappears. Even the neighboring tower may be present only as a vibration traveling through a bridge rail.

Then a wind cuts the cloud apart.

For a short while the full country emerges: the old level land extending from basin to basin, crowded with dark vegetation and layered construction; reservoirs shining among roofs; factories embedded in rounded hills; cable lines crossing voids; the whole settlement arranged along the worn bones of an ancient surface. Beyond it stand other plateau islands, similarly flattened and impossibly high, each carrying faint structures and green terraces.

Some are connected by bridges. Some have been isolated for centuries. Lights occasionally appear on the farthest one, where no operating settlement is recorded.

When the mist returns, those lights remain visible for a little longer than the land beneath them. Then they, too, go dim, and the high city resumes its quiet work of catching water from the air.
