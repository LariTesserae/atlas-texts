The city lies in a broad, newly emptied basin, lower than all the surrounding country. From the rim it appears almost level with the horizon only because it fills so much of the hollow: roofs, courts, walls, kilns, storage mounds, scaffolds, and narrow lanes packed together across the raw floor. Beyond them, the opposite escarpment rises in a pale band several miles away.

There is no river entering the basin now, though water plainly made it. The enclosing slopes are steep and undecorated, their strata exposed in clean horizontal cuts: chalk, gray clay, a seam of black gravel, then red earth below. They have not yet softened into gullies or acquired the scars of long weather. Every rain begins the first version of the landscape. Thin streams trace tentative paths down the slopes, hesitate on shelves of harder stone, and vanish into fresh cracks. A week later another storm chooses different routes. Nothing has flowed long enough to become inevitable.

The basin floor remains damp under its crust. Footsteps darken it. Heavy carts sink to their hubs if they leave the reinforced ways. Water gathers in shallow depressions around old tree stumps and in pits where clay has been dug for walls. Some of these pools last through the cool season; others shrink within days, leaving a skin of salt and densely printed footprints. Wells are rarely deep. Water seeps slowly into them from the surrounding sediment, cloudy at first and then clear.

Because the hollow is open to the sky, daylight is ordinary and abundant. Morning reaches the eastern districts first, illuminating thousands of roof edges while the western quarter remains blue with shade. At noon the whole city is exposed beneath a cool, colorless brightness. Toward evening the encircling rim takes the sun, while the settlement below loses it early. Condensation then appears on plaster, ropes, timber joints, and the undersides of awnings. By dawn, gutters may have collected a cup or two from air alone.

The buildings are low, rarely more than two stories, but there are almost no gaps between them. Walls of rammed clay share their thickness with neighboring houses. Roofs overlap above alleys narrow enough to touch both sides. Where good timber is scarce, builders use bundled reeds, woven brush, split bone, rawhide lashings, and slabs pried from the exposed strata. Foundations are broad mats of stone and brush intended to keep structures from sinking into the wet ground. They work unevenly. Doors lean. Floors buckle gently. Entire rows settle together, preserving their alignment while descending a finger’s breadth each year.

Construction is continuous. This is not a ruined city being consumed by earth but a city trying to become solid before the earth has decided what shape it will take. New streets are laid with cobbles carried down from the rim. Old ones are lifted and relaid above mats of branches. Retaining walls brace excavation faces. Drainage ditches are cut by hand, lined with clay, abandoned when they fill with sediment, and cut again somewhere lower. The city is dense partly because firm ground is precious: each stable patch has been occupied, subdivided, roofed over, and connected to every adjacent patch.

There is little metal. Stone mauls and antler picks strike wedges into the softer rock. Copper knives, where they exist, are kept for fine work rather than digging. Earth moves in baskets, on sledges, or in small two-wheeled carts pulled by people. Raised timber tracks cross the worst mud, polished by runners. Cranes are simple frames of logs with hide ropes and counterweighted baskets. From a distance the city looks intricate and permanent; close at hand, nearly everything bears the marks of palms, blades, cords, and repeated repair.

Vegetation has barely established itself. Pale moss grows where water seeps from the northern wall. Rushes occupy a few drainage sumps, and wiry grasses appear along the outer embankments before being cut for roofing. In protected courtyards stand small plots of beans, onions, bitter greens, and medicinal herbs, their soil brought down from the uplands in sacks. A few stunted fruit trees grow against south-facing walls, trained flat to catch warmth. Outside these tended pockets, the basin remains mineral: gray clay, white stone, black wet gravel, red spoil heaps.

Animals are uncommon. The descent is difficult, forage is poor, and the settlement leaves little unclaimed space. Small goats are kept on roof terraces and fed on imported brush. Dogs move quietly through the lanes. Rats exist but have not yet become numerous; the city is too young, its granaries too closely watched. After rain, frogs sometimes appear in temporary pools, though no one knows from which surviving pocket they came. High overhead, birds cross the basin without landing. Their calls descend faintly and seem to belong to the upper world.

For such a busy place, it is subdued. Clay walls absorb voices. Soft ground muffles feet and wheels. Work proceeds with the low scrape of shovels, the knock of stone against wooden wedges, the creak of loaded ropes, and occasional instructions passed from scaffold to scaffold. Even the markets are quiet, compressed beneath reed canopies where sound has nowhere to gather. The loudest events are structural: a retaining wall giving way with a wet sigh, a cart overturning, a roof beam cracking, stones cascading briefly from the rim. Afterward, silence returns quickly.

Weather is felt less as spectacle than as consequence. Rain seldom falls for long, but every shower rearranges the labor of the city. Roof channels begin dripping. Courtyards become cisterns. Lanes acquire a slow brown current. Workers hurry to brace walls whose clay has darkened with moisture. Then the rain passes, mist rises from warm kilns and damp earth, and the hollow resumes drying from the top downward.

The city’s principal rule is simple: nothing may obstruct water without offering it another route. Every wall has a drain. Every lane slopes toward a sump or channel. Householders are responsible for the flow past their own thresholds, and disputes concern levels more often than boundaries. A neighbor who raises a floor may send runoff into the house below; a workshop that clogs a ditch can flood an entire quarter. Surveyors walk with plumb cords, water bowls, and marked staffs, measuring differences too slight for the eye but large enough to determine which district survives the next storm.

A second rule follows from the first: excavation and building are the same act. Clay removed for a cistern becomes the wall around it. Stone cut from a drainage trench paves the street above. Spoil from one district raises another. The settlement consumes the basin floor and remakes it into itself, gradually exchanging raw geology for organized mass.

At the edges, the process remains visible. Fresh houses stand against freshly cut earth, with no weeds between them. Beyond those houses lie survey stakes, heaps of reeds, wicker screens, and lines scratched into clay indicating streets that do not yet exist. Farther still, the untouched floor extends toward the escarpment, apparently empty but already measured.

The basin is a place before erosion has become history. Water is present, but its customs are not established. Slopes have not learned how to fail, pools have not learned where to remain, and plants have not had generations to find the cracks that suit them. Only the city behaves as if the future is known. It presses outward and downward, dense and deliberate, making streets while streams are still deciding where streams should be.
