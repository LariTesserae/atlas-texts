The country stands high above any basin that might once have taught it the shape of a river. It is a broad mineral tableland, warm under a plain white sun, extending from one clean horizon to another. There are no channels, no deltas, no rounded stones. Nothing here has been carried. Nothing has fallen long enough to wear a path.

The ground appears newly made, though it is not changing. Vast sheets of pale basalt lie fitted together along seams too fine to admit dust. Elsewhere the surface is salt-gray, iron-yellow, or the muted green of nickel minerals. Low ridges rise abruptly from the flats with the blunt geometry of material that hardened in place: unfinished escarpments, shallow domes, long pressure blisters. Their faces have no scree beneath them. Cracks begin and stop without branching. The landscape possesses all the first gestures of geology and none of its consequences.

It is as though erosion has only just been invented here and has not yet been permitted to act.

There is no water. The atmosphere contains no vapor, the ground no buried ice, the cells of living things no aqueous interior. Warm, dense air moves so little that it is felt mostly as pressure against the skin. It smells faintly of hot stone, ozone, and something resinous but metallic, like insulation warmed inside a silent engine.

Across the plateau, very far apart, stand the groves.

From a distance each looks luxuriant: a dark, many-layered mass of branching forms, dense enough to conceal its own center. Yet between groves are kilometers of emptiness. There may be only six or seven visible across the whole high plain, each isolated in a basin or gathered around the foot of an unweathered ridge. Thus the country is both lush and vacant. Life is abundant wherever it occurs, but occurrence itself is rare.

The growths are not plants in the familiar sense. They have no sap and drink nothing. Their upright trunks are columns of fibrous ceramic, cream-colored outside and black within, rooted through microscopic contact with electrically active seams in the bedrock. Plates unfold from them in overlapping tiers. Some are thin and green as leaves; others are coppery, translucent, or coated in a velvet of catalytic filaments. Their surfaces take in sunlight, atmospheric carbon compounds, sulfur, and the slow voltage produced where unlike minerals touch.

A grove is less like a forest than a naturally occurring refinery.

Tall forms collect light. Low forms separate gases. Bulbous forms compress and store them in honeycombs of mineral foam. Under the ground, root lattices sort atoms with extraordinary precision, laying down veins of pure metal and drawing useful elements back into circulation. Nothing was designed, assembled, or built. There are no factories, towers, pipes, wires, roads, or tools. Yet the ecology has arrived, by evolution or by some more impersonal process, at industry.

Production is continuous and almost perfectly balanced.

Along the margins of a grove, slender black stalks rise in ranked clusters. Their heads resemble turbines sealed inside flowers. They do not spin visibly, but a hand held near one would feel a constant vibration: charge being stepped up, chemical potentials exchanged, waste heat returned to the air. Broad fronds act as radiator fins. Glassy nodules click inward once every several hours, though the interval never seems to vary. Deep in the grove, pale membranes shuttle back and forth through fixed arcs, pumping non-aqueous solvent—liquid ammonia is impossible in this warmth, and hydrocarbons would evaporate, so the substance is stranger: a stable ionic melt held in capillary meshes, dark and slow as mercury but far less dense.

The result resembles machinery without manufacture. It has throughput but no ambition, efficiency but no demand. Each grove produces exactly what it consumes. No surplus accumulates. No waste mound grows. The mineral exhaust from one organism becomes the structural feedstock of another, and every released gas is captured elsewhere before it can alter the atmosphere.

Nothing eats the groves. Nothing pollinates them. There are no insects hidden among the plates, no birds above, no burrowing things beneath. No animal has ever marked the flats with a track. Reproduction occurs by extension: at the extreme edge of a grove, a filament may advance beneath the stone, sometimes for hundreds of meters, and establish a new catalytic node.

But even this advance has the quality of a completed fact rather than an event. The filaments are always found in the act of reaching and never observed to move.

That is the deeper rule of the plateau. Everything operates, but little changes.

The sun crosses the sky, yet illumination remains nearly constant, as if the atmosphere redistributes the light. Shadows are soft, proportionate, ordinary. The warmth does not build toward afternoon or drain away at night. Perhaps there is no night; perhaps the white sun is one member of a ring of distant lights. In any case, the temperature holds. Chemical reactions continue at steady rates. Voltages persist. The faint turbines maintain their inaudible work. The groves are alive, but their life is equilibrium rather than history.

Even the air scarcely travels. It drifts around ridges in broad, weak circulations and returns to where it began. There are no storms because there are no meaningful gradients from which storms might grow. Dust does not rise. Seeds do not blow. Heat does not shimmer. A loose mineral flake can remain balanced on its edge for ages.

The silence is consequently immense, though not absolute.

Near the barren flats there is only the small electrical hiss of the atmosphere against sharp rock. At a seam in the ground, one may hear a dry, intermittent tick as crystalline stresses redistribute without producing a fracture. Inside a grove, the quiet becomes crowded with minute sounds: the contraction of ceramic fibers, the soft clack of a valve-flower closing, a low induction hum felt more in the teeth than in the ear. These sounds never gather into rhythm. They are too sparse, too isolated, each separated from the next by stretches of warm stillness.

The high ridges provide views over a region large enough to contain several distinct mineral provinces. To the east, the tableland breaks into terraces of purple-black stone, each terrace sharp-edged and immaculate. To the south lies a white expanse whose surface is cellular, like a salt pan formed without brine. In the north, narrow spires rise from a shallow depression. They are raw extrusions, glossy and undecorated, with no talus at their feet. A single grove occupies the depression among them, spreading dense bronze plates through the gaps so that the spires look like unfinished parts embedded in an enormous green machine.

Westward, the land becomes almost empty enough to lose scale. A dark line on the horizon might be a cliff twenty kilometers away or a mineral lip only a few hundred meters distant. There is nothing between to measure it against. The plateau’s openness is not the openness of grassland, where innumerable stems texture every view, nor the openness of a desert shaped by dunes and wind. It is the openness of unused volume.

Yet “unused” is not quite right. The emptiness may itself be functional.

The groves seem positioned at the intersections of subterranean electrical fields. Each is separated from the others by distances that prevent their conductive roots from interfering. The barren expanses are insulation. Ridges partition charge. Mineral plains radiate heat. The whole plateau may be one ecological circuit, with apparent vacancy serving the role that empty space serves inside an engine.

If so, its scale is regional rather than local: a factory with no walls and no maker, operating through geology. The terrain is not the setting for the machinery. The terrain is part of the machinery.

And still it seems embryonic. The sharp ground, the unweathered scarps, the arrested cracks—all suggest a world moments after formation. Perhaps the plateau is perpetually being born into the same state. Perhaps each infinitesimal change is countered elsewhere, so that rawness can persist without youth and life can flourish without succession. No grove ages. No fallen plate decays; plates rarely fall, and those that do are slowly disassembled where they lie until the original clean stone is restored.

There are no ruins because there were never structures. There are no fossils because death leaves no residue. There are no paths because nothing travels.

Standing there, one would not feel threatened. There is no predator, no weather, no collapsing ground, scarcely even cold or heat to endure. The unease comes from another source: the sense that the plateau has eliminated accident. Every object has a stable state. Every process returns its materials. Every living form is integrated into a cycle so exact that evolution itself may have stopped.

The place is lush where it chooses to be lush, empty where emptiness is required, industrial without civilization, young without becoming, alive without movement.

Under the ordinary light, the nearest grove shines in green, copper, and black. Its thousands of plates face the sun. Somewhere inside, hidden catalytic chambers maintain their warm, tireless exchanges. A valve closes with a delicate ceramic tap.

Then the high country is silent again, and nothing has changed.
