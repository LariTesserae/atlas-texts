Below the surface lies a town with no streets.

It occupies a warm, drowned hollow scarcely larger than a cathedral close: a basin of black basalt enclosed by steep walls and roofed, far overhead, by the trembling silver underside of the sea. Every available span has been filled. Towers press against cisterns; cisterns nest inside the ribs of old factories; conduits knot around columns thick as tree trunks. Nothing stands free. Even the water seems packed, burdened with pollen, mineral dust, suspended roots, and the soft green smoke of microscopic plants.

The settlement is called the Compression, though whether that is its name or merely a description is unclear.

Its oldest structures were cut directly into the basin. Long immersion has rounded their corners and opened their joints into dark, furred seams. Later buildings were fastened onto these foundations: iron galleries, tiled pressure chambers, ceramic chimneys, copper cages, and bulbous machine-houses assembled in layers. Newer construction is difficult to distinguish from old. In the warm mineral water, metal tarnishes quickly and stone acquires a living skin. Bronze turns green, iron black, glass cloudy. Everything becomes part ruin, part reef, even while it remains in use.

There are no doors in the usual sense. Buildings communicate through flooded apertures and narrow throatlike passages. Some rooms hold water of a different density or temperature, kept separate by invisible membranes. Passing through one feels like pushing through heavy silk. A chamber may be cool and clear on one side, warm and green on the other. Workshops are marked not by signs but by currents: a faint outward breath of heated water indicates a foundry; a descending spiral of pale sediment marks a milling room; the smell of iodine and wet copper belongs to the electrical quarter.

The machines are everywhere, although few move visibly. Great pumps sleep behind grilles of shell-encrusted brass. Turbines rotate so slowly that their blades resemble fixed architectural fins. Pressure looms draw fibers from mats of cultivated weed, braiding them into rope, filters, and flexible walls. Electrolytic gardens gather metals from the water grain by grain, depositing copper in delicate branching plates. At the center of the basin, a squat engine called the Lung compresses dissolved gases into translucent bladders. Once every several hours, it contracts. The whole town gives a nearly imperceptible shudder, and a chain of bubbles is released into pipes leading downward.

Here, bubbles sink.

They descend with stately purpose through the green darkness, collecting in reservoirs beneath the town. This is one of the basin’s settled laws. No one attempts to explain it anymore. Buoyancy behaves normally beyond the enclosing cliffs, but within the Compression, gas is drawn toward the basalt floor, while loose stones sometimes drift upward and lodge among the roofs. The oldest ceilings are crowded with such stones: smooth pebbles suspended like a second, inverted riverbed.

Vegetation thrives in every margin the construction has failed to occupy, and then occupies the construction as well. Ropes of crimson weed hang between balconies. Pale fronds open from drainage mouths, folding shut when the pumps cycle. Dense mosses cloak insulated pipes and glow faintly where electrical leakage nourishes them. In the warmer upper chambers grow orchards of bladder-leaf: dark plants whose translucent leaves are filled with buoyant sap and tethered to iron trellises. Their roots do not descend into soil but fan outward through the water, combing minerals from the slow currents.

The lushness is not wild. It has been pruned, harvested, redirected, and allowed to escape so many times that cultivation and invasion have become the same process. A machine may be repaired by encouraging the correct vine through its cracked casing. A wall may be strengthened with generations of calcifying algae. Whole tenements are held together by woven roots that contract in darkness and relax in light. Gardeners work as engineers; engineers carry pruning knives.

There is little open water. To cross the settlement is to pass through curtains of weed, pipe forests, dangling cable, scaffold, root, chain, and the porous shells of abandoned apparatus. Visibility seldom exceeds a few body lengths. Beyond that, forms overlap into a dense blue-green obscurity. Lamps are rare and heavily hooded. Some burn with a dull amber electrical glow; others contain colonies of luminous bacteria whose light pulses over days rather than seconds. Most illumination comes indirectly—from leakage around shutters, from fungal seams, from the distant surface filtered through layers of growth and machinery.

The water is pleasantly warm near the inhabited chambers and almost feverish around the industrial core. Heat does not disperse quickly. It accumulates in pockets, held by dense saline layers, so that one may swim from a cool corridor into a room of nearly motionless warmth. These thermal boundaries bend light. Buildings appear to breathe or lean. A straight passage may seem to curve away from itself.

Despite the vegetation, animals are scarce. The basin’s chemistry discourages them, as does the constant low electrical charge carried through the water. Now and then a blind eel appears in a cable duct, white as wax and nearly motionless. Tiny glass shrimp graze the inner surfaces of gauges, their organs visible as flecks of red. There are stories of a single broad-backed creature living beneath the Lung, feeding on the sinking bubbles. No one has seen more than a slow displacement of sediment and a row of pressure dials falling to zero one after another.

Silence here is not an absence of activity. Sound is muffled, absorbed by vegetation and layered construction. Machines communicate mostly through vibration. A pump’s labor is felt in the teeth. The opening of a distant valve passes through handrails as a faint metallic coolness. Chains click once, then settle. Leaves brush ceramic housings. Occasionally the Lung contracts and every suspended particle trembles in place.

Voices, where there are voices, remain close to the speaker. They travel poorly across the crowded water. Messages are tapped along pipes or released as colored streams from small chemical valves: violet for pressure loss, yellow for harvest, white for a death, green for the arrival of something from above. Because currents are slow, news moves slowly too. A warning may take an hour to coil through the town.

Nothing in the Compression happens abruptly if it can happen by accumulation. Structures fail by softening. Machines stop over months. Corridors close as roots thicken by fractions. Mineral deposits narrow pipes from within, adding one pale ring after another. Maintenance is less an act of restoration than a continuous negotiation with change. A gallery abandoned this year will become a garden in ten; in fifty it will be mistaken for a natural cave; in a century someone will excavate it and call its corroded turbines ancient stone flowers.

The basin has long since reached a kind of balance. Waste heat feeds the upper orchards. Plant matter becomes oil, insulation, and filter cloth. Corroded metal is dissolved and plated elsewhere. Silt caught in the outer grilles is pressed into bricks. Even darkness is treated as a resource: certain chambers are kept unlit for the growth of black membranes used in batteries.

Above, the open sea may have tides and weather. Their effects arrive diminished. Once or twice a season, the silver ceiling sinks lower and the entire settlement compresses by a measurable degree. Roots tighten around masonry. Membrane doors bow inward. The great engines pause, listening through their pressure plates. Then the weight recedes, slowly enough that no single moment can be called its turning.

At the lowest point of the town is a small square paved in glazed tiles. It is one of the only places deliberately kept empty. No vines are permitted there, no cables cross it, and no structures project into its water. Suspended stones gather at its upper edge but are removed before they can form a roof.

In the center stands a dry fountain.

Its bowl is filled not with water, since water is everywhere, but with a perfectly clear sphere of air, held down against the basin floor by the local law of sinking bubbles. The sphere quivers without rising. Within it grows a single small tree, silver-barked and leafless, rooted in a pot of old brown soil.

No one enters the air. The tree has been dead for generations, or perhaps has simply adapted to a slower measure of life. Once every few years, witnesses say, one of its bare branches shifts slightly toward the dimness above.
