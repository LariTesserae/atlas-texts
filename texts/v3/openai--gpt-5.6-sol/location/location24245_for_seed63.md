At the crown of a long-dead volcanic range lies a hollow called, by those few who have seen its edge, **the Press**.

It is not large. From rim to rim it could fit beneath the roof of a cathedral, though no roof could survive there. The hollow is steep-sided and nearly circular, a bowl bitten into black basalt at an elevation where the surrounding air has become thin, metallic, and hot. Daylight reaches the rim but seems reluctant to descend. The walls are crowded with overhangs, ribs, shelves, and deep vertical seams, all of them darkened by heat-glaze. By the time the sun stands overhead, only a dull umber reflection reaches the floor. At all other hours the Press is almost completely black.

There is no water. No dew forms. Nothing drips from the rock. The mineral crusts that resemble old waterlines were laid down by ascending vapors: sulfur, iron chloride, bitter white salts carried through pores in the mountain and baked into terraces. The floor is a mosaic of volcanic glass, pumice compacted hard as bone, and red-black plates of oxidized metal. Here and there a fumarole exhales without steam, releasing transparent gases that bend the darkness like hot air above a kiln.

The temperature never falls far enough to become comfortable. At night the rock continues to radiate the heat it gathered over centuries of eruption and sun. Crevices breathe furnace-warm air. Loose fragments are too hot to hold for long. Yet there are no open flames and little glow. The place’s heat is mostly invisible, stored inside stone and flesh.

Almost every apparent stone is flesh.

The fauna of the Press occupies it completely. There is scarcely a palm’s breadth of bare floor. Layered creatures cling to the walls in overlapping shingles. Larger ones fill the gullies, leaving only narrow passages between armored backs. Minute forms inhabit the seams between those larger bodies, and smaller forms live in their joints, mouthparts, and breathing pits. The landscape is less a habitat crowded with animals than an animal multitude forced into the shape of terrain.

Most are dark and low, conserving themselves against the dry air. Their shells are basaltic—sometimes literally so, incorporating dust, iron, glass splinters, and salt into thick outer plates. The oldest have weathered until they are indistinguishable from the surrounding cliff. Their backs bear cracks, impact scars, mineral blooms, and the smooth facets made by generations of abrasive wind. Some carry smaller animals in such density that their own outlines disappear.

Nothing grows upward. There are no stems, leaves, fronds, or mats. The primary life of the Press consists of tiny heat-feeders embedded in porous rock. They do not photosynthesize. They draw energy from the slow chemical disagreement between volcanic gases and metallic salts. In darkness, unseen membranes exchange sulfur and iron; stone is tarnished, reduced, and tarnished again. These simple colonies form gray films only a few cells thick. They are grazed by needle-mouthed mites, which are grazed by coin-shaped crawlers, which are sifted from cracks by larger, many-jointed animals.

The dominant creatures are the **tiles**. Each is broad, flat, and roughly hexagonal, with six short legs hidden beneath a shield of glassy armor. Thousands cover the basin. They move so slowly that their migrations resemble subsidence. A section of wall may appear unchanged for hours, then settle by the width of a finger as every tile shifts toward a newly active vent. Their shells scrape together with a faint dry sound like charcoal being sorted in another room.

Among them stand the **spires**, tall only by comparison: jointed predators that balance on three rigid limbs and fold the others against their bodies. They are almost perfectly black. They hunt by sensing heat gradients, but in the Press everything is hot, so they wait for the cooler signatures of movement—the brief shadow in the thermal field made when a tile lifts itself from the stone. A spire’s strike is too rapid to see. Afterward there may be a soft click, and one patch of the living pavement is empty. The space does not remain empty for more than a few breaths.

Beneath the pavement are the burrowers. Their tunnels account for much of the hollow’s established form. For ages they have consumed weakened rock and excreted it as dense, vitrified pellets. The walls are honeycombed, though nearly every opening is plugged by an animal, a cast shell, or a carefully fitted wad of mineral fragments. The Press has been eroded less by wind than by digestion. Its basin deepens grain by grain, passed through uncounted bodies.

The heat produces weather of its own. At irregular intervals, gas rises through the mountain and gathers under the packed floor. The first sign is a tightening that passes through the tiles: thousands of bodies lowering themselves at once. Then the ground swells. Fissures open with muted snaps. Hot, dry breath pours upward, carrying salt crystals fine as smoke.

At this, the hollow becomes active.

The pavement comes apart. Tiles climb over one another in slow panic. Spires unfold and hunt recklessly. Pale thread-creatures burst from breathing pits and knot themselves around anything cooler than the gas. Shell-borers emerge from the dead armor in which they have waited, sometimes for years. The walls seem to ripple downward while the floor climbs upward to meet them. Yet even in this upheaval the Press remains mostly quiet. The air is too thin for roars, and most of its animals have no organs for making them. Their alarms are conveyed through pressure, heat, and contact: a lifted plate, a tremor through stone, a sudden cooling of the body.

The loudest sound is often a single shell cracking.

Wind enters only from above. It comes over the rim in brief, violent descents, already dry from crossing the high plateau. Laden with volcanic grit, it scours every exposed surface and pours through the basin in a slow spiral. The inhabitants lock their limbs together. Small animals push beneath large ones. Entire sections of the living floor become braided rafts of armor, each creature holding several others and being held in turn.

This is one of the Press’s governing rules: **nothing exposed remains alone**.

A solitary body loses heat too quickly at night, overheats too sharply during gas-rise, or is levered away by the wind. Survival belongs to layers, chains, infestations, and crowds. Even predators are hosts. Even parasites carry smaller parasites. Death does not reduce the density for long. A carcass becomes shelter before it cools and food before it stiffens.

Scattered through the hollow are structures that at first seem deliberate. Low rings of fitted stone occupy several ledges. Narrow arches bridge cracks. Against the northern wall stands something like a roofless hut, no higher than a person’s chest, made from black plates arranged in overlapping courses.

No tool marks appear on any of them.

They are the work of **bearers**, blind six-limbed animals that collect flat objects and carry them uphill. A bearer does not distinguish between stone, shed armor, eggshell, or bone. It places each piece where the heat gradient changes most sharply, building a shelter around the site without understanding shelter. Generations repeat the behavior. Their cairns become chambers; chambers collapse and are rebuilt; predators learn to wait beside the entrances. The oldest structures have settled into the cliff and appear far more purposeful than they are.

Or perhaps purpose is simply what repeated instinct looks like after ten thousand years.

The strangest feature lies at the basin’s center. There, amid the crowded movement, rises a bare column of pale mineral. Nothing clings to it. No burrow opens at its base. The column is smooth, slightly twisted, and cool enough to disturb the thermal senses of every creature nearby. Animals circle it but do not touch it. The tiles leave a narrow black ring around its foot—the only persistently empty ground in the Press.

During gas-rise, the column gives a low vibration. It is not exactly a sound; one feels it in the teeth and behind the eyes. The living pavement turns toward it. Predators pause with limbs raised. Burrowers stop chewing. For several moments the whole packed hollow becomes absolutely still.

Then the column warms, the vibration ends, and the basin resumes.

No one knows whether the pale stone is a geological intrusion, the cast of some extinct organism, or the one surviving remnant of an older structure. It bears no inscription. It performs no obvious function. Wind has polished it, salts have failed to stain it, and every form of life refuses it.

Above, the stars are hard and numerous. From the bottom of the Press they appear inside a small round opening, as though the entire landscape were enclosed in a vast black vessel. Sometimes winged forms cross that opening—high-altitude scavengers with translucent membranes stretched between long ribs. They seldom descend. There is nowhere to land that is not already occupied.

The Press endures by continuous replacement. It is ancient but never static. Rock passes into microbes, microbes into grazers, grazers into hunters, shells into walls, walls into grit, grit back into shell. Nothing blooms. Nothing flows. Little cries out. Yet beneath the darkness, in heat almost without flame and dryness beyond thirst, life is everywhere—stacked, armored, feeding, molting, hunting, building without tools, and slowly wearing the mountain into the shape it requires.
