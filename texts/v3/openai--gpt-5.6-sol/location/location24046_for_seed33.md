At first it appears to be a city on a mountain. From farther away it appears to be the mountain’s exposed interior, arranged with improbable civic order: avenues, terraces, towers, courtyards, long roofless chambers. From farther still, when distance begins to behave incorrectly, it seems that the entire range is one city folded many times through itself.

There is almost no water here. The air is too thin and cold to carry more than traces, and the pale sky gives no rain. Old mineral salts lie in the deepest courts, but they are dry, hard as fired clay, their crystal surfaces unmarked by any memory of shorelines. Frost is possible only in sealed cracks where single molecules accumulate over centuries. When the sun reaches such a crack, the frost vanishes directly into the air with no intervening melt. Nothing trickles. Nothing drips.

The settlement occupies a shelf near the summit of a continent-wide escarpment. On one side, the ground rises in tiers toward serrated peaks whose upper portions are hidden not by cloud but by a failure of perspective. On the other, the land falls away for many vertical kilometers into a dim basin. The basin floor is visible in extraordinary detail—individual stones, polygonal salt seams, the circular mouths of vents—yet it cannot be judged as near or far. Objects dropped over the edge diminish for hours and then hang motionless, as if the abyss has exhausted the idea of falling before the objects have reached its bottom.

The structures have no maker.

No tool marks occur anywhere. No hinge, fastening, carved symbol, or fitted joint can be found. The towers are single crystals extruded from the bedrock, split internally into rooms by planes of weakness. The streets are the spaces left between them. Colonnades have formed where erosion spared parallel ribs of a softer gray mineral enclosed within harder black stone. Stairways descend in regular flights because the mountain fractures at nearly equal intervals. Doorways are arches produced by stress, squares produced by cleavage, or tall narrow slits where two incompatible geometries meet.

It is urban without being technological: organized construction without construction, architecture without intention.

Some districts are dense enough to resemble the center of an ancient capital. Galleries cross above intersections. Empty balconies overlook plazas paved in hexagonal plates. Deep shafts connect one level to another, though they rarely remain vertical all the way down. Elsewhere the city thins abruptly. A solitary wall stands on a plain of powdered glass. A roof spans nothing. Five towers mark the vertices of a figure whose remaining points appear only at dusk. Beyond these outliers lies open stone, then the immense descending country.

No vegetation softens any surface. There is no soil. The exposed materials are basalt, salt, glass, pumice, metallic dust, and a bone-pale substance that is not organic despite its layered grain. In the lowest shadows, fungal-looking stains spread over walls, but they do not feed or reproduce. They are patterns of electrical charge trapped in the crystal, slowly rearranged by pressure from the range. Over decades they migrate from chamber to chamber, passing through solid walls while leaving the walls unchanged.

The city’s weather is almost entirely made of pressure.

At irregular intervals the thin atmosphere contracts and slides down from the peaks in broad, invisible sheets. Dust does not whirl in these winds. It rises a finger’s width from the ground and moves sideways in disciplined layers, each grain traveling at the same speed as its neighbors. The stream divides at corners, follows avenues, enters halls, and pours upward through chimneys. Seen against the dim sky, the moving dust gives the empty city a temporary circulation, as though its streets were veins and the mountain had begun to breathe.

The air remains nearly silent. Even the strongest pressure tides produce only a faint abrasion, a dry whisper too low to locate. Loose slabs strike the ground without the expected crack. Their impacts are heard minutes earlier as soft clicks in distant rooms. Occasionally an entire tower shears from its foundation and leans across an avenue. Its fall may take a season. During that time, its shadow continues to stand upright.

Erosion is old and balanced here. Edges are rounded, but only on their northern faces. Courtyards have collected centuries of metallic dust in smooth concave beds. Towers have been hollowed into lace by slow chemical exchange with the atmosphere. Yet the city does not diminish. As outer walls weather away, deeper planes emerge in equivalent arrangements: another wall beneath the wall, another arcade inside the arcade. The mountain seems to contain an inexhaustible urban plan, and erosion merely reveals the next layer.

This is one of the rules of the place: removal is a form of building.

Another rule concerns altitude. Height is not entirely a measure of distance above the basin. In certain streets, walking north raises a traveler even when the paving slopes downward. In others, climbing a staircase brings one back to the same landing under a colder sky. The highest point is said—if anything can be said of a place with no speakers—to lie in a cellar beneath the western terraces. That chamber has no ceiling. Looking up through it reveals stars in daytime, arranged around a black summit that hangs overhead like an inverted mountain.

Light enters reluctantly. The sun is small and copper-white, often caught behind the sharp ridges. Dawn travels through the streets as a narrow band rather than a general brightening. One tower may be illuminated while the avenue below remains in night; a courtyard may hold afternoon for several days after the surrounding district has passed into evening. Shadows have a granular texture. They accumulate against walls and slide downslope after sunset, gathering in the basin until the whole depth becomes a single dark mass.

There is fauna, although sightings are rare.

The most common creatures are thin, many-jointed forms called glass striders, if names are useful here. They are nearly transparent and no longer than a hand. Their bodies contain no liquid. Flexible lattices of carbon and salt contract when heated, allowing them to move during the brief passages of sunlight. They graze on differences in electrical potential, pressing their flat mouths to charged mineral stains. A strider may spend years crossing a plaza. When shadow reaches it, it locks its limbs and becomes indistinguishable from crystal debris.

Higher on the towers live hollow-winged animals that never descend. Their membranes are rigid and perforated, more like mineral fans than flesh. They do not flap. Pressure tides pass through them and lift them from one spire to another. Their nesting sites contain no young, eggs, or food—only shed geometries, delicate cages that retain the shape of bodies that may not yet have existed. Once in several years, a flock crosses the city. It makes no wing noise. Its moving shadows, however, produce a faint sound like distant breathing.

There may also be something in the abyss.

At night, rows of pale openings appear along the basin wall, too evenly spaced to be vents and too large to be windows. They brighten one after another from bottom to top. No creature emerges, but sections of the city subtly reorient toward them. Arches turn without moving. Streets that previously led east arrive at western overlooks. The glass striders freeze with their forelimbs raised. By morning the lights are gone, and a new district has become accessible somewhere among the lower terraces.

The landscape is active in this manner: not frantic, not loud, but ceaselessly revising its conditions. Gravity changes direction in narrow corridors. Dust migrates uphill. Fractures open and close with the passage of the unseen moons. Whole plazas exchange their locations during periods of darkness. The mineral stains conduct slow storms through solid rock, flashing dull violet beneath the surfaces. The escarpment settles by fractions of a degree, and those fractions propagate across hundreds of kilometers, causing distant towers to flower into new rooms.

Nothing here grows, but forms proliferate.

Nothing flows, but the city circulates.

Nothing speaks, but silence is passed from chamber to chamber as though it were news.

From the highest accessible terrace, one can see the city continuing across the range: ranks of slender towers, empty squares, walls worn smooth by atmospheric centuries. Past them are more peaks, and on those peaks more streets, each district isolated by enormous spans of near-vacuum. The architecture grows smaller toward the horizon, then larger again beyond it. In the last dimness before the sky, upside-down towers descend from no visible ground, meeting the upward towers tip to tip.

At those points of contact, the stone is always new.
