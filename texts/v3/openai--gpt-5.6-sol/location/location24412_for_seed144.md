### The Condenser Range

The range begins above the last dependable river and rises almost without foothills: a broad, crowded province of stone lifted three or four kilometers into the hot, thin air. From a distance it resembles a single dark plateau. Within it, the ground is divided into thousands of parallel ribs—basalt fins, tilted shelves, blunt towers, and narrow ravines packed so closely that there is seldom an empty horizon. Every surface appears occupied by another surface behind it. Cliffs overlap cliffs; ledges interlock; dry channels braid beneath talus fields and emerge several valleys away.

Yet the country is open. From the higher saddles one can see across entire districts, through ranks of rust-red rock softened by atmospheric haze. There are no forests to interrupt the view and few structures large enough to dominate it. Scale comes from repetition: forty kilometers of fins, terraces, chimneys, scree fans, and pale salt basins, all variations of the same ancient uplift.

The heat is severe. At noon, exposed metal burns skin and the black rock releases a wavering second sky. The elevation does not cool the range as much as it should. Warm air rises from the low desert and is trapped among the ribs, while the stone—dense with iron and old volcanic glass—holds sunlight well into the night. Even before dawn, the deeper ravines remain hot enough to smell faintly of baked clay.

Water is present, but rarely in the form of a visible stream.

Moist air arrives from a distant sea in high, nearly transparent layers. Where it passes over the range, it condenses against the coldest shaded faces: beneath overhangs, inside cracks, and along the undersides of iron pipes left from an abandoned industrial system. For an hour after sunrise, these surfaces sweat. Droplets gather, travel along mineral seams, and fall into hollows polished by centuries of intermittent dripping. Some basins are no wider than a hand. Others lie beneath the rubble as buried cisterns, their existence betrayed by damp air and the tracks of animals.

Rain comes several times a year, sudden and vertical. It does not soften the landscape. It strikes hot stone, fills every seam at once, and sends shallow silver floods down channels that had seemed decorative moments before. Within hours the water has vanished into fractures, evaporation, and underground sumps. Mud persists only in sheltered slots, where it becomes crowded with prints.

Erosion here has reached a kind of mature agreement with the rock. The great collapses happened long ago. Loose faces have already fallen; unstable towers have already broken at their natural joints. What remains is weathered but settled—rounded at the edges, varnished dark by age, and furrowed by routes water has followed for generations. Scree fans are broad and shallow, their stones graded by size. Wind has combed the finer sediments into fixed ripples between heavier fragments. The landscape changes constantly in small ways and almost never in large ones.

Vegetation is sparse enough that each plant appears deliberate. Low silver mats occupy condensation lines. Thorn shrubs grow at the mouths of damp cracks, their roots descending many times farther than their branches rise. There are stiff jointed grasses around hidden water and occasional pillar cacti in the lower basins, pale green beneath coatings of mineral dust. On shaded walls, black and amber lichens form maplike colonies. After rain, hair-thin red shoots emerge from gravel, flower before noon, and fold back into the ground by the following evening.

Animal life is far more abundant than the vegetation suggests. Most of it is small, and nearly all of it is economical.

Dark beetles climb exposed fins before dawn and stand with their rear bodies raised, collecting condensation on waxed shells. Long-legged lizards wait below them. Rock mice live in the thermal depth between sun-facing and shaded stone, moving vertically through the day to remain at the same temperature. Colonies of papery, ash-colored swifts nest in cracks so narrow that the birds seem to vanish directly into the cliff. Their flights begin together at dusk, filling the ravines with motion but surprisingly little sound.

Larger animals use the range as a network of corridors. Lean, high-shouldered grazers cross the shelves in single file, browsing thorn tips and licking moisture from pipe joints. Pale hunting cats follow them without haste. There are also heavy, nearly blind burrowers that live beneath old slag heaps, where the industrial debris has created stable chambers warmer than natural caves. Their digging continually exposes buried bolts, ceramic insulators, and blue-green copper crusts.

The old industry is scattered throughout the range rather than concentrated in a city. No large factory remains. Instead there are stations: small concrete housings sunk into saddles, narrow-gauge tracks ending at cliff edges, rusted hoists above sealed shafts, and lengths of pipe spanning gaps on stone piers. Their purpose becomes clear only from above. Together they formed a regional condensation works.

The system harvested night moisture from high metal vanes and carried it downhill through pressure lines to mines, refining kilns, and settlements that have since been dismantled or buried. Most of the vanes are gone, but thousands of their mounting sockets remain. A few surviving panels still turn on seized bearings when the temperature changes. Pipes contract before dawn with soft internal knocks. In several valleys, solar-driven pumps continue to operate without supervision. They wake only when their reservoirs are sufficiently full, move water from one hidden chamber to another, and stop. Some have repeated this cycle for decades.

Because the machinery is intermittent, the range is mostly quiet. There is wind at the high edges, the dry brushing of insect wings, grit moving down stone, and the brief ticking of heated rock after sunset. Sounds do not blend into a constant background. Each one stands apart. A falling pebble can be followed across three ledges. A pump starting in a neighboring basin sends a low vibration through the cliffs rather than through the air. Animals freeze when it happens, then resume their activity as if the mountain had merely cleared its throat.

The machinery has become part of the ecology. Leaking valves support mats of moss no larger than plates. Warm motor housings shelter reptiles. Mineral runoff from old condensers has created narrow blue pools where translucent shrimp breed during wet weeks and survive the rest of the year as sealed eggs in salt crust. Swifts orient to the straight lines of elevated pipelines. Predators patrol service roads because prey still prefers their easy grades.

There is one mildly troubling property to the water system: it does not always carry water downhill.

At certain temperatures, droplets on disconnected pipes begin moving toward the highest surviving condenser fields, creeping along interior walls against gravity. The effect is slow and reliable. Local animals anticipate it. Before a moisture reversal, beetles abandon the lower basins, birds climb to upper nests, and the long-legged grazers begin ascending even in the afternoon heat. Old pressure gauges respond as well, their needles turning though the lines are open to air.

No one has established whether this is caused by capillary action, thermal gradients, unusual mineral films, or some surviving control system buried inside the range. The phenomenon is accepted as one of the mountain’s rules: water descends after rain, rises before clear nights, and remains still during dust weather.

Those who cross the range travel according to moisture rather than distance. Paths are marked by small ceramic tags hammered into cracks, each stamped with a droplet, a sun, or a coiled line indicating which way water is likely to move. Shelters are rare—low stone rooms beside former maintenance shafts, usually without doors. Their roofs collect condensation into floor jars. Many contain no furnishings beyond a metal grate and a ledger sealed in an oilcloth box. Travelers record pump cycles, animal movements, and whether the water rose or fell.

By late afternoon, the whole country becomes visually severe but not dim: ordinary hard daylight on black, ocher, and white materials. Salt shines in ravine bottoms. Mica flashes from broken stone. Far-off pipe runs appear and disappear with the angle of the sun, briefly revealing the industrial skeleton stretched across the natural one.

Then the shadows fill the narrow places. Heat continues to rise from the rocks, carrying scents of iron, dust, resin, and hidden damp. The fauna emerges in layers—first insects, then mice and lizards, then the silent hunters. Condensation begins on the highest vanes. Drop by drop, the abandoned works wakes across the range, not as a ruin returning to life, but as something that never entirely stopped.
