The place is called the **Rain Court**, though it has no open sky and no one now remembers who gave it that name.

It is a shallow, enclosed basin about the size of a small hamlet, built entirely from fitted stone, fired clay, timber pegs, woven reed, shell lime, and dark slabs of hammered copper. There is no earth beneath it. Even the mud is manufactured: centuries of powdered brick, soot, guano, fish scales, rotted cordage, and lime ground together under feet and rain. Wherever this sediment is washed away, the floor reappears—an intricate pavement of tilted gutters and overlapping tiles, worn smooth as old teeth.

Low walls define the Court. They lean inward, though not enough to roof it, and their upper edges vanish into a dim condensation haze. Perhaps there is a ceiling far above; perhaps only a system of black rafters and suspended catchments. Light descends without a visible source, gray-blue and meager. It glances from wet copper and gathers in the pale seams between masonry blocks. Nothing casts a clean shadow.

Rain does not fall evenly here. It starts in one corner as a patter in the overhead darkness, then travels across the basin in narrow bands. Sometimes it comes as fine mist. Sometimes water abruptly streams down a wall from a row of carved animal mouths. Every surface has been shaped to receive it. Steps cup inward. Pillars are grooved. Walls weep through porous ceramic plugs. Channels intersect, divide, vanish beneath grates, and emerge elsewhere with altered currents. The builders seem to have regarded flowing water as a creature that must be coaxed, delayed, startled, and entertained.

The Court is never deeply flooded. Water is too carefully moved for that. It lies a finger’s breadth over portions of the floor, collects in square cisterns, and runs continuously through knee-wide races. At the basin’s lowest point stands a circular drain house no taller than a person, made of black stone rings. Water spirals around it but does not visibly enter. Instead, it disappears through hundreds of hairline cracks in the surrounding pavement. Pressing an ear to the drain house reveals a vast underground roar, wildly disproportionate to the little Court.

There is almost nothing upright.

A few short columns mark the watercourses, each supporting a shallow copper bowl. Three roofless huts cling to the eastern wall. Their doors are low, their interiors empty, and their bricks have been rounded by generations of damp. Across the main pavement stands a timber footbridge, gray with age, spanning a channel scarcely wider than a hand. It is extravagantly reinforced, as if meant to cross an abyss.

The emptiness makes each object seem ceremonial. A clay scoop left beside a cistern becomes a monument. A length of knotted rope hanging from a peg suggests some forgotten office. Pale stones arranged in groups of seven might be tools, counters, traps, or prayers. There are no ruins in the ordinary sense: everything is maintained by the place’s inhabitants, though not necessarily for its original purpose. Broken tiles are reset. Leaking joints are packed with moss fiber and wax. Copper sheets are patched with shell, leather, or smaller pieces of copper stitched through drilled holes.

No metal tool more complicated than a knife, hook, awl, or small hammer is visible. The whole elaborate hydraulic court appears to have been made—and continues to be repaired—by hands, wedges, fire, gravity, and patience.

Vegetation is scarce. There is nowhere for roots to go.

Thin moss rims the north-facing channels in bruised green lines. Bladderwort floats in one cistern, its tiny yellow flowers opening only when the rain stops. In cracks where sediment has accumulated, colorless grass grows in sparse tufts, each blade translucent and cool to the touch. One dwarf willow occupies a ceramic shaft near the western wall. Its roots have been trained through a lattice of clay pipes, and its seven remaining branches trail over the water like loose hair. After every storm, beetles strip it nearly bare. It persists anyway, not flourishing, not dying.

The animals are another matter.

They fill the Rain Court without filling its space.

Most live inside the built fabric: in drains, behind tiles, under bridges, within hollow pillars, along the undersides of gutters. The open pavement often looks deserted until water moves. Then the Court erupts.

Hundreds of slate-colored frogs climb from circular holes and begin calling at once, their throats pale and nearly luminous. Needle-legged crabs emerge from wall joints to comb edible films from the stone. Silver minnows shoot through channels no deeper than their own bodies. Long translucent eels knot themselves inside the ceramic downspouts, bracing against sudden torrents. Snails as large as fists travel the parapets, scraping lime and algae from the masonry with a sound like distant sawing.

The loudest creatures are the rain crows. They are not true crows but hunched, flightless birds with black oil-slick feathers, broad feet, and shovel-shaped beaks. They patrol the channels in groups, turning tiles over to expose worms and clicking pebbles against the pavement. Their cries resemble wooden clappers struck underwater. When one begins, the others answer from alcoves and drain mouths until the entire enclosure resounds.

Above them live the pulley bats.

They hang from ropes rather than rafters, gripping the braided fibers in long rows. Their weight slowly draws up counterweighted sluice gates. At dusk—or at whatever dimming passes for dusk here—the bats release together, the gates drop, and stored water surges through the Court. This is the basin’s daily flood pulse. Fish spill from high cisterns into low channels; crabs abandon their feeding grounds; frogs scramble onto the walls; rain crows race the advancing water, screaming.

The ropes rise again as the bats return to roost.

Whether this arrangement was devised by builders or discovered by the animals is impossible to tell. The counterweights are plainly handmade: clay jars packed with stone chips. Yet the rope knots are renewed with strips of bark, gut, and woven bird feather. Small claw marks cover the wooden cleats. The system functions, but no single species appears to understand all of it.

Sound makes the Court feel larger than it is. Water knocks in closed pipes. Air moans through narrow ceramic throats. Copper bowls ring when struck by heavy drops. Frogs pulse from one wall to another. Crabs tap, birds clatter, bats rustle, and unseen things chew behind the masonry. Every few minutes a hidden reservoir overturns with a deep hollow boom. The echo rolls around the low walls, doubles back, and seems to descend beneath the pavement.

Silence occurs only just before a major release of water.

At those moments every animal pauses. Even the snails retract their feelers. The dimness thickens, and condensation stands motionless on the copper. Then somewhere overhead a wooden catch clicks. The Court inhales through all its vents at once. Water comes down in ropes.

The place has rules, though they are hydraulic rather than moral.

Nothing may remain dry for long. Anything left on the pavement will eventually be carried to the western grate, but nothing passing through that grate is ever seen below. Channels do not always lead in the direction their slope suggests. A floating husk may vanish under one wall and return from a higher outlet several hours later. Water drawn from the square cistern tastes faintly of iron; water from the round cistern tastes of smoke; water gathered directly from the rain tastes salty.

No animal drinks from the triangular pool.

That pool lies in an otherwise bare corner enclosed by three low curbs. Its surface remains perfectly still during storms. Rain passes through it without making rings. At intervals, a pale fish appears beneath the surface, far larger than the pool could contain. It turns one slow eye upward and sinks out of sight. The rain crows avoid the corner. Frogs calling nearby lower their pitch.

The Rain Court is old enough to have settled into itself. Its walls are bowed but stable. Erosion has not destroyed it; erosion is one of its operating mechanisms. Grooves worn by generations of runoff now carry water more efficiently than the original channels. Stone lips have rounded into spillways. Snail rasping keeps certain vents open. Crabs remove silt from pipe mouths. Bird droppings seal porous brick. What looks like decay is often maintenance, and what looks repaired may be the beginning of collapse.

There are signs that people still visit.

Fresh thumbprints sometimes appear in the wax around a leaking joint. A broken bridge peg will be replaced overnight with a newly carved one. Bundles of dry fiber are left inside the roofless huts, though keeping anything dry here requires knowledge of the rain’s hidden sequence. Small bare footprints cross the wet sediment and end at blank walls.

No settlement surrounds the Court. No road enters it. The only obvious gate has been bricked shut with stones older than the wall.

Yet, on certain cool intervals when the animal noise subsides and mist lies knee-high over the tiles, someone can be heard hammering far below: three measured blows, a pause, then two. The drain house vibrates in answer. Water wells briefly from every seam in the pavement, carrying bubbles, white eyeless shrimp, splinters of worked wood, and now and then a curl of warm smoke.

Then the frogs begin again, louder than before, and the little constructed world returns to its endless labor of catching, releasing, wearing down, and remaking the rain.
