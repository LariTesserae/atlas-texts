At first the darkness seems to come from weather. Warm cloud presses against the high slopes, reducing distance to a succession of gray walls and black openings. Moisture gathers everywhere without becoming abundant water: beads along parapets, slickens stair treads, hangs under leaves, and drips at long intervals into unseen courts. Rain comes often enough to stain the masonry but rarely enough to leave the place thirsty between storms.

Then the darkness resolves into layers.

There is no bare ground. Every slope is a roof, a stair, a collapsed vault, a paved incline, or a retaining wall buried under centuries of leaf mold. The entire upland has been constructed. Ridges are long masonry embankments. Valleys are sunken avenues. Cliffs are façades whose windows have lost their shutters and whose lower stories have disappeared behind terraces of rubble and soil. Even the narrow streams descend through lined channels, spill from worn stone mouths, vanish beneath arcades, and emerge much farther down among foundations.

The settlement is too extensive to take in at once. It occupies a warm mountain basin and climbs the surrounding shoulders in crowded tiers. Across the valley, through breaks in the cloud, whole districts can be glimpsed: pale stepped walls softened by moss; towers truncated by old weather; bridges linking hills that may themselves be artificial. The forms have been settled into their erosion. Corners are rounded, inscriptions have become shallow grooves, and stairs bow in the middle from the passage of water, roots, paws, and innumerable small feet. Nothing looks recently ruined. Collapse has reached a mature state, with fallen stones wedged securely, old fractures furred with lichen, and vines thick enough to have become structural.

The builders are absent. There are no working gates, lamps, wheels, pipes, or mechanisms. Whatever purposes the structures once served have drained from them. A channel is now simply where frogs breed. A gallery is where bats roost. A dome is a dry place beneath which antbirds forage. Architecture remains, but technology does not. The city has become substrate.

Vegetation occupies nearly every seam. Tree ferns rise from assembly halls. Figs root in high cornices and lower pale cords of roots through ten or twelve stories before reaching accumulated humus. Broad-leaved trees crowd courtyards, their crowns closing over streets and making a second roof beneath the cloud. Orchids cling to carved lintels. Bromeliads collect spoonfuls of rain in the shoulders of statues worn nearly featureless. Moss grows thickest where condensation runs nightly down the walls, while sunnier upper terraces carry tough grasses, flowering shrubs, and low trees bent by mountain wind.

The growth is lush but uneven. Moisture persists in pockets rather than across the whole basin. One stairwell may be green and dripping, the adjacent chamber dry enough for dust. Cisterns hold dark, leaf-stained water. Shallow canals fill during rain and empty slowly through cracks. In the deepest courts, where sunlight almost never reaches, long rectangular pools remain through the dry intervals, crowded with larvae, snails, tadpoles, floating seeds, and small fish descended from populations trapped there long ago. Farther upslope, plants survive on fog and dew, sending roots deep into mortar beds in search of stored dampness.

Warmth never quite leaves. Even at night, stone gives back the heat it gathered during the few bright hours. The air under the canopy is heavy and close, smelling of wet lime, crushed leaves, animal musk, fungal rot, flowers, and guano. On exposed terraces the altitude can be felt in the thin wind and the rapid cooling of sweat, but enclosed passages remain warm as burrows.

The animals make the place seem inhabited beyond capacity.

They are in every opening. Swiftlets pack the upper galleries. Bats occupy ceilings in black, shifting draperies. Monkeys cross the city without touching the lower levels, moving through branches, roof ridges, vines, and broken bridges. Parrots crowd the fruiting trees until the foliage appears to change color. Lizards flatten themselves against sun-warmed walls; snakes follow them through drain slots and cracked mosaics. Beetles work beneath bark and plaster. Termites raise earthen ribs against columns. Crabs live in the damp lower channels, far from any sea, feeding on fallen fruit and dead frogs. Moths sleep by the thousands under ledges, their wings patterned like stone stains.

The noise is nearly continuous and often overwhelming. It does not come from one source but from accumulation. Cicadas drive a metallic pulse through the afternoon. Frogs answer from separate cisterns, each court holding its own pitch. Birds shriek across the basin, their calls ricocheting from façades and returning altered. Monkeys bark from the canopy. Wings beat inside chambers. Fruit falls on roofs with hollow blows. Rain, when it arrives, drums on leaves, tiles, stretched roots, water surfaces, and vaults, each material contributing a different density of sound.

At dusk the daytime chorus does not cease; it changes membership. Cicadas descend in pitch. Frogs multiply. Bats pour from towers in such numbers that the air itself seems to rasp. Nightjars call from parapets, insects strike leaves, and unseen animals move through litter accumulated over pavements. The old city magnifies everything. Covered streets become resonant throats. Domes hold calls for several seconds. A single troop of monkeys crossing a roof can sound like masonry giving way.

Actual collapse is rare. Change here is slow.

A root widens a joint over decades. A wall leans for generations before settling into the neighboring structure. Pools deepen grain by grain as acidic water eats mortar. Trees fall occasionally, opening brief columns of hard white daylight through the canopy; within a few seasons vines curtain the gap, and within a lifetime the opening is remembered only by a patch of younger growth. Animal paths endure longer than individual animals. Repeated claws polish certain ledges. Guano builds shallow floors. Seeds carried by birds determine which towers become groves and which remain bare.

Water governs the city without ever dominating it. During rain, the basin reveals an old drainage pattern. Channels wake. Carved gutters overflow. Sheets of water descend broad stairways and gather in plazas whose paving tilts almost imperceptibly toward buried conduits. For an hour, sometimes a day, the place becomes riverine: streets run brown, courtyards become ponds, and cascades appear between districts. Then the water withdraws. It sinks through rubble, remains in cisterns, or leaves only condensation and dark margins on the walls.

No creature seems to possess the whole place. Territories overlap vertically and by time of day. A stair belongs to monkeys in the morning, butterflies at noon, frogs after rain, and snakes at night. A tower may hold flowering vines on its exterior, bees in its cracks, swifts in the upper chambers, bats below them, and blind insects in the compacted debris at its base. Predation is constant but dispersed. Nothing dramatic needs to happen for countless lives to end before sunset.

The highest structures rise above the canopy into moving cloud. Their roofs have become small meadows, brighter and windier than the city below. Grass bends around rows of weathered stone uprights. Mountain birds nest in shallow niches. From these heights, when the cloud lifts, the scale of the construction becomes apparent: terraces circle the basin, walls continue over saddles into neighboring valleys, and straight causeways cut through forest until haze erases them. There is no visible edge where the made world gives way to untouched mountain. Perhaps none remains within reach.

Yet below, in the enclosed streets, the place feels intimate and nearly blind. Passage is measured by touch—the cool wall beside a path, the worn lip of a step, the yielding mat of leaves over pavement. Light comes in green shafts and pale reflections from wet stone. Eyes shine briefly from doorways. Every room contains another population, every roof supports another patch of forest, and every pool has its own crowded, audible night.

The governing rule is simple: everything built has become habitat, and everything living must borrow a shape made long before it arrived. The city does not awaken, heal, or return to nature. It has already reached a more durable condition than any of those. Stone, water, root, darkness, and animal abundance have settled into one landscape, loud with immediate life and almost motionless in its larger form.
