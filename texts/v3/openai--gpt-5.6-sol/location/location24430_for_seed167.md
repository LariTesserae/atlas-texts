The city lies beneath a warm, shallow-bellied sea, though nowhere within it feels shallow. Water fills every street, chamber, and courtyard, rising far above the tallest structures into an almost unbroken dark. There is no visible surface. What little light penetrates from above has been strained through miles of suspended silt and dense plankton until it is no more than a brown-green pressure in the water—a direction rather than illumination.

The ground is low and broadly level, an old basin settled beneath the weight of the sea. Its few elevations are gradual: limestone swells, banks of compacted shell, long ridges where currents have sorted heavier rubble from mud. The city occupies several such ridges and the troughs between them. It extends beyond sight, district after district revealed only when one enters them: walls emerging at arm’s length, avenues opening like black canals, towers sensed first by the alteration of the current around them.

No hands built this place. There are no tools, no machines, no worked metal, and no memory of fire. The architecture is biological.

The oldest walls began as reefs formed by pale, calcifying animals. Generation after generation raised partitions, vaults, and branching buttresses. Other creatures enlarged the pattern. Burrowing colonies opened passages through dead limestone; shell-bearing animals sealed weak sections with their own bodies; soft polyps spread across exposed surfaces and hardened after death. What looks like masonry is an accumulation of lives following inherited rules.

The result has the organization of a city without its intention. Broad channels divide dense blocks. Narrower lanes wind through clusters of hollow towers. There are chambers large enough to resemble public halls, their ceilings supported by pillars grown downward from the roof and upward from the floor until they met. Round openings repeat at regular intervals because the animals that made them keep a fixed distance from their neighbors. In some districts, the reef has produced terraces like amphitheaters. In others, thousands of parallel tubes rise from the mud, crowded as tenements.

Everything is weathered into equilibrium. Corners have been rounded by centuries of passing water. Old facades are furred with mineral crust and the small mouths of filter feeders. Fallen structures do not remain distinct ruins for long: their blocks sink into the soft bottom, become foundations, and are built over by new colonies. Erosion and construction are not opposites here. The current takes material from the outer walls and carries it inward, where other organisms bind it into fresh partitions. The city is slowly dismantling itself at its edges and rebuilding itself at its center.

Vegetation is scarce. There is too little light for forests of weed, and the sea floor is mostly bare mud, shell, and animal-made stone. In rare openings where a weak gray radiance reaches down, short black-green blades grow in sparse rings. Their leaves are broad and delicate, adapted to catch almost nothing. A few threadlike algae cling to the highest ridges. They grow slowly, are grazed quickly, and never dominate the landscape.

Animal life does.

The walls breathe through innumerable pores. Fans unfold from cracks to strain edible dust from the current, then vanish at the touch of pressure. Long translucent grazers move over the stone, polishing it in pale tracks. Blind fish crowd the avenues in loose rivers, keeping close enough to feel one another’s movement. Their scales are dark red, violet, or nearly transparent—colors that exist here without being seen.

Crabs occupy the lower chambers, sorting shell fragments into defensive rings. Eels knot themselves inside drain-like tunnels. Rays rest under thin layers of sediment, their buried gills marking the ground with paired pulses. Predators pass through the upper streets: slow-bodied sharks, broad-headed hunters, and ribbonlike animals that hunt by sensing the minute electrical disturbance of muscle.

Nothing has eyes large enough to suggest hope of daylight. Many creatures have no eyes at all.

The city is dense with bodies, but it is mostly quiet. There is no surf and little hard collision. Mud absorbs impacts; living walls flex. The dominant sound is a low, irregular patter of shells closing, claws touching limestone, and mouths scraping film from stone. At intervals an entire district contracts in response to a pressure wave. Millions of feeding structures snap shut almost together, producing a muffled rush like rain falling on a roof far away.

Then they reopen, one by one.

Currents provide the city’s weather. Warm flows arrive through the eastern channels carrying plankton, eggs, larvae, and the metallic scent of distant storms. The streets fill with feeding limbs. Fish abandon their shelters and face into the flow. Predators follow them. Hours later, the water may reverse, drawing clouds of waste and shed tissue into the western trenches.

Seasonal floods occur entirely underwater. Dense, silty water pours down from the unseen world above, advancing as a wall along the bottom. It buries plazas, chokes low chambers, and erases tracks. The inhabitants climb the towers or seal themselves inside their shells. When the flood thins, scavengers descend in great numbers, and new neighborhoods begin growing over the deposits.

There are rules here, though none are written. Empty space is temporary. A clean surface will be occupied. A blocked current will find or make another street. Anything dead becomes structure, food, or both. Anything that grows too high is broken by the flow and redistributed below. The city persists not by resisting change but by giving every disturbance a place in its architecture.

Its strangest feature is discovered only by remaining still.

At first, the towers and walls appear fixed. Gradually their alignments shift. Openings narrow as colonies swell. A passage that admitted a large animal last season may now permit only small fish. Elsewhere, synchronized burrowers hollow a new corridor through what had seemed solid rock. Over years, whole avenues migrate sideways. The plan of the city circulates as slowly as the water.

In the deepest central district, the structures stand so close that the sea becomes a network of narrow vertical shafts. Warm water rises through them continuously. Here the darkness is complete, yet life is thickest. Pale animals hang in tiers from every surface. Their faint chemical glimmer does not illuminate the chambers; it merely outlines mouths, spines, and drifting threads before being swallowed by the water.

From a distance, the district gives off a dim, uneven pulse.

It may resemble habitation seen through windows at night, but there are no windows and no inhabitants separate from the buildings. The city itself is the population: wall feeding beside wall, arch reproducing into arch, street opening and closing with the needs of the current. It has stood for ages, though no part of it has remained the same.
