The plateau begins above the weather.

Clouds lie far below its rim, not as a moving sea but as a pale, apparently solid floor filling the lower world. Above them rises a country of worn black stone, immense in extent and almost level despite its altitude. Mountains must once have stood here. Their roots remain as broad discolorations in the ground: rings of basalt, iron-gray ribs, shallow fans of pulverized crystal. Every sharp contour has been abraded away. Cliffs finish in rounded lips. Boulders have become low domes polished like old bone. The land looks not recently ruined but completed, reduced through uncounted ages to the shape it can keep forever.

There is no water. There are no channels that could plausibly have carried it, no frost in shadow, no dampness beneath overturned stones. The pale material gathered in hollows is not salt, though it resembles salt. It is a dry efflorescence of nitrates, shed shell, powdered mica, and something faintly organic that sublimes when warmed by the hand. Nothing flows. Even loose dust does not drift.

The air is cool and thin. It has a mineral taste, like a copper coin held beneath the tongue, and carries a muted odor of feathers stored in stone. Breathing is possible, though each breath feels strangely local. Air drawn in beside one outcrop may be bitter and heavy; a few paces away it may be almost absent. These pockets do not mix. Wind cannot be felt, and yet narrow pennants hung from distant poles stand rigidly toward the east, as if remembering a wind that ended before the plateau was old.

Normal daylight covers everything. There is one sun, colorless and neither enlarged nor dimmed. Shadows fall with ordinary precision. This plainness of illumination makes the abnormalities harder to dismiss.

The plateau is full of animals.

At first they resemble stones because none of them move.

Small six-legged creatures crowd the fissures, fitted shell to shell in numbers beyond counting. Long-bodied grazers stand along the slopes with their narrow heads lowered to patches of gray lichen. Birds occupy every ledge, every weathered pillar, every available span of ground. Some sit with wings folded. Others are suspended several body lengths above the plateau, wings extended, held in the posture of flight without falling or advancing. Herds cover the open basins so densely that their backs form patterned fields: dun, ivory, russet, black. Between them rise the antler fans of larger beasts, branching so profusely that neighboring animals have grown—or always been—interlocked.

There are predators too. Pale cats crouch among the herds. Hook-jawed things lean eternally into the instant before biting. A vast, low creature armored in overlapping plates occupies an entire ridge. Its spine could be mistaken for a line of ruined walls until the regular openings along its sides are recognized as breathing pores.

No breathing can be seen.

The animals are not dead. Their eyes remain clear. If approached, their pupils contract. The change is so slow that certainty requires marking the pupil’s edge with a thread and returning hours later. Warmth persists under fur and shell, but it is a dry warmth unrelated to blood. There is no pulse. Wounds reveal no liquid; cut tissue separates into delicate mineral fibers and closes over a period of years.

Their lives proceed without locomotion.

The grazers consume by proximity. Gray crusts thin beneath their mouths, molecule by molecule, and reappear along the shaded sides of rocks. Predators feed through alignment: when the shadow of a crouching hunter touches the shadow of prey, mass is transferred in quantities too small to weigh but sufficient across centuries. The flying animals migrate by changing which patch of sky contains them. One does not see the transition. A bird remains fixed overhead for decades, and then, between two glances, the same bird is found many valleys away, still in precisely the same posture.

Birth is an issue of accumulation. Dust gathers around certain eggs, cocoons, and hollow antler-knots until a complete young animal is present inside. Death, when it occurs, is equally gradual. An animal becomes less distinguishable from the terrain, its hide taking on the grain of basalt, its bones extending into nearby strata. Much of the plateau may be ancestral fauna settled so deeply into geological stillness that the difference between ecosystem and bedrock has ceased to be useful.

This explains the density without explaining the silence.

Millions of throats and wings produce almost nothing. Close to the insect-filled cracks there is a faint ticking, but it may be thermal contraction in the rock. The herds make a soft pressure in the ears, a sound below sound. Occasionally, when someone passes between two suspended birds, there is a single dry note like a fingernail touching pottery. It does not echo. Echoes here return before the noise that makes them, and so are usually mistaken for expectation.

Vegetation is rare and mostly difficult to recognize. Black lichens form diagrams on the sheltered faces of stones. Wire-thin stalks grow from the powdery hollows, each bearing one translucent bladder filled not with sap but with compressed gas. Their roots do not seek moisture. They anchor themselves in magnetic seams and draw energy from the infinitesimal strain of the plateau against the mass of the mountain beneath it.

A few larger plants stand in the northern basins: low, many-branched forms resembling shrubs cast in pewter. Their leaves are flakes of flexible silica. They never open, fall, or turn toward the sun. Herds gather around them anyway. Some relationship exists, although it may not be feeding. The animals facing these shrubs are always younger than those facing away.

Scattered among the crowded life are structures built by hands.

They are small, circular shelters of stacked slate, roofed with hide and ribs. Most have sunk only a finger’s breadth into the ancient surface despite appearing thousands of years old. Beside them stand tripods of bone, chipped-stone scrapers, shallow ceramic bowls, and blades hammered from native copper. The copper has not tarnished. There are no wells and no cisterns. Containers are made to hold dust, breath, shadows, or small animals; none are shaped for pouring.

Paths wind between the herds. They are not cleared routes but agreements in posture: a corridor where every creature happens to stand turned aside. At intervals, waist-high cairns mark safe changes in perspective. Each cairn is topped with an eye made from obsidian. Travelers are expected to cover these eyes before passing.

The builders—when they can be found—wear layered felt and cloaks sewn from thin plates of shed carapace. They use stone needles, bone wedges, copper hooks, cords of hair. They do not hunt in any familiar manner. To kill an animal would take generations. Instead they harvest what the motionless abundance gives up: antler tips, shell flakes, mineral wool, eggs that have failed to finish becoming. They speak with their hands close to the body, making tiny signs. Spoken words are avoided, not because sound carries, but because names attract continuity.

Their shelters are built for concealment from duration rather than weather.

On this plateau, persistence is not evenly distributed. A thing observed too steadily becomes fixed. A thing ignored may alter. The people therefore veil objects they wish to use again. Tools are wrapped between tasks. Children sleep under masks. Doorways bend twice so that the interior cannot be held in one uninterrupted view. No one watches a cooking stone while it heats. No one looks directly at an egg expected to hatch.

Conversely, anything dangerous is surrounded by staring figures, polished disks, painted eyes, or tethered animals positioned so that at least one clear gaze rests upon it at all times. In several basins, rings of thousands of beasts face inward toward empty patches of ground. The people do not enter those rings. They say the centers contain storms, fires, diseases, or cities that would resume as soon as they were unobserved.

Distance is unreliable in quieter ways. A ridge may require half a day to reach from the south and seven years to leave by the north. Two shelters visible side by side may belong to different centuries. Looking through an antler fork sometimes reveals the plateau before its erosion: serrated peaks, fountains of ash, enormous organisms swimming through stone as if it were liquid. Looking again shows only the worn plain.

Nothing actually changes while watched. This appears to be the deepest rule of the place.

The sun travels, but never perceptibly. Shadows are found in new positions rather than seen moving. The coolness holds from hour to hour. There are no gusts, storms, seasons, or sudden cries. Even catastrophe settles here into posture. In the far west, the plateau ends at a precipice where a section of the world has broken away. Rocks, animals, fragments of dwellings, and slabs of earth hang beyond the edge in a vast descending cloud. They are all falling. None descends.

At the brink, the density of life becomes almost unbearable. Birds fill the open air beneath one’s feet. Wing overlaps wing down through the cloud layer and perhaps beyond it. Their eyes are turned upward, millions of bright points in the depth. There is no sound of flight. There is no motion. Only the ordinary sun, the cool dry air, and the accumulated attention of an ecology that has outlasted change.

From there the plateau extends behind the observer, crowded to every horizon: an ancient high country with no rivers, almost no plants, and more animals than empty space. It is not frozen, precisely. Freezing would imply an interruption, a before and after.

This is equilibrium made into terrain.

Everything is alive.

Everything has already arrived.
