The plain is not earth, though at first it passes for earth.

It extends beyond the clean reach of sight: a low, hot expanse of ocher plates fitted edge to edge, each plate broad as a field and subtly bowed by heat. Their surfaces have been roughened with grit and scored by shallow grooves. Dust gathers in those grooves, softening the geometry until the joins resemble dry streambeds or the polygonal cracks of a salt flat. Yet every line is deliberate. Beneath the dust, beneath the pale mineral film left by rain, the whole country is fabricated.

There is no exposed natural substrate. The distant ridges are retaining walls buried under aggregate. The knolls are hollow vaults filled with rubble and compacted clay. Even the scattered stones are fragments of manufactured ceramic, rounded only slightly, their broken interiors showing layers of red, cream, and charcoal. Nothing moves within the structures. There are no mechanisms, no cables, no lights under the ground. Whatever work made this place ended before weather began its work upon it.

That work is only now beginning.

The landscape is young in the sense that a newly abandoned courtyard is young. Its surfaces are intact. Edges remain sharp where the wind has not yet found them. Rain has traced the faintest channels but has not cut gullies. At the bases of inclines, fans of washed dust are scarcely thicker than cloth. The plain has the potential for canyons, dunes, deltas, and badlands, but these exist only as tendencies: a darkening seam here, three finger-width runnels there, a patch where ceramic grit has begun to sort itself by size.

Heat governs the broad hours. By midday, the plates radiate upward until distance trembles. The air smells of baked mineral, dry clay, and occasionally something faintly metallic released from wet surfaces. There is little shade. The largest vertical features are low embankments, broad ramps, and widely spaced pylons of fused stone that rise no higher than modest hills. They appear less like ruins than like parts of the terrain’s original design—markers, buttresses, or simply places where the immense constructed surface had to breathe and settle.

Water arrives in several ways, none of them abundant.

At night, condensation gathers on the cooler faces of the pylons and along the undersides of overhanging slabs. Droplets swell in silence, then descend through etched channels into shallow basins. Some basins hold water for days: round, clear pockets with pale bottoms and rings of green around their margins. Others leak through imperfect joins and nourish narrow lines of growth beneath the surface.

Rain comes infrequently but with force. Warm clouds build over the vast plain from moisture released by distant reservoirs and by the wet seams themselves. Their shadows travel for hours before the first drops fall. A storm may darken an area the size of a province while sunlight remains visible at every horizon. Water sheets over the plates, finding the designed grooves first and accidental cracks second. For a short time, the empty country becomes a branching network of silver threads.

The channels do not lead to rivers in the ordinary sense. They converge into broad, shallow courts sunk below the plain. There the water spreads, slows, and gathers behind low lips of masonry. Some courts become temporary lakes, only a handspan deep but many kilometers across. Their surfaces reflect the sky almost perfectly. Within a week, heat reduces them to scattered pools; later, to dark dampness under a skin of salt; later still, to dry mosaics of silt.

A few plants have learned these cycles.

Most are low and widely separated. Gray-green cushions occupy cracks where condensation persists. Tough grasslike blades grow in single clumps along drainage seams, their roots descending into the manufactured fill. In the temporary basins, dormant films wake after rain and turn the margins olive, then bright green. Slender stalks rise quickly, shed dust-fine spores, and collapse before the water is gone. There are no flowers requiring visitors, no fruit, no burrs designed to catch fur. Reproduction belongs to wind, water, fragmentation, and patience.

Nothing grazes. Nothing flies.

The absence of animals changes the character of every living patch. Leaves remain uneaten until sun and fungus take them. Seed cases lie where they fall. No tracks cross the drying mud. Pools are never disturbed by mouths or feet; their only rings come from droplets, gas bubbles, or small pieces of mineral flaking from the basin walls. Even the microbial films grow in uninterrupted bands, recording each retreat of the water as a fine dark contour.

From a distance, the plain often seems motionless. Up close, it is busy.

Heat expands the plates during the day and releases them at night. Joins tighten, slip, and settle by fractions. Moisture enters hairline flaws, dissolves binding salts, and leaves pale blooms when it evaporates. Dust migrates steadily down the shallowest inclines. Thin crusts curl away from wet basins. Plant roots pry apart seams grain by grain. After rain, rivulets carry dark sediment over clean orange surfaces, sketching the first drainage systems of a geology that has not yet had time to become convincing.

The sounds are correspondingly small. Wind whispers over grit. Water ticks from the cooling pylons before dawn. A drying pool makes faint clicks as its mineral skin contracts. At the hottest hour, a buried slab may shift with a single deep knock that travels a long distance through the hollow ground. Thunder is immense when it comes, amplified by open space and by the chambers under the plain, but storms pass, and the silence closes again.

There are no buildings standing apart from the landscape because the landscape itself is the building. A ramp several kilometers long rises so gradually that it feels like a natural slope until one notices its exact curvature. A “cliff” reveals rows of fitted blocks where a corner has recently failed. Broad terraces repeat toward the horizon, too large to read as steps from ground level. Their purpose, if they had one beyond shaping water and carrying weight, is no longer apparent.

The place has no active technology and offers no evidence of repair. It obeys ordinary heat, gravity, evaporation, capillary action, and growth. Its strangeness is mostly a matter of extent. A paved court can be understood; so can a cistern, a retaining wall, a drainage bed. Here those familiar things have been enlarged until they make weather. The basins summon clouds. The warm plates push columns of air upward. The buried chambers moderate nighttime cold. The country functions, but only passively, as a stone cup functions when it catches rain.

Toward evening the glare softens. Long amber bands lie across the ramps, and every scattered plant casts a precise shadow. Moisture begins to gather where surfaces face away from the sinking sun. The temporary lakes, if any remain, turn copper and then gray. Far off, a slow veil of rain may descend without sound.

It is a complete world made of unfinished consequences: a vast constructed lowland handed over to water before water has had time to remake it. Given centuries, its seams will become ravines, its courts marshes or salt pans, its hollow ridges collapsed hills. Soil will accumulate in sheltered angles. Vegetation may spread.

For now, almost everything is still visible in its first condition—the immense design beneath a dusting of weather, and weather, patiently beginning.
