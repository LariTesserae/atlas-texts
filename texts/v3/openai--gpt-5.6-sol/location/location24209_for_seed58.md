The place lies only a little way below the surface, though the heat makes it feel much deeper.

It occupies a collapsed seam beneath a plain of black gravel: a compact underground district no larger than a railway terminus, compressed between a floor of newly fractured basalt and a low, load-bearing ceiling. In some chambers a person could touch both walls with outstretched arms. Elsewhere the seam opens abruptly into halls tall enough to hold cranes, chimneys, and narrow stacks of buildings. Everything has been fitted into these openings with almost no wasted space. Pipes cross alleys at shoulder height. Stairways climb around tanks. Brick workshops lean against exposed stone, and newer steel frames have been inserted through older masonry as if the settlement were continually being tightened.

There is no natural horizon. Distance ends in walls, ducts, gates, or the dark undersides of bridges.

The rock is geologically young and still looks broken rather than worn. Faces are sharp, blue-black, and faintly glassy. Where construction exposed the stone, drill marks remain crisp. Rubble has corners keen enough to cut cloth. Even the settlement’s ruins are fresh: buckled rails shine where they have split, and fallen tiles have not yet softened under dust. Erosion here is not a patient smoothing but a sequence of small, sudden failures. A hot crack opens. Condensation enters it. Mineral salts grow overnight. A slab flakes loose with a dry report and lies bright and angular on the floor.

The district is hot at all hours. Heat rises through the basalt from some broad, shallow intrusion below, collecting beneath roofs and in the upper galleries. Metal handrails are unpleasant to hold. Tar bleeds from joints. Old paint has blistered into scales. The narrowest passages trap pockets of air that smell of iron, wet cement, machine oil, and heated stone.

Yet the place is damp.

Moisture comes intermittently from above. After rain on the surface, water finds fresh fractures and threads downward for several hours or several days. It appears first as dark points on the ceiling, then as trembling beads. Soon every pipe and lintel sweats. Gutters wake, cisterns tick, and thin curtains of water descend through ventilation shafts. The rain never forms a true underground river. It gathers instead in dispersed pockets: a trench between rails, a boiler foundation, a tiled reservoir beneath a pumping house, the hollow footprint of a dismantled turbine.

By the end of the wet interval, the lowest alleys may stand ankle-deep. Then the pumps begin to gain on the seepage. Water retreats into grates, leaving islands of grit and oily iridescence. For weeks afterward, the district dries from the top down. Walls pale. Salt feathers emerge from brickwork. Basins contract into warm, dark pools, and finally into rings of mineral crust.

This cycle is the settlement’s principal weather.

The city—or factory, for it is difficult to tell where one ends and the other begins—was built around the management of that water and heat. Its central structure is a vertical pump hall occupying the deepest natural fissure. Six piston pumps stand in paired rows beneath a crane gantry. Their housings are riveted iron, patched with ceramic collars and copper sleeves. Broad flywheels turn slowly behind mesh guards. The machines are not loud. Their bearings are well aligned, and the surrounding rock absorbs vibration. Each stroke produces only a low pressure in the air, less a sound than a sensation in the chest.

From the pump hall, the settlement spreads in tight layers.

The lowest layer contains sumps, settling tanks, sluice gates, and channels tiled in dark green ceramic. Above it are workshops and narrow foundries with furnaces banked but never cold. Higher still, where the ceiling allows, stand blocks of tiny rooms made from soot-red brick. Their doors open onto catwalks rather than streets. Bridges connect roof to roof. Chimneys curve sideways into shared exhaust conduits because there is no sky to receive them.

Nothing is monumental by intention, but density gives the place a kind of grandeur. A valve chamber becomes a chapel of wheels and stems. A transformer gallery, with its glazed partitions and rows of porcelain insulators, resembles an austere civic hall. The main condensation tower rises only thirty meters, yet nearly touches the cavern roof, so it appears immense. Warm vapor moves through its latticework and collects on suspended copper sheets. Droplets descend from sheet to sheet, ringing very softly.

The lights are few and functional. Amber lamps burn in wire cages over junctions, ladders, and machinery. Their reach is short. Beyond each pool of illumination, the district withdraws into reddish gloom. Indicator windows show pinpoints of green or dull white. Occasionally an electrical arc flashes inside a cabinet and casts the entire cavern into hard-edged clarity: pipes, balconies, wet stone, ranks of empty windows. Then darkness closes again before the eye can hold the image.

No animals live here. There are no rats in the workshops, no insects around the lamps, no bats in the exhaust shafts. Even the pools lack larvae. The heat, metal salts, and irregular flooding have excluded them, and the surface plain offers little nearby life from which they might return. The absence is most apparent in the stillness of discarded things. Sacks remain ungnawed. Wires have no nests among them. A puddle may stand for days without a single ripple not made by machinery or falling water.

Vegetation survives only in specialized fragments.

Near the upper seepage cracks, pale stems emerge from pockets of windblown soil. They bear two or three narrow leaves, colorless at first and darkening to green only where a maintenance lamp reaches them. Their roots descend farther than the visible plant rises, threading mortar and following dampness toward the reservoirs. In abandoned gutters there are mats of black-green algae, leathery and thin. During a wet cycle they soften, shine, and release bubbles. During the dry weeks they become indistinguishable from paint.

A few hot cisterns support orange microbial films. These are the most vigorous living things in the district. They spread along the waterline, retreat as the basins evaporate, and leave rust-colored terraces behind. Much of what appears to be corrosion is partly their work. Much of what looks organic is mineral. The distinction changes from chamber to chamber.

The place remains active despite being empty.

Automatic gates respond to rising water. Float switches lift in hidden shafts. Pumps engage one by one. Pressure travels through the district, opening old valves in distant walls. Thermal expansion makes the pipework shift against its brackets. Conveyor chains advance at long intervals, carrying baskets of mineral scale toward a crushing mill. Fans turn behind grilles, too slowly to be seen at first. At the hottest part of the cycle, roof vents open and exhale into the surface gravel, creating brief wavering columns in the air above.

Most of these processes were designed to serve inhabitants or workers, but they have outlasted them. The machinery now maintains the conditions required by the machinery. Pumps protect electrical galleries from flooding. Electrical galleries power fans that cool the pumps. Condensers recover water to feed boiler loops. Boiler loops drive auxiliary turbines when the grid falters. Mineral deposits are scraped from channels, crushed, and used to patch ceramic linings. The district has become a closed municipal reflex.

Its laws are simple and local.

Water must always descend, but the city is permitted to interrupt it.

Heat must always rise, but the city catches and redirects it through ducts.

Nothing is discarded if it can become a brace, a patch, a counterweight, or a layer beneath something newer.

Every open space will eventually acquire a pipe.

Every pipe will eventually sweat.

The quiet is therefore not true silence. It is crowded with small events: condensation tapping on sheet metal; a relay clicking in a locked room; the soft drag of chain over a sprocket; the brief hiss when cool seepage reaches a hot plate. At intervals a pump takes a deeper stroke, and water booms through an underground main. Afterward, there may be ten minutes in which almost nothing can be heard.

Then somewhere overhead, beyond stone and gravel, rain begins.

The first drops arrive underground much later. They gather invisibly in cracks, choosing paths through the fresh rock. Lamps blur behind increasing humidity. Dry channels darken. The orange films loosen in their cisterns. Float mechanisms rise from the bottoms of wells, and one after another the machines acknowledge the returning water.

Across the little buried city, green indicator lights come on.
