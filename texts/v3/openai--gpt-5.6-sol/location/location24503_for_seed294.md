Far above the true floor of the ocean, a drowned plateau spreads through cold, dim water. It is not rock. There is no rock anywhere within reach, nor soil, nor sand in the ordinary sense. The plateau, the escarpments beneath it, and the distant ridges standing along its rim are all constructed from the same pale, close-grained material: slabs fitted to slabs, arches packed beneath terraces, immense hollow blocks locked together without mortar.

Whatever made them left no mechanism behind. There are no doors that open, no lamps, no cables, no inscriptions that still resolve into language. The place is entirely artificial and wholly without technology, as a shell is made but contains no tools.

Age has softened everything.

Edges once sharp enough to divide the water have been rounded into long gray shoulders. Reliefs have been rubbed blank. Towers have become low stacks of convex disks; stairways descend one broad, shallow hollow at a time until they vanish beneath drifts of ceramic grit. In places, erosion has passed through ornament, wall, room, and foundation alike, leaving only parallel ribs where whole districts once stood. Their arrangement can still be sensed across the open plateau: courtyards the size of lakes, radial avenues, enormous circles nested inside larger circles. Yet no single structure survives clearly enough to reveal the plan.

The water is almost black overhead and a deeper black below. A faint blue twilight enters from impossibly far above, strained through leagues of storming sea. It catches only the upper surfaces: the smooth backs of parapets, the lips of circular shafts, the crowns of arches emerging from the plateau like old bones. Everything facing downward disappears.

The plateau is high enough to lie in the path of several deep currents. They arrive from different directions and do not agree.

One current presses steadily from the north, dense with cold. Another surges upward through the hollow architecture, having traveled inside the buried levels. At irregular intervals a broad lateral tide strikes from the west. Their collisions make the landscape turbulent without making it loud. Water folds over itself in vast, nearly silent masses. Columns of suspended white grit rise, flatten, and are torn into streamers. Clear regions become opaque in moments; opaque regions are suddenly swept clean. Through plazas and broken galleries move invisible fronts strong enough to roll loose blocks larger than houses.

Sound does not carry here in any familiar way. There is mostly pressure: a weight felt in the body before it becomes noise. Now and then the fitted plateau gives a deep, restrained knock as some buried chamber collapses or reseats itself. A distant groan travels through the constructed ground. Small pieces tick softly along a channel. Then the place returns to its immense aquatic quiet, though nothing in it has stopped moving.

There are no plants. No fronds wave from the walls, no moss softens the joints, no photosynthetic skin brightens the exposed heights. The dimness is too complete, the surfaces too cold, the currents too merciless. Even the simplest films have difficulty holding to the polished material. Most exposed slabs are bare.

Animal life, however, is everywhere—not as a crowd, but as innumerable scattered presences inhabiting the large intervals.

Pale swimmers travel in loose processions across the plazas. They are narrow as blades and nearly transparent, their internal organs gathered close to the spine to reduce drag. When a current catches them, they turn edgewise and vanish. Round-bodied grazers, though there is nothing here to graze, rasp mineral residue from the seams. They live on bacterial dust brought up from warmer fractures far below, following it through the architecture as livestock might follow seasonal grass.

In the shafts hang colonies of soft, bell-shaped animals. Each is tethered by a single black filament. They open when the upward current arrives, filtering the water, then close all at once when pressure changes. A thousand bells folding in silence can make a chamber appear to blink.

Larger creatures keep to the edges of visibility. Their slow silhouettes pass behind arcades or across the blue-gray distance above the plateau. Some have broad, flexible wings for riding turbulent layers; others are long and jointed, able to anchor themselves between opposing walls. They do not build nests. They occupy hollows already made, entering through windows worn round and leaving by breaches opened over millions of years.

Predators rely less on pursuit than on geography. They wait in the mouths of conduits where currents funnel prey. A dark animal may remain braced there for weeks, indistinguishable from a broken bracket, then unfold into a many-limbed net when a shoal is swept through. Afterward it closes again, and the architecture seems empty.

The plateau’s broadest feature is a depression called, if naming is possible here, the Basin of Steps. Concentric terraces descend for many kilometers toward a central aperture. Every terrace is built, but each has been abraded until it resembles a natural shelf. Water streams continuously toward the center and yet the basin never drains. The aperture exhales as often as it swallows. During an inhalation, animals flee outward and grit pours down the terraces in silver avalanches. During an exhalation, black water erupts from below and rolls upward in a slow mushroom cloud, carrying warmth, nutrients, eyeless crustaceans, and fragments of structures from buried layers.

These fragments show that the plateau is not merely a surface. It is the roof of a constructed depth.

Below lie level upon level of chambers, streets, reservoirs, colonnades, and sealed voids. No natural foundation has ever been found. Where the outer escarpment has broken away, the exposed interior continues downward as repeating courses of masonry until darkness and pressure end the view. The entire highland may be one object, or the worn summit of an object greater than the region itself.

Its material is neither metal nor stone. It is cold to the water and dull under light, faintly pearlescent where newly exposed. It cannot be worked by the animals living here. Broken edges do not corrode, though they are slowly polished. Under sufficient strain it fails without warning, cleaving along planes too precise to be geological. The slabs then tumble, collide, and settle into configurations that currents will refine over another age.

This slow destruction is the place’s only construction now.

At the plateau’s rim, the terraces give way to an abrupt vertical drop. Buttresses descend into darkness, each one broad enough to support a city. Between them are openings like windows, though nothing indicates they were meant for sight. Cold water wells through these openings with tremendous force. Shoals gather nearby to feed, holding position in the current with rapid, barely visible movements. Behind them drift immense filter-feeders shaped like collapsed umbrellas. Their skins give off a weak violet shine, the only native light in the landscape.

The glow does not illuminate so much as disclose. For a moment it reveals a row of arches continuing beyond the animal, a blank wall with thousands of shallow sockets, or a statue-like protrusion worn past all anatomy. Then the creature turns and the image is withdrawn.

Storms far above sometimes reach the plateau. They do not arrive as waves but as changes in the whole body of the sea. The twilight dims. Pressure thickens. Currents reverse through streets and halls. Animals abandon the exposed ground and wedge themselves into seams. Hours later, a descending wall of cold water strikes.

Then the quiet turbulence becomes violent.

Grit darkens the plateau from edge to edge. Ancient blocks lift from their beds. The Basin of Steps boils in both directions at once. Creatures are swept through courtyards, down shafts, over parapets; many die, and many are carried to parts of the structure they could never otherwise reach. Whole colonies migrate in a single involuntary event. A weakened tower may come apart soundlessly, its rounded courses separating and sailing outward before falling through the murk.

Afterward the water clears slowly. The architecture has changed, though seldom in ways the eye can measure. A passage is gone. A sealed chamber has opened. One avenue lies buried, while another, untouched for ages, is exposed as a pale line stretching toward the horizon.

Animals emerge and redistribute themselves. Scavengers arrive first, delicate and numerous. Tethered colonies fasten to new surfaces. Predators choose new constrictions. Life occupies the alteration without healing it.

There is no greenery to suggest renewal, no ruin overgrown into softness. The plateau remains naked, fabricated, and old beyond memory. Its surfaces are continuously erased, but its sheer depth keeps presenting more surface to be erased. It has reached a kind of final state that is not stillness: an equilibrium between endurance and destruction.

Everything moves.

Nothing progresses.

Across the high drowned plain, dim currents race through empty avenues while scattered living shapes lean into them, feeding, hiding, drifting, and returning. Beneath those avenues lie older avenues, and beneath those, others—constructed layers descending toward a foundation that may never have existed.
