The Foundry Canopy stands on a high plateau where the mountains have been worn almost level. Their former summits survive only as broad swells beneath the vegetation, like knuckles under a tightly drawn cloth. There is no exposed stone. There is scarcely such a thing as exposure here. Every surface has been occupied, plated over, rooted through, or replaced.

From a distance, the plateau resembles an immense black orchard. Up close, it is difficult to say whether it is an orchard, a city, or a single organism assembled in the shape of an industrial district.

The air is hot and extremely dry. Heat does not descend from a visible sun; the sky is usually hidden by interlocked crowns and by a ceiling of soot-colored spore tissue. Warmth rises from below, conducted upward through the old mountain mass and distributed by root mains buried under the plateau. In the deeper galleries it is furnace-hot. On the upper terraces, slow currents of air carry the odor of resin, scorched cellulose, metal salts, and the sharp medicinal sweetness of ruptured stems.

There is no water cycle. Nothing drips. Nothing runs.

The plants use a dense, non-aqueous sap: an oily ionic fluid that remains stable at temperatures which would cook ordinary tissue. It glows faintly when exposed, not brightly enough to illuminate a room, but enough to trace damage in hair-thin amber lines. This fluid circulates through pressure vessels, capillaries, and great ribbed trunks that function as pipelines. Where a conduit splits, the sap flashes into vapor and hardens into translucent varnish. The wound seals almost at once.

Everything has been made, although no maker remains distinct from what was made.

Floors are compressed root laminate, laid in overlapping courses and polished by centuries of thermal contraction. Walls rise as woven trunks trained around ceramic matrices. Towers are hollow columns of lignin and black ironwood, reinforced with spiral bands grown from mineral-fed cambium. Bridges are bundled tendons. Chimneys are flower organs. Vaults are seed cases expanded to architectural scale.

Even the bolts are alive. Their threaded bodies slowly thicken inside their sockets, maintaining pressure as structures settle.

The plateau is packed so thoroughly that open ground exists only as a theoretical layer far below. The oldest avenues have narrowed to fissures between swollen buildings. Staircases close behind the traveler as their steps put out new rootlets. Courtyards fill with pale scaffolding shoots, which harden, interlace, and become new workshops. A vacant chamber may remain vacant for a few hours before exploratory tendrils arrive through seams in the walls.

Yet the landscape feels open rather than enclosed, because it extends without obvious boundary. Through gaps in the upper canopy one sees district beyond district: low black terraces, branching stacks, fan-shaped cooling leaves, and distant processional ridges crowded with vertical gardens. The plateau’s far edge is lost in darkness and heat shimmer. Occasionally a whole quarter lifts its flowering chimneys at once, briefly describing the curvature of the ancient upland.

There are no animals. No insects work the blossoms, no birds occupy the rafters, no worms turn the substrate. Pollination is mechanical. Stamens mounted on articulated stalks strike neighboring flowers with measured taps. Seed capsules travel along conveyor vines. Some roll on woody bearings; others are handed from tendril to tendril across the canopy.

The absence of animals gives the place an uncanny cleanliness. No gnawing, no droppings, no nests, no eyes watching from holes. Fallen matter is not scavenged. It is gripped by fine feeder roots, pulled apart, dissolved in caustic sap, and returned to circulation.

Most processes are quiet.

The vast machines do not roar. They flex. They tighten. They release pressure through soft exhalations. Deep in the structures, one hears the occasional wooden concussion of a piston changing direction, followed by a long silence. Leaves designed as radiators tick as they cool. Seed bearings whisper along dry tracks. Electrical charge passes through conductive veins with a faint insect-like hiss—the only insect sound in a world without insects.

At intervals, a pressure bloom opens somewhere beyond sight. The sound is a low, padded thump, and warm air moves through the streets carrying yellow dust.

The darkness is nearly complete. The canopy admits only rare red slits from the sky, and even these are harvested by layers of black leaves before they reach the lower city. Most growth is powered by heat, chemical gradients, and electricity drawn from the mineral core. Photosynthetic tissues survive only on the highest vanes, where they spread as matte surfaces darker than charcoal.

Below, perception depends on other lights: the dull orange pulse of sap behind translucent bark; violet corona around charged thorns; greenish fermentation lamps grown into alcoves. None cast far. Architecture appears in fragments—a rib, a wet-looking cable, the underside of a leaf broad as a roof—while everything behind it merges into blackness.

The plateau is ancient enough to have reached a final, eroded form, but the living works upon it are never still. Their turbulence is not the turbulence of weather. It is internal and organized only in retrospect.

Districts are continuously dismantled and remade. A factory that spent three thousand years producing heat-resistant seed shells may abruptly flower, tear open its roof, and convert its assembly chambers into a forest of pollen towers. Root pressure can buckle an avenue upward into an arch. Towers lean together, fuse, and become walls. Entire neighborhoods are digested when their function is no longer needed.

These changes occur with astonishing speed and very little sound. A facade wrinkles. Its windows seal. Structural ribs slide beneath the surface like fingers under fabric. By the following dark cycle, the building has become a grove of low turbines turning in the hot air.

There are storms, although there is no rain. Pressure differences between the deep thermal shafts and the upper canopy produce sudden horizontal winds. They race through the crowded districts, lifting sheets of dry pollen and abrasive mineral spores. The storm fronts are almost invisible until they reach a charged quarter, where every particle ignites with muted blue static.

Branches collide. Cooling leaves turn inside out. Long transmission vines lash against towers, throwing sparks. The landscape rearranges its surfaces to meet the gust: walls become porous, streets clamp shut, and whole groves kneel flat in coordinated waves.

Afterward, the plateau is altered. Not damaged—altered. Broken trunks root where they fall. Severed cables flower. Crushed machinery divides into smaller machines.

Nothing here distinguishes repair from reproduction.

The oldest structures lie toward the center, on the broadest part of the worn summit. They are squat, dense forms almost entirely buried beneath later growth. Their masonry is petrified plant tissue cast around metal frameworks whose alloys have softened and migrated over immense time. Erosion has rounded every accessible corner. Hot dust has polished handrails no hand ever touched.

Inside these central works, the vegetal architecture becomes more formal. Pillars stand in grids. Conduits run in perfect parallel. Vast chambers contain dormant production lines, their tools held above empty belts by curled stems. Instructions are embossed in repeating patterns of leaf scars. Some appear to be maintenance records; others may be liturgy or merely the natural grammar by which the structures grew.

At the very center is a vertical shaft descending through the entire plateau. Its walls are packed with root bundles under colossal tension. They pulse slowly, transferring heat and dissolved minerals from the mountain’s interior to every district above.

There is no visible bottom.

Around the shaft, the city continuously invents new organs. Most fail. They are absorbed within hours. Others spread outward and alter the whole plateau: a new kind of hinge, a tougher conductive bark, a flower capable of storing static charge through the long dark. Innovation occurs without intention, production without consumers, architecture without inhabitants.

The landscape is both abandoned and fully occupied.

Its governing rule seems simple: no space may remain unused, no material may remain dead, and no form may persist merely because it existed yesterday. Yet beneath the constant conversion lies a deeper stability. The plateau cannot grow outward indefinitely; the surrounding heights have been erased by age, and the hot lowlands beyond are chemically barren. So it grows inward, upward, and through itself, rebuilding the same finite territory in countless configurations.

From a high cooling vane, when the canopy briefly parts, one can see the whole region stir.

Far-off stacks bend in sequence. Terraces split and unfold. Black orchards retract into factory vaults as new towers rise among them. Dust storms move across the plateau like pale scars. Beneath everything, steady as a buried heart, the pressure mains contract.

Then the canopy closes, the last red light disappears, and the Foundry returns to its characteristic near-silence: hot, crowded, endlessly productive, and entirely alone.
