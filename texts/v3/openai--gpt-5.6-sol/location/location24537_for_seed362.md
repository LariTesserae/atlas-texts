The plain has no ground beneath it.

What appears from a distance to be a low, pale continent is the upper surface of an immense constructed body: decks of rootwork, compressed stems, ceramic trellises, buried conduits, and load-bearing trunks laid one upon another until their combined thickness is measured in kilometers. Nothing here is raw geology. The oldest “stone” is fired plant ash. The dust is powdered bark, insulation fiber, pollen glass, and the chalky residue of exhausted batteries. Even the horizon is manufactured.

The land remains almost perfectly low. It rises in broad swells and shallow sags rather than hills, as though the whole continent were a sheet stretched over slow machinery. In some places the surface descends into dim maintenance basins hundreds of kilometers wide. Elsewhere it lifts onto long, ribbed platforms whose inclines are so gradual that travelers do not notice them until the stars change angle.

Vegetation covers all of it, but there are no forests in the familiar sense. There is no separation between plant, soil, architecture, and machine.

Pillar-plants hold up the upper decks. Their trunks are square in section, grown around steel reinforcing cages, with regular seams where one generation was grafted onto the next. Their crowns spread into luminous canopies: millions of thin leaves plated with reflective salts, all turned toward the hard white sky. Cable vines pass between the pillars in taut bundles, carrying sugars, electrical current, chemical messages, and structural tension. Low mats of felted growth seal cracks. Fan-shaped radiator fronds release waste heat. Black-rooted accumulator groves draw charge from the continent’s buried grid.

Every surface lives, or once lived, or is waiting to be made living again.

There is no rain. Water does not run beneath the decks or circulate in the trunks. The organisms of the plain were designed for a world where liquid water is unavailable and, in open air, almost impossible. Their cells use a cold ionic sap: a clear, bitter mixture of ammonia salts, fluorinated oils, and mobile mineral complexes. It crawls through narrow vessels without evaporating, sometimes thick as resin, sometimes nearly gaseous. When a stem is cut, it does not bleed. The wound briefly shines with silver vapor, then closes under a crust of self-grown enamel.

Moisture, in the old sense, is a contaminant. Ancient warning marks remain on many access doors: the symbol for water enclosed in a red diamond. Some are so weathered that the danger looks ceremonial. Deep in sealed sectors, droplets are said to survive in archival jars. The surrounding vegetation has grown away from those chambers, leaving circular clearings no maintenance system can explain.

The sky is bright without warmth. Across the whole plain, towering lamps stand at intervals beyond easy perspective, their heads lost in a high veil of pale atmospheric dust. They do not imitate a sun. They produce an even, shadow-thinning radiance, cool and relentless, strongest in the wavelengths the canopy can use. When one lamp fails, darkness does not fall. Instead, a gray wedge thousands of square kilometers across slowly appears, and the plants within it begin a coordinated migration toward its edges. Root anchors release. Pillars lean. Entire districts inch sideways over decades.

From above, these migrations resemble weather.

Other weather moves through the canopy. Pollen fronts advance as brilliant yellow walls, though the grains are heavy enough that they rarely rise more than a few meters. Electrostatic tides sweep over the reflective leaves, turning them in coordinated waves. At certain intervals, the plain exhales. Billions of vents open at once and release cold metabolic gases, producing a low, ground-hugging haze that pours into the shallow basins. It has no water in it, yet it curls like fog around stems and factory towers.

The factories are difficult to distinguish from the vegetation because they have been cultivated into it.

Older works have smokestacks of layered horn and porcelain. Their assembly halls are vaulted seedpods reinforced by ribs thick enough to contain elevators. Conveyor belts are braided tendrils whose surfaces ripple forward through muscular growth. Presses close with the slow force of tree roots splitting masonry. Refinery orchards take in dust, atmospheric nitrogen, discarded structural fiber, and ore recovered from the lower decks. They produce replacement beams, light-sensitive membranes, insulating cork, conductor filaments, and dormant organs packed in hexagonal crates.

Nothing moves quickly. That is partly why the place seems silent.

A continental mechanism is always operating, but its cycles are measured in minutes, seasons, or centuries. Gears rotate too slowly to hear. Pressure valves open with a soft inhalation. Cable vines tighten one after another, making faint dry clicks that pass toward the horizon like cooling metal. Leaves rub together with the whisper of paper. In a grafting yard, an automated knife may spend an entire day descending through a trunk, its edge producing a barely audible crystalline hiss.

There are no engines roaring, no chains hammering, no crowds. Industry here has learned the pace of growth.

Only the lamp towers sometimes make a sound large enough to fill the landscape. During changes in illumination, their internal fields resonate through the decks as a pure tone at the edge of hearing. Dust lifts from every surface. The pillar-plants bow fractionally toward the source. Then the tone ceases, leaving a silence so complete that small structural strains can be heard traveling upward from buried levels.

The plain is ancient enough to have its own erosion.

The edges of leaves are frosted by centuries of airborne grit. Old trunks have been hollowed into fluted columns by pollen storms. Ceramic pathways are worn into shallow troughs by the dragging roots of migratory districts. Abandoned machinery rounds itself beneath layers of lichen-like circuitry, each generation digesting a few microns of metal and replacing them with green-black tissue. Nothing decays cleanly. Materials pass back and forth between manufacture and growth until their origin is impossible to determine.

Here and there stand remnants of earlier geometries: staircases that climb sideways, walls built according to a discarded direction of gravity, rows of doors only a handspan apart. The vegetation incorporates these without comment. Vines thread the sideways stairs. Trunks grow through the narrow doors. Reflective leaves orient toward lamps that no longer exist.

Reality is least reliable near the maintenance basins.

Their interiors appear shallow from the rim, but descending ladders can continue for days. Objects dropped into them sometimes land before they are released. Rows of identical pylons become more numerous when counted and fewer when photographed. A traveler may cross a field of low silver fronds and find that the horizon has exchanged places with the ground: the lamp towers extend downward into brightness, while beneath one’s feet lies a cool white sky, held rigid by root mesh.

The native systems treat these events as ordinary load problems. When distance stretches, cable vines grow longer. When a chamber develops a second interior, partition plants seal one copy and ventilate the other. If gravity turns by ninety degrees, trunks bend over several generations and establish a new vertical. Factories continue production even when their outputs emerge decades before their inputs arrive. Finished components are tagged with instructions to be stored until the materials used to make them can be harvested.

Time is another resource in the supply chain.

Fauna are rare, perhaps because there is little here that plants have not already learned to do. Pollination is performed by charge gradients. Seeds travel on conveyor vines or fire themselves from pneumatic pods. Dead tissue is dismantled by mobile rootlets. Even rapid responses belong to specialized leaves that snap shut around damage, sparks, or foreign chemistry.

Still, a few animals persist.

Glass-backed crawlers live in the warmth beneath active conduits. They are no larger than a finger and have many delicate legs, each ending in a ceramic hook. They eat electrical leakage, leaving dark, branching trails where current has been drained from living cables. Their bodies are almost transparent except for a bright pulse moving continuously around the rim of the shell.

Far rarer are the tall frame-walkers. From a distance they resemble loose arrangements of sticks striding between the pillar groves. They have no visible organs and cast shadows in directions unrelated to the lamps. They stop beside damaged structures and remain motionless for years. When they leave, the structure is often repaired, though according to a design not used anywhere else on the plain.

The vegetation avoids their footprints.

Near the continent’s center lies the Production Crown, though it is neither elevated nor circular. It is an expanse where the density of living architecture becomes almost absolute. Trunks stand close enough to touch. Canopies interlock in dozens of layers. Factories grow inside factories, each chamber lined with smaller chambers, each surface cultivating tools. The bright sky is reduced to vertical threads. Air moves through planned channels, carrying powdered minerals and coded scents.

There, the plain is said to manufacture not objects but laws.

One district produces weight. Another extrudes lengths of direction, wound onto enormous reels. A cold orchard buds sealed intervals of time. In the calibration fields, workers—or plants shaped by an inherited memory of workers—test new versions of distance by walking between two white posts. Failed distances are cut free and sent below.

This may explain the inconsistencies spreading outward from the center. Or the Crown may have been built to repair them. No surviving map agrees on which came first.

Beyond it, the continent continues for so long that its slight curvature becomes a kind of enclosure. Brightness lies over everything. Pale leaves turn. Root presses close. Silent mills shape the bodies of future forests. Beneath the living decks, older decks bear their weight, and beneath those are still older constructions, compacted, mineralized, and forgotten—not natural ground, but the accumulated decisions of an ecosystem that has never encountered anything outside itself.

The place is not overgrown.

It is grown completely.
