At the summit of an immense, weathered escarpment stands a town no larger than a cathedral close.

The mountain beneath it is so old that it no longer resembles a mountain in the vigorous sense. There are no fresh ridges, no splintered peaks, no pale scars of recent collapse. Everything breakable has already broken. Wind and time have reduced the summit to a low oval of dark stone bounded on every side by precipices. From the town’s edge, the land falls almost vertically through layers of brown, violet, and iron-black rock, then disappears into a permanent depth-haze. The plains below are rarely visible. When they are, they look flat and faded, as if seen on an ancient map.

The height should make the place cold, but the plateau remains warm. Its rock stores the sun, and slow breaths of heated air rise through deep fissures. Even at night the paving holds a mild, animal warmth. Houses are built over these cracks, not to capture any dramatic furnace, but to borrow the steady heat seeping from the mountain’s interior.

There is no water. No spring, cistern, gutter, well, or seasonal torrent marks the town. The oldest channels carved into the streets are wind channels, made to carry dust away rather than rain. Drinking vessels are absent from kitchens. Basins are used for sorting seeds, grinding pigment, or holding hot stones. The inhabitants obtain moisture indirectly from thick-leaved plants, sour fruits, animal blood, and the faint condensation gathered inside sealed clay chambers at night. Even this last substance is treated less as water than as medicine: a few cloudy drops scraped from a cool stone lid.

Despite the dryness, the town is green.

Its vegetation does not spread in lawns or leafy canopies. It crowds upward through every available pocket. Pillar succulents rise between houses, some as tall as towers, their ridged skins dark green in shade and chalk-white where exposed. Rosettes fill roof gardens. Thorn trees, twisted into compact forms by centuries of wind, lean across alleys and mesh their branches with opposing walls. Curtains of gray-green air plants hang beneath balconies, feeding on dust, breath, and the thin dampness of dawn. Lichens varnish the oldest masonry in amber, black, and dull orange.

The result is lush not in softness, but in density. Every plant is deliberate, armored, and close-packed. Nothing wastes space. Roots share cracks with foundations. Vines as tough as rope are trained over doorways, their seed pods rattling against plaster. Edible pads, medicinal bulbs, resin shrubs, dye lichens, and fragrant night flowers are layered into tiny vertical gardens. The town smells of warm stone, bitter sap, crushed herbs, smoke, hides, and the faint mineral odor rising from the fissures.

There is scarcely an empty surface.

Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder around a central court. They are made from the summit itself: fitted slabs of basalt, sun-dried clay, thornwood beams, woven reedlike stems, and plaster colored with ochre. Most structures are only one or two rooms across, but they rise in uneven tiers. Roofs serve as streets. Stairs climb over kitchens and sleeping chambers. Narrow bridges cross alleys too tight for two people to pass. A household may occupy one dark room at ground level, a shaded platform above it, and three planted ledges reached by ladder.

Metal is rare. Knives are chipped glassy stone or hammered from small pieces of soft copper traded up the mountain. Door hinges are leather. Pins are bone. Mortars have been worn smooth by generations of grinding. Heavy work is done with wedges, levers, fiber ropes, and patient hands. Nothing is quickly replaced. Objects become polished, repaired, shortened, drilled through, bound together, and finally incorporated into walls.

The town’s dimness comes partly from its density. Sunlight reaches the lower lanes only in narrow blades. Awnings of woven fiber stretch from roof to roof, protecting plants and people from the hard high-altitude glare. Dust in the surrounding air turns the sky pale bronze, while the streets remain brown-green and cool-looking beneath the thorns. Interiors are darker still. Lamps burn resin or animal fat, making small yellow pools under soot-black ceilings.

The central court is the settlement’s only broad space, though broad here means perhaps thirty paces across. An ancient, flattened outcrop rises in its middle. It is too hard to carve and too old to remove, so the town has grown around it. Generations have polished its upper surface with feet, tools, sleeping mats, market baskets, and funeral litters. At certain hours it serves as a market. At others it becomes a workshop, council floor, threshing stone, or communal bed in the warm season.

The place is loud.

Birds occupy every roofline. Small black seed-eaters quarrel in the gardens; pale cliff swifts pour upward from the abyss in screaming flocks. Heavy-beaked scavengers pace the parapets and knock bones against the stone to open them. Lizards rustle through dry leaves. Beetles click beneath paving slabs. Tethered goats with narrow bodies and astonishing balance complain from ledges outside the walls. Their bells are made of wood, shell, and fired clay, each producing a different hollow note.

Human sound is woven through this animal clamor: pounding mortars, scraping stone, creaking rope, hand drums used to signal from one tier to another, and voices made sharp by the need to carry through crowded passages. Because the mountain falls away on all sides, sound has nowhere nearby to settle. It rebounds from the close walls, then escapes over the cliffs. A single dropped bowl may be heard rattling through half the town before silence swallows it below.

Yet little truly changes.

The summit’s boundaries are absolute. There is no room for another district, no soft ground to clear, no nearby forest to cut. New buildings can only replace old ones or rise upon them. Paths follow routes established before anyone’s genealogy begins. The thorn trees are pruned into forms inherited from earlier gardeners. Households trade rooms as families grow and contract, but the town’s overall shape remains fixed.

Even erosion has become slow. The mountain has already surrendered almost everything the wind could easily take. Its surviving stone is dense and rounded, with edges softened to the final curves of great age. Dust still leaves the summit, grain by grain. Walls lean by fractions. Steps deepen beneath bare feet. Cliff nests shift as cracks widen over centuries. These are the town’s major transformations.

At the outer parapet, the settlement ends abruptly. Gardens press against the last wall; goats lie in its shade; children peer through gaps between ancient stones. Beyond is only air, immeasurable distance, and the warm haze covering the lower world.

From below, if anyone can see it at all, the town appears less like something built upon the mountain than the mountain’s last intricate crust: a dark, crowded crown of walls, spines, wings, smoke, and stubborn green life, occupying the one small place where the height briefly becomes level.
