### The High Pelagic

There is no shore.

The ocean hangs at altitude, a planetary mantle of water suspended above a dim, unreachable interior. It is not held in a basin. Gravity weakens and changes direction through its depth, so that “down” is only locally true. In some regions the water falls toward the buried planet; elsewhere it streams sideways along invisible gradients or rises in slow continental upwellings. Entire seas pass over and through one another without mixing, divided not by walls but by differences in pressure, salinity, electrical charge, and stranger properties for which there are no familiar measurements.

The inhabited layer—if anything without animals can be called inhabited—is high above the planet’s core. Here the water is cool and lucid, generally the temperature of shaded stone. Light comes through it with the ordinary breadth of daylight: white, soft gold, occasional blue-gray dimness. There is no visible sun. Illumination enters from every distant direction, bent around the world by the ocean’s outer layers and redistributed through suspended molecular lattices. Shadows exist, but they are faint and mobile. A solitary rock may cast six pale shadows that rotate independently as storms alter the optical density of the surrounding sea.

Distances are difficult to judge. Visibility can extend for hundreds of kilometers through volumes containing almost nothing. A black ridge appears close enough to touch and remains that way for days of travel. Beyond it, further silhouettes repeat: raw mountain-spines, detached shelves, columns of newly condensed mineral matter. They are not ruins and have never been worked. Their sharpness is geological infancy. Faces meet at clean angles; pinnacles taper into translucent edges; broad plains of basaltic glass retain the smoothness of material only recently cooled or assembled. Erosion has barely begun.

The world is still making its ground.

Minerals precipitate directly from the water in immense, silent acts of formation. At first they appear as stains: a slight darkening across a volume large enough to contain a terrestrial country. The stain organizes into threads, the threads into planes. Within hours, a reeflike escarpment may emerge—not a biological reef, but a lace of silicates, nickel foam, carbon crystal, and porous stone. It grows without foundations because there is no dependable bottom. Some formations descend toward the core; others spread horizontally along pressure boundaries; others become hollow mountains floating in equilibrium.

They are exceedingly sparse. Between one mass and the next lies open water on a scale normally reserved for interplanetary space. The emptiness gives each presence an almost ceremonial isolation. One black needle stands alone in a luminous blue reach. One pale shelf extends farther than the eye can follow, yet is no thicker than a leaf at its edge. A knot of green filaments clings to a mineral seam, the only visible growth in a hundred kilometers.

Vegetation is rare and simple. It does not form forests or meadows. Thin phototrophic ribbons anchor themselves to newly made stone, usually where light and dissolved metals overlap. Their colors are subdued: bottle green, rust violet, smoke-yellow. They grow in slow radial fans, each blade only a few cells thick. Because there are no grazers, no insects, no fish, they need neither thorns nor poisons nor rapid reproduction. Their chief enemy is the instability of the world itself. A pressure shear can peel an ancient colony from its ledge and stretch it into a green line tens of kilometers long before dissolving it.

Some plants never attach. They remain as widely dispersed veils, too sparse to cloud the water, opening their microscopic surfaces when light strengthens and folding them when electrical storms pass. They are the nearest thing the ocean has to weather-sensitive leaves. No creature swims among them. Nothing browses, hunts, nests, calls, or leaves tracks. There are no shells in the sediment, no bones in the stone, no eyes reflecting from the dark. The absence of animal life is absolute enough to feel like a law rather than an accident.

Yet the ocean is loud.

The sound does not come from living things. It comes from the sea’s enormous internal weather. Pressure fronts collide like moving cliffs. Currents traveling in opposing gravitational directions shear past each other, producing sustained roars that can continue for months. Mineral shelves vibrate under the flow, sounding deep tones through the water. Newly formed ridges crack as their crystal structures settle. The fractures ring out with clear metallic reports, followed by long descending murmurs.

At times the whole ocean gives a single low concussion. It arrives first as a vibration in the stone, then as a thickening of the water, then as sound: a note too large to locate. Sparse plants close along their entire length. Loose grains lift from ledges. Far away, a mountain may be assembling—or ceasing to exist.

Storms here have no sky. They are volumes within volumes: rotating continents of altered water, transparent until they pass through a field of light and reveal their spiraling boundaries. Some spin around vertical axes; others tumble end over end. Lightning travels inside them as branching tubes of white fire, moving slowly enough to watch. The discharge does not simply flash and vanish. It writes transient conductive pathways into the water, and currents follow those pathways for hours, turning the storm into a vast temporary machine.

This is where the world’s impossible sophistication becomes apparent. There are no buildings, cables, vessels, gates, or instruments. Nothing looks manufactured. Nevertheless, the water performs operations beyond any ordinary chemistry.

Its molecules can briefly arrange themselves into coherent structures spanning kilometers. Currents store patterns. Pressure waves are corrected as they travel, as though the ocean dislikes corrupted signals. Certain mineral formations act as processors without having been designed as objects: they sort isotopes, redirect heat, and alter local gravity by fractions too precise to be geological coincidence. A broken crystal plain may reconstruct not into its former shape, but into a shape better adapted to the forces that broke it.

Whether this is technology is uncertain. It may be that technology here has passed beyond artifacts and become a condition of matter. The ocean does not contain machines. It has learned machine-like possibilities.

No central intelligence is evident. There are no messages in recognizable form, no symbols, no repeating beacon. Instead, responsiveness appears everywhere and intention nowhere. A storm approaching a fragile new ridge may divide around it—but another storm will strike a similar ridge and pulverize it. A current may carry dissolved minerals across half the world to repair a fracture, while beside it a stable plateau is disassembled grain by grain for no visible purpose. The system experiments at planetary scale, rapidly and without sentiment.

Its principal rule seems to be that permanence is postponed.

Ground condenses, hardens, and begins to acquire the first signs of erosion. Then gravity rolls sideways. An abyss turns into an upwelling. A plateau folds through itself and becomes a curtain of stone dust. Sediment seldom has time to become old. Canyons begin as hairline cracks and are erased before they deepen. The landscape remains raw not because nothing happens, but because too much happens for age to accumulate.

There are quiet intervals. In the wake of a storm, enormous reaches become almost motionless. Light steadies. The water clears until distance loses meaning. A solitary floating escarpment can be seen far below—or far above—its charcoal surfaces edged with young crystal. Sparse ribbons of vegetation open against it. Mineral snow rises past them in slow, glittering sheets.

Then a pressure boundary arrives from beyond sight.

The escarpment begins to sing. Green filaments stream flat against the rock. The surrounding light bends into long white corridors. Across the empty ocean, newborn ranges darken as currents carry their shadows forward faster than the stone itself.

The roar follows.

And the high sea resumes making a world that has not yet decided what land is.
