The high country begins before there is any clear ascent. The air thins, the colors lose warmth, and the land opens until distance stops behaving as a measure. What first appears to be a plain is an uplifted world of benches, shallow basins, and weathered escarpments, extending far enough to make its own cloud systems. Ranges rise within it like islands, yet even their summits barely interrupt the long, cold horizon.

Everything is old in shape. There are no fresh cliffs, no raw fractures bright with new stone. The exposed rock has been rounded, fluted, and darkened by ages of frost and abrasive mist. Canyons are broad-bottomed and settled, their walls leaning back in immense terraces. Loose debris has already found its angle of rest. Boulders sit half-submerged in gravel as if the plateau has grown around them. Even where erosion continues, it does so without violence: a grain loosens under ice, a film of silt slips downslope, a river changes its bed by the width of a hand over a century.

The stone is mostly black and iron-gray, with greenish seams that shine when wet. Pale mineral crusts spread across sheltered flats. In places, frost has sorted the ground into polygons many kilometers wide, their borders marked by low ridges of shattered slate. From above they would resemble the plates of some dormant animal.

Water is present but rarely abundant. It beads on stone, gathers beneath overhangs, and condenses in the furrows of the land. Thin rain sometimes passes beneath the cloud deck, too weak to make a general wetness. Instead it selects surfaces. One slope darkens while the next remains dry. Hollows hold shallow, steel-colored pools with rims of salt and black mud. A few rivers cross the plateau, but they are narrow compared with the channels that contain them, wandering through valleys carved by wetter ages. They seldom freeze solid. Heat rising from deep mineral strata keeps a dark thread moving beneath shelves of ice.

There is little direct sunlight. The sky is a permanent architecture of high ash-colored cloud, luminous in patches but never bright. Day is distinguishable from night chiefly by degrees of gray. At noon, shadows are blurred stains. At night, the cloud base sometimes takes on a faint violet glow from electrical weather far beyond the visible horizon.

Vegetation survives close to the ground. There are mats of black lichen, rust-red moss around seepages, and wiry plants whose leaves resemble folded metal shavings. They grow yards apart, leaving most of the soil bare. Their roots descend farther than their stems rise, following cracks into warmer stone. Some remain dormant for decades and unfold within hours of rain, spreading translucent fans to catch water before the wind can take it.

At first the plateau seems empty.

Then one notices that almost every dark streak on the distant flats is moving.

The animals are beyond counting, though rarely crowded in a single place. They are distributed through the vastness: in the rivers, under the gravel, along the escarpments, inside the slow clouds. The apparent emptiness is only the spacing required by an ecology of immense numbers.

Small six-legged grazers move between lichen patches in files hundreds of kilometers long. Their bodies are low and insulated, armored in overlapping plates dulled by mineral dust. They do not chew. They press their mouthparts to stone and draw away microscopic films of life, leaving clean marks like strokes of an eraser. Behind them come pale scavengers that digest the grazers’ shed armor. Behind those come creatures that feed on the scavengers’ heat.

Over the open plains travel the great carriers. Each is tall enough that its back rises into the lower mist, though its legs are so slender it can be mistaken for a set of distant pillars. Long curtains of fiber hang from its underside. These collect condensation and channel it into expandable organs along the ribs. The carriers walk ceaselessly between cold basins and geothermal ground, transporting water, dissolved minerals, spores, eggs, and entire colonies of smaller animals. Their migrations are the nearest thing the plateau has to rivers that keep their course.

They move with extraordinary slowness. A raised foot may remain suspended for minutes before descending. Mud displaced by one step can take a season to settle.

No cry accompanies them. There are no roars, no wingbeats, no choruses. The air is too thin to reward loudness, and predation has favored stillness. Communication passes through pressure in the ground, changes in body heat, electrical pulses, and bands of color too dim for human eyes. Even the vast aerial populations travel silently. They are bladder-bodied drifters with almost no weight, steering by altering the charge along their skins. Whole migrations can fill the cloud layer without producing more than a faint darkening overhead.

The world’s industry belongs to these animals.

Nothing here has been built. There are no walls, roads, towers, tools, or ruins. Yet the biological processes operate at industrial scale. Herds refine metal from mud within furnace-hot stomachs. River colonies separate salts through miles of living membrane. Burrowing swarms ventilate the plateau’s deep fractures, drawing cold air downward and expelling warm, mineral-rich vapor through fields of pores. Electrical species gather in shallow lakes, linking bodies until they form temporary grids. During certain seasons, blue current passes silently from pool to pool across hundreds of miles.

These systems are not deliberate in any human sense. No animal commands them. The refinery-herds consume stone because stone is food. The lake grids form because the creatures breed when charged. The carriers haul water because their young can develop only in distant warm basins. Nevertheless, the result is a mechanized ecology: extraction, transport, concentration, discharge, recycling. Ore veins have been thinned by digestion. Hillsides have been sifted into uniform grades of gravel. Certain valleys are coated in glassy slag excreted by generations of heat-feeding animals.

The plateau has been transformed, but never constructed.

Its largest machines are alive and temporary.

Beneath the open ground, the fauna is even more abundant. The seemingly solid plateau contains deep layers of porous rock warmed by pressure and radioactivity. Within them extends a cold, slow biosphere of soft-bodied miners, filterers, and bacterial furnaces. They occupy cracks too narrow to see. Their combined metabolism raises the ground temperature by a fraction of a degree and powers the constant exhalation of mist. Sometimes an entire basin breathes: fog rises evenly from millions of tiny vents, hangs waist-high for several days, then sinks back into the gravel.

This underground abundance explains the surface’s restraint. Almost nothing is wasted upward. Leaves are small because nutrients belong below. Surface predators hunt infrequently because prey is long-lived and widely spaced. A carcass does not rot in the open. Within hours, the soil beneath it becomes porous, and pale threads lift the remains down piece by piece. By the next dim morning, only a shallow depression and a trace of warmth remain.

The dominant sensation is still quiet.

Snow falls without wind. Condensation slides down the sides of the carriers without splashing. Rivers move under ice with only the softest internal pressure, felt through the soles rather than heard. A nearby animal may reveal itself by the gradual interruption of mist, or by frost melting in an oval around an unseen body.

On rare occasions, the silence acquires a single note. Far below, one of the deep living conduits contracts, and the plateau gives off a low vibration, more bodily than audible. Pools tremble. Loose grains align themselves. The great carriers halt with one foot raised. A million small grazers flatten against the rock.

Then the vibration passes. Movement resumes.

The seasons are measured less by temperature than by charge and moisture. For years, the western basins may remain dry and dormant while condensation accumulates in the east. Then the upper winds shift. Clouds drag their undersides across the plateau, combed open by the high ridges. Rain falls lightly for months. Rivers widen by inches. Dormant plants expose their dark fans, and animals buried since the previous wet cycle rise through the softened ground.

Reproduction follows in slow waves. Eggs that resemble pebbles crack after fifty or sixty winters. Drifters descend from the clouds and settle over whole watersheds, their translucent bodies covering the stone like dim glass. The electrical lakes kindle. Great carriers converge from all directions, still separated by miles but so numerous that the horizon seems to walk.

Nothing happens quickly enough to look catastrophic. Even death advances as a gradient. A drying basin may take centuries to lose its fauna. A species shifts north over dozens of generations. A river is captured by another valley one frost-fracture at a time. The land’s balances are not peaceful, but they are patient.

From a high escarpment, one can see the plateau in several of its truths at once: bare, crowded, dry, damp, natural, industrial. Gray plains stretch beneath a ceiling of cloud. Black rivers bend through old valleys. Sparse vegetation stains the seepages. Far-off carriers move like pieces of weather. Underfoot, unseen populations sort stone, circulate heat, and pass silent currents through the crust.

There is no untouched emptiness here. The emptiness is part of the living system: the distance between bodies, the long interval between rains, the unused air around each low plant, the centuries allowed for a path to become a valley.

The landscape does not announce what it is doing. It has no smoke, no hammering, no lights burning against the dimness. Its engines are metabolism and migration. Its factories disperse when the season changes. Its power lines swim away.

And across the ancient high plain, beneath a sky that never quite becomes day, the whole continent works in silence.
