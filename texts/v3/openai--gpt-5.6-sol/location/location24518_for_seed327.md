### The Bell-Cirque

The place is no larger than a small hamlet, yet it hangs at an elevation where whole countries appear to lie below it.

It occupies a shallow cirque cut into the shoulder of a black mountain: a crooked room of stone, perhaps half a mile across, enclosed on three sides by weather-rounded cliffs and open on the fourth to the sky. Beyond that opening there is no neighboring slope, no descending meadow, no reassuring sequence of foothills. The ground simply ends. Clouds move through the gulf beneath, revealing and concealing distant ranges like dark islands under white water.

Despite the height, the cirque is not barren. Moisture gathers here with peculiar reliability. Wind drives vapor against the mountain, and the cold stone combs water from the air. Every wall weeps. Droplets bead along seams in the rock, join into silver threads, and descend through cushions of moss. Rain is frequent but seldom heavy; more often the landscape seems to condense from within. After sunset, water appears on leaves even under a clear sky.

The basin floor is an old arrangement of shelves, hollows, and low ribs of stone. Nothing looks freshly broken. The cliffs have been softened into durable forms: blunt ledges, shallow overhangs, pillars with rounded shoulders. Fallen blocks are sunk deep into soil and vegetation, their surfaces furred with gray-green lichens. Erosion continues, but slowly and almost ceremonially. Water follows channels worn over centuries. Frost loosens only a handful of scales each year. The mountain has settled into its face.

Near the rear wall lies a tarn scarcely wider than a house. It is dark, cold, and fed from below by water passing through fractured stone. Its level rises after long clouds and falls during bright, windy weeks, exposing a rim of copper-colored gravel. One edge drains into a stream narrow enough to step across. The stream crosses the cirque in many indecisive bends, disappears under a mat of roots, emerges beside a boulder, and finally spills over the outer brink.

The fall is invisible from above. One sees only water flattening into transparency on the final slab and then vanishing into open air. In strong wind, much of it returns as spray. It blows backward across the cirque, sometimes falling on the same moss twice before any portion escapes the mountain.

Vegetation grows thickly wherever it can grow at all. There are few upright trees. The wind has made height expensive. Instead, life lies close to the ground in dense, interlocking forms: dwarf willow with red stems, creeping juniper, leathery herbs, sedges, mosses deep enough to retain a bootprint, and broad patches of small blue flowers that open only when direct light reaches them. Sheltered beneath the cliffs are crooked groves of mountain birch. Their trunks are no taller than a person, though their twisted grain and ringed bark suggest great age.

The lushness is concentrated. A ledge may carry a garden of orchids, ferns, and dripping moss, while the stone a few feet beyond it is nearly bare. The cirque is made of such islands, each rich and sharply bounded, separated by stretches of naked rock and open air. Seen from above, it would resemble a handful of green cloth scraps laid on a dark floor.

The emptiness is most apparent vertically. There is little between the basin and the sky. No forest canopy, no towers, no smoke, no wheeling dust. Above the low vegetation rises an immense volume of clear, cool air. A person standing at the tarn may feel less enclosed by the cliffs than exposed beneath the enormous vacancy overhead. Even the clouds usually pass below.

Yet the cirque is loud.

Wind strikes the outer lip and divides among the stone ribs with a sustained, organlike note. Cracks in the cliff whistle at different pitches. The stream chatters over gravel, the hidden drainage beneath the moss makes hollow gulping sounds, and the waterfall beyond the brink gives off a continuous low thunder that seems to come from underfoot. When clouds push uphill, their arrival is announced by water beginning to tick from thousands of leaves.

Animals add the rest.

Small cliff birds nest in nearly every protected seam. They launch in sudden companies, crossing the basin so quickly that their shadows seem to arrive before them. Thick-billed choughs patrol the turf, turning stones and calling harshly into the wind. Long-winged hunters hover over the brink, nearly motionless, then fold and disappear downward.

The wet ground supports clouds of tiny insects during the mildest hours. Beetles shelter beneath slate flakes. Pale moths cling to the undersides of leaves. In the tarn, black-bodied larvae graze on submerged moss, and narrow silver fish rise when rain dimples the surface. Their presence is difficult to explain: there is no visible waterway connecting the tarn to anywhere else.

Hares live among the dwarf birches, changing from mottled brown to dirty white as the seasons turn. Stocky mountain ungulates visit by routes that seem impossible from within the cirque. They arrive at dawn, wet to the knees, crop the sedges, and leave over shelves that appear to end in sheer air. Their hooves have made only a few paths. The paths are ancient, narrow, and polished into the turf.

Predators follow. A small gray cat inhabits the fallen stones beneath the western wall. It is rarely seen except as a tail withdrawing between rocks, but its prints surround the tarn after rain. Larger tracks sometimes appear near the outer edge—broad, canine, and solitary. They never cross the center of the basin. Local tradition, if the abandoned signs here can be trusted, held that such animals belonged to the drop and should not be followed.

Human marks are sparse.

Three low shelters stand under separate overhangs. They are built from dry-laid stone, roofed with turf over bent birch poles. None has a door. Within one are a soot-blackened hearth, a cracked clay cup, and hooks carved from horn. Another contains a sleeping platform woven from willow switches. The third has almost collapsed; moss fills its interior, and birds nest in the wall.

Near the stream, a line of flat stones forms the remains of a working place. There are fragments of chipped volcanic glass, bone needles, a stone-headed mallet, and a shallow basin used for soaking hides or roots. A few iron rivets lie among them, heavily rusted and clearly precious once, but most things were made from what the cirque supplied: stone, wood, horn, fiber, leather.

At the brink stand seven narrow cairns. They are no higher than the knee and widely spaced, each built around an upright shard of pale rock. In rain, the shards darken except for a thin band near their tops. No symbols have been cut into them. The wind has polished the stones more thoroughly than hands could have done.

The cirque has simple rules, though they are learned by watching rather than written anywhere.

Nothing tall survives outside the shelter of stone.

Water never takes the shortest path.

Animals may enter from any direction, but people have only one safe approach: a steep cleft behind the eastern birches, marked by old pegs hammered into cracks. The pegs are wood hardened by fire, and many have been replaced over generations. Miss the cleft, and every other apparent path eventually arrives at the brink.

Sound travels unpredictably. A voice spoken beside the tarn may return from the rear cliff, faint but intact, several breaths later. A shout cast outward may be heard again from below. During certain winds, the waterfall’s thunder stops entirely within the basin even though its spray continues to rise. At such times the animals become still. Birds settle. Hares press themselves into the grass. Even the insects seem to disappear.

Then the wind changes, the whole cirque breaks into noise again, and movement resumes at once.

Light here is ordinary in its brightness but startling in its reach. When the sky clears, sunlight fills the basin without haze, revealing every water bead and hair of moss. Shadows are hard-edged. The wet cliffs shine as though varnished. Toward evening, the valleys below darken long before the cirque does, and for an hour the little basin remains suspended in gold above a continent already blue with night.

Afterward, cold gathers quickly. Water stiffens in the smallest channels. The birches click in the wind. Animals retreat into stone, roots, and turf. The tarn continues to breathe mist.

By morning the ice is often gone, the stream has shifted another finger’s width through its moss, and the place is loud with wings again: a small green room at the edge of an immeasurable emptiness, old enough to seem finished, yet never for a moment still.
