Beneath a shelf of pale desert stone lies a chamber no wider than a village square. Its entrance is not a cave mouth but a vertical crack, warm air breathing through it with the smell of hot dust, bruised herbs, and struck flint. The crack narrows twice before opening suddenly above the hollow. From there, the whole place can almost be held in one gaze—though the dimness makes its boundaries uncertain.

The chamber is newly made and strangely unworn. Its walls are split into black, glassy planes, each edge still sharp enough to cut skin. Fans of fractured mineral lie where they first fell. Nothing has rounded them; no runoff has carved channels, no dripping roof has raised columns, no buried stream has polished the floor. There is no water here—not in pools, not in mist, not even as dampness under stones. Powder remains powder. Dust touched by a hand keeps the whorls of the fingers indefinitely.

The warmth comes from the rock itself. It is steady, close, and bodily: the warmth beneath a sleeping animal, without pulse or fluctuation. The air never cools. There is no dawn beneath the surface and no seasonal turning, only the same mineral heat passing through everything at the same rate.

Across the floor stand scattered gardens.

They are lush in themselves but separated by broad spaces of bare stone. Each garden rises from a crack or shallow basin as a crowded knot of fronds, cords, fans, and inflated translucent lobes. From a distance they resemble cycads, fungi, and coral assembled according to the memory of someone who had seen none of them clearly. Their colors are difficult in the dim light: soot-green, copper-black, violet-brown, with veins that glow the muted red of banked charcoal.

These are not plants in the surface sense. They do not drink. Their roots are dry braids forced deep into fresh fractures, where they strip sulfur, carbon, salts, and metallic vapors from the stone. Broad leaves hold thin films of waxy solvent within sealed skins. The films circulate without evaporation, carrying mineral food from root to crown. If a leaf is cut, it does not bleed sap; a soft amber resin bulges from the wound, hardens at once, and closes it like glass.

Nothing here wilts. Nothing grows in any way the eye can catch.

The oldest-looking thickets are no larger than the youngest-looking ones. New shoots remain forever half-unrolled. Seed cases hang perpetually at the instant before opening. Fallen fronds do not rot, though pale insects occupy them. The chamber has the appearance of exuberant life arrested at full tension—not dead, not dormant, but held exactly in its chosen gesture.

Between the gardens, the floor is vacant and severe. Flat plates of dark stone form open courts dusted with white mineral flour. A few needle-like crystals have thrust upward through them, fresh and translucent. Narrow fissures cross the courts in ruler-straight lines. Crossing one produces a faint pressure in the teeth, as if the two sides of the chamber disagree about which way is down.

The fauna are abundant, though the emptiness initially conceals them.

Small six-legged grazers crowd the undersides of leaves. Their shells are parchment-thin and marked with concentric eyespots. Long-bodied animals, no thicker than cords, hang between branches without webs, supported by the chamber’s weak electrical fields. Palm-sized burrowers sit in cracks with their wedge-shaped heads exposed. There are colonies of warm, velvety creatures that could be mistaken for fruit until all of them open their mouths together.

Higher up, pale gliders cling to the ceiling. Their wings never beat. They slide in slow, identical circuits through the chamber, riding convection that never varies. Each follows the same path: down past the red-veined crowns, across the bare central court, around the black spur of rock, and up again. They do not collide. They do not deviate. Seen long enough, they cease to resemble animals traveling and begin to resemble a single event repeated in many places.

The noise is immense.

Every shell-backed grazer scrapes the leaves with a mineral tooth. Every hanging cord-creature clicks its body segments. The burrowers strike the walls of their holes with blunt forelimbs, and the ceiling gliders produce a low, steady booming as air crosses chambers in their wings. The gardens themselves contribute: resinous trunks contract against the heat with wooden knocks, while hollow seed pods whistle through pinhole vents.

Because the cavern is small, all these sounds strike the hard walls and return. There is no soft soil to absorb them, no water to blur them, no breeze to carry them away. The chorus becomes architectural. It fills particular parts of the chamber like invisible pillars. In one spot the clicking is so concentrated that it numbs the lips. In another, the gliders’ drone cancels itself almost perfectly, creating a circle of silence no wider than a stool.

The sounds never quicken, weaken, or stop. There are no alarm calls. There are no mating seasons. No predator breaks the established rhythm. A narrow-jawed hunter crouches eternally among the roots, occasionally taking a grazer at precisely spaced intervals. The grazer population does not diminish. Somewhere, hidden among resin folds, an equal number emerge—not as offspring visibly born, but as though the chamber has corrected its count.

This is the governing strangeness of the hollow: it permits motion but forbids change.

Creatures eat, yet gardens are not reduced. Resin is harvested, yet the sealed cuts remain neither more nor less numerous. Dust rises beneath a foot and falls back into its former arrangement. A crystal accidentally broken is found whole when no one is touching it, while the fragment remains in the breaker’s hand until carried beyond the entrance crack. Fire burns here only in fixed shapes. A torch flame leans always toward the eastern wall, regardless of how the torch is turned, and consumes its fuel so slowly that generations could mistake the same stick for an heirloom.

Scattered among the thickets are signs that people discovered the chamber long ago.

The constructions are small and cautious. Three low shelters stand against the wall, built from fitted shards of black stone and roofed with overlapping mineral leaves sealed in amber resin. None is large enough to stand in. A ladder of bone and twisted fiber descends from the entrance ledge, its rungs polished by hands but not worn thin. Near the circle of silence sits a work block with shallow hollows pecked into its surface. Flakes of flint remain arranged beside it by size, along with scrapers made from grazer shell, awls of split bone, and bowls pressed from resin and ash.

There is no metal except for a few soft copper beads hammered cold and threaded onto a cord. Their makers possessed no machinery beyond leverage, percussion, grinding, and heat. Yet they understood the chamber’s rules well enough to exploit them.

On the wall above the shelters, finger marks record practical knowledge:

A spiral means a safe path through the hanging animals.

A row of dots marks the interval between the hunter’s feedings.

A hand with two thumbs warns against the straight fissures.

A closed eye indicates the silent circle.

Other signs are harder to interpret. One shows a person entering the chamber with an empty basket and leaving with the same basket apparently full, while an identical person remains behind. Another depicts seven gliders above a human figure, although only six can ever be counted in the air.

No inhabitants are present now. Or none are clearly present. In the rearmost shelter lies a rolled sleeping mat, a stone knife, and a bundle of dry aromatic fronds. They are warm. The dust around them bears one fresh footprint. It never fades, but neither does it lead anywhere.

The chamber’s dim light comes mostly from the life within it. Veins pulse under leaves without altering brightness. Eye markings on the grazers shine like dull stars. The ceiling carries a dusting of luminous bacteria, each colony isolated from the next, so that the rock resembles a sparse and unfamiliar sky. There are enough points to see by but never enough to feel exposed.

At the center of the hollow, where the bare courts meet, a single plant grows alone.

It is lower than a person’s knee and shaped like an open hand. Five thick leaves surround an empty space. Unlike the other vegetation, it gives no light and makes no sound. Animals circle it but never touch it. The stone immediately around its roots is not cracked, though every other surface in the chamber bears the violence of recent formation.

Placed in its central hollow is a small clay cup.

The cup is unfired, yet hard. It contains a glossy black liquid that is not water, does not evaporate, and does not cling to a dipped finger. Its surface remains perfectly level even when the cup is tilted. Looking into it reveals neither reflection nor depth, but the chamber as it might appear after something has changed: gardens swollen together across the empty courts, shelters collapsed into soil, walls blunted by age, and the air silent at last.

When the cup is set down, the vision is always the same.

Around it, the loud, warm, waterless hollow continues exactly as before. Gliders follow their appointed circuits. Teeth scrape leaves. Seed pods whistle. Resin hardens in open cuts. The fresh black walls retain every edge.

Nothing advances. Nothing recedes.

The place is lush, crowded with lives, and forever at the first instant of becoming.
