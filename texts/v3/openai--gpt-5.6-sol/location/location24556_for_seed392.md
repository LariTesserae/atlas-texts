The land is a shallow, brilliant basin assembled entirely by hand.

There is no bedrock beneath it, no ancestral soil, no buried geological dark. Wherever the ground opens—at the edge of a pool, beneath an upturned paving slab, in the narrow cracks made for roots—it reveals further construction: courses of pale fired clay, woven palm fiber, shell-lime mortar, and tightly fitted blocks of greenish glass. The deepest wells eventually strike the undersides of other courtyards. Those courtyards, impossibly, remain beneath the basin and open to the same white sky.

At first the place resembles a low tropical city after rain. Broad aprons of cream-colored tile extend between scattered groves. Water lies in shallow joints, reflecting a light too bright to look into directly. Raised walkways cross beds of glossy vegetation. Here and there stand roofless pavilions, cistern towers, stairs, retaining walls, and long colonnades with no buildings attached to them. Everything is widely spaced. One may walk for an hour across warm pavement and encounter only a shade frame, a cluster of fruit trees, and a single stair descending into water.

Yet the basin is not ruined. Its edges are sharp. Steps preserve the tiny ridges left by stone chisels; clay bricks still bear fingerprints. No moss softens a corner unless a deliberate groove was cut to receive moss. There are no wind-rounded surfaces, no old collapses, no accumulated dust. The whole region has the freshness of something completed this morning, though nothing is ever seen being built.

The heat is intense but even. It does not rise toward noon or relax toward evening. There is no noon and no evening. The white sky has no visible sun, although every object casts a short, hard-edged shadow toward the west. Under leaves the shade is green and humid. On exposed plazas the tiles become almost painful to touch, but never quite hot enough to blister skin. Condensation beads continuously on the shaded sides of walls. It gathers, swells, and hangs there without falling.

Water is everywhere near the surface, but seldom deep. It runs in narrow channels shaped from blue ceramic, passes beneath footbridges, and spreads into marsh courts filled with reeds and broad-leaved plants. Rectangular pools occupy former-looking foundations, though they were plainly designed as pools from the beginning. Their bottoms are mosaics of black fish, white spirals, and human hands. Actual fish swim above the pictured fish, matching each image so exactly that it becomes difficult to say which layer is alive.

Rain seems to have just ended. The paving is wet, roofs drip, leaves carry bright droplets, and every gutter holds a trembling thread of runoff. But the last drop never falls. The wetness neither dries nor increases. Channels flow without lowering their cisterns. Pools accept water without overflowing. If a cup is filled, the level in the nearest channel remains unchanged; if the cup is emptied, the poured water spreads for a moment and then becomes part of the fixed sheen already covering the pavement.

The vegetation is luxuriant where planting beds have been made and absent everywhere else. Trees crowd narrow brick enclosures, their crowns overflowing into broad islands of shade. Vines ascend cord trellises and plaster walls, carefully avoiding doors and inscriptions. Giant leaves rise from the marsh courts, waxy and ribbed, some green, some blue-white, some bearing a repeating pattern like woven cloth. Orchards produce heavy orange fruit, split pods, purple figs, and translucent gourds full of cool water.

There are also plants that reveal the basin’s artifice more plainly. A grove near the center has trunks braided from living stems around clay armatures. When cut, the trunks show concentric rings of wood alternating with thin bands of painted ceramic. Certain flowers are folded from insect wings yet continue to open and close. A pale creeper roots only in shadows, treating darkness as if it were soil. Reeds in the western pools pass through the tiled bottom and emerge upside down from ceilings in the lower courtyards.

There is rich life, though the landscape remains strangely sparse to the eye. Bright lizards rest on parapets far apart, each occupying its own stretch of wall. Long-legged marsh birds stand among the channels. Small monkeys move through the orchards, but never in groups larger than three. Beetles travel the grout lines as if the paving were a map laid down for them. Butterflies cross the open plazas one at a time, at regular intervals, their shadows arriving a heartbeat before they do.

The animals make almost no sound.

Birds open their beaks in elaborate calls, throat feathers trembling, but only a soft click is heard, like two wet stones touching. Frogs swell and pulse in the cisterns without croaking. Monkeys land on tile without slap or scrape. Even insects are silent except for an occasional dry ticking from inside the walls. The loudest persistent noise is the faint movement of water, and even that seems less like flowing than like someone quietly repeating the idea of flowing in another room.

Human work is visible everywhere, but no human settlement coheres. Tools are simple: chipped-stone scrapers, wooden mallets, shell adzes, bone needles, clay plumb weights. They lie neatly beside unfinished-looking tasks. A basket rests beside a mound of mortar. A stone blade sits in the notch of a half-cut beam. Fiber ropes are looped around blocks several times larger than houses. No pulley, wheel, crane, furnace, or advanced mechanism appears anywhere. Nevertheless, the blocks are fitted with impossible precision, and some bridges cross distances that bundled timber should not survive.

The architecture obeys methods rather than engineering. A lintel remains aloft because three handprints have been pressed into its underside. A wall is watertight if its builder’s name has been scratched into every seventh brick. Roofs stand on reed columns as long as no one counts them. When counted, there are always two more columns than were visible from outside.

Most structures are open to the sky: platforms, arcades, stepped gardens, ceremonial ramps leading nowhere. Buildings are less like shelters than instructions embodied in space. One courtyard teaches water to turn corners. Another teaches roots to divide around buried masonry. A circular plaza appears to instruct shadows where to fall. Near the basin’s eastern margin, thousands of low clay ridges persuade the bright atmosphere to condense. Each ridge wears an identical line of droplets, each droplet holding a minute inverted image of the entire region.

There are no broad roads. Instead, footpaths appear as subtle differences in glaze. Pale yellow tiles lead between cisterns; red tiles go toward orchards; black tiles lead to places beneath the ground. Following black tiles always involves descending, even when the path visibly slopes upward. After several hours one arrives at a marsh under the open sky, surrounded by the same basin, but all shadows now point east. Travelers who turn back return by a route they do not remember taking.

Distance is similarly unreliable but never chaotic. The place has stable impossibilities. A tower glimpsed across a pool is always thirty paces away, regardless of how long one walks toward it. Two neighboring groves may be separated by a day’s journey in one direction and a few minutes in the other. The basin’s outer wall remains visible from almost everywhere: a low white line behind the vegetation. No one has reached it.

Nothing ages. Fruit may be eaten, branches cut, tiles broken, and animals killed, but the alteration cannot spread through the place. The bitten fruit is whole again when no mouth touches it. A severed branch remains severed while watched; look away and both branch and tree resume their original forms. Broken tile fragments can be carried for some distance, but gradually become increasingly accurate models of the tile from which they came, shrinking until they are tiny glazed replicas resting in the palm. Dead animals do not decay. They become flat images in the nearest mosaic, and elsewhere the same animal steps from painted water into real water.

This is not experienced as repetition. Events do occur, but they have no power to establish a past. Footprints persist only beneath the feet that make them. Memory remains in the traveler, while the landscape refuses evidence. Thus every pavilion looks newly abandoned, every meal newly prepared, every tool just set down. The wet clay beside the cistern bears fresh finger marks, but touching one reveals that it is hard-fired ceramic painted to resemble wet clay. Beneath the paint is another layer depicting the same finger marks. No one has found the last layer.

At the basin’s center is a wide empty field paved with unglazed white brick. It is too bright for vegetation, though moisture shines in every seam. Scattered across it are waist-high frames made from branches lashed with fiber. Each frame supports nothing. Seen from certain angles, however, the frames become doorways opening onto dense green country: jungles, estuaries, flooded forests beneath enormous suns. Birds sometimes pass through these apparent openings and vanish. Their shadows remain on the white field, perched and preening without bodies.

The frames cast no shadows of their own.

Beyond them rises the basin’s only hill, if it can be called a hill: a broad, low accumulation of hand-molded bricks, each stamped with a picture of a mountain. Water weeps from the seams and runs down its many shallow terraces. Saplings grow from square sockets. At the summit is a circular depression full of reeds, frogs, and warm black water. Looking into it does not show a reflection. It shows the underside of the basin—rafters, lashings, ceramic vaults, roots threaded through designed gaps—and beneath those supports, another white sky.

The prevailing impression is not of machinery or magic, but of craft extended past all reasonable limits. The world has been woven, mortared, carved, planted, and instructed. Its makers possessed poor tools and perfect rules. They built water that does not run out, heat that does not vary, animals whose abundance never becomes crowding, and a wet season held forever in the instant after rain.

Nothing here is deteriorating. Nothing is approaching completion. The basin is young without having begun, lush without succession, inhabited without noise. It waits in hard white light, freshly made at every moment, while its silent creatures move through the same inexhaustible brightness.
