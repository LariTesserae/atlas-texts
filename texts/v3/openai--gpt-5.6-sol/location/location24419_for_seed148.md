The place occupies a shallow depression no wider than a small town, though its boundaries are difficult to cross and impossible to map consistently. From outside, it resembles a low, black-green mound rising from a plain of pale clay. From within, it is almost entirely hollow.

Vegetation forms the walls, streets, machinery, and ground. Enormous stems rise at long intervals, leaving cathedral-sized chambers between them. Their bark is smooth and cold, more like cast iron than wood, and carries branching seams of dim phosphorescence. High overhead, leaves overlap in thousands of layers. They admit no sky. Even at noon the interior remains in a diluted darkness, lit by fungal numerals, the occasional blue spark of an insect, and the dull red glow of industrial processes taking place inside transparent roots.

There is very little solid earth. What seems to be pavement is a braided mat of living rhizomes, firm beneath the foot and faintly responsive to weight. It tightens around fractures before they can widen. In quieter districts, the rhizomes have drawn themselves into regular blocks, curbs, gutters, and raised platforms. Elsewhere they imitate rails, stairs, pipes, and empty foundations. Some of these structures appear designed; others may simply have learned the pressure-patterns left by a former population.

The city has almost no inhabitants in any ordinary sense. Its avenues are broad and vacant. Towers stand far apart, each grown from a single trunk whose interior has been partitioned into floors. Windows occur as rows of transparent blisters. Doorways open when approached, though not always into the same room. A person can look through one entrance and see a narrow service corridor, step across the threshold, and arrive in a chamber larger than the entire mound.

This is not considered a contradiction here. Size is one of the things the vegetation metabolizes.

The ground is low and newly forming. Beneath the root pavement lies raw gray sediment, soft as potter’s clay and almost without stones. The basin has not yet decided where its channels will be. After rain, water runs along one gutter for an hour, then divides and begins flowing uphill through another. Thin streams appear in abandoned stairwells or pass from wall to wall without falling. They leave behind delicate mineral ribs: the first suggestions of banks, ledges, caves, and ravines. Erosion is still embryonic. Nothing has been worn old. Every groove looks experimental.

Moisture comes intermittently. Rain seldom reaches the streets from above; the canopy consumes most of it. Instead, condensation gathers inside the towers. Drops swell on the ceilings of upper rooms and descend through the building’s vascular shafts. At irregular intervals the whole city sweats. Walls darken. Handrails become slick. Roots open shallow cisterns in the pavement, filling them with clear, metallic-tasting water. These pools may persist for days, crowded with life, and then vanish at once—not by evaporation, but because the basin has inhaled.

The air is cool and carries the smells of bruised leaves, wet iron, cold ash, and the faint sweetness of fruit kept too long in storage. No wind moves at street level. Instead, pressure travels through the city in slow pulses. Leaves turn over together though they are hidden far above. Hanging cables sway. Doors along an avenue open one after another, as if something enormous but invisible were passing.

The industrial quarter lies near the lowest point, although “lowest” changes over the course of a week. There, plant and machine cannot be separated. Cylindrical trunks are girdled by copper bands swallowed halfway into bark. Pistons of polished lignin move inside chambers of sap. Root turbines turn in underground trickles, generating a weak current that travels through veins and old wires alike. Conveyor systems carry seeds, bones, mineral nodules, empty cocoons, and sealed packets of pollen between buildings.

No operators are visible.

The factories continue in near silence. Their belts are soft. Their gears are grown rather than cut, with cartilage teeth that flex instead of striking. Pressure valves exhale only once every few hours. A vast press closes around layers of leaves and produces thin black sheets resembling slate. In another building, pale roots sort rainwater into separate reservoirs according to the memories dissolved in it. Somewhere deeper, a machine repeatedly assembles the skeleton of an animal that has never existed, disassembles it, and returns the pieces to labeled drawers.

Electricity appears as slow green lightning beneath the bark. Lamps are rare and often illuminate only things that are absent: the outline of a missing bridge, the shadow of an unbuilt staircase, workers standing at stations where no bodies stand.

Despite the empty streets, fauna is abundant. Much of it lives in the margins of perception.

Moths as large as hands sleep against the trunks, their folded wings indistinguishable from rusted plaques. Blind birds nest in ventilation ducts and navigate by listening to the flow of sap. Their calls are too low to hear directly; one notices them when puddles tremble. Small translucent mammals travel inside the walls, visible for a moment as pale organs moving behind thin bark. Beetles drag lengths of copper wire into geometric heaps, apparently mistaking them for twigs.

In the cisterns, whenever water arrives, entire aquatic ecosystems emerge within minutes. Silver larvae hatch already mature. Flat, many-legged fish graze the reflections from the surface, leaving the water beneath unchanged but the mirrored world stripped bare. Long-necked amphibians climb out during condensation cycles and fasten their eggs to warm electrical junctions. When the pools drain, these animals do not die. They become dry markings on the pavement and wait there until wetted again.

Predation is frequent but almost soundless. Vine-cats—lean quadrupeds composed of braided runners—wait beside game trails with flowers closed along their spines. They pursue glass-bodied rodents through the stations and warehouses. When they catch one, predator and prey often root together, remaining as a single shrub for several days before separating and resuming their former roles. Flocks of needle-winged insects hunt the vine-cats’ shadows, which are considered a distinct species and do not always remain attached to their owners.

This is one reason portable lights are dangerous. A moving beam may create wildlife.

The city’s quiet is not peaceful. It is the quiet of innumerable processes engineered to avoid collision. Leaves unfold without rustling. Animals communicate by changing color, temperature, or local gravity. Pipes carry vibrations away into deep roots. Even violent events—a tower splitting, a predator striking, a whole district turning ninety degrees—occur with little more than a damp click or a sigh through the canopy.

Certain sounds therefore acquire disproportionate importance. A single dripping pipe can be heard across three plazas. The scrape of a shoe may cause windows to cloud. If a bell rings, every animal freezes, though no bells have been found.

The local rules are inconsistent but not arbitrary. The place obeys patterns more faithfully than causes.

Anything repeated four times becomes infrastructure. Walk the same route four mornings in succession and a path will grow beneath you, complete with drainage and dim guide-lights. Knock four times on a wall and, by evening, there will be a door. Leave an object in the same place four nights and roots will build a room around it. This is believed to be how much of the city arose: not from plans, but from habits made architectural.

Conversely, anything left unused begins to lose reality. An abandoned stair first gathers moss, then ceases to reach its upper landing. A vacant apartment becomes shallow. A forgotten street may flatten into a painted line along another street’s wall. Whole neighborhoods survive only because animals continue to cross them.

Directions are biological rather than geometric. North is where unopened flowers face. Down is wherever water is currently expected. Distances are measured by fatigue, not length, and can contract if one is being pursued. Maps germinate when buried but produce different cities depending on the soil.

Time is distributed unevenly. In the cool storage towers, fruit ripens backward toward blossom. Beneath the factories, rust appears before metal is installed. Rain sometimes condenses into drops that contain tomorrow’s insect tracks. The oldest structures look unfinished, while new growth bears scars and obsolete signage. There are construction sites where cranes made from jointed branches patiently lower beams into the past.

At the basin’s center is the largest empty space: a circular plaza perhaps sixty paces across. Nothing grows from its floor. No animal crosses it. The canopy opens above, but instead of sky there is another plaza, inverted and equally dark, hanging overhead. Occasionally condensation falls upward from one to the other.

Around the circle stand transit shelters, ticket machines embedded in trunks, and rails that enter the surrounding vegetation. No train has been seen arriving. Yet at long intervals the rails become warm, the plants draw back, and the entire ecosystem prepares for a departure. Moths wake. Predators hide. Factory lines halt. Thousands of unseen creatures crowd the darkness without making a sound.

Then a pressure passes through the plaza.

For a moment, every doorway opens onto the same cool, rain-smelling field beyond the city—a field under a gray morning sky, with no vegetation except a single new shoot pushing through raw clay.

The doors close. The machinery resumes. Water chooses new channels. The shoot is later found somewhere inside, already thickening into a tower, its roots arranging the first foundations of another empty district.
