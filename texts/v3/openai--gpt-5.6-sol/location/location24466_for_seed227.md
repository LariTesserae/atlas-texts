The place is called the Drum Hollow, though from the open moor there is almost nothing to suggest a hollow exists.

The land lies low and cold, a broad waste of peat and pale grass only a little above the sea. Its surface has been worn into modest, settled shapes: rounded banks, shallow runnels, long shelves of exposed stone. Nothing is sharp except where frost has lifted a flake from the bedrock. The moor seems empty. Here and there a juniper crouches close to the ground, or a row of stones shows through the heather like old knuckles. In rain, water gathers briefly in black pools; in dry weather these contract to damp seams under the moss.

Near the foot of a long gravel ridge, a slit opens between two slabs. It is narrow enough to pass without notice and low enough that anyone entering must turn sideways and bow. Cold air breathes from it. The walls are polished by generations of shoulders, animal hides, and dripping water.

Beyond the slit, the earth opens into a single chamber.

It is not large. A person can cross it in a few dozen paces, though the darkness makes it feel much greater. The roof rises out of sight at the center and slopes down around the edges, forming ledges and shallow alcoves. The floor is mostly bare stone: old gray basalt, cupped and smoothed, with broad hollows where water once worked patiently. Some hollows hold a finger’s depth of clear water. Others are lined with silt as fine as soot. Moisture arrives by seepage and condensation rather than by any permanent stream. Droplets collect on the roof, swell slowly, and fall at long intervals.

Around these small sources of dampness, vegetation grows with improbable richness. Moss pads the lower walls in deep green folds. Liverworts spread over the stone like overlapping scales. Pale ferns unfurl from cracks, their stems bent toward the entrance’s meager light. There are mats of creeping sorrel, tiny mushrooms with translucent caps, and long veils of algae beneath the steadiest drips. The growth is lush but confined. A hand’s breadth beyond the damp margins, the rock is naked again.

The chamber is therefore both green and nearly empty: gardens no larger than cloaks scattered around a volume of darkness.

Its defining presence is sound.

Far beyond the visible walls, the sea strikes a buried shelf of rock. The water itself never enters the hollow. It moves through deeper fissures and caverns, forcing air along passages too narrow for a person. Each offshore swell drives a pressure wave into the chamber. The result is not the recognizable crash of surf but a continuous, bodily thunder.

At times it is a low concussion that makes the floor tremble under the knees. At others it rises into a vast rushing roar, as if a river were falling just behind every wall. Small stones quiver. Water in the floor-cups wrinkles without being touched. Loose fern fronds shudder in the still air. Speech becomes useless except with a mouth held close to an ear, and even then words arrive more as vibration than sound.

The roar is so complete that smaller noises disappear inside it. A drip cannot be heard. Footsteps cannot be heard. A dropped tool vanishes without a clatter. Someone may move through the chamber unseen and entirely silent, not because they make no noise but because the hollow is already full.

The thunder changes slowly with tide, weather, and season. During a calm low tide it retreats to a distant grinding hum. With winter storms it thickens until breathing seems to require effort. Those who know the place can read the sea from the pitch of the stone. A high, strained note means waves are striking the outer cliffs directly. A pulsing beat means water has filled one of the lower tunnels. When the sound suddenly diminishes during a storm, the knowledgeable leave at once: a blockage has formed somewhere below, and when it breaks, the returning pressure can knock a standing person to the ground.

There are signs that people have used the hollow for a very long time, though no one has ever made it comfortable.

Three low walls divide the driest edge into shallow bays. They are built without mortar from flat stones chosen to fit the curve of the chamber. Smoke has blackened the roof above one alcove, but no fire burns well here; the air’s hidden movements pull flames sideways and sometimes extinguish them. In another bay stands a rack of antler and driftwood lashed together with sinew. Its cords have been replaced many times. Stone lamps rest in niches, each a simple dish with a groove for a moss wick. Their weak flames illuminate hands and faces but never the whole room.

Bone pegs have been driven into cracks. Nets hang from some of them, made from twisted grass and inner bark. There are chipped stone blades, shell scrapers, wooden wedges, and heavy mauls for splitting driftwood. Metal is almost absent. One green-stained copper hook, too soft and precious for ordinary use, lies wrapped in hide beneath a marked slab.

No path connects these things clearly. Each artifact sits alone in the dark with several paces of bare rock around it, so the chamber resembles an abandoned storehouse whose contents have been reduced to a few essentials. Even when occupied, it never feels inhabited by more than traces.

People come here for tasks that benefit from cold and concealment. Meat can be hung in the rear clefts, where the stone stays near winter temperature through most of the year. Roots and berries are stored in covered pits. Fishers twist cord in the dimness when the weather above is dangerous. Certain families leave bundles of seed, dry tinder, and spare tools here against lean seasons. No one sleeps deeply in the hollow. The sound enters dreams, and there is always the unease that another person could approach unheard.

The animals are few. Tiny pale moths cling to the roof beyond the reach of lamplight. Black beetles feed in old plant litter beneath the racks. A small population of cave spiders survives in the quieter cracks, building dense, untidy webs that tremble continuously with the thunder. In autumn, a solitary fox may squeeze through the entrance and nose among the storage pits. It rarely stays. Bats do not roost here; the noise and pressure confuse them.

Once or twice each winter, a seal finds its way into the lower flooded passages. Its calls rise through the stone, stretched and distorted, almost indistinguishable from the chamber’s own groaning. There is nothing supernatural in this, though those hearing it for the first time often believe otherwise.

Life in the Drum Hollow follows strict practical rules.

Nothing is placed directly beneath a roof crack, because frost can loosen stones without warning. No lamp is left unattended, because a shift in air pressure may scatter sparks into the hanging moss. Food is sealed in hide or clay against condensation. Tools are tied to their owner’s belt; anything dropped in darkness may be impossible to locate by sound. Visitors touch the wall as they move, not merely to guide themselves but to feel changes in vibration.

Most important, no one trusts the apparent dryness of the floor. The sea remains hidden, but it is present in every tremor. During exceptional tides, clear water wells up silently through the lowest cups. It rises no faster than the growth of a shadow, sometimes taking half a day to cover a hand’s width of stone. Then, just as patiently, it drains away, leaving ribbons of kelp and salt at the edge of the moss.

The hollow has not altered dramatically within living memory. Fern beds expand and retreat by inches. Stone walls slump one rock at a time. Soot accumulates overhead in a layer scarcely thicker than skin. The entrance narrows as frost shifts the slabs, and every few generations people lever it open again with timber and wedges.

Outside, the moor remains sparse beneath its low clouds. Inside, the small green margins continue drinking from the walls. Beneath both, the sea works in darkness, shaping chambers no one sees.

Its labor is ancient, slow, and deafening.
