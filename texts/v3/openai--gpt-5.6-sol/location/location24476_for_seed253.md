Far below the reach of weather, a green-black country clings to the side of an abyss.

Its elevation is difficult to understand because there is no horizon. The water is too dark to reveal one, and the land does not lie beneath the water so much as descend through it: terraces, overhangs, hollow escarpments, and immense vertical gardens dropping for leagues. The uppermost shelves begin in cold twilight, where a diluted gray sometimes filters down from the far surface. The lowest foundations vanish into a depth where even pressure seems to acquire weight and direction.

There is no exposed stone anywhere.

What appears at first to be a mountain is an architecture of plants. Layer upon layer of ancient growth has enclosed, dissolved, and finally replaced the original geology. The rock around which the first roots gathered is long gone. In its place stand ribs of compressed wood, vaults knitted from root fiber, pale cellulose buttresses, and walls made from innumerable leaf-sheaths laid down one over another. The whole escarpment is constructed, though no hand designed it and no tool has touched it.

Its builders are not individuals in any ordinary sense. They are lineages of colonial plants that have been growing so long that growth has become indistinguishable from masonry.

The broad terraces are floored with a dark, dense grain like timber petrified without becoming stone. Narrow seams in the floor open into planted chambers. The chambers contain vertical fronds as tall as towers, but the fronds are widely spaced, each rising alone into the water. Their leaves are reduced to black fans and threadlike combs that gather traces of dissolved nourishment. Between them lie open courts hundreds of body-lengths across. Water fills these voids cleanly. Nothing drifts through them except animals.

Thus vegetation is total, but the place is not crowded. Every solid thing is plant-made; most of the country is emptiness.

From the terrace edges descend root colonnades. Some are straight and fine, disappearing into the dark below. Others merge into arches large enough for whole shoals to pass beneath. Their surfaces have been worn smooth by ages of water, yet erosion has nearly ceased. Everything vulnerable has already been taken. The softer tissues vanished epochs ago, the weaker bridges collapsed, the exposed corners rounded away. What remains is the final shape the water could not improve upon: blunt parapets, low curves, deep sockets, and load-bearing trunks aligned precisely with the slow pressure of the sea.

Even decay has reached equilibrium. When a fiber weakens, a neighboring filament thickens into the space it leaves. When a leaf is consumed, another unfurls with the same dimensions. Nothing visibly advances. No terrace expands; no wall falls. The garden maintains itself with such exactness that an observer returning after a thousand years might find the same frond bent at the same angle.

The water is cool and almost perfectly uniform. There are no warm springs, no cold falls, no seasonal layers. Currents follow fixed circuits through the architecture: inward through high perforations, down the hollow trunks, outward through the lower galleries, then upward along the outer face. They move steadily but carry no loose sediment. Long ago, every grain that could be transported either escaped into the abyss or was caught and incorporated by roots.

The darkness is not complete, though at first it seems so. On the walls, minute living points shine with an exhausted blue. They are not lamps but fruits: hard, glassy vesicles produced by the oldest colonies. Each glows for centuries. Their light is too weak to illuminate the open water, but it outlines doorlike clefts, buried spirals, and the rims of enormous circular wells. At a distance, these scattered lights make the escarpment resemble a drowned settlement whose windows remain occupied.

There are no actual doors. The resemblance to a city is an accident repeated everywhere.

Passages form where roots refuse to grow. Balconies occur where broad leaves once overlapped and fused. Towers are hollow stalks built up by generations, each plant growing inside the husk of its ancestor. Great chambers open behind the outer wall, roofed by interlocked boughs. Their proportions feel intentional: high nave-like cavities, narrow side aisles, round apertures looking into the sea. Yet there are no joints, carvings, cut faces, or fastenings. Every span is continuous tissue. Every ornament is reproductive or structural.

Animals are abundant, though rarely obvious in the open courts. They inhabit the architecture the way blood inhabits a body.

Flat silver grazers cling to the walls in overlapping rows, scraping films from the living grain. Long translucent swimmers hang head-down between the root columns, barely moving their fins. Pale crustaceans occupy the seams, each fitted so closely to its niche that only their eyes show. In the vaulted interiors are slow clouds of tiny animals, and beneath them move heavy, blind browsers whose mouths are shaped to crop old plant tissue without harming the growing layer underneath.

Predators travel the open spaces. They are spare, dark forms with wide sensory veils and almost no visible eyes. Because the glowing fruits never vary, hunters navigate by memorized constellations. A certain blue point at the lip of a terrace has guided the same migratory route for longer than many surface species have existed.

Life here is numerous but dispersed. A shoal may contain ten thousand bodies and still seem small against the gulf between two pillars. A creature as large as a ship may cross a courtyard and disappear without touching either wall.

The place is loud.

The sound does not come from storms or collapse. It is the continuous voice of equilibrium. Water driven through millions of fine root channels produces a deep, organlike pressure-tone. Hollow trunks answer at fixed pitches. Broad membranes stretched across old openings tremble in the current, sustaining notes too low to be heard only by the ear; they enter through bone and swim bladder and the soft cavities of the body. Animals add clicks, scrapes, pulses, and long droning calls, but even these have settled into recurrent patterns.

There are hours-long chords in the lower galleries. There are terraces where every grazing mouth rasps in rhythm because the floor grain directs them so. In the upper arches, colonial animals knock their shells against the wooden walls with the precision of rain. The combined noise is immense, yet it never becomes chaotic. It has sounded this way for ages. Nothing begins, crescendos, or concludes.

Silence exists only inside sealed chambers.

Those chambers lie deep within the plant mountain, where the surrounding tissues have closed over former canals. Their interiors contain trapped water colder than the outer sea. Some preserve obsolete ecologies: colorless leaves from an earlier chemistry, soft-bodied grazers without living relatives outside, drifting spores that will never find a surface on which to settle. The walls remain alive enough to feed them. They are enclosed worlds, complete and nearly motionless, carried inside the greater construction like memories that cannot be forgotten.

Farther down, the terraces broaden and become fewer. The escarpment breaks into isolated piers rooted in the unseen depth. Each pier is a whole forest compressed into one structural column. Between them open black chasms several leagues wide. The circular current passes through these gaps with a low, permanent roar.

No floor has ever been found below.

Roots descend past every region occupied by animals, thinning into cables and then into single strands too strong to break and too dark to follow. Whether they anchor to some buried foundation or simply continue downward is part of the place’s oldest uncertainty. The architecture gives no sign of strain. Its bridges remain level. Its towers remain upright relative to a gravity almost lost in the water.

Here, height is measured not by summits but by how much darkness hangs beneath a ledge.

The rules of the country are simple and absolute. Nothing remains dead; it is eaten or built into the walls. Nothing grows without replacing an equal volume elsewhere. Water may circulate but never arrive or depart. Species may multiply, but only until the ancient allotment of bodies is restored. The plants maintain openings, spans, and chambers as organs, while the animals maintain the plants by grazing, carrying spores, cleaning channels, and surrendering their bodies to the substrate.

The result is neither wilderness nor ruin, but something older than the distinction.

It is a made world without makers, a forest that has become its own mountain, a city whose streets are water and whose buildings are still faintly alive. It extends beyond sight in every direction available to it—across the drowned heights, down the abyssal face, inward through sealed green-black halls.

And everywhere, in cold darkness, the same deep chord continues.
