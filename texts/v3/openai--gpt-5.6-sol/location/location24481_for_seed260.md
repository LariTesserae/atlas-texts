The country begins above the clouds, though there are no clouds here.

A chain of black summits stands in a dimness that does not appear to belong to night. The light is diffuse, iron-colored, without a visible source. It catches on ridges newly forced out of the deep: planes of unweathered basalt, pale seams of salt, glassy fractures sharp enough to divide a shadow. Nothing has yet been rounded. Scree lies where it was made, each shard fitting the cliff above like a piece dropped only moments ago. Yet the mountains rise so slowly that no living witness could see them move.

Between the summits extends a high, open basin, perhaps fifty leagues from rim to rim. Perspective is unreliable there. A cairn that appears a morning’s walk away may be reached in twenty steps, while a nearby outcrop can recede for days. The basin floor is not level but composed of immense tilted facets, as though the landscape were still deciding which direction should count as down. On some slopes, loose stones fall upward. On others, they rest at an angle to the ground, held by a gravity that points toward a vanished center.

There is no water. There has never been rain, ice, mist, blood, sap, or dew. The pale channels descending from the heights are not streambeds but unfinished faults: grooves along which the mountain intends, eventually, to split. They branch uphill as often as down. Salt gathers in them despite having no solvent to carry it, blooming directly from the rock in white hexagonal crusts.

The vegetation grows from other hungers.

Across the dark mineral flats stand scattered gardens of pressure-fed life. At a distance they resemble low groves of fern, heather, and dwarf cedar. Nearer, their strangeness becomes plain. The fronds are thin laminations of mica threaded with flexible black fibers. They unfold when the surrounding stone contracts in the cool and close when it expands. Their roots do not seek moisture. They enter hairline cracks and drink stress from the mountain, converting compression into growth.

Where the stresses are strongest, the growth is lush. Great plumes crowd fault edges, overlapping in copper, soot-green, bruised violet, and the dull silver of old mirrors. Some bear broad leaves softer than felt, but when cut these leaves reveal no wet tissue—only nested membranes filled with cold dust. Others rise as jointed columns whose translucent skins contain slow-moving sparks. A few resemble trees: low, wind-bent crowns on trunks braided from mineral fibers. Their “wood” can be unwound into long, tough ribbons, and their broken branches continue curling for years toward the nearest source of pressure.

Between these thickets lie enormous empty intervals. A single grove may occupy a ravine in extravagant abundance while the next ten miles hold nothing but raw black slabs and salt. There are no animals among the leaves. No insects hide beneath them, no birds cross the basin, no worms turn the dust. Pollination, predation, grazing, and decay have no place here. The gardens reproduce through contact with possibility. When two shadows overlap in precisely the right way, a seed may appear in the overlap: a hard, many-sided object that was not present in either shadow alone.

The sound is continuous and overwhelming.

It does not come as wind, for the air is nearly still. It is the voice of the country being made. Every cliff carries a bass note too deep for hearing and too powerful to ignore. It enters through the feet, climbs the bones, and makes the teeth ache. Above it grind immense, sustained tones—the friction of adjacent directions, the scraping of one version of the mountain against another. At long intervals, sometimes separated by decades, a high ridge shifts the width of a fingernail. The resulting noise arrives everywhere at once: a metallic bellow vast enough to erase thought.

Conversation is possible only inside certain shadows. There, the roar becomes visual instead of audible. It moves over surfaces as bands of distortion, bending outlines in rhythmic pulses. Outside those shelters, speech tears apart as soon as it leaves the mouth. Words may be found later, flattened into thin gray flakes on the ground. The inhabitants, when there were inhabitants, gathered such flakes and stored them in stone boxes.

Their structures remain scattered throughout the basin.

They are small, low, and severe: huts built beneath overhangs; circles of fitted slate; narrow towers no taller than three people. No mortar was used. The blocks were chipped with harder stones, then wedged together using strips of fibrous trunk. Doorways face not east or west but toward periods of silence—directions that exist only during certain phases of the mountain’s chord.

Inside the huts are simple objects: pressure knives with glass edges, bowls woven from metallic leaves, hammers of dense green stone, and masks packed with salt. There are no bones, graves, footprints, or images of bodies. It is unclear whether the builders departed, ceased to exist, or were never creatures in the usual sense. Some walls bear handlike marks, but each has seven narrow impressions, and the marks occasionally change position when unobserved.

The largest construction stands near the basin’s center, though “center” is unstable there. It consists of three unfinished arches rising from bare rock. Each is made of enormous fitted slabs, yet the tool marks are crude. The builders possessed only wedges, levers, cord, and patience. One arch supports nothing. Another frames a view of a summit that cannot otherwise be seen. Through the third, the landscape appears older: cliffs softened, channels deepened, mineral forests thick with fallen leaves. Crossing the threshold does not lead into that future. It merely leaves the traveler with memories of having lived there.

Nothing changes quickly. That is one of the landscape’s cruelties. Its impossibilities are patient.

A stone may spend a century becoming transparent. A path may gradually forget its destination. One of the high peaks has been dividing into two almost identical peaks for at least twelve thousand years; from decade to decade the separation increases by less than the thickness of a leaf. Near the northern rim, a whole valley is turning inside out. Its strata are exposed like the leaves of a slowly opening book, and every few generations another layer faces the dim sky.

Erosion has scarcely begun. There is no water to cut the rock and little wind to polish it. Instead, form emerges through repetition and contradiction. A cliff develops where the ground is remembered as steep. Dust accumulates around objects that might one day crumble. Ravines appear first as dark lines, then deepen over millennia as the surrounding stone accepts their existence. The mountains are not being worn down so much as revised.

The dim light obeys similar laws. Shadows can precede the things that cast them. At certain hours—which cannot be predicted by any sun—the high groves cast shadows across empty space, and only much later do new ridges rise beneath those shapes. Thus the vegetation may be older than the ground supporting it. In some places roots hang in midair, waiting for stone to arrive.

Cold gathers in hollows like an invisible mineral. The uplands are merely cool, but the basins contain pockets of profound chill, sharply bounded. A traveler can put one hand through such a boundary and feel the fingers stiffen while the wrist remains comfortable. The gardens often cluster around these cold seams. Their leaves turn slowly toward them, not for warmth but for the clarity cold gives to matter. Within the deepest pockets, objects become more certain. Their outlines sharpen; their histories narrow; their alternate forms die away.

Elsewhere, certainty is scarce.

Walk around a boulder and it may have no reverse side. Look into a crack and see the underside of the sky. Throw a stone from a precipice and, after several minutes, hear it strike behind you—though the sound is nearly lost in the greater roar. Distances loosen at the edges of sight. A person standing on a far ridge may appear enormous enough to straddle it, while their voice, if they call, issues from beneath your feet.

The basin has rules, but they are local and temporary. Gravity belongs to slopes rather than worlds. Sequence belongs to objects rather than time. A hammer may strike before it is raised, while the hand holding it continues in ordinary order. Names adhere physically: speak one often enough within a sheltered hut, and the word’s flakes will laminate the walls until the building takes on the named shape.

For this reason the surviving structures are mostly unnamed.

At the highest point of the range is a plateau of dark glass. It is broad, almost level, and empty except for a single dense grove surrounding a shallow depression. The plants there are taller than anywhere else. Their trunks twist together into vaulted chambers, and their countless dry leaves shiver despite the stillness. They do not rustle. Instead they answer the mountain’s deafening chord with a vibration just beyond hearing, visible as silver ripples passing through their crowns.

At the center of the grove, the depression contains no pool. It contains the absence of a pool: a smooth, concave space into which neither dust nor light will fall. The plants lean toward it. Their roots stop at its edge. Salt flowers around the rim in intricate geometries, each crystal containing a tiny image of rain.

No rain exists here, but the image is persistent.

Perhaps the basin is slowly inventing it. Perhaps, over the next million years, the mountain’s roar will deepen the hollow, the mineral forests will learn softness, and one impossible drop will form in the dark. For now there is only the cool air, the raw peaks, the scattered handiwork of absent builders, and the vast gardens feeding on strain.

The land continues to rise by increments too small to witness.

Its thunder never stops.
