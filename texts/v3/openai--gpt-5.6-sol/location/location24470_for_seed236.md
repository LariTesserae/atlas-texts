At the top of a newly risen plateau stands a city that no one built.

The plateau is small enough to cross in an afternoon. Its sides fall almost vertically into a pale mineral haze, and its summit is enclosed by a ring of black, glassy crags. These crags are young: unworn, knife-edged, still showing the folds and stretched bubbles of whatever hot material thrust them upward. Nothing has had time to round them. Splinters lie where they fell. Long cracks cross the ground without dust in them.

Within this sharp crown, however, nearly every available space is occupied.

The city grows from the stone in narrow streets, vaulted chambers, clustered towers, and layered courts. Its architecture is biological, though not quite vegetal. Ochre columns rise in bundles and divide into supporting ribs. Pale membranes span the gaps between them, making roofs that admit a warm, ordinary-looking daylight. Rows of green-black bladders cling to walls like shutters. Spiral buttresses, grown by slow accretion, brace the taller structures against the plateau wind.

There are no tools here, no fitted stones, no hinges or lamps. The apparent planning comes from rules of growth. Each organism responds to shade, pressure, scent, and the minute tension of its neighbors. Where traffic is frequent, the ground-colonies withdraw and leave smooth lanes. Where warmth gathers, towers crowd together. Where the wind enters through notches in the crater rim, overlapping walls rise to redirect it. The whole settlement has the logic of an old city and the substance of a reef, though neither sea nor rain has ever touched it.

There is no free water. The exposed ground is dry enough to draw oils from porous material. Life here carries its chemistry in dense, amber solvents secreted inside sealed tissues. Cut one of the living walls and it does not bleed red or clear; it beads with warm resin that hardens almost immediately into a translucent scab. Roots do not seek moisture. They follow veins of sulfur, carbon salts, and soft metallic compounds within the young rock. Their finest filaments dissolve these minerals and pass them upward in slow, bitter currents.

The vegetation is lush only by the standards of this dry world. Nothing is soft with water. Nothing wilts.

Low mats of copper scales overlap across the plazas. Tall blade-plants grow in crowded fans, each blade stiff and slightly reflective, with edges fine enough to shave dust from the wind. In shaded alleys, forests of jointed rods rise shoulder-high and terminate in folded black leaves. These leaves open not for rain but for warmth, tracking the sun through gaps in the roofs. When fully opened, they show interiors of luminous green, the freshest color in the city.

The largest growths form the towers. Their outer skins are ribbed and chalk-white, cooling the tissues beneath. At intervals they swell into chambers connected by vertical pores. Warm air rises through these pores, pulling trace gases from lower streets and carrying spores toward the upper terraces. The towers are not hollow in the architectural sense; their interiors are packed with layered organs, galleries only because some layers retract from persistent movement.

Animals use these passages.

They are abundant, but the city is strangely quiet. Most are small and light. Broad-footed grazers move over walls without scratching them, cropping the microscopic film that forms on sunward surfaces. Their bodies resemble folded scraps of dark leather walking on six or eight delicate limbs. They communicate by changing the sheen of their backs: dull gray for safety, violet for alarm, silver for mating.

Needle-faced climbers hang beneath the roof membranes, feeding on trapped spores. Their wings are too thin to beat against the rarefied highland air. Instead they climb to warm places, open themselves, and glide down through streets in long, soundless curves.

There are also heavier animals in the lower courts: round-backed creatures whose plated mouths grind nodules from the bases of columns. They move with enormous patience. A single feeding circuit may take several days. Behind them come clusters of pale scavengers, gathering crumbs of resinous tissue before the exposed surfaces seal.

Predation is similarly quiet. Narrow-bodied hunters lie pressed into the seams between walls, matching the mineral grain. They do not leap or chase. They wait for hours, sometimes for an entire cycle of daylight, and then close around passing prey with a slow muscular contraction. Their victims rarely struggle. Sudden motion is costly here, and most life has evolved to conserve both heat and volatile chemistry.

From a distance, the density of bodies can be mistaken for ornament. Animals line the tower ribs. Egg cases tessellate the undersides of arches. Dormant young cling in bunches around warm vents. Colonies of tiny filter-feeders fringe every opening, their threadlike feeding organs extended into the air. Yet almost nothing calls. There is no birdsong, no insect drone, no rustling canopy. The membranes are too taut to flap, and the atmosphere too thin to carry small sounds far.

One hears, at most, the faint tick of contracting skins as evening comes. Occasionally a hardened seed case splits with a soft click. Deeper in the grown structures, pressure passes through a column and produces a low, momentary tone, like a finger drawn around the rim of a stone bowl. Then silence settles again.

The city occupies a warm basin within the plateau’s summit. Sunlight falls into it for most of the day, neither dim nor glaring, filtered only by the pale air and the patchwork roofs. The stone retains heat after sunset. Narrow streets remain warm longest, while the upper terraces cool first and acquire a brittle frosting of condensed hydrocarbons. By dawn, this frost has vanished into the porous skins of the towers.

Change is slow, despite the place’s youth. The plateau may have risen violently, but everything since has happened by increments. A wall thickens by a finger’s width over years. A blocked street closes over during the lifetime of several generations of small grazers. A new court appears when neighboring colonies shade one another and retreat, leaving an accidental open space between them. Towers lean toward heat so gradually that their motion cannot be seen, only inferred from old growth bands.

The summit has boundaries more absolute than walls. Beyond the black rim there is no gradual transition to another ecology. The outer slopes are naked glass and crumbling mineral, too steep and chemically simple for most life. Wind sweeps them clean. The inhabited basin is an island suspended above a vast, dry expanse.

Perhaps the organisms arrived as hardened spores when the plateau first split the surface. Perhaps their buried ancestors had waited below for the heat and gases of the uplift. The city offers no record beyond its own layers. Its oldest structures are only slightly older than its newest cliffs.

At the center stands the broadest and most ambiguous formation: a low, many-arched mass surrounding a circular court. Every street eventually curves toward it, though none runs directly there. Its arches are proportioned as if for inhabitants larger than any creature now present. Within the court, the ground is bare, smooth, and warm.

Nothing grows there. Nothing crosses it.

At midday, when the sun is directly above, the surrounding organisms turn their reflective surfaces inward. Light gathers on the empty stone without becoming painfully bright. Thousands of animals stop along the arches and face the center. They make no sound and perform no visible act. For a few minutes the dense city becomes wholly still.

Then the leaves turn back toward the sun, the grazers resume their circuits, and the slow architecture continues building itself around an emptiness it carefully preserves.
