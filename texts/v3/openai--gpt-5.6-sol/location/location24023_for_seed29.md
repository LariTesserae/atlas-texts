### The Blind Seam

High in a scorched plateau, beneath a slope of black volcanic rubble, there is a fissure scarcely wider than a hand. It does not appear on maps. From outside it is only a dark line between two slabs of basalt, breathing heat into the already hot air.

Within, the seam widens by degrees into a low, steeply tilted chamber. It is less a cave than a flaw in newly settled stone: a pocket left when one layer of lava cooled against another and drew away. The ceiling hangs close enough to touch. Walls advance from every side. Shelves, splinters, mineral blisters, bodies, shed skins, dust and fallen fragments occupy nearly every interval. Emptiness exists only as a system of narrow passages through what is packed there.

The rock is young and hardly weathered. Its edges have not yet been softened into familiar cave forms. There are no stalactites, no polished runnels, no sediment fans. Water has had almost no authorship here. The surfaces are fractured glass, clinker and dense basalt, colored charcoal-black except where sulfur has bloomed in dull yellow crusts. Iron oxides gather as dark red fingerprints around cracks. In places, the stone still shows the folded texture of the flow that made it, each frozen wrinkle abrupt and sharp.

Heat enters from above and remains trapped. By afternoon the outer plateau has become a furnace, and the chamber stores that furnace long into the night. The air is dry enough to tighten membranes and turn waste to powder. Nothing drips. Nothing gleams with moisture. There is only a faint greasy sheen on the most frequently touched rock, laid down by thousands of wings and feet.

The chamber belongs to bats.

They are small, high-desert free-tailed bats, compressed into the ceiling seams in numbers the space seems unable to contain. They hang shoulder to shoulder and flank to flank, layered wherever the basalt gives purchase. At first the ceiling appears shaggy. Then the shag resolves into ears, folded wings, narrow jaws and claws no larger than thorns. The whole roof is alive.

No individual can move without disturbing several others. A shifting body produces a local wave: wings flex, heads lift, teeth click, pups squeal, claws scrape for purchase. The colony does not rest silently. Even at its calmest it maintains a continuous, crowded agitation, a high metallic shrilling woven through with dry rustles and sudden leathery claps. Most of the sound lies above human hearing, but enough descends into an audible pressure—peeping, rasping, ticking, an electrical chorus reflected again and again by the close walls.

Beneath them, the floor is not truly stone anymore. It is a compacted deposit of guano, wings, beetle cases, mineral dust and generations of fine bones. It has no lushness and produces no green life. It is dry, hot and granular, banked deeply against the lower wall like industrial material accumulating beneath a hopper.

Dermestid beetles move through it in astonishing numbers. Their larvae, bristled and brown, work in the buried layers. Pale mites crowd the joints of carcasses. Pseudoscorpions wait beneath curled flakes of basalt, tiny claws lifted. Cave crickets occupy the cooler outer crack, and parasitic flies shuttle through the fur above. Nothing here browses or grazes. There are no leaves, roots, seeds or mosses. Life survives on life: blood, skin, dung, dead insects, failed young, the bodies of returning hunters. The food chain is short, crowded and efficient.

The industrial world is not visible, but it is present in the stone.

Farther along the ridge, beyond several dozen meters of solid basalt, a quarry works the plateau. No beam, cable or tool reaches the chamber; its interior remains entirely unbuilt. Yet compressors transmit their pulse through the mountain. Rock saws rise and fall as distant, toothless whines. At intervals, a drill begins, and the whole fissure acquires a low vibration. Loose grains tremble down the sloping floor. Beetles pause. Thousands of bats tighten their claws at once.

The machinery and colony resemble each other without understanding each other. Both are dense systems of heat, noise and repetition. Both process matter continuously. Aboveground, belts carry crushed basalt toward graded heaps. Here, beetles reduce carcasses to bones and dust. Aboveground, fans draw hot air through filters. Here, evening pressure and the beating of innumerable wings ventilate the seam. The chamber is natural, but it sounds as if it is enclosed inside an engine.

Most change is patient. A crack lengthens by a finger’s width over many hot seasons. Sulfur crystals thicken around a warm vent no wider than a hair. Guano rises grain by grain. Bones settle, become buried, and eventually take on the color of the mineral floor. Even the quarry’s vibration does little at first. It merely encourages the newest fracture, testing it each day.

The fissure is so recently opened that erosion has barely begun to claim it. Small black scales detach from the cooling surfaces. Razor edges become fractionally dull. Dust gathers in corners where no corner existed a century ago. The chamber is still becoming a chamber.

At sunset, the temperature outside drops faster than the temperature within. A current forms through the entrance, weak but unmistakable. The bats nearest the opening stir first. Their calls sharpen. Movement travels inward through the packed colony, not quickly, but as a long gathering readiness. For nearly an hour the seam seems to wind itself tighter.

Then the first bodies leave.

They do not emerge as a broad cloud; the opening is too narrow. They pour through it in a concentrated stream, one after another, wings folded and opened in exact succession. The sound becomes enormous inside the pocket: claws releasing, air struck repeatedly, voices ricocheting from raw rock. Dust lifts but has nowhere to go. Beetles retreat beneath the crust.

Afterward, the chamber is not empty. Pups remain clustered in warm masses. Adults unable to fly shift among them. The floor continues its minute consumption. Machinery continues beyond the ridge. Heat continues to bleed from the basalt.

Darkness here is never peaceful. It is occupied darkness—hot, dry, resonant and full to its smallest cracks.
