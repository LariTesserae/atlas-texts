The place is hidden inside the summit.

Not beneath it in any geological sense: there is no mountain here, no bedrock, no buried stratum. The summit itself is a fabricated shell, a steep-sided accumulation of riveted chambers, ballast vaults, pressure ribs, ceramic armor, and sealed reservoirs. From the distant lowlands it might resemble a black peak holding snow. Up close, every apparent cliff resolves into joined plates. Every ledge has a serial number beneath its rime. The snow is condensed exhaust frozen around refrigeration fins.

Deep within the uppermost structure is a pocket called, on obsolete maintenance plans, **Condensation Room 6**.

It is scarcely larger than a railway carriage, though its interior refuses easy measurement. Banks of pipes consume the walls and ceiling, crossing and recrossing until the room seems woven rather than enclosed. Some are thick enough to crawl through; others are capillaries of copper, glass, or pale sintered metal. The floor is a grille suspended over another grille, and below that lies a dense obscurity of valves. Nothing opens onto soil. Nothing in the chamber was found here. Even the grit caught in its corners consists of ceramic beads, welding slag, powdered insulation, and tiny flakes of numbered paint.

It is intensely cold. Water nevertheless occupies the room in every available form.

Black droplets hang from the lowest pipes. Films of condensation give the walls a dim, muscular sheen. Frost grows along bolts in white collars, while narrow runnels pass underneath it without freezing, warmed by concealed conduits. The grille stands ankle-deep in clear water, yet the water does not drain or rise. Each perforation holds a trembling convex lens. Behind the pipes, weeping seams make miniature marsh edges where moisture gathers against packed mats of wire.

There is no true current. No drop is ever observed to fall.

The chamber exists in a permanent instant immediately after rain.

Its surfaces appear newly made. Welds remain bright beneath the wet. Cut edges are sharp enough to divide a glove. Rivet heads retain the iridescent stains of heat, as if the workers withdrew only moments ago. Here and there, a skin of unfinished material bulges through the construction: foamed alloy that has not yet decided whether to become a brace, translucent resin holding the ghost of a future stair, extruded ceramic ridges marked by the teeth of a machine that never completed its pass. These raw formations resemble embryonic geology—faults without displacement, valleys no water has had time to deepen, mineral fans only one grain thick.

Despite the perpetual wetness, nothing is rounded. There is no rust, no scouring, no sedimentary memory. Erosion has begun but cannot proceed. Every droplet presses into the first infinitesimal stage of making a channel. Every channel remains unborn.

Light comes from service lamps sealed behind cages. Their filaments glow a bruised amber through frost, too weak to illuminate the whole room. Most detail arrives by reflection: a dull glimmer passed from pipe to water, from water to an eye, from an eye to a wet wing. Red indicator bulbs occupy the deeper recesses, unwavering and numerous. They look less like instrumentation than a second, buried fauna watching through the machinery.

The actual fauna is everywhere.

Small white bodies pack the chamber so thoroughly that at first they seem to be insulation. They cling beneath handrails, fill cable trays, line the warm seams, and layer themselves over dormant motors in soft, breathing geometries. Each animal is about the length of a finger. They have six hooked limbs, translucent abdominal sacs, and heads protected by black overlapping plates. Their wings—if they are wings—are narrow metal-veined membranes folded along their backs. Condensation beads upon them without sinking in.

There are millions.

They leave only the strips of floor required by the room’s old safety markings, as though they obey regulations no caretaker remembers. Yellow lines remain perfectly clear. So do labels, inspection hatches, emergency handles, and the circular perimeter around a drain that has never drained. Everywhere else, the creatures stand near-touching, layer upon layer, young nested beneath adults, pale shed skins inserted into gaps like packing material.

They do not feed on vegetation. Almost none grows here. A few colorless filaments protrude from a crack in the resin wall, less a plant than a hesitant proposal for one. Around a lamp cage there is a flat stain of blue lichen, geometrically square, ending exactly where the lamp’s heat ends. In one condensation gutter, three rigid black reeds rise no higher than a thumbnail. Each bears a transparent capsule of water rather than a seed.

The animals drink from the pipes, but their drinking never lowers a droplet. They hatch, yet their eggs do not diminish. Their numbers are overwhelming without increasing. Dead bodies can be found in the lower grilles, perfect and cold, though no living individual has ever been seen to die.

And they make the room deafening.

The sound is not a swarm’s changing buzz. It has no swell, pulse, or rhythm. It is a single continuous mechanical roar produced by every membrane vibrating at precisely the same pitch. The pipes catch it and return it. The water carries it through the floor. The unfinished resin stiffens around it. Within seconds—or what would be seconds elsewhere—the noise passes through ear protection and enters the jaw, the ribs, the fluid of the eyes.

Behind the swarm, great industrial engines contribute lower notes. Their housings occupy the walls: compressors, pumps, refrigeration turbines, pressure equalizers. None has a visible moving part. Their flywheels stand fixed. Belts hang taut. Gauges are locked at mid-scale. Nevertheless, the engines emit the exact sound they would make under impossible load. The chamber is full of labor without motion.

A loose spanner on the grille trembles but never changes position.

The water dimples but produces no ripples.

The animals’ wings blur, although each can also be seen in perfect stillness.

This is the governing rule of the pocket: **events may persist, but they may not advance**. Heat may be transferred without temperatures changing. Water may condense without vapor being depleted. Engines may operate without completing a revolution. Sound may be produced without passing away. A wound made here would remain forever in the act of opening, neither healed nor worsened. A spoken word would join the chamber’s roar and remain inside it, its first fraction prolonged beyond endurance.

Perhaps that is why the room was sealed.

The only entrance is an oval service hatch half concealed behind a curtain of pipework. Its wheel has been freshly greased for longer than the summit has existed. On the inside, stenciled letters warn:

**DO NOT REMAIN DURING STABILIZATION.**

Stabilization appears to have succeeded completely.

Beyond the hatch, the fabricated mountain may contain thousands of other chambers: furnaces burning a motionless flame, reservoirs receiving the same water forever, assembly lines holding unfinished objects between stations. But Condensation Room 6 is especially dense, as if the mountain’s entire ecology has crowded into this one cold pocket. The animals fill every cavity that a hand cannot reach. They inhabit the screw threads, the lamp cages, the narrow annulus between nested pipes. Their black eyes reflect the dim amber service light. Their bodies are warm enough to keep the water liquid and cold enough to collect frost.

No individual looks toward a visitor. All face inward, toward a blank section of wall.

That wall differs subtly from the rest. It has no bolts, no seams, no inspection marks. It is made of a gray material with the softness of wet clay and the luster of machined steel. Shallow lines are forming across it—watersheds, perhaps, or the first creases of a face, or plans for another room compressed into the thickness of a panel. Droplets stand along these lines, ready to excavate them.

They have been ready forever.

The creatures hold their deafening note.

The machinery bears its uncompleted load.

The hidden landscape remains raw, wet, crowded, and newly constructed: a high cold world no larger than a room, made entirely by hands or machines, waiting at the first instant of its own formation and forbidden to become older.
