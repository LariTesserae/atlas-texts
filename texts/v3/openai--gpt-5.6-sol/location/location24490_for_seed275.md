### The Rootworks of Orra

The city lies beneath a salt plain that has never known rain.

Above it, the surface is a white, unbroken mineral glare, but the city is not close to that surface. It occupies a chain of newly opened chambers several kilometers down, where pressure has folded the salt and black basalt into long horizontal vaults. These caverns are young. Their walls still hold the shapes of their making: sharp cleavage planes, glassy fractures, abrupt shelves of stone that have not yet been rounded into ledges. There are no streambeds, no softened gullies, no sediment fans. Fallen material remains where it landed, angular and undecided.

Orra itself is still excavating the chambers.

Its roots enter hairline faults and inflate there, separating slabs without grinding them. A wall will stand intact for decades, then open along a perfect seam. Fresh rock appears pale beneath the older mineral skin. The city sends exploratory tendrils into the crack, then structural roots, then light. In this way, districts do not spread so much as germinate inside the geology.

There is no free water anywhere—not in pools, vapor, tissues, or hidden aquifers. Water is chemically dangerous here. Many of Orra’s organisms seal themselves or shed entire limbs when exposed to even a trace of it. Their vital fluid is a cool, oily ionic sap made from salts, carbon compounds, and metallic complexes. It does not evaporate easily and does not freeze at the cavern’s usual temperature. Cut a living wall and it bleeds a dark silver paste that crawls back toward the wound under electrical attraction.

Everything visible in the city is, in some sense, a plant.

The floor is a continuous living felt, though it does not resemble moss. It is charcoal-gray, fibrous, and tough enough to carry freight engines. Under pressure it briefly pales, leaving ghostly footprints that fade over several hours. This floor organism is both soil and roadbed. It draws minerals directly from the stone beneath it, passes electrical charge along aligned fibers, and grows specialized surfaces where the city requires them: soft in nesting places, ridged on slopes, polished and conductive in factories.

Yet the city does not feel crowded.

Its chambers are immense, and its structures rise at long intervals from broad tracts of open floor. A transit avenue may cross two kilometers of dim plain between one occupied trunk and the next. There are plazas wide enough to contain weather, but only three or four towers stand around them. Vegetation fills every available surface while preserving enormous volumes of emptiness. The result is a sparse forest and a sparse metropolis at once.

The towers are grown from columnar plants whose outer tissues become harder than fired ceramic. Some are hollow, with spiral galleries winding inside their trunks. Others have been trained into ribbed factories, their branches locked together in vaulted bays. Habitation bulbs cling high on the columns, cool and pale, accessible by elevators that rise through vascular shafts. Bridges consist of braided stems tensioned across the dark. Ventilation ducts are long tubular vines. Storage silos swell seasonally, then harden around their contents.

Nothing is ornamented in the usual way, but growth leaves its own elaborate marks: repeated branching scars, metallic leaf veins, hexagonal pores, rows of sealed buds awaiting instructions. Doors are muscular diaphragms. Windows are patches of transparent bark. When a district must be abandoned, its entrances simply heal over.

Light comes from the ceiling forests.

High above the streets, widely spaced luminous crowns spread against the vault. Their leaves are flat, ivory plates that emit a balanced white radiance, close to daylight but without a visible sun or direction. Each crown brightens and dims on a civic schedule. Morning travels through the city as a slow band of illumination, district by district, while behind it the lamps cool to a blue-gray evening.

The light is ordinary enough to make the cavern’s scale more unsettling. Distances are clear. Shadows behave properly. Colors are faithful. Looking down a boulevard, one can see freight lines narrowing between dark trunks, maintenance gantries crossing overhead, and far beyond them the hard edge of a newly exposed rock face. Only the absolute absence of sky gives the scene away.

Orra’s industries are biological in structure but industrial in pace. In the refining wards, root furnaces digest basalt and separate iron, magnesium, sulfur, and rare salts into distinct nodules. Mechanical harvesters move along the furnace beds, cutting away ripe deposits with ceramic saws. Press-houses compress fibrous tissue into beams. Seed foundries manufacture standardized buds for pipes, insulation, lamps, structural columns, and replacement organs.

The machines themselves are not alive, though it can be difficult to tell. Low freight crawlers travel on six articulated legs to avoid tearing the road-felt. Their engines consume dry resin wafers and turn almost silently inside thick plant-fiber housings. Cable looms click in enclosed halls. Induction mills hum beneath roots that absorb the vibration. Pistons operate slowly, with immense force, pushing fresh tunnel supports into place as the cavern opens.

Industrial noise does not travel far. The atmosphere is thin and cool, and nearly every surface absorbs sound. Bark, felt, fiber, and porous stone swallow echoes before they can gather. A working factory heard from across a plaza may amount only to a low pulse underfoot. Close by, there are small noises: the dry zippering of leaves changing angle, a valve opening with a sigh, mandibles testing a beam, the distant knock of a freight crawler placing one foot after another.

The loudest natural event is a wall-birth.

First comes a tension too low to hear, felt in the teeth and in the city floor. Nearby plants retract their delicate organs. Traffic stops. Then a sheet of stone separates from the cavern side with a single report. It may lean outward and remain standing, or descend intact into a waiting bed of shock-root. Dust rises in a slow mineral cloud. Survey vines immediately extend through it, tasting exposed seams. Within hours, pale anchoring threads sketch the first streets of a district that did not exist that morning.

The fauna of Orra is abundant but seldom obvious.

Most creatures are small and live inside the city’s tissues without seriously harming them. Flat silver grazers move beneath translucent bark, scraping away dead cells. Six-winged pollinators drift between lamp crowns, their wings nearly silent in the thin air. Pin-legged mineral feeders cross open plazas in scattered herds, pausing over hairline cracks to lower needle mouths into the stone. Their bodies contain no liquid cavities; organs are layered membranes lubricated by wax and ionic gels.

Larger animals inhabit the high architecture. Pale sailbacks cling to bridges and spread thermal fins into warm industrial drafts. Long-bodied vault hunters wait upside down among ceiling roots, sensing the electric signatures of passing fliers. When one strikes, it releases its grip and falls rather than leaps, steering with ribbed skin before catching its prey. Far below, reclamation beasts follow freight routes and eat anything shed by the city: bark, damaged cable fiber, packing husks, corpses, obsolete signs.

Predation is frequent, but almost noiseless. There are no roars. Alarm travels by scent, charge, and abrupt changes in reflected light. A flock may vanish from a luminous crown all at once, leaving the entire upper chamber strangely bare. Seconds later, tiny fragments of discarded wing drift down through the white illumination.

The ecology runs on gradients more than seasons. Factory heat produces warm intervals. Mining exposes nutrient-rich stone, triggering blooms. Electrical surges call certain pollinators out of dormancy. When a new chamber opens, pioneer growth spreads over it as a fine black geometry: widely spaced threads first, then loops, then a load-bearing mesh. Animals arrive along the threads. Lamps bud only after the floor has stabilized. Buildings follow light. Industry follows buildings.

Old districts are rare because Orra itself is young. Even its deepest avenues retain unfinished edges. Streets end against raw fracture planes. Towers hold sealed chambers that have not yet differentiated into rooms. Transit lines divide into rails that lead nowhere, awaiting neighborhoods still enclosed in stone. Throughout the city, one encounters signs of intended scale: an empty interchange vast as a canyon, foundations for a hundred trunks where only seven have risen, a luminous ceiling extending over vacant black ground.

This incompleteness gives Orra its governing atmosphere. It is not a ruin and not a frontier. It is an embryo already operating at metropolitan size.

The city seems to know more of itself than presently exists. Growth plans are encoded in the floor. Roots pause at future intersections. Buds sleep in walls, labeled by subtle bands of color. Machines carry materials toward unopened rock. The inhabitants—whether they designed Orra or merely learned to live inside its unfolding—treat empty space as occupied by what is yet to grow.

At the far end of the newest chamber, beyond the last functioning lamp, the road-felt narrows to a black strand. It crosses bare salt, passes between fresh splinters of basalt, and disappears into a crack scarcely wider than a finger.

From within that crack comes a faint, regular pressure.

Not a sound exactly, but the slow effort of roots making room for another city.
