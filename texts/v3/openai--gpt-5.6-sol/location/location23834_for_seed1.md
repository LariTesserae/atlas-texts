The land is almost level, though never smooth. It extends beneath a low iron-colored sky as a vast, shallow basin worn down past the age of mountains. What remains is the root of terrain: black shelves, compressed clays, ribs of pale mineral, and old impact hollows filled with water so dark they resemble openings rather than pools.

Nothing grows here. There is no grass to soften the flats, no moss on the stones, no buried seed awaiting warmth. Every exposed surface is naked, polished by ages of rain and lateral ice. Yet the basin is crowded almost beyond movement.

Water occupies all the narrow spaces. It lies in films over the rock, runs through hairline seams, gathers under translucent crusts, and trembles in millions of shallow depressions. Rain is frequent but difficult to see. It condenses a few meters above the ground, appearing first as silver grains in the air, then as a steady downward hiss. At other times the water rises from below. The flats sweat. Cracks fill from their own depths, and beads emerge along mineral veins until they join into creeping sheets.

The cold is severe without being clean. It does not sharpen the horizon or preserve things in white silence. It is a wet, adhesive cold, full of brine and metallic vapor. Ice forms in soft skins that flex when stepped upon. Puddles freeze from the bottom upward. Rain remains liquid long after striking the stone, but freezes instantly if reflected in another surface. Thus every pool has two conditions: dark moving water when looked at directly, and cloudy ice in its reflection.

This is one of the basin’s older laws. There are others.

Distance does not accumulate reliably. A ridge seen on the horizon may be reached in minutes, while a nearby channel may take half a day to cross. Sound travels according to the age of the material beneath it rather than according to space. A blow against newly exposed stone is heard only nearby. A tap against the oldest bedrock rolls across the entire basin, returning from places that no longer exist.

For this reason the landscape is loud.

Beneath the rain is a continuous industrial thunder: hammering, cyclic grinding, piston coughs, turbine shrieks, the deep arrhythmic concussion of enormous presses. No factories stand here. There are no walls, roads, towers, pipes, tools, or ruins. Nothing has been built. The machinery is alive—or occupies the category that life fills elsewhere.

Across the flats move dense herds of low, many-jointed creatures whose shells are layered in oxidized plates. Their bodies are broad and close to the ground, each supported by dozens of short hydraulic limbs. Water enters through grilles along their flanks. Internal chambers compress it, heat it briefly, and eject it in white jets from vents along their backs. They travel in packed formations, scraping mineral films from the stone with rotating mouths. From a distance a herd sounds like a failing mill: belts slipping, bearings screaming, wet gears catching and releasing.

They do not eat vegetation, because there has never been any. They consume gradients. Salt against fresh water. Warmth trapped beneath ice. Electrical charge between unlike stones. Minute differences in pressure. A herd crossing a shallow pool leaves it chemically uniform, thermally dead, and perfectly still.

Smaller creatures swarm in the furrows behind them. These are bright, quick things like loose assemblies of washers and glass needles. They congregate wherever the larger animals have shed heat. Their motion generates a high metallic chirring, and when frightened they disassemble into rings that roll independently across the wet rock. Hours later, sometimes kilometers away, the rings find one another and become animals again. They rarely reassemble in the same order.

Above them drift the basin’s aerial fauna. There is little true flight in the dense cold air. Most creatures hang from pressure differences, broad-bodied and translucent, with sacs that inflate and collapse to the rhythm of distant storms. Their interiors flash with slow blue discharges. Long conductive tendrils drag across the ground, striking sparks from metallic seams and occasionally hooking smaller animals. When several pass overhead, the dimness deepens and the air fills with the smell of ozone, cold oil, and opened stone.

Predation resembles manufacturing.

In the western reaches, slender black hunters stand folded inside water-filled cracks. When a herd approaches, they unfold in stages, each limb locking into place with a report like a rifle shot. They seize one animal, clamp it against the bedrock, and separate it along its natural joints. Nothing is wasted. Plates are swallowed first, then pressure bladders, then the warm solution stored around the central organs. For weeks afterward the hunter carries fragments of its prey externally, fitted into cavities along its body. Whether these are armor, digestion, or offspring is unclear. Sometimes the fragments begin moving on their own.

Elsewhere, enormous circular mouths migrate beneath the basin floor. The stone above them sags by degrees. Pools tilt inward. Herds crowd at the rim, unable to understand the slope until the entire surface gives way and descends in one broad slab. The mouths close without biting. Months later, the animals reappear far to the north, altered, polished, and walking backward through their own tracks.

Tracks are among the few things that persist here, though they do not always remain where they were made. The flats are engraved with crossings from uncountable ages: parallel grooves, impact pits, drag marks, branching traces like electrical diagrams. New trails slip into old ones. Some mornings the marks rise from the ground as ridges, retaining the exact shapes of vanished feet. These relief-tracks migrate in slow files toward the lowest parts of the basin, where they pile together so densely that the terrain becomes almost impassable.

The central lowland is the most crowded region. Water there is rarely more than ankle-deep, but it covers hundreds of kilometers in an unbroken reflective sheet. Millions of animals occupy it: shelled grazers, needle swarms, raftlike colonies, pressure-bellied crawlers, floating membranes, and tall tripodal forms that move with stately mechanical precision. There is no open water between them. Each body presses against several others. Waves cannot form; disturbances pass directly through flesh, shell, and joint.

The whole population surges with the weather. When the atmospheric pressure falls, every creature turns east. When the pressure rises, they turn west. The rotation begins at no discernible point and travels through the mass as a grinding wave. Shells scrape. Limbs lock. Vents burst open. Water is squeezed upward in curtains, and for several minutes the basin resembles an ocean boiling under heavy machinery.

Then the tripods sound.

They are the tallest inhabitants, though even they rarely rise above six or seven meters. Their three legs end in broad pads that never quite touch the ground; a thin layer of compressed vapor supports them. Suspended between the legs is a bell-shaped organ of dense gray metal, apparently grown rather than forged. At irregular intervals it contracts.

The note is too low to hear at first. It arrives as pressure in the teeth and liquid trembling inside the eyes. Pools dish inward. Ice clouds. Small creatures stop moving. Then the audible part comes: a vast horn blast layered with the clatter of chains and the drawn-out groan of stressed hulls.

Each call changes something.

After one, water may begin running uphill.

After another, all the animals in a ten-kilometer radius acquire an extra shadow.

A third may cause the rain to pause in the air. Droplets hang motionless by the billions, revealing columns and corridors within the atmosphere. The fauna move through these suspended drops, collecting them from one side and shedding them from the other. When the rain resumes, it falls in several directions at once.

No one knows whether the tripods control these changes or merely announce them.

Beyond the central waters, the basin divides into long, tightly packed ridges of eroded stone. They are the remains of something older than geology’s present habits. Their surfaces have been worn into parallel flutes, and every flute carries a thread of black water. Here the land itself performs work. Water enters narrow pores at the ridge crests, descends through pressure chambers, and returns from vents below as brine, steam, or fine mineral powder. The ridges knock and shudder as internal cavities open and close.

They resemble refineries only because refineries elsewhere resemble them.

Veins separate useful elements. Cavities sort grains by size. Thermal faults distill volatile compounds. Electrochemical seams plate exposed rock with copper, nickel, silver, and unfamiliar blue metals. But there is no intention in it, no maker and no product awaiting use. The ancient basin has always processed itself. Its creatures evolved—or condensed, or were remembered into existence—to inhabit these cycles.

Some species spend their entire lives serving as temporary components. Slender red organisms lodge inside vents and act as valves. Soft, pale disks seal leaks until pressure ruptures them. Chain-bodied animals connect electrically active pools, conducting current through their linked shells. When one burns out, the chain closes around the gap. Nothing directs this labor. It is appetite, reproduction, migration, and weather all at once.

Even death is an industrial phase. Bodies are not left as carcasses. In the cold water they flatten, their joints opening into complex branching sheets. Other creatures settle into the gaps. Mineral-rich rain plates the sheets in metal, and over centuries they become new portions of the basin floor. Some of the oldest bedrock is composed entirely of former animals compressed beyond recognition, still twitching microscopically when storms pass overhead.

There are no fossils because extinction does not move cleanly into the past here. Lost species sometimes emerge from reflections. At dusk—which is only a further dimming of the gray sky—pools show crowded migrations that are absent from their shores. Vast segmented shapes pass beneath water a finger’s depth deep. Their backs are scarred by vanished climates. Occasionally one notices the living fauna making room for them.

The reflected herds leave real wakes.

Toward the basin’s far margin, the land becomes so old and so thoroughly eroded that form begins to fail. Rock loses its preference for solidity. Broad areas sag like wet hide. Channels cut through one another without intersection. A black strand of water can pass over a white strand without mixing, though both occupy the same shallow groove. Animals entering these regions return with incompatible numbers of limbs or with surfaces that display the weather of previous days.

Here, echoes precede sounds. The thunder of a herd arrives before the herd itself, growing louder until the animals appear and fulfill it. Predators sometimes hunt these anticipatory noises, attacking the place where prey will later be. If the strike is successful, the prey arrives already wounded. If it fails, the landscape retains the unused injury: a gash suspended in the air, slowly filling with rain.

Despite all this movement, the basin feels terminally ancient. Nothing is young, even when newly divided or born. The smallest animals emerge already abraded. Their shells bear scratches from journeys they have not taken. The water tastes of stone that has not yet been exposed. Every process seems less like change than the continuation of a final state established immeasurably long ago.

The horizon remains low and obstructed by weather. Condensation drifts in dark horizontal banks, hiding and revealing the packed multitudes. Blue electrical flickers travel under the clouds. On the ground, reflected flashes run in the opposite direction. There are moments when the sky appears beneath the water and the basin hangs above it, crowded with its grinding, pumping, calling life.

Then the tripods contract again.

The sound moves through the oldest stone. Every pool trembles. Every creature stops except those that have not happened yet. Far across the flats, something immense turns under the nearly level ground, raising no hill, only changing which way the water believes is down.

The rain begins upward.

The herds open their vents.

And the whole cold basin, untouched by hand and packed to its dim horizon, resumes its ancient production of itself.
