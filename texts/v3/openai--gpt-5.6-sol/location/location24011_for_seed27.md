### The High Dry Garden

The plateau is older than the mountains beneath it.

Those mountains still rise for thousands of meters, but their summit has been worn into a broad, nearly level country: an ancient surface lifted into the cold upper atmosphere and left there. Ridges have softened into long backs. Cliffs have withdrawn to pale, distant rims. Loose stone is rare. Every exposed mineral has already endured its possible breaking and settled into a final shape—flat slabs, rounded teeth, powder packed hard as ceramic.

From the edge, the plateau appears sparsely occupied. Dark towers stand kilometers apart. Low green-black ridges cross the plain without urgency. Between them stretch immense dim fields, giving the place the openness of a desert or an abandoned high city.

Yet there is no bare ground.

The ground is a plant.

A continuous living sheet covers the plateau from escarpment to escarpment. It is only a few fingers thick in some places and hundreds of meters deep in old basins, where its roots have digested entire beds of stone. Its upper hide resembles felted charcoal or weathered leather. Polygonal seams allow it to contract in the cold. Fine metallic hairs lie flat beneath the wind and rise when the air becomes still.

Nothing here uses water. There is almost none to use.

The plateau’s life arose—or was made—around a different fluidity. Within the black ground-sheet, slow capillaries carry clear ionic sap: salts held liquid by elaborate organic cages, remaining mobile far below the freezing point of water. Its cells are not swollen sacs but nested mineral foams, flexible because their walls continually fracture and repair at microscopic scales. Their chemistry consumes carbon dioxide, nitrogen, sulfur dust, sunlight, static charge, and the faint electrical gradient between the high plateau and the dense atmosphere far below.

Water is an irritant here, sometimes a poison. A snowflake falling from the upper dark does not melt. It lands with a small hiss. The nearest hairs bend toward it, isolate it in a glassy blister, and split it into hydrogen and oxygen before either can react indiscriminately with the tissue. During the rare fall of comet frost, the whole plateau smokes.

The dominant plant is called the foundation only because every other name for it—soil, street, organism, nation—describes too little.

Its open fields are not empty. They are resting surfaces, kept smooth so that dim light can reach the photosensitive layers below. Underfoot, slow color passes through the hide: bruised violet, dull silver, a green so dark it registers more as depth than hue. These changes are not caused by clouds. They are messages traveling from one horizon to another.

From the foundation rise scattered structures.

Some look like trees, though their trunks are hollow lattices of carbon fiber and amber mineral. Their crowns are not leaf-filled. Each supports only a few vast vanes, black and thin, turning edge-on to the wind. They gather light, electric charge, and atmospheric chemicals. At dusk the vanes fold with explosive cracks that roll over the plateau like distant artillery.

Others are unmistakably urban. There are avenues, plazas, towers, bridges and terraces, but none were assembled block by block. They were persuaded to grow.

The towers stand far apart because each commands a large feeding territory. Their bases spread into root-districts several kilometers wide, mapping themselves onto buried seams of useful metal. Above, they narrow into fluted shafts pierced by chambers. The walls are pale, almost bone-colored, formed from mineral extracted atom by atom from the plateau. They are cool to the touch and faintly resilient. If struck, they do not chip. The force disappears into the whole building and returns minutes later as heat in the crown.

Windows open only where someone is present. Empty rooms seal themselves and lapse into cellular sleep.

Roads are bands of harder tissue woven through the foundation. They have no fixed lanes. Ridges rise under travelers and carry them onward in smooth peristaltic waves, while unused branches flatten back into the plain. From a distance, transit looks like a series of dark ripples moving between towers. Freight goes below, enclosed in root vessels that constrict and relax over hundreds of kilometers.

No lamps line these ways. Light is costly, and darkness is productive.

The sun is small and copper-colored, often veiled by the high atmospheric haze. Even at noon, objects cast uncertain shadows. The plants collect what light there is with ruthless efficiency. Reflective surfaces turn inward. Brightness is trapped in wells, folded through translucent tissues, and passed down to chemical foundries under the city. The inhabited plateau remains in perpetual dusk, illuminated here and there by narrow seams of blue bioluminescence or by the slow red pulse of sap ascending a tower.

The dimness makes distances difficult. A nearby spire may seem to stand beyond the horizon, while a ridge fifty kilometers away appears close enough to touch. The air contains suspended conductive dust, too fine to settle, and faint halos gather around anything with a charge. Every moving animal carries its own blurred outline.

There are many animals, though most are small and widely dispersed.

Long-legged grazers cross the fields in files of thirty or forty. They are light enough to stand on the foundation without piercing it, with six jointed limbs and low bodies covered in insulating quills. Instead of biting off plant tissue, they comb electrical nodules from its surface. Their mouths flash as they feed. The foundation tolerates them because their passage aligns its metallic hairs and because they carry reproductive dust between distant sectors.

Flocks of foil-winged creatures circle the towers. They do not flap. They alter the stiffness of their bodies and surf the standing pressure waves produced by the city. Their translucent wings are printed with branching circuits, partly grown and partly maintained by swarms of symbiotic mites. When a flock turns, the sky briefly fills with dull mirrors.

Burrowers live inside the plant rather than beneath it. Some are parasites; some are gardeners; some may be mobile organs whose independence became permanent long ago. At night they push up temporary chimneys through the surface. Warm chemical exhaust pours out, carrying smells of hot metal, resin and bitter ozone. Predators wait nearby, sensing pressure through the ground.

The largest animals are rarely seen whole. They move below the foundation in the deep root galleries, displacing neighborhoods as they pass. A rising back may lift a plaza by several meters. Towers lean, flexible bridges slacken, and the roads calmly redirect themselves. Afterward the surface settles without a crack. These subterranean giants graze on obsolete roots and dead infrastructure. Their migrations are part of the city’s maintenance.

Despite the thinness of the high air, the plateau is loud.

Much of the noise does not travel through the atmosphere. It travels through the living ground.

Every footfall enters the foundation and returns magnified as a dense subterranean resonance. Root pumps beat in long, overlapping rhythms. Freight vessels hammer through constrictions. The buried animals grind mineral plates against the walls of their galleries. Towers tune themselves against the wind, producing organ-like notes too low to hear cleanly but strong enough to shake the sternum.

Above this bass are sharper sounds: vanes snapping open; grazing herds clicking their insulated joints; wing-flocks rattling like thrown sheet metal; seed vessels detonating in the cold; construction polyps screaming ultrasonically as they deposit new layers of wall.

At intervals, the entire city sounds at once.

A pressure front crosses the plateau, invisible except for the flattening of hairs. Towers rotate their crowns. Bridges tighten. Miles of foundation contract in sequence. The resulting chord begins below hearing, rises through a roar like stone dragged over stone, and ends in a high metallic singing from every spire. Animals crouch. Transit halts. Dust leaps from the air and forms temporary geometric sheets.

This is not an alarm. It is computation.

The settlement’s oldest technology is grown into the plateau’s resonant anatomy. Distances are measured by echoes passing through root, stone and atmosphere. Problems are encoded as tensions. Towers act as nodes; animal migrations supply random variation; weather fronts provide energy. When the city needs to calculate a new district, redirect a deep grazer, repair a failing climate boundary, or predict the approach of comet frost, it plays the landscape.

The answer appears as growth.

A road may divide the following morning. A tower may open a chamber that did not exist before. A field may wrinkle into terraces, each crease precise to the width of a cell. Sometimes a black arch rises where the chord was loudest, framing no road and supporting nothing. Passing through such an arch alters direction without producing any sensation of turning. Two neighboring districts can be a month apart by foot in one direction and several minutes apart in the other.

The inhabitants accept this as ordinary infrastructure.

They occupy the tower hollows, though it is difficult to say whether they built the garden, were built by it, or arrived after both had become inseparable. Their machines resemble fruits, insects, bones, folded shadows. Tools attach themselves temporarily to the hand and then crawl back into wall niches to be digested and remade. Information is cultivated in branching scars. Doors recognize intention before approach.

There are ruins, but no dead buildings. A tower abandoned for centuries lowers itself over decades, its upper chambers flowing down through the trunk as reusable matter. What remains is a ring-shaped grove of short mineral stalks. These groves are among the few crowded places on the plateau. Animals gather there for warmth, and the foundation produces brilliant seed-lights among the old walls.

Beyond the settled reaches, the same living surface continues toward the eroded rim. Towers become fewer, then cease. Roads narrow into veins. The land stays open and nearly level beneath the dim sky.

At the edge, the foundation folds over the cliff in immense, dry curtains. Root cables descend through cloud into the unseen lower world. They sway in winds from the abyss and strike the cliff face with concussions audible across the plateau.

No rivers leave this country. No rain has carved its valleys. Its final erosion is biological: stone lifted as ions, carried through sap, sung into walls, worn down by use, and eventually returned as smooth mineral scales beneath the black hide.

The plateau is ancient enough to have consumed its own archaeological record many times. Nothing remains unless the living city continues to find it useful.

And yet it is never still. Under the sparse silhouettes and the cold dusk, every open mile is breathing without lungs, thinking without silence, and slowly rebuilding the shape of the world.
