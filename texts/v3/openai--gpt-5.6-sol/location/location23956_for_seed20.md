At the summit of a drowned mountain, nearly two kilometers above the abyssal plain but still far below any ordinary sea surface, there is a basin called—not by anyone living—the Thunder Cup.

It is small enough to cross in an hour if the currents permit. Black ridges enclose it on three sides, fresh volcanic stone standing in blades and broken fins. Nothing here has had time to become smooth. The mountain has risen recently, perhaps in a single season, perhaps this morning. Its slopes are armored with glassy crusts that split without warning and expose warm orange seams. Pebbles do not lie still. They lift, orbit one another, and settle in new arrangements as pulses move through the rock.

The water is warm and almost opaque. It has the weight and mineral thickness of broth. Clouds of pale sulfur, iron-red dust, and volcanic milk travel through it in distinct layers, folding over one another without mixing. At times the basin appears no larger than a stone chamber. At other times its far wall retreats into blue-black distance, and the summit seems to contain an entire inland sea.

There is no silence anywhere.

The mountain is still being born, and its birth is deafening. Stone booms beneath the floor. Vents hammer in rapid succession. Long cracks roar as superheated water forces its way upward, and the surrounding ridges answer with metallic groans. Sound does not merely pass through this place: it has mass. Each concussion strikes the body from all directions, compressing flesh, shaking loose fragments from the cliffs, blurring the water into visible rings. The loudest reports produce temporary hollows—clear spherical spaces in which no sediment hangs and no creature can swim. These hollows expand, tremble, and collapse with a crack like the breaking of a world.

Above the basin hangs a storm, though there is no sky.

Rain falls through the sea.

The drops are darker than the water around them and maintain their shape as they descend, each one containing a tiny reflection of lightning. They plunge from an indistinct ceiling high overhead, pass through currents without dispersing, and strike the volcanic floor hard enough to raise dust. Sometimes the rain reverses. Then droplets tear themselves from the stone and fly upward in sheets, drumming against unseen surfaces. No source for them can be found. If one is caught, it proves to be full of cold air and the smell of distant soil.

The sparse life of the basin keeps low. Thin ivory filaments grow in cracks where heat seeps through. They resemble roots but have no trunks or leaves; they enter the rock at both ends and flower briefly in the middle, opening translucent cups whenever thunder passes. Patches of black moss cling to sheltered overhangs. Their fronds point not toward light but toward the loudest sound, turning together when the mountain shifts.

There are a few larger growths: solitary red fans spaced widely across the basin floor. Each stands higher than a person and is so delicate that it should be shredded by the turbulence. Instead, currents divide before touching it. Sediment streams around the fans in perfect arcs. During the dimmest intervals they emit a dull maroon radiance, revealing small white animals sheltered within their branches—blind things with too many joints, seen only for a moment before they withdraw.

Fauna is otherwise rare. A long, transparent fish sometimes circles the basin, though no two sightings agree on its length. It may pass close enough for its ribs to be counted, then appear simultaneously along the distant ridge, large enough to eclipse it. Its mouth is always open. From within comes the muted sound of surf.

Tiny shell creatures inhabit the rain craters. They emerge after each impact, arrange themselves in rings, and vanish when the next shock wave arrives. Nothing is seen eating. Nothing is seen dying. Now and then a pale skin, several meters long, is found snagged on a stone needle, still flexing as though remembering an animal.

Scattered through the basin are structures.

They are simple and intimate: low arches, waist-high walls, circles of stacked basalt. No metal, hinges, carving, or tool marks appear anywhere. The stones look selected rather than cut, fitted by patient hands or patient currents. Some form roofless cells barely large enough to crouch inside. Others are only thresholds standing alone on the open floor.

The structures cannot be ruins, because the rock beneath them is younger than they are.

Warm lava has hardened around their foundations, preserving corners already softened by centuries of wear. Shell deposits coat surfaces that were exposed only minutes before. One wall occasionally comes apart, its stones rising in a slow spiral, and rebuilds itself several paces away. It never adopts precisely the same plan. Each rebuilding adds a narrow doorway or removes one, as if the place is trying to recall an architecture from before architecture existed.

Passing through these thresholds changes the basin.

Through one arch, the water beyond appears dry and full of blowing ash. Through another, the sea becomes vertically striped, alternating bands of warmth and cold so sharp that they resemble transparent pillars. A third opens onto the same basin viewed from above, including the observer standing at the arch. These effects disappear when approached from the opposite side. There is no machinery, no source, and perhaps no distinction here between a doorway and the idea of passage.

Light comes reluctantly. A dim green haze filters from no fixed direction, intermittently drowned by clouds from the vents. There are moments of total blackness when the basin seems to turn inside out. In those intervals the stone remains visible—not by illumination, but as a pressure against the mind. Every ridge can be felt at once. Every cavity seems to occupy the body. When the green gloom returns, landmarks have shifted.

The central feature is a deep cleft no wider than a house. Its edges are fresh, sharp, and continually crumbling, yet the gap never fills. Water pours into it from all sides in a roaring horizontal cataract. Red mineral clouds, loose stones, broken shells, even sound itself seem dragged toward the opening. From below comes a slower countercurrent, warm enough to shimmer, carrying fragments of impossible environments: a yellow leaf despite the absence of trees; a bubble containing snowfall; a pinch of dry sand; the shadow of a bird with no bird attached.

At irregular intervals the cleft closes.

The two sides of the basin strike together with a concussion that turns the water white. For several breaths there is only an unbroken floor of newborn basalt. Then a hairline crack appears. It widens, branches, and opens again, but never in quite the same place. The arches topple. The red fans bend. Rain shoots upward. The transparent fish passes through the stone as if fleeing the wrong direction.

This is the governing rule of the Thunder Cup: nothing may remain merely itself.

Water becomes weather. Height becomes depth. New rock acquires old ruins. Noise becomes current, and currents become walls. Distance expands when observed and contracts when crossed. The basin is constantly making distinctions—between sea and sky, organism and mineral, past and present—then destroying them before they can settle.

Yet amid all the violence, there are sheltered pockets behind the black ridges where the water grows nearly still. Warm sediment drifts down. Ivory filaments open their sound-fed flowers. A single low arch frames the dim red glow of a fan, and for a moment the place resembles a submerged garden.

Then thunder strikes from beneath.

The garden folds sideways, the mountain rises another fraction into the dark sea, and the rain begins falling up.
