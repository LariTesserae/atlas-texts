The place occupies a shallow basin high on the shoulder of a mountain, though from within it the greater mountain cannot be seen. A rim of old stone encloses it on three sides, no higher than a grove’s canopy, and the fourth side opens toward white distance. Clouds pass below that opening. When they rise, they enter without wind and fill the basin from the ground upward.

It is warm here—not the hard warmth of exposed heights, but a close, retained heat. The rocks hold it. Water holds it. Even after sunset, the basin exhales the day through wet stone and dark soil. Moisture gathers constantly: in hollows, beneath leaves, along the undersides of ledges. Nothing is ever quite dry, though there is little standing water. The water remains near the surface, moving under a thin skin of earth, revealing itself wherever the ground dips or a root has loosened the soil.

The basin is small enough to cross in several minutes, but it does not feel cramped. Its few presences stand far apart.

There are perhaps twenty trees. None grow close enough to touch another. Each rises from its own low island of moss and pale root, with open ground between: warm black mud, slabs of weather-softened basalt, shallow films of water clear as breath on glass. The trees are slender and tall, their bark the color of steeped tea. Their crowns begin high, spreading into sparse fans of broad, waxy leaves. Every leaf ends in a long translucent tip, and water gathers there even when it has not rained. Drops swell slowly, hang for minutes, then fall.

Because the trees are separated, each drop can be heard.

A tap on stone.  
A soft puncture in mud.  
A nearly inaudible joining of water to water.

There are no insects to answer. No birds move in the crowns. No frogs wait in the seeps, no worms turn the soil, no grazing thing has clipped the low growth. The absence is complete, extending even to the smallest visible life. Flowers open without pollinators. Fruit, where it forms, contains no bite marks and no seeds. Fallen leaves remain whole until water and warmth loosen them directly into pulp.

Vegetation persists by other means.

The trees bud from themselves. At long intervals, one root lifts through the mud and extends across the open ground, not branching randomly but feeling its way from stone to stone. Its tip is hard and glossy, chipped into a rough edge. It scrapes lichen from a suitable crack, deepens the crack over years, and wedges itself inside. Once anchored, the root swells. A new trunk rises there, while the connecting length gradually thins and dissolves.

Other plants show the same patient aptitude. Low ferns grow only beside flakes of stone that they have pried from the basin wall. Their stiff stems lever these flakes upright, making narrow shaded chambers where condensation collects. A creeping vine uses fallen basalt chips to bruise its own seedless nodes; each wound becomes a root. Along one seep, cup-shaped leaves have arranged pebbles into loose crescents—not walls, not channels exactly, but enough to slow the water and let silt settle.

Nothing here is built, yet certain things have been done.

There are no huts, paths, fences, carvings, hearths, or tools left lying apart from the bodies that use them. The basin has never been settled. Still, the landscape carries a primitive craft of pressure, leverage, fracture, and placement. Stone edges are tested by roots. Mud is packed by stems. Water is persuaded into pockets by leaves laid one across another. The work is so slow that it resembles geology until watched for a season.

At the center lies the basin’s only pool. It is scarcely wider than a room and never more than knee-deep, though no feet have entered it. Its floor is made of flat green stones laid in overlapping scales by erosion, each worn smooth and slightly concave. Water wells between them without bubbling. The surface is almost always still.

The pool reflects incorrectly.

It does not show the sky directly overhead. In clear weather it reflects a dense gray cloud; in cloud it shows open blue. At noon, when light is ordinary and shadows lie plainly beneath the trees, the pool may hold a dim amber dusk. These changes are not rapid, and there is no dramatic transformation. One must remain beside it for hours to be certain that the reflection belongs to some neighboring weather rather than the present one.

Rain arrives quietly. It forms first as condensation on the warm rim-rocks, then gathers into threads. The rock faces weep from hundreds of fine seams. Water descends without splashing, following grooves established so long ago that every runnel has become polished and dark. Some grooves are deep enough to cradle a hand; others are hairlines full of silver. Their courses curve around resistant knots in the stone, divide, reunite, and eventually vanish beneath the basin floor.

The erosion here is ancient but not ruinous. Nothing appears freshly broken. Every edge has already found the shape it can keep.

The enclosing cliffs are low, rounded, and hollowed into shallow niches. Mineral bands show through their damp surfaces: charcoal basalt, green-gray clay, a dull rose stone, and occasional seams of white crystal softened by water until they resemble buried bone. Where droplets fall repeatedly, the rock has developed small bowls. Each bowl is full to its lip. Overflow passes to the next, and then the next, in a descending chain so slow that the water seems to move by decision rather than gravity.

Mist often enters through the open side of the basin. It does not race or billow. It climbs the slope in a level sheet, covers the black ground, and stops at the height of the moss islands. Tree trunks rise from it like widely spaced pillars. For a while the pool disappears, though its displaced reflection may remain as a muted brightness under the fog.

There is little undergrowth. Mats of moss occupy protected stone, and solitary ferns stand in the seep-lines. Here and there grows a knee-high plant composed of three thick leaves, always three, each turned toward a different portion of the rim. Their surfaces are punctured with neat rows of holes. Water passing through these holes falls in separate threads onto selected points below, where the soil is darker and faintly warm.

The plants do not crowd one another. Perhaps they cannot. Around each trunk, fern, or three-leafed growth lies a clear interval in which nothing sprouts. Boundaries overlap nowhere. The open spaces are not barren; they are maintained. Fine roots beneath them continually sift the mud, removing new shoots and carrying their soft tissue back toward the established plants. Thus the basin remains lush in color and moisture but sparse in form: isolated greens surrounded by generous black and gray.

Decomposition makes almost no sound and gives off little smell. There is no rot in the animal sense. Fallen matter pales, becomes translucent, and collapses into wet fiber. Fungi do not fruit. Mold never blooms. The warm air smells of mineral rain, crushed stems, and the faint metallic scent released when stone is split.

Sound does not travel far. The enclosing rock seems to absorb it, and the open side gives it away to the cloud-filled drop. Most of what can be heard is intimate: a bead of water descending a leaf vein, the minute rasp of a root sharpening itself against basalt, the settling click of a pebble after months of pressure. Thunder, when storms move across the lower valleys, reaches the basin only as a softness in the ground.

The largest changes are measured in years.

A runnel deepens by the width of a hair.  
A tree turns one leaf toward the pool.  
A fern raises a stone chip from flat to upright.  
A mineral bowl overflows into a different groove.  
One of the widely spaced trunks completes a root-crossing and begins another grove-generation.

Nothing comes to feed, mate, hunt, nest, or die. There is no migration, no sudden alarm, no swarm after rain. Life here is vegetal and deliberate, with the slowness of things that have never needed to flee.

Beyond the open rim, the mountain falls through cloud toward lands that cannot be seen. The edge is cushioned with low, slick moss, but no plant grows across it. Roots approaching the drop stop several finger-widths short. Their tips harden and turn sideways, as if the basin were not merely enclosed by terrain but defined by a rule.

Water alone crosses.

It gathers at the lip in a thin transparent hem and spills into the white air below. There is no audible fall. The stream becomes mist before it can strike anything, joining the clouds that will, in time, rise again into the basin and bead upon the leaves.

So the place keeps itself: warm stone, separated trees, quiet workmanship, and water passing through every form without ever leaving for long.
