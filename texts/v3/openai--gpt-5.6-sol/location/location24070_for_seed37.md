Beneath a plain of pale, unbroken stone lies a cavity no wider than a cottage and no higher than a chapel. There is no visible entrance. The surrounding rock has not been worn away by any river or patient sea; it appears instead to have drawn apart recently, opening along a soft seam. Its surfaces are raw and undecided. Some are smooth as the inside of an eggshell, others granular, still holding the angular impressions of minerals that have not quite become stone.

The chamber is brilliantly lit.

The light does not enter from above. It occupies the open space itself: clear, cool, almost white, strongest a hand’s breadth away from any solid surface. Every wall therefore stands in a faint border of shade, while the empty center shines. A loose pebble casts its shadow inward in all directions. If lifted, it grows darker; if set down, it develops a narrow halo. Nothing here seems to burn or radiate. Brightness is simply one of the properties of distance.

In this inverted illumination, the chamber looks both exposed and secret.

The floor slopes toward three shallow hollows. Water gathers in them intermittently, though no stream feeds them. Condensation forms on dry stone as isolated silver beads. The beads swell, tremble, and begin to roll sideways or uphill, following seams too fine to see. Sometimes several meet and become a sudden thread of water, quick as a startled animal. It crosses the floor, fills one hollow, then vanishes into porous clay before the basin is half full.

Rain also occurs, but locally. A patch of air near the eastern wall may darken and release twenty or thirty cold drops while the rest of the chamber remains bright. The drops fall only a short distance. Some strike the ground. Some stop in midair and hang there for hours, quivering when living things pass beneath them. Around these suspended droplets the light bends into tiny rings, each containing an image of the chamber from a slightly different age.

Vegetation survives sparsely along the wettest cracks. There are thin black mosses with a metallic green sheen, several stands of jointed white stems, and one low plant whose four translucent leaves lie flat against the floor. None grows in continuous cover. Each patch is isolated by broad expanses of bare mineral: blue-gray clay, powdery calcite, and fresh planes of reddish rock that smell faintly of iron after rain.

The plants have no evident source of nourishment beyond moisture and brightness. Their roots do not divide. Each descends as a single filament until it becomes too fine to follow. The white stems open cup-shaped tips when the chamber is dry and close them when water arrives. The moss changes position so slowly that it appears stationary, but over several days its scattered patches exchange places, crossing the bare floor without leaving tracks.

Animal life is everywhere, though at first the chamber seems empty.

Small pale creatures cling motionless to the shadowed margins of stone. Most are no longer than a finger joint. There are glass-backed beetles, six-legged lizards with soft mouths, threadworms arranged in loose spirals, and mothlike animals whose wings are covered not in scales but in cool droplets. Tiny eyeless fish inhabit the temporary pools before those pools exist. When water gathers around them, they begin swimming as though they had merely been waiting in the dry air.

The largest residents are long-bodied, rabbit-sized grazers that move on many narrow feet. Their skin resembles damp parchment. They feed on the white stems without uprooting them, trimming each plant to the same height. Despite their numbers, they rarely touch one another. Each maintains an invisible interval from its neighbors, so a herd crossing the chamber resembles a moving constellation.

Predators are fewer. Flat, charcoal-colored animals hide where wall meets floor, compressing themselves until they are indistinguishable from the border of shade. They hunt by altering distance. A grazer may be several body lengths away; the predator unfolds one limb, and for the duration of that gesture the intervening space becomes very small. The capture is nearly silent. There is no chase, only a brief rearrangement and the soft sound of feet scattering across clay.

Nearly all life here is quiet. Wings open without fluttering. Beetles communicate by changing the direction of their shadows. The grazers warn one another by becoming still in sequence. Even feeding produces little more than a dry, delicate ticking.

The loudest sounds are geological: a dull click from inside the wall, the faint tearing of a seam extending by the width of a hair, or the sudden patter of local rain. At irregular intervals the chamber gives a low, almost inaudible sigh. Afterward it is marginally larger. New stone appears along one boundary, moist and pliable for a few minutes before hardening. This is not a cave being hollowed out. It is a cave still being made, though whether it is growing into the earth or the earth is withdrawing from it cannot be determined.

Scattered across the floor are small constructions. None suggests tools or hands. Arches of rib bone stand over certain cracks. Pebbles have been sorted into rings according to warmth. A wall niche is lined with hundreds of shed insect cases, all facing inward. The grazers occasionally add a bone to one of the arches, carrying it carefully in their mouths. Beetles maintain the pebble rings, rolling displaced stones back into place after rain. These structures serve purposes that overlap without agreeing: shelter, courtship ground, trap, nursery, memorial.

One miniature enclosure near the central hollow is built of dried stems woven through natural holes in the floor. It is always empty when observed directly. In reflections on suspended droplets, however, it is crowded with animals not otherwise present in the chamber: dark birds, miniature antlered things, a coiled creature with a luminous throat. Their reflections look outward with the alert patience of captives.

The place obeys several dependable rules.

Water remembers downwardness, but not always the same downwardness.

Living creatures cast one shadow for where they are and another for where they will next rest.

No animal can cross the exact center of the chamber alone. Solitary creatures turn aside without seeming to notice. Two may cross together, or a predator may cross while carrying prey.

Anything buried in the fresh clay returns to the surface altered but not aged. A seed emerges as a tooth. A bone emerges porous and leaf-shaped. A dead beetle emerges alive, though belonging to a different species.

And the chamber cannot be counted consistently. From one end it contains three pools; from the other, four. There are seven white-stem colonies when approached along the northern wall and six when approached from the south. Attempts to mark the discrepancy fail because the marks themselves become part of what is being counted.

Yet this instability does not make the place chaotic. Its ecology is precise. Condensation awakens the suspended fish. The fish consume threadworms. Their movement shakes droplets from the air, watering the moss. Grazers follow the moss as it migrates. Predators thin the herds. Bones become arches where mothlike animals lay clear eggs. When the eggs hatch, their empty membranes collect brightness, causing nearby stone to soften and the chamber to widen.

Thus life participates in the making of its own hidden world.

During the driest intervals, the pools contract to dark stains and the white plants fold flat. Most animals withdraw into seams too narrow to admit them. The brightness intensifies until the chamber is almost without visible depth, a white volume interrupted by islands of stone. In that severe light, the raw walls can be seen flexing slowly. Mineral crystals turn within them like unborn teeth.

Then a coolness gathers. A single drop appears in the open air. Others form around it, each perfectly still. The black moss begins its imperceptible migration. Fish tails flick inside water that has not yet fallen. From the wall comes a soft click.

Somewhere beyond the chamber, perhaps centimeters away and perhaps impossibly far, the earth draws apart again.
