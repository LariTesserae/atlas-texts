The coast is almost entirely underwater. Only the highest points of the old mountains remain above the surface: black stacks, low skerries, and a few narrow islands whose summits are continually stripped by wind. From them the flooded range falls nearly four kilometers to the ocean floor. The descent is not gradual. It is a country of submarine cliffs, drowned valleys, knife-backed ridges, and immense stairways worn into the bedrock before the sea rose over them.

The stone is very old. Basalt lies beneath paler layers of slate and quartzite, all softened by ages of pressure, glaciation, rivers, and finally salt water. There are few truly sharp edges left. Even the cliffs have broad, bowed faces, as if the entire range has been handled for a very long time. Boulders rest in hollows polished to their shape. Canyons follow faults that were ancient before the first forests grew on land. On the abyssal plain, the last debris has settled into long gray fans of silt, and the mountains seem less ruined than finished—eroded down to forms that storms can batter but no longer substantially change.

The water above them is not calm.

Cold offshore currents strike the drowned range and are forced upward. Twice each day the tide pours through its passes with the force of a river in flood. In the narrowest channels, the sea becomes a horizontal cataract. Sand streams over the bottom. Ropes of kelp stand nearly flat in the flow, their blades snapping and flickering in ranks. At the surface, opposing currents raise steep, close-packed waves even in fair weather. During storms the whole upper sea turns white, and rain closes the distance between ocean and sky until there appears to be no boundary at all—only a thick, gray, moving volume with islands embedded in it.

Yet the water is often clear below the storm layer. Daylight penetrates the upper ridges in broad green shafts. On calm mornings, the submerged slopes can be seen from boats as alternating bands of forest and naked rock, descending until color fails. The light is ordinary daylight, changed only by depth: green among the kelp, blue along open precipices, then a dim metallic dusk. Far below, the terrain is visible in fragments under the lamps of maintenance vehicles and deep-diving submersibles.

Vegetation occupies every surface that receives enough light. The drowned summits carry dense kelp forests, some growing from rock thirty meters below the surface and reaching almost to the air. Their trunks are thick, rubbery, and scarred by grazing animals. Fronds gather into a shifting canopy under which the water is cool and shadowed. Red algae coat the deeper ledges. Bladder wrack and rockweed crowd the tidal margins of the surviving islands, while eelgrass fills sedimented saddles between ridges. In sheltered bowls, detached vegetation accumulates in slow-turning rafts several meters deep, alive with amphipods, juvenile fish, and feeding birds.

There is little bare ground in the shallows. Where plants cannot hold, mussels do. Where mussels are torn away, barnacles and tube worms settle. Sponges occupy the undersides of overhangs. Pale anemones pack the walls of the channels so densely that the rock appears furred. The abundance is layered rather than spacious: plants over rock, shellfish under plants, fish within both, and clouds of plankton passing through everything.

Farther down, the kelp gives way to animal forests. Cold-water corals grow along the current-facing cliffs in brittle white thickets. Basket stars open their arms above them. Sea pens stand in fields on the softer terraces. The deepest slopes are less colorful but not empty. Brittle stars carpet the sediment; crabs travel in loose columns; eels occupy cracks in the slate. The upwelling carries enough food that even the darkness is busy.

Schools of herring move through the drowned valleys like weather. They tighten into silver walls when pursued by seals, tuna, and dolphins, then loosen again across kilometers of water. Cod and pollock gather around the ridges. Sharks patrol the outer escarpment. In spring, whales enter the channels to feed where the current concentrates plankton and small fish. Their blows are briefly visible between waves; underwater, their calls travel from one mountain basin to another.

The place is loud. Near the surface there is the continuous concussion of waves against rock, the hiss of rain, the crack of kelp stems under strain, and the shouting of seabird colonies. Beneath it is a heavier register: cobbles rolling in surge channels, boulders knocking together, hulls laboring at anchor, chains dragging, propellers turning, whales calling, and the persistent mechanical hum of tidal generators. Sound carries far in the cold water. A vessel may be heard long before its lights appear. During the strongest tides, the submerged passes produce a low roar that can be felt through a hull or through the soles of boots on an island pier.

Human structures are few because building here is difficult and keeping anything intact is more difficult still. A lighthouse stands on the largest island, short and massively buttressed, with no decorative height. Its windows are small; its doors face away from the prevailing weather. Around it are a weather station, a fuel shed, and the concrete foundations of earlier buildings that the sea took.

Elsewhere, construction is mostly submerged. Power cables lie in trenches cut across the high saddles. Tidal turbines occupy several deep channels, each mounted low between armored pylons so that ships can pass above. Their rotors turn slowly in the current, large enough to be mistaken at a distance for dark openings in the rock. Service platforms cling to a few sheltered cliff faces. They are plain structures of steel grating, cranes, pressure housings, and yellow-painted railings. Every exposed part is rounded, reinforced, and replaceable. Paint peels quickly. Steel rusts wherever coatings fail. Within a few seasons, mussels and anemones obscure the distinction between machine and reef.

There are also remnants of older industries. A corroded winch sits on a submerged quay built when the water was lower. Rail tracks emerge from gravel and vanish beneath kelp. On one island, roofless stone sheds mark a former quarry. Their cut blocks were once lowered to boats through a narrow harbor; now seals sleep on the loading ramp, and waves pass through the workers’ rooms. Several wrecks lie in the deeper valleys, but they are treated less as monuments than as hazards and habitats. Nets snag on them. Conger eels live in their engine compartments. Copper fittings are slowly stripped by salvage crews when the weather allows.

Nothing here stays unattended for long. Storms arrive quickly from the open ocean. Tides reverse with little pause. Fog forms when damp air crosses the cold upwelling and can cover the islands while the water below remains bright. Boats follow marked routes along the ridge tops because the valleys are full of crosscurrents. Divers plan around minutes rather than hours. Loose equipment is tied twice. Any surface not cleaned becomes living substrate.

The basic rule of the landscape is that the land is stable and everything around it is in motion. The mountains have reached their old, enduring shapes, but water races over them; forests bend and regrow; shoals gather and scatter; turbines turn; sediment lifts, travels, and settles elsewhere. Change is constant at the scale of minutes and seasons, while the underlying country barely alters at all.

Seen from above in clear weather, it is a dark archipelago in a cold gray sea. Seen from below, it is an immense vertical province: sunlit forests on the summits, crowded ledges along the descent, industrial machines in the passes, and old valleys continuing downward through blue water into darkness. It is open enough to feel oceanic, dense enough that almost every current carries life, and loud enough that true silence exists only inside the stone.
