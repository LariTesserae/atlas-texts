The plateau begins above the ordinary weather.

It is not a single tableland but an immensity of fresh black shelves, tilted against one another as if a continent had risen too quickly and not yet decided how to bear its own weight. Ridges run for thousands of kilometers without softening. Their edges are sharp, vitreous, newly split. Escarpments expose bands of green mineral, black glass, sulfur-yellow crystal, and pale stone still hot enough to blur the air. There are few valleys in the old sense. The low places are cracks: deep, narrow wounds where condensation gathers and where brief rivers appear after rain.

Despite the altitude, the air is warm. Heat rises continuously from below, passing through the plateau in broad invisible columns. Clouds form low over these vents, sometimes directly against the ground. They are dense, charcoal-colored clouds with no bright upper surface. They extinguish distance. Even at midday the landscape lies in a bruised twilight, illuminated less by the sky than by the muted radiance of minerals, fungal tissues, and electrical discharges moving beneath translucent leaves.

Moisture is intermittent but everywhere anticipated. Nothing here expects a permanent stream. Life gathers around recurrence rather than abundance.

Rain comes in narrow territories. A storm may occupy a strip only twenty paces wide and several hundred kilometers long, traveling like a curtain dragged across the plateau. On one side of it, dust remains dry and warm; beneath it, rain falls in heavy vertical ropes; on the other, droplets rise from the ground and join the clouds. These moving rain corridors leave temporary ecosystems behind them. Black hollows fill. Cracks become torrents. Shallow basins shine for an hour, then vanish into thirsty stone or are lifted whole as mist.

The vegetation is thickest in the fractures.

From above, each fissure looks packed with dark green fur. Close by, the apparent softness resolves into layered growth: blade-forests, braided mats, inflated membranes, jointed trunks growing downward as often as upward. Nothing resembles a tree for long. Tall organisms raise ribbed columns into the dim air, open arrays of heat-sensitive vanes, and then collapse into coils when the ground trembles. Creeping plants cross bare rock on thousands of tiny contractile roots. They do not grow continuously; they advance in coordinated pulses, stopping all at once to drink condensation from the stone.

In the dampest ravines, vegetation becomes nearly impassable. Broad leaves overlap like roof scales, though no roof has ever been made here. Their upper surfaces are matte black and absorb the weak light; their undersides glow a submerged blue. Hanging threads sift minerals from fog. Bladder-like fruits collect rain and preserve it for months, becoming small suspended ponds crowded with larvae and translucent grazers.

There are no ruins, roads, fences, towers, or tools. No hand has arranged the plateau. Yet much of it has the character of an industrial region glimpsed at night.

The industry belongs to the land itself.

Along certain ridges stand fields of hollow mineral chimneys, grown by pressure and chemical deposition. Warm gases pass through them in measured cycles. Valves of living stone open and close. Deep underground, vast chambers compress vapor until the crust flexes; then entire chimney fields exhale together, producing clouds of metallic pollen. Basalt belts creep over rollers of harder crystal, carrying fresh lava-glass from vents to cooling plains. In caverns below, magnetic strata turn against one another like buried turbines. Their rotation electrifies the plateau and powers ecologies that have no use for sunlight.

Nothing was designed, but everything appears to be processing something.

One slope refines airborne salts. Another separates metals from rainwater through forests of conductive reeds. A plain of soft gray nodules absorbs heat by day and releases it into the roots of migrating vegetation at night. Pools accumulate dense, iridescent liquids, which are pumped uphill by the synchronized contractions of organisms kilometers long. Waste products emerge as reefs of chalk or as warm, sterile winds. Production is immense, continuous, and purposeless—or its purpose occurs at a scale at which purpose and geology are the same thing.

For all this activity, the plateau is mostly quiet.

The moving belts make no scrape. Chimneys vent below the threshold of hearing. Rain enters leaves without pattering. Even rockfalls descend in eerie silence, their sound arriving minutes or days later, detached from the event. At times a traveler might see a cliff collapse across the horizon, watch the dust spread, and feel nothing through the ground. Then, while standing beside a still pool weeks later, the missing impact comes up through the water as one soft, colossal note.

The animals have adapted to this separation of cause and sound.

They are abundant but rarely conspicuous. Warm-bodied herds move beneath plant mats, visible only as traveling humps. Six-legged browsers cling to vertical glass faces, licking condensation with fans of silver cilia. Dense clouds of seed-sized flyers gather around electrical fields but do not beat their wings; they alter their buoyancy and are carried along invisible gradients. Long, low predators inhabit the boundary between stone and shadow. They flatten themselves until they are only changes in reflectivity, then rise when prey steps over them.

Many creatures carry water inside them. Their bodies are reservoirs, and smaller lives gather at their joints and breathing pores. A great plateau walker may cross the dry shelves on dozens of pillar-like limbs, bearing an entire damp thicket across its back. In that mobile garden live grazers, scavengers, pollinators, parasites, and small aerial hunters that never descend to the ground. When the walker pauses over a crack, roots unfold from its underside and temporarily graft it to the ravine ecology. For a few hours, two worlds exchange blood, spores, heat, and memory. Then the roots detach and the animal moves on.

Because the terrain is young, no species is fully settled. Evolution seems hurried here. Forms alter between generations, sometimes between one side of an animal and the other. A brood born beside a sulfur vent may develop mineral lungs; its siblings carried into fog grow sails. Eggs respond not merely to temperature and moisture but to the future conditions of the place where they will hatch. This is not foresight. Time on the plateau leaks backward in small ecological quantities.

The instability becomes stronger toward the interior.

There the land does not remain reliably beneath the sky. Distances fold during warm fogs. Two ridges separated by a hundred kilometers may touch for the duration of a rainfall, allowing animals and root systems to cross, then withdraw without leaving a path between them. A ravine can have several depths at once. Stones dropped into it return wet, scorched, frozen, or covered in mature moss. Some never fall; they become smaller with distance until they resemble stars below the observer.

Storms do not merely move across the plateau. They revise it.

A dark front passes, and a field of chimneys becomes a forest of fleshy tubes. Another storm strips color from a basin and deposits it in a neighboring valley, where every organism gleams too vividly for a season. Lightning strikes before the clouds assemble. Ponds rain upward. Cliffs molt. Entire food webs condense overnight from mineral vapor, swarm for three days, and harden into red stone.

The life here treats such transformations as weather. Creatures migrate not only toward water and warmth but toward favorable versions of physical law. Flocks cluster where gravity weakens. Burrowers follow zones in which stone briefly becomes porous to flesh. Predators wait at the edges of causal reversals, where a fleeing animal may encounter the wound before it encounters the hunter.

There are rules, but they are local.

In the eastern shelves, weight increases with age. Ancient spores lie embedded like lead shot while newborn giants step lightly over leaves. In the high central fracture, living things cast shadows toward sources of moisture rather than light. Around the magnetic caverns, symmetry is metabolically expensive: every creature grows deliberately uneven, with mismatched limbs and spiraled organs, shedding any repeated feature before it can draw a charge from the ground. On the western escarpments, names would be dangerous if there were anyone to give them. Repetition fixes a thing in place, and fixed things cannot survive the moving geology.

The plateau’s largest events occur almost without warning. A region the size of a sea may rise by several hundred meters in an afternoon. Fresh walls split open, gleaming and uneroded. Heat-loving vegetation spills from the cracks already mature, as if it had been waiting inside the rock. Animals pour after it in orderly, silent multitudes. Behind them come slow waves of mineral machinery: rotating crystal masses, peristaltic ore-veins, towers extruded by steam. Within days the new province is dense with exchange and predation. Within weeks it may fold back underground.

Nothing acquires the patience to become old.

There are pockets of calm. In sheltered hollows, condensation pearls along black stems and drips into shallow pools. The water is warm and perfectly clear. Pale swimmers hang motionless beneath the surface, each surrounded by a faint halo of its possible movements. Small creatures come to drink, placing their feet carefully among reflections that do not always belong to them. Above, the cloud ceiling turns slowly. Far away, a line of rain advances across a copper-colored plain.

In such places the plateau seems almost gentle. The air smells of wet stone, hot leaves, ozone, and something faintly sweet fermenting underground. A membrane opens to catch a drop. A glass-backed grazer shifts its weight. Blue light moves through a root.

Then the pool shivers though there is no wind. Its surface tilts toward a horizon that did not exist a moment before. The swimmers choose among their halos and disappear into motions they have not yet made. Across the plateau, millions of living vanes fold shut in absolute unison.

Still no sound comes.

The cliffs begin to rise.
