High on the back of an almost vanished mountain, where the summit has been worn down to a broad pavement of red-black stone, there is a hollow no larger than a village square.

From a distance it cannot be seen. The mountain appears finished: an ancient, level-backed mass standing above warm plains, its cliffs rounded and fluted by ages of wind. Nothing sharp remains. Even the hardest outcrops have softened into low ribs like the buried bones of some enormous animal. Rain has passed over this summit for so long that the rock remembers water better than the country below does, though water itself is rarely present.

The hollow lies beneath an overhang only a few paces high. Its entrance is a horizontal seam in the pavement, screened by pale grasses and the downward-curving roots of a figlike shrub. One crawls through hot light and dry dust, then descends abruptly into green shadow.

Inside, the world is warm, damp, and deafening.

The hollow is a weathered stone bowl, perhaps forty meters across, set deep within the summit. Its walls are not cliffs so much as surfaces polished by incalculable passage. Mineral bands wander through them in faded colors—rust, smoke, dull violet, old cream. There are no loose blocks and almost no gravel. Every vulnerable edge has already been removed. Channels once cut by torrents have widened, merged, and settled into shallow grooves that lead toward a chain of black pools.

These pools are seldom more than knee-deep. They appear and disappear without obvious relation to rain. At noon, when the plateau above is driest, beads of condensation gather under the overhang and run down hair-fine threads stretched across the chamber. At night, the pools shrink. Sometimes, during a storm, they remain perfectly level while the walls sweat and the plants drip. Water here does not behave as if pulled only by gravity. It moves according to older, more local agreements.

The cause—or accomplice—is scattered throughout the hollow.

Set into the stone are several structures like little roofless shrines. The largest could be enclosed by two outstretched arms. Their surfaces resemble dark ceramic, though they are neither fired clay nor metal. They have been rounded by erosion along with the native rock, suggesting that they have stood here longer than the present mountain shape. Yet at certain angles their faces remain impossibly exact. A hand placed on one feels a faint inward pressure, as though the material contains more distance than its dimensions allow.

Each shrine holds an empty aperture. Not a hole, precisely: an oval absence in which the far wall cannot be seen. Gnats pass into these apertures and emerge from others seconds or hours later, wet with water from no known source. Roots enter them and return as pale branching filaments. During the hottest part of the afternoon, transparent droplets fall upward through the ovals and spread across the ceiling.

No machinery hums. No lights pulse. The devices work quietly, if work is what they are doing. Their only visible activity is the arrangement of moisture, temperature, and living traffic. They seem less like pumps than edits made to the hollow’s conditions.

Around them vegetation grows in dense islands separated by open stone. This gives the chamber a contradictory character: luxuriant without being crowded. Mats of liverwort and thick emerald moss occupy the grooves where water travels. Broad-leaved plants rise in isolated clumps, their leaves held nearly vertical to shed the heavy drips that fall without warning. Small ferns grow in rings around bare patches, avoiding the precise places where the stone occasionally becomes cold.

There are three small trees. None is taller than a person. Their trunks are silver, swollen, and hollow, holding reserves of clear water. Their crowns do not touch. Between them lie expanses of polished rock on which nothing grows except a crust of orange microbes and scattered blue-green coins of lichen.

Every plant seems individually placed, though no gardener is present. Seedlings that sprout too close together are cut apart by beetles. Vines crossing the open pavement are clipped by small grazing lizards. Fungal threads below the moss poison roots that trespass into neighboring patches. The spacing is not peaceful; it is enforced.

The animals are everywhere.

They exceed the visible capacity of the hollow. They crowd the air, water, soil, bark, and stone pores, yet leave the open spaces strangely clear. Clouds of metallic midges occupy fixed columns beneath the roof. Thumb-sized moths sleep in overlapping tiles under leaves. Ants travel in six permanent roads, each road a different species and color. Pale crabs live in the highest wall cracks, far from the pools, catching condensation in their raised claws. Tree frogs no larger than seeds inflate translucent throats. Long-bodied lizards pour across the warm rock in sudden coordinated streams, then vanish so completely that the pavement seems empty again.

The noise is immense. Cicadas produce a continuous grinding tone that vibrates in the ribs. Frogs answer in percussive bursts. Wings rattle against leaves. Beetles click from inside the stone shrines. From the pools comes the wet slap of small fish pursuing smaller fish, though some of these pools have been dry for weeks. Above everything sounds the intermittent booming of the overhang itself: a low, resonant report, like distant thunder inside a sealed room.

At first the calls appear chaotic. After long listening, patterns emerge. The entire hollow quiets for one breath every seventeen minutes. In that silence, water changes direction. Droplets lift from leaves. The black pools dimple from below. Shadows shift by a few degrees, although the sun has not moved enough to account for it.

Then one insect begins again, and the rest return in layers.

Larger animals visit by routes too narrow for them. A spotted cat appears occasionally beside the central pool without disturbing the grass at the entrance. It drinks, watches the oval apertures, and leaves through a gap only a crab could use. Troops of small, black-faced monkeys descend from the ceiling vines at dawn, despite there being no forest above the hollow. Their fur is often dusted with yellow pollen from flowers that do not grow anywhere on the mountain. Once they have fed, they climb behind one of the dark shrines and are gone.

Predators are numerous but brief. Nothing hunts for long. A mantis takes one midge and freezes. A snake swallows one frog, then lies still for days. The cat never kills within the chamber. Appetite seems to be measured and rationed, as if excess predation would violate a physical limit. When one species multiplies too quickly, the hollow does not produce famine. Instead, its surplus individuals begin disappearing into the apertures. Weeks later, different animals may emerge: related in posture or pattern, but altered to fill some newly vacant role.

Warmth remains even at night. The bare summit outside cools under the stars, but the bowl keeps the temperature of sun-warmed skin. Brief rains cross the plateau in hard silver sheets. Most water races over the ancient pavement and plunges from the mountain edge. Only a little enters the seam. Yet within the hollow every shower initiates days of activity. Moss swells. Fungi unfold paper-thin fans. Winged ants erupt in such numbers that the air becomes granular. Frogs lay ropes of eggs in temporary films no deeper than a fingernail.

By the time the outside rock is dry, the hollow has stored the storm in a hundred forms: tree trunks, snail shells, swollen roots, animal blood, cool ceramic surfaces, and the unreachable interior of the apertures.

Human traces are rare. Near the entrance, shallow footholds have been polished into the descending stone. Three low walls, each only two courses high, stand around a patch of bare floor. Their masonry is crude compared with the dark shrines and much younger, though still old enough for mineral skins to have grown across the joints. A corroded bowl rests in a niche. Beside it, someone long ago scratched rows of animals into the wall, counting not individuals but entrances and exits.

The final row is unfinished.

No path leads to the hollow from the plains. No ruin crowns the mountain to announce it. From above, even sophisticated instruments would find only old stone, sparse shrubs, and heat. The chamber is concealed not merely by its size but by the way attention slides from it. Travelers crossing the summit tend to walk around the entrance without noticing they have altered course. Birds flying overhead avoid casting shadows across the seam.

The place may be a refuge, a laboratory, a condensation engine, or the remnant of some planetary system whose other components have eroded into dust. It may have been designed to preserve species, though it preserves relationships more faithfully than species themselves. Bodies change. Niches persist. A pollinator is always present, but not always the same kind of creature. A hunter occupies the warm wall, a grazer trims the vines, a burrower opens the moss, a singer marks the water’s reversal. The roles are ancient; the actors are provisional.

That is the chief rule of the hollow: nothing living is permitted to become indispensable.

Its second rule is that emptiness must remain. The polished spaces between green islands are never colonized for long. Seeds land there and germinate, but by morning they have been moved. Moss advances and is peeled back. Insects swarm above the bare stone without settling. The open areas seem to be reservoirs of possibility, kept vacant so the chamber can rearrange itself when conditions change.

Its third rule is less certain.

Anything that stays through enough cycles begins to be counted among the landscape.

Boot prints cease to gather dust. Breath joins the rhythm of condensation. The insects adjust their silences around a new heartbeat. Eventually, one of the small shrines acquires another aperture—an oval dark enough to hold no reflection, shaped approximately to the visitor’s standing form.

Outside, the old mountain remains broad, warm, and nearly barren. Wind crosses its worn summit. Occasional rain darkens the stone and is gone.

Inside, hidden within that final, flattened form, the little world roars on.
