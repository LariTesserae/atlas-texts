The place is a low limestone basin, no wider than a small hamlet and sunk only a little below the surrounding plain. Its boundaries are close and definite: a ring of weathered rock, pale as old bone where dry and dark as iron where wet. Nothing grows along the rim. No grass threads the cracks, no moss softens the shaded faces. From a distance the basin looks abandoned and almost empty. From within, it is crowded beyond measure.

Water has shaped every handspan of it.

The floor is packed with rounded limestone bosses, travertine shelves, shallow cups, drainage lips, calcified channels, and blunt pillars joined at their bases. There is scarcely room to place a foot flat. Warm water lies between the formations in innumerable basins, some no larger than a palm, others broad enough for a person to crouch in. It stands just beneath crusts thin as pottery glaze and seeps through porous stone wherever pressure permits. Along the walls, moisture condenses and gathers into clear beads. Each bead swells, releases, and falls into the same hollow worn by generations of drops.

The formations are not young or jagged. Their edges have been rounded into settled shapes: saddles, knuckles, bowls, low dams. Old channels have polished interiors the color of wax. Deposits have sealed their own fractures, so the basin seems both eroded and repaired, worn down and built up by the same water. The pattern is dense but orderly, the result of a flow so consistent that each mineral lip has reached a kind of balance with the current crossing it.

There is no open stream. Water moves by seepage, overflow, and capillary crawl. A pool fills exactly to its rim, passes a transparent sheet into the next, and remains apparently unchanged. Beneath the floor, a warm spring supplies the basin at nearly the same rate that evaporation and a single buried outlet remove it. The whole place persists in equilibrium. Water is always moving, but the landscape does not visibly alter. The falls do not strengthen after rain. The pools do not shrink in dry weather. There are no flood marks or stranded rings showing former depths.

The air is warm and close. It smells faintly of wet chalk, salt, and sulfur, though none of these odors is strong enough to sting. Moisture collects on skin and cloth. Metal, where any remains, tarnishes quickly; wood softens, blackens, and eventually disappears. Sound is absorbed by the irregular floor and low encircling walls. What remains is minute: the tick of droplets, the nearly inaudible hiss of water passing over stone, an occasional hollow plink from somewhere beneath the crust.

Light reaches the basin weakly. The surrounding rim blocks the low sun, and pale vapor hangs in the depression without ever becoming thick fog. At midday the water catches dull silver reflections. Under overcast skies, stone and water become difficult to distinguish. Depth is read less by color than by the way reflections tremble—or fail to tremble—over a surface.

The absence of vegetation makes the place unusually exposed. Every old cut and scrape remains legible. On a dry shelf near the eastern wall stand three small structures made without mortar. They are little more than waist-high windbreaks of fitted limestone, roofless now, with narrow openings turned away from the basin. Their stones have been selected for shape rather than dressed. Beside them are shallow working hollows pecked into the rock, a broken hammerstone, and several wedges of dense dark stone brought from elsewhere.

Whoever used the basin appears to have harvested mineral crusts from its margins. Parallel scoring marks survive on a few shelves where deposits were pried loose in slabs. Small cairns mark safe footholds through the crowded pools. A narrow crossing has been made from flat stones laid one after another, though mineral water has swollen their edges and fixed them to the natural floor. There are no wheels, pipes, hinges, or elaborate mechanisms—only evidence of hands, leverage, baskets, and patient repetition.

Nothing suggests permanent habitation. There is no hearth, no midden, and no burial ground. The warm damp would have spoiled stores, and there is nothing here to feed on. The structures were probably shelters for brief work, built by people who entered, gathered what they needed, and left before dark.

Animal life is scarce. At long intervals, a pale gnat may rest on the water without laying eggs. Tiny translucent crustaceans inhabit a few underground channels, seldom appearing except as a quick movement beneath a thin calcite ledge. Birds avoid landing because there are no clear open surfaces and no prey worth searching for. Tracks are almost absent. When they do appear in a patch of soft mineral mud, they are usually those of a solitary fox or small hoofed animal that came to drink, tested the bitter water, and departed by the same narrow route.

Below the visible waterline, some pools carry a colorless microbial film. It is detectable only as a slight drag on the reflection or a slickness under the fingers. It forms no mats and stains no stone. Otherwise, this is a mineral ecology: water sorting dissolved salts, vapor feeding condensation, calcite closing one pore as pressure opens another.

The basin has simple rules. Anything left in the seepage acquires a white shell. Anything placed across a channel eventually becomes part of the channel. Loose dust is washed into the lower cups. Heavy objects settle until the stone grips them. Warmth remains near constant because the spring rises from deep rock, and the enclosing walls blunt changes in weather. There are no sudden events here. Even decay proceeds quietly, concealed beneath successive mineral skins.

Near the center is the largest pool, only a few paces across but difficult to approach through the packed formations. Its surface is perfectly level, dark in the dimness, and warm enough to raise a faint breath of vapor. A stone measuring bowl has been set into one edge. Its rim is worn smooth by handling. The bowl is full, and a single thread of water passes through a notch cut in its side.

It may have been left yesterday or centuries ago. In this basin, the distinction is hard to see.
