The city stands on a low shelf of pale mineral stone, scarcely higher than the warm sea. From a distance it seems to have been built out over the water; nearer, it becomes difficult to tell where construction ends and the ground begins. Walls, lanes, terraces, cistern lips, and harbor steps are all made from the same chalk-colored substance, a soft stone continuously laid down by springs rising beneath the city.

Nothing here looks weathered. Corners remain blunt but unbroken. Courtyards have no hollows worn by feet, and the faces of the buildings show none of the vertical stains of age. The land is young—not dramatically volcanic, not fractured or smoking, but still in the quiet stage of becoming solid. Warm water seeps through every joint carrying dissolved lime. Wherever it reaches the air, it leaves a skin of stone. A dropped rope stiffens. A broken jar acquires a white rim. Shells, bones, posts, and old baskets are slowly incorporated into the masonry.

The city must therefore be chipped free of itself.

Its inhabitants work with stone hammers, shell blades, hardwood wedges brought by boat, and a few dark copper tools treated as precious communal property. Every doorway has a shallow gutter around it. Every stair is recut each morning, though “morning” is only a customary word beneath the permanent overcast. Crews scrape the channels before mineral crusts can seal them. The pale flakes are gathered, crushed, and mixed with wet clay to patch roofs and raise new walls. Building is not separate from maintenance. The whole settlement is an argument conducted by hand against the tendency of water to make everything one piece.

The terrain beyond the houses is broad, level, and crowded with shallow basins. Some are natural spring pools; others have been squared and embanked until their origin is uncertain. Water lies near the surface everywhere. Press a heel into the gray sediment between stone ridges and a clear film wells around it. The sea enters through hundreds of narrow channels, while warm inland seepage pushes outward beneath it. Fresh, salt, and mineral waters meet without ever fully mixing. There are milky ponds, transparent green runnels, sulfur-yellow trickles, and black tidal trenches whose bottoms cannot be seen under the dim sky.

There is no vegetation. No grass softens the margins. No moss grows on the damp walls, and no reeds stand in the marshy reaches. The land contains stone, water, salt, clay, shell, skin, feather, rope, pottery, and bone, but nothing leafed or rooted. Food plants do not grow here. Timber, fiber, fruit, and grain arrive in low boats from distant coasts, wrapped against the wet. Their rarity gives the markets a severe, fragrant richness: dried dates, plaited cord, flour sealed in pots, strips of bark, bundles of reeds already cut elsewhere. Even a splinter of wood is collected after use.

Yet the place is crowded with animals.

The mineral flats support dense mats of small water creatures: translucent shrimp, red worms, brine fleas, soft-shelled crabs, and clouds of midge larvae. They are not obvious at first. The pools appear empty until a shadow passes over them, when the whole bottom shivers. Fish move through the deeper channels in packed, silver-sided schools. Eels knot themselves in the warm drain mouths. Crabs occupy gaps in the masonry and emerge in such numbers that the streets seem to crawl sideways at the turning of the tide.

Birds cover every unclaimed ledge. White, gray, black, and rust-colored bodies stand shoulder to shoulder on roofs and harbor walls. Long-legged waders patrol the basins. Broad-billed birds sift the pale mud. Diving birds strike the canals like thrown stones. Their nesting places are built from shells, pottery fragments, fish spines, cord scraps, and bits of human refuse, since there are no twigs to be found. Eggs lie directly in shallow cups pecked into mineral crust.

The people keep few land animals. Lean dogs sleep on warm paving slabs, their coats perpetually damp. Small pigs are confined to raised stone pens and fed fish offal and imported husks. Most animal life belongs to the water and the air, and almost none of it is quiet.

The sound is immense.

Warm springs rise under pressure, pouring through vents with a continuous subterranean roar. Water drums from roof to roof and rushes along channels cut between the houses. Waves strike the outer shelf, not with separate impacts but as a sustained crushing note. Birds scream over every court. Fishers shout from one bank to another because ordinary speech cannot travel. Shell hammers ring against crusted stone. Potters beat air from clay. Crabs rattle in baskets. Dogs bark at birds that do not move.

In the lower quarters, where masonry encloses narrow resonant alleys, the noise becomes physical. It vibrates in jars and teeth. Doors are covered with thick hanging hides, not for warmth but to soften the pressure of sound. People speak close to the ear or use a developed language of hands, brows, and shoulders. Market prices are signaled by finger positions. Harbor warnings are given with colored slabs lifted above the crowd. Children learn gestures before they learn to shout.

The light has no clear source. A lid of humid cloud lies over the coast, bright enough to reveal detail but too dense to cast shadows. The air is warm and nearly saturated. Moisture beads on skin, polished shell, and fired clay. At times droplets condense from the air without any distinct rain beginning; at other times a fine rain falls, but the landscape does not seem altered by it. Everything was already wet.

There is also no visible season. The sea maintains the same height within its channels. The springs run at the same force. Birds nest, hatch, feed, and nest again, but across the city these stages overlap so completely that no progression can be read from them. There are always eggs, always fledglings, always molting adults, always dead birds being cleared from cistern covers. Boats arrive and depart, walls are raised and chipped back, children become workers and workers disappear, yet the city’s total form seems fixed.

From above it would look less like a collection of buildings than a crowded mineral reef adapted for human habitation. The streets divide and rejoin like tidal channels. Houses are low, flat-roofed, and nearly windowless, pressed together to preserve dry interior space. Roofs carry shallow settling trays where drinking water is allowed to shed its mineral load. Public reservoirs occupy the center of each district, capped by heavy stone plates to keep out birds. Kilns stand on the outer margins, fed with imported charcoal, dried dung, fish oil, and cakes of rendered fat. Their smoke remains trapped beneath the clouds, lending the dim air a faint brown cast and a smell of wet limestone, salt, feathers, and burning grease.

No tower rises high. The ground is too soft beneath the crust, and height would gain nothing in the low cloud. Civic importance is expressed by thickness and enclosure instead. The central meeting house is a vast squat chamber supported on ranks of stone piers. Its interior is one of the few quiet places, though quiet here means that the roar becomes distant enough for voices to exist. The walls sweat clear water. Mineral curtains have begun to form along the ceiling joints, but attendants scrape them down before they can close the room.

Outside the last dense quarters, causeways extend across the flats toward fishing stations, shell beds, and boat landings. They are lined with cairns so travelers can find them in sea mist. On either side lies a seemingly endless field of shallow water and newborn stone. Pale ridges rise just beneath the surface, not yet land but no longer entirely seabed. Pools are crowded with life; the spaces between pools are bare as pottery.

There are no ruins. Anything abandoned is sealed, filled, and smoothed over by mineral deposition until it becomes ground. The city has no deep visible past because the landscape does not permit one. It preserves objects perfectly by burying them in stone, while erasing their outlines from the surface. Beneath any plaza there may be earlier rooms, boats, pens, or streets, all intact within the white mass, but excavation is difficult and rarely attempted. The inhabitants build upward by a hand’s breadth, then chip downward by the same amount. Across generations, the city neither rises nor sinks.

Its governing principle is balance. Water brings stone; hands remove stone. The sea brings animals; the city consumes them. Boats bring the products of distant vegetation; nothing green takes hold locally. Waste feeds the shoals, shoals feed the birds and people, and shells return to walls and roads. Noise never ceases long enough to mark an event. Warmth never relaxes into cold. The sky never opens.

The place is not dead or frozen. It is intensely inhabited, laborious, wet, crowded, and fertile in every way except the green one. But all its movement occurs inside a larger stillness. Seen on any day, in any generation, the same pale city crouches beside the same warm gray sea, newly made and already complete, while water passes through it without end.
