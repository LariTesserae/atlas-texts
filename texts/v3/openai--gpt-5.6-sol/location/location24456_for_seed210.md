The place occupies a shallow notch near the summit of a black-green mountain, no larger than a village common. Its boundaries are exact. On three sides, young cliffs rise in sharp, undecayed plates; on the fourth, the ground ends at a bank of cloud. There is no path into the notch and no visible route down. The stone looks newly separated from the mountain, its edges still keen, its fractures blue-black and faintly glassy. Nothing here has had time to become rounded.

And yet everything suggests that it has existed forever.

Water is everywhere without quite accumulating. It pearls on every leaf, coats the cliffs in trembling skins, and stands just beneath the thin soil, so that a heel pressed into the moss would bring up a dark meniscus. Narrow channels lace the basin, but they are not streams in any ordinary sense. The water within them shudders in place. Droplets appear beneath overhangs, lengthen, fall the width of a finger, and return to their original shape. In the center of the notch lies a tarn as smooth as cooled metal. Rain dimples it continuously, though no rain can be seen crossing the air above it.

The basin is cold enough that breath would show. Moisture condenses in pale ropes between the rocks, hanging low among the plants. The sky is a lid of gray radiance, neither morning nor evening, and the surrounding cloud gives back no sun. What light there is seems strained through wet wool. It catches along the sharp stone edges and on the backs of insects, but leaves the hollows nearly black.

Vegetation is luxuriant, though not crowded. Great distances of saturated moss separate individual plants, each one isolated as if deliberately given working room. There are ferns with thick, lacquered fronds; low shrubs bearing translucent leaves; pale sedges whose blades vibrate without bending. A few small trees stand widely apart. Their trunks are silver and knotless, and their crowns spread horizontally in layered fans, collecting condensation. No leaf ever drops. No new leaf unfolds. The lushness here is not growth but a permanent completed condition.

The roots form the first part of the machinery.

Under the moss, they run in parallel bundles through grooves in the rock. Their surfaces are ribbed like hoses and pulse with a green-white light. They enter stone at one end and emerge from a different face several paces away, although the intervening rock is solid. Where several roots meet, they turn around one another in slow-looking helices that never actually change position. A deep vibration comes through them, regular and forceful: the cadence of heavy engines under load.

Nothing in the notch has been built. There are no walls, tools, cut surfaces, fasteners, tracks, chimneys, or abandoned objects. Nevertheless, the entire basin behaves as an industrial plant.

The cliffs exhale through vertical seams. Every few seconds—or perhaps continuously, divided into the idea of seconds—jets of cold vapor stand out from the rock in rigid white columns. Beetles with copper-colored shells are arranged around these vents in sparse rings. They hold their wing cases open, revealing membranes that hum like electrical transformers. The sound is disproportionately large, filling the basin with a harsh, steady resonance.

Along the tarn’s edge, colonies of amphibians sit half-submerged. They are long-limbed and dark, with bright sacs in their throats. Each animal produces a different mechanical note: clank, whine, hammer-stroke, turbine shriek. Together they create the noise of a factory floor heard through thick walls. Their throats remain inflated. Their mouths do not move. If watched closely, one discovers that there is no moment at which a call begins or ends. The sound simply belongs to the animal, as color belongs to skin.

Higher on the cliffs cling hundreds of moths, snails, wet-feathered birds, and many-legged creatures for which there are no familiar categories. Fauna is abundant, but distributed through every scale of the place rather than massed together. Tiny glass mites occupy droplets. Slender fish can be seen in films of water no thicker than a fingernail. Birds perch alone on distant ledges, each separated from the next by broad areas of bare rock. Their numbers become apparent only slowly, as eyes adjust and discover that nearly every dark speck is alive.

None of them travel.

A bird may be seen with its wings extended above the tarn, every feather under tension, yet it does not advance or fall. Its reflection lies directly beneath it, except that the reflected bird beats its wings furiously. Fish hold fixed bends in their bodies while water passes through their gills in both directions at once. A column of midges rises over the moss, but every midge occupies a permanent point in the column. They generate a dense electrical buzz by remaining absolutely still.

The loudness does not indicate activity. It indicates condition.

Near the back of the basin, the stone opens into a cluster of fresh angular fissures. From within comes a white light and a grinding roar. The cracks are too narrow to admit even a hand, but looking into one reveals an immense interior: ranks of wet black ridges revolving around shafts of lightning, forests of cilia combing luminous slurry, chambers contracting with enough force to shake continents. There are no manufactured components. Every apparent gear is the mouth of an animal; every piston is a column of water enclosed in living membrane; every cable is fungal, nervous, or vascular. The scale inside the fissure is impossible. The mountain’s interior appears larger than the sky outside it.

It is producing something.

Perhaps it produces the water, which emerges already used. Perhaps it produces the continual engine noise. Perhaps the clouds below the summit are exhaust. Yet there are no piles, no emissions carried away, no consumed material, and no measurable output. Production occurs without change. Raw matter and finished matter are the same matter, present simultaneously at opposite ends of a process with no duration between them.

This is the governing rule of the notch: events may exist, but they may not alter the state of the place.

Rain falls, but the tarn never rises. Animals feed, but prey are never diminished. Moss drinks from the soil, though it neither spreads nor thickens. Stone fractures endlessly along its brilliant young edges, but no chip separates. Mist is expelled from the cliff and drawn back in during the same instant. Decay is absent—not because things are preserved, but because consequence cannot accumulate.

Even erosion is held at its first gesture. Water has found every weakness in the rock but has worn none of them smooth. The basin is eternally young, eternally post-rain, eternally at the instant when an untouched summit becomes a wetland. Its cliffs retain the violence of their emergence. Its ecology has all the complexity of an ancient forest but none of its history.

There are localized failures in distance. Two ferns standing ten paces apart may touch if their shadows overlap. A beetle vanishing beneath one leaf may emerge from the underside of the sky. The tarn is only a few dozen steps across, yet its reflected depth contains descending mountain ranges, each carrying another identical basin at its summit. Sometimes the suspended bird’s reflection looks upward and sees the observer—not as they are, but as one of the fixed animals assigned to the shore.

Sound bends even more severely. The roar from the fissures may seem far below, then suddenly occupy the space behind the teeth. The amphibians’ metallic calls cast echoes before the calls themselves, though neither echo nor call has a beginning. Beneath all of it is the root-throb, a low industrial rhythm felt through bone and water. It is loud enough to suggest enormous labor, but steady enough to become a kind of silence.

There is no ruin here and no builder. The machinery is not hidden civilization, nor imitation, nor nature reclaiming a factory. The distinction never arose. Metabolism, weather, geology, and mass production are one continuous mechanism, operating at full capacity while producing the equilibrium that permits it to operate.

At the cloudward edge, moss ends in a clean green line. Beyond it is white emptiness. Water beads along the final blades but does not fall. Looking down reveals the mountain descending thousands of meters—and, at the same time, the underside of the tarn directly overhead, its dark surface stippled by invisible rain.

The place feels enclosed despite the open sky: a cool, wet room without walls, crowded with life but spacious between each living thing. Nothing approaches. Nothing retreats. The world here has broken not into chaos, but into perfect function.

It will remain exactly as it is.

It always has just begun.
