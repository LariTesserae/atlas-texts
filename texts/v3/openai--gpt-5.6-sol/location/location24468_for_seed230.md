The town occupies a shallow saddle high in the mountains, where two long slopes meet without forming either a true valley or a peak. From a distance it resembles a spread of pale stones caught in a fold of darker rock. There are no towers and almost no trees. Rooflines remain low against the wind, stepping gently across the saddle, interrupted by empty courts, cistern mouths, retaining walls, and lanes that seem unusually broad for the few people who use them.

The surrounding mountains are old in appearance. Their faces are rounded rather than jagged, their exposed stone weathered into shelves and blunt ribs. Thin seams of slate divide masses of gray limestone. Water has worked on them for centuries without violence, hollowing shallow channels, smoothing thresholds, and leaving mineral shadows beneath overhangs. There are no active landslides, no fresh scars. Fallen stones have settled into the soil, and soil has settled around them. Even the rubble looks established.

The saddle receives moisture without ever seeming wet. Clouds often lie against the higher ridges, and at dawn condensation beads on walls, roof slabs, ropes, and the iron-dark leaves of the few hardy shrubs. Rain comes lightly and irregularly. It is rarely enough to produce runoff across the whole town. Instead, water appears in pockets: in rock basins beside stairways, in covered cisterns, in narrow channels cut along the center of courtyards. A small stream passes beneath the eastern quarter, unseen except at three stone openings where people lower clay jars into the cool dark.

Every surface is shaped to collect a little water. Roofs are shallow dishes of fitted slate. Courtyard paving leans almost imperceptibly toward plugged sumps. Window ledges have grooves that guide condensation into jars. Nothing is elaborate. The system is old, repaired by hand, and understood through habit rather than calculation. A broken channel is patched with clay and crushed limestone. A cracked slab is turned over and used again. Timber is too scarce to waste on hidden structures, so lintels, drains, stairs, benches, and even many doors are made from stone.

The settlement is urban in its organization but sparse in its occupation. Streets have names. Quarters have boundaries. There is a market square, a council court, communal ovens, storehouses, workshops, shrines, burial terraces, and a gate at either end of the saddle. Yet long distances lie between signs of daily life. One may pass six closed houses before hearing voices. Some courtyards hold only a stack of fuel cakes and a covered water jar. Others are kept swept though no one appears to live around them.

The buildings are clustered in small groups separated by open ground. Their walls are thick, laid from irregular limestone blocks packed with earth mortar. Most are one story; a few descend farther into the slope than they rise above it. Rooms are cool, dim, and close to the temperature of the rock. Doorways are low to retain heat. Roofs carry a thin layer of gravel that muffles rain and prevents the slate from lifting in wind.

There is metal, but little of it. A bronze knife may serve several generations. Copper rivets are removed from worn objects and hammered into new ones. Most work is done with stone mallets, wooden wedges, bone needles, ceramic scrapers, and simple saws whose teeth must be continually reset. Pottery is everywhere: broad storage jars, narrow-necked water vessels, lamps, roof vents, drain collars, and small tiles used to mark property lines. The clay fires to colors between dull red and smoke-gray, so that even new objects appear old.

Vegetation survives in cracks and deliberate enclosures. Low juniper crouches behind walls. Gray grasses grow in scattered clumps where dust has accumulated. There are mats of thyme, bitter cress near the cistern spillways, and a few twisted fruit trees trained close to south-facing masonry. Their branches are short and heavily pruned. The garden plots are small sunken rectangles, each protected by stone edging and fed by household wastewater. They produce onions, lentils, hardy greens, and medicinal herbs, but never luxuriantly. Bare ground remains the dominant condition.

Beyond the last houses, grazing land begins almost without a boundary. It is a pale expanse of gravel, shallow turf, and exposed bedrock. Goats are kept, though not in large herds. They are tethered or watched closely because the mountain cannot support careless feeding. Their bells are wrapped in leather when brought through the town, both to reduce noise and because sudden clamor carries too far in the clear air.

Wild animals are seldom seen. Small birds visit the water courts at dawn, landing on the rims of basins and departing before the streets warm. Lizards live beneath sun-facing stones, but the climate keeps them sluggish. Occasionally a mountain hare crosses the outer terraces. Ravens pass overhead in pairs, their calls sounding larger than the settlement. There are stories of foxes among the burial shelves, though most residents know foxes only from tracks pressed into damp dust.

The prevailing sound is restraint. Wind moves over the roofs with a low, continuous breath. Water sometimes taps inside a covered drain. From behind walls come the dull knocks of hand tools, a grinding stone turning, the scrape of a broom, the brief calling of names. In the market square, speech does not gather into a roar; there are too few traders and too much open paving. Footsteps remain distinct. At night, the town is quiet enough that a jar set down in one courtyard may be heard in the next.

Light is clear but rarely harsh. The elevation gives the sky a clean breadth, while thin cloud softens the sun. At midday the stone shows its true colors: blue-gray slate, warm chalk, brown mortar, dark stains under gutters. Shadows are ordinary and legible. Nothing glows or vanishes. Mist sometimes fills the lower valleys, leaving the saddle above it, but even then the town seems practical rather than dreamlike—an inhabited ledge above a white layer of weather.

The place does not visibly expand or decline. New houses are uncommon, but ruins are uncommon too. When a wall leans, it is dismantled and rebuilt on the same footing. When a family leaves, another household may use the rooms for storage until they are inhabited again. Streets are neither widened nor abandoned. Names persist even when their origins have been forgotten. The market meets on the same days. Water is drawn in the same order. Fuel is stacked against the same north walls.

Change here takes the form of maintenance.

Rain fills a cistern; the cistern is emptied by use. Frost lifts a paving stone; hands reset it. A juniper dies; its roots hold the terrace until another cutting is established nearby. Children learn the channels by cleaning them, and become old while the channels remain. The settlement has achieved no perfection, only balance: enough stone, enough water, enough labor, enough distance between households to keep the thin soil and sparse pasture from being exhausted.

Its boundaries are plain. At the western gate, the road descends into a dry ravine paved with ancient steps. At the eastern gate, it crosses the hidden stream and climbs toward a pass marked by cairns. North and south, steep slopes close around the saddle. A person can walk from one end of the town to the other in less than half an hour, yet the repeated courts, blank walls, and turning lanes make it feel complete—a small city reduced to what the mountain can sustain.

Nothing dramatic occurs in the landscape itself. The ridges remain where they are. The rain never becomes a flood. The soil does not creep far downslope. The town’s smoke rises thinly and disperses. Its people live inside a long-settled agreement with stone and cold air, taking little, discarding little, and leaving the overall shape of the place unchanged.
