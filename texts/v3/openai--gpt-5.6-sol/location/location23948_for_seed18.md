The place is called the High Hollow, though it is neither especially large nor truly hollow. It occupies a single chamber near the summit of an industrial structure whose base cannot be seen. The chamber is perhaps the size of a cathedral nave: narrow enough that a thrown object might strike one wall, tall enough that the ceiling disappears behind galleries and glare. Beyond its open sides lies an altitude so extreme that distance loses its familiar order. Clouds pass below the floor. On clear days, the horizon bends faintly, and the land—if there is land—appears as a dark, featureless stain far beneath the atmospheric haze.

Everything here was made.

The “cliffs” are cast ribs of ceramic concrete. The “soil” is a meter-deep packing of charcoal beads, rust flakes, pulverized insulation, shed bark, bird droppings, and fibrous filter waste accumulated in steel planting trays. The “river” is a condensation channel stamped into the floor. Even the stones are furnace clinker, rounded over centuries by their slow circulation through settling basins. There is no exposed natural substrate anywhere, no seam of ancestral rock beneath the construction. Dig deeply enough and one finds older floors.

The floor descends in broad mechanical terraces around a central void. Each terrace is edged by a railing once painted yellow, now polished to black metal where vines and maintenance cables have rubbed against it. Grates alternate with shallow pans. Some pans are dry and red with oxide; others hold warm, tea-colored water to the depth of a hand. Between them rise pillars, ducts, and bolted housings large enough to resemble monuments. Their surfaces are softened by age: corners rounded, lettering erased, welds scaled with mineral crust. This erosion is not ruinous. It has reached equilibrium. Rust thickens, flakes away, feeds the beds, and is replaced. Water carves the same narrow routes through the same deposits. Roots enter joints, die there, and leave passages for later roots. The chamber has been weathered into stability.

Most of its volume is empty.

The vegetation grows densely, but only in a thin lower stratum and along a few vertical seams. Above it, hundreds of meters of open shaft rise between widely spaced braces. A single gantry crosses this vacancy. One ladder ascends through it. Here and there hangs an abandoned lamp or a hook on a long chain, each isolated by so much air that it seems more distant than it is. The emptiness gives every solid object unusual importance. A leaf spinning down from the upper dark can remain visible for minutes.

At floor level, however, growth is extravagant. Heat from the machinery and moisture from the condensers make a narrow artificial tropic. Ferns spread through drain baskets. Broad translucent leaves unfold against warm pipes, their undersides silvered with dust. Ropes of vine climb cable trays and return in loops, unable to find earth or sky. Moss grows in emerald pads wherever a bolt head weeps. Pale fungi sheath the old insulation in tiered fans. There are dwarf trees as well, though none stand in soil: their roots fill perforated drums, mesh cages, and the hollow spokes of disused wheels. Their trunks have been bent by the architecture into elbows and spirals. Some bear small bitter fruit with skin the color of oxidized copper.

The plants do not conceal the machinery. They describe it. Green lines mark every warm leak, every seam that catches mist, every surface that remains undisturbed for decades. Bare metal means heat, vibration, poison, or recent repair. Dense foliage means safety, though not necessarily solidity.

Moisture comes intermittently. For long periods the High Hollow is merely damp. Condensation gathers on overhead cooling veins, enlarging with great patience until drops fall through the shaft. Each drop strikes a leaf, railing, or pool with a sharp visible splash, though its sound is immediately swallowed by the greater noise. Once every few days, valves open somewhere above. Then warm rain descends in perfectly parallel lines. It lasts perhaps twenty minutes. Channels fill; rust-colored rivulets cross the terraces; cistern flowers open their cupped leaves; insects emerge from behind ceramic plates. The water drains away almost as soon as the valves close, leaving pockets in warped floor panels and sealed machine housings.

No storm forms here, yet the chamber has weather. Clouds sometimes enter through the eastern lattice and thin themselves among the columns. In the morning, sunlight comes plainly through the open wall: ordinary yellow-white daylight, neither enchanted nor particularly dramatic. It illuminates the nearest leaves, throws rectangular shadows from the grating, and reveals vast quantities of suspended lint. By afternoon the opposite wall brightens. At night, red obstruction lamps and blue inspection tubes divide the vegetation into colored islands.

The air is warm. Near the condensers it is humid enough to bead on skin; beside the open edges it turns thin and dry. Breathing is possible because the chamber is pressurized by the machine below. At the outer rail one can feel the atmosphere pulling away through every gap. Loose cloth strains outward. Seeds that cross the boundary do not fall at once. They are caught by the tower’s rising thermal plume and vanish upward.

The sound is overwhelming.

Far below, turbines turn at a speed no living thing can perceive directly. Their operation arrives as a continuous physical roar—a low, enormous pressure that seems to originate in the bones rather than the ears. Over it runs the hard metallic shriek of drive systems, the periodic thunder of counterweights, the clatter of relay banks, and the sustained rushing of air through ducts. Every railing trembles. Pooled water stipples without wind. Leaves quiver even when untouched.

Conversation is conducted by hand, lamp, or contact: forehead to forehead, mouth against the hollow of a pipe, words felt as vibration through metal. The animals have adapted accordingly. Few rely on airborne calls. Their signals are flashes, scents, tremors, and touches.

The smallest and most numerous are the rivet mites, bright red creatures that graze on mineral films around fasteners. Dense colonies trace structural stresses in living color. Glasswing roaches sail short distances between warm housings, their clear wing cases vibrating too rapidly to be heard separately from the machinery. Condenser frogs cling to the undersides of dripping pipes. Their throats inflate, but instead of croaking they drum their toes against the metal, sending patterned pulses through the plumbing.

Higher in the vines live cable lemurs: long-bodied, six-limbed mammals with bare gripping pads and dark membranes over their eyes. They travel upside down along bundled wires, eating fruit, beetles, and the soft insulation exposed by age. They are abundant but rarely seen all at once. One notices a hand retreating behind a junction box, then a tail laid briefly across a beam twenty meters away. Their colonies mark territory by weaving shed fibers into compact knots. The knots resemble maintenance tags and have occasionally been mistaken for instructions.

Birds enter from outside, though “bird” is an approximate term. The local sailbacks have narrow bodies and enormous rigid wings. They do not flap in the thin air. They circle the tower’s thermal updraft for days, landing only to drink from the upper basins or to lay eggs in the hollows of decommissioned sirens. Their feathers are edged with metal dust. When they spread their wings in sunlight, they shine like old tools.

Predators are scarce but not absent. Something pale inhabits the central void. It climbs only during valve-rain and leaves behind emptied shells arranged in arcs. No one has observed its full body. The plants react to its passage by closing, one after another, in a descending spiral.

Despite the noise, the High Hollow changes slowly. The machinery operates continuously, but its purpose unfolds over spans longer than a human life. One wheel advances a single tooth every nine days. A piston the width of a house rises through the chamber by less than a finger’s breadth each year, its oily crown serving as a moving garden terrace. Cables tighten across generations. Painted alignment marks that were once separated now nearly meet.

The entire chamber is part of a lifting engine, though what it lifts is unclear. The common explanation is that the tower raises the summit itself, maintaining a prescribed distance from the world below as the structure settles. Another holds that the engine winds a counterweight suspended on the far side of the planet. A third claims the High Hollow is not near the summit at all, but travels slowly up and down inside the tower, completing one circuit every several centuries. Evidence exists for each account. The altitude indicators disagree. Old nests appear on terraces that should never have shared the same elevation. Every few decades, gravity inclines a fraction of a degree toward a different wall, and the drainage channels must be extended.

This is one of the place’s rules: downward is maintained, not given.

Another is that no surface remains merely a surface. Anything horizontal collects water. Anything that collects water acquires a film. The film attracts mites, the mites bring droppings, the droppings host spores, the spores prepare a bed for roots. Left alone long enough, a machine becomes terrain. But the terrain never ceases to be a machine. Beneath every garden is a panel that may open without warning; within every tree drum runs a live conduit; under every pool waits a valve.

There are signs that people once occupied the chamber in greater numbers. Narrow booths stand along the western wall, each containing a stool, a speaking tube, and a panel of dead gauges. Walkways lead to missing doors. A canteen counter survives under flowering creepers, set with steel cups chained in place. Yet the High Hollow feels uninhabited rather than abandoned. It is too functional to be a ruin and too overgrown to be a workplace. If keepers remain, they visit infrequently. Their paths are marked by recently greased hinges and clean cuts through vines. One may wait years without meeting one.

At the lowest terrace is a small circular clearing where nothing grows. Its floor is smooth black composite, warm to the touch and perfectly dry. All the chamber’s vibrations cancel there. The roar remains audible, but the body no longer feels it. Animals avoid the circle. Falling drops bend away from it. A ladder descends from its center into an illuminated shaft, though the first rung is several meters below the rim and there is no visible means of reaching it.

Around that silence, the lush machinery-garden presses close: leaves glossy with condensation, frogs tapping messages into pipes, mites burning red along the seams, old trees gripping their steel cages. Beyond them is the railing, and beyond the railing almost nothing—blue air, white cloud, immeasurable descent.

The engine continues its ancient, deafening labor. The forest trembles without wind. Warm rain gathers overhead, one drop at a time.
