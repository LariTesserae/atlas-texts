### The Bell Chamber

Near the summit of a drowned mountain, hidden inside a knob of translucent stone, there is a chamber scarcely larger than a meeting hall. It lies thousands of meters above the old lowlands but still beneath the upper ocean. On clear days the water’s bright skin is visible far overhead, silver and restless as hammered metal.

The chamber is not a cave hollowed by age. It is younger than the mountain around it.

Mineral-rich currents rise through fractures in the summit and precipitate layer by layer, building pale walls around a temporary emptiness. Ribs of limestone branch inward like unfinished bones. Curtains of silica grow along invisible boundaries, brittle and almost transparent. Some surfaces remain soft enough to mark with a fingernail; others have hardened into blue-gray stone. The whole place resembles an organ assembling itself around the water trapped within it.

There is no dry air. Water fills everything.

It pours through the chamber by a hundred narrow throats, entering as white jets and leaving in sudden vortices. The currents collide in the center, collapse, reverse, and begin again. Loose objects rarely fall directly downward. They are carried in broad circles, held motionless against walls, or flung upward into the milky vault. Gravity is present, but locally it is often the weakest force.

The noise is immense.

Water hammers the young stone continuously. Each opening gives a different note: low booming from the floor vents, a shriek from hairline cracks, rapid clapping where two currents shear past one another. The chamber’s thin mineral walls flex under pressure, making a deeper toll that can be felt through the teeth and ribs. At intervals the summit shifts. Then every passage changes pitch at once, and the entire place rings like a submerged bell.

No voice carries here. Speech becomes a trembling blur against the face. Those who enter communicate by touch: knuckle taps on the wrist, pressure of fingers against the palm, cords tugged in short sequences. Even thought seems to acquire rhythm from the surrounding thunder.

Daylight reaches the chamber through the water and the semitransparent cap of the mountain. It is surprisingly ordinary light—cool white at midday, greenish toward evening, briefly gold when the low sun finds a path through the waves. The chamber has few true shadows. Its walls glow from within, revealing trapped seams of black sand and tiny bubbles sealed in the forming rock. During storms above, light races across the vault in fractured bands, while pressure waves make the stone flash faintly where crystals grind together.

There is little life.

A few colorless streamers cling behind sheltered ridges, neither quite weed nor fungus. They feed on minerals and the faint organic dust carried upward from the drowned valleys. Each streamer grows only where the current is steady enough not to tear it free. Their tips are red, as if singed, though the water is cold.

Small blind shell-creatures inhabit the quieter fissures. They emerge only after a major pressure change, extending threadlike legs to gather fresh deposits. Occasionally a long, translucent animal enters with the current. It has no obvious head and folds itself like ribbon around the chamber’s central whirl. Most are dashed against the stone or swept onward before anyone can study them. Their remains vanish quickly beneath new mineral crust.

Human marks are scarcely more permanent.

On the least turbulent wall, several crude platforms have been wedged between stone ribs. They are made from split wood, hide cord, bone pegs, and slabs of porous rock. Everything is tied redundantly. Nothing is trusted merely to rest in place. Nets hold clay jars against the ceiling. Stone knives hang in loops. A row of handholds crosses one wall, polished by generations of gripping fingers but already being swallowed by fresh calcite.

The builders have no machinery capable of mastering the currents. They work with levers, wedges, woven rope, and their own weight. To cross the chamber they hook themselves to guide-lines and wait for the right phase of the flow. A badly timed movement can wrench a shoulder from its socket or sweep a person into one of the narrow outflow throats.

Their most important structure is a small cage of bent ribs surrounding a resonant stone. The stone is struck with a bone mallet during the brief lulls. Its vibration travels through the chamber walls farther than any shouted signal. Other hidden chambers in the mountain answer, sometimes after several breaths, sometimes hours later. In this way the scattered inhabitants know which passages remain open and which have been sealed by growth.

They do not carve the mountain. Carving is too slow, and openings heal.

Here, stone grows faster than it erodes. A rope left untouched becomes furred with crystals, stiffens, and disappears into a wall. Foot-wide passages can close within a season. New hollows open where warm mineral water meets colder currents and lays down curving partitions around turbulence. The summit is therefore always under construction, but no builder directs it. Rooms are born, connected, divided, and entombed according to the pressure of the sea.

This gives the place its central rule: emptiness is temporary.

The chamber exists because the water is moving. If the currents ceased, minerals would fill it solid. If they grew much stronger, the fragile shell would rupture and the room would become part of the open ocean. Its survival depends on unending violence of exactly the wrong kind—too turbulent for safety, yet balanced enough to preserve a hollow.

Despite the roar, the Bell Chamber feels empty. Structures occupy only one wall. Life hides in cracks. The luminous middle is broad and bare except for spiraling grit. From any handhold, one looks across an expanse of cold moving water in which there is almost nothing to see, though everything is in motion.

Sometimes a dark silhouette passes beyond the translucent outer wall: something large moving through the high ocean. Its shape cannot be judged through the distorted stone. The chamber’s few animals withdraw. The human inhabitants flatten themselves against the ribs and extinguish their oilless crystal lamps, though daylight still shines around them.

Then the silhouette is gone. The currents change. Somewhere in the raw mountain, a new passage breaks open with a sound like the world splitting, and the chamber begins, once again, to become a different room.
