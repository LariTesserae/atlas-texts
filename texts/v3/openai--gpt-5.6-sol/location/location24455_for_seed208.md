At first it seems to be a drowned plain.

Water lies everywhere, but seldom deeply. It occupies long seams in the ground, fills shallow pans, and travels in sheets no thicker than skin over expanses of dark stone. The land is so low and so thoroughly worn that its slopes are almost imperceptible. A person can walk for hours believing the ground level, only to turn and see that every rivulet has been moving in the same direction. There are no hills in the ordinary sense. There are only broad swells of ancient bedrock, rounded until they resemble the backs of immense buried animals.

The stone is older than any visible ruin. It has passed beyond crumbling. Softer layers have already been carried away, leaving a final pavement of gray-green mineral, black glassy ribs, and occasional fields of pale nodules that rain cannot dissolve. Cracks have widened into channels. Channels have become long, low corridors of water. In places, the rock weeps continuously—not from springs, but from its entire surface. Moisture condenses inside microscopic pores and gathers into silver beads, so that even between storms the plain seems to sweat.

Nothing grows.

There are no reeds at the water margins, no moss on the stone, no lichen coloring the ruins. Pools have bare rims. Islands are merely higher pieces of rock. The absence of vegetation makes distance difficult to judge; a low black ledge might be fifty paces away or half a day’s walk. The horizon remains open, sparsely interrupted by standing stones, broken shelters, and herds.

The life here is almost entirely mobile.

Small, many-legged grazers travel over the wet pavement in dense fronts, scraping invisible films from the rock. Their food is not leaf or root but a translucent skin of mineral-eating cells that forms after rain and vanishes beneath their mouthplates. The film has no color except when disturbed, when it flashes briefly blue-white, like moonlight struck from metal. Behind a feeding swarm, the stone is left clean and dull.

Larger animals follow them. Some are low-slung and broad, with overlapping shells that knock together as they move. Others stand on long jointed limbs and carry bell-shaped bodies above the shallows. They lower nets of hair-fine tendrils into flowing water, filtering out eggs, cells, fragments of shed armor, and the soft larval forms released during floods. Pale swimmers inhabit channels only a handspan deep, their bodies flattened sideways so they can turn through cracks in the bedrock. When pools begin to dry, they cross the wet stone in muscular leaps, slapping down with enough force to be heard far away.

There are predators as well, though they are often mistaken for pieces of the terrain. In shallow basins lie gray plates with ragged edges. When a herd passes, a plate unfolds into six hooked limbs and a circular mouth. In the deeper channels, narrow animals brace themselves against the current and hunt by vibration. They have no visible eyes. Their heads are crowned with hollow prongs that tremble at footsteps.

The plain is never quiet.

The water alone would make it loud: rain ticking on exposed stone, runoff hissing through a million grooves, sheets of water pouring into sinkholes. But beneath these sounds is the voice of the land itself. The whole pavement is perforated. Tides in a buried aquifer drive air through caverns and vertical shafts, turning the plain into an enormous instrument.

Some openings whistle with a pitch too high to locate. Others give out slow foghorn notes that seem to arrive through the soles of the feet. Long cracks chatter when water crosses them. At intervals, pressure builds below sealed basins and escapes in a single concussion, throwing rings across every nearby pool. The animals answer: shells clatter, resonant bodies boom, feeding swarms produce a continuous dry rasp. During migrations, the combined noise becomes physical. Water shivers. Loose stones creep across the ground. Speech is possible only mouth to ear, and even then much of it is felt rather than heard.

The loudest places are marked by old constructions.

They are scattered widely: semicircles of fitted slabs, knee-high walls, roofless huts with floors sunk into the rock. Their builders used no mortar. They cut grooves with stone tools and wedged blocks together using shell, bone, and strips of cured hide. Many structures appear crude until the wind changes. Then their purpose becomes clear. Openings in the walls catch specific notes from the subterranean chambers and cancel them, creating pockets of comparative silence.

Inside such a shelter, the roar of the plain drops to a muffled pressure. One can hear droplets falling from the ceiling. One can hear breathing.

The oldest shelters have been worn nearly flush with the ground, yet they still work. Their builders understood the voices under the stone better than later peoples did. Newer walls are taller, rougher, and crowded with repairs. Some hold racks of hooked spears tipped with volcanic glass. Others contain shallow bowls hammered from native copper, though metal is scarce and usually reserved for needles, scraping teeth, and small resonant plates used to test the weather.

There are no roads. Travel follows sound.

Low, steady booming means firm ground above broad caverns. Rapid clicking marks thin crust and hidden hollows. A rising whistle warns that underground water is climbing and will soon burst through the vents. People move between silence shelters by memorizing sequences of tones. In fog, when even nearby landmarks disappear, a journey may be described as: three deep breaths of the western shaft, then the double knock, then keep the crying fissure on the left until the ground begins to hum.

The air is cool and continually near condensation. Fog does not descend so much as assemble at knee height, gathering over pools and spreading sideways. It smells of wet stone, salt, animal oils, and the faint metallic sharpness released when black rock is freshly broken. Rain comes often, usually as a fine slanting fall, but storms can arrive with almost no visual warning. The dim sky simply lowers. The first sign is that the animals stop making voluntary sounds.

Then the storm begins, and the plain becomes deafening beyond measure.

Rain strikes shell and rock. Buried chambers fill. Vents exhale towers of spray. Water reverses in channels as pressure rises from below. Shelters tremble. Herds press together on the old stone swells while predatory plates unfasten themselves from flooded hollows. For several hours the boundaries between land, water, and air become uncertain. Every surface runs. Every cavity speaks.

Afterward, the world is changed in detail but not in form. New pools occupy old depressions. Carcasses lie wedged in fissures, already surrounded by scavengers. Egg-cords cling to the sheltered sides of standing stones. The invisible food-film brightens across miles of pavement, and the grazing fronts begin to move.

This cycle has repeated for so long that erosion here no longer feels like destruction. The landscape has already surrendered everything it could easily lose. Storms do not carve dramatic new valleys; they polish the existing world by fractions. Ruins diminish grain by grain. Shells become pale grit. Even bones, left in the open, are rolled smooth and incorporated into low bars along the channels.

Yet the plain is active, crowded in its own sparse way. Nothing rises high, nothing roots itself, and little remains where it was born. Life survives by moving across the final surface of the land, feeding on what water draws directly from stone.

Above it all hangs the low, unlit sky. Beneath it lies another sea, trapped in darkness and breathing through the rock.

The plain is the thin, resonant partition between them.
