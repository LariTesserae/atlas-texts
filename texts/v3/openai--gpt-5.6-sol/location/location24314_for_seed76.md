The land begins above most weather.

It is a continent-sized tableland lifted into a white, lucid sky, its average height greater than many terrestrial mountains. Along its margins, escarpments descend in tiers too immense to take in at once. The lowest terraces are lost beneath pale atmospheric haze; the uppermost stand in hard sunlight, their shadows blue-black and astonishingly cold.

There are no rivers. No snowfields feed the ravines, and no hidden water waits beneath the soil. The channels crossing the plateau are older than the present climate, though they were never cut by rain. They are wind-courses: shallow valleys aligned with prevailing pressure tides, polished over ages by airborne salt and pumice. Where currents converge, the ground has been combed into parallel ribs. Where they separate, broad basins lie smooth as worn bone.

The stone is mostly a porous, chalk-colored silicate veined with amber minerals. Exposure has rounded every projection. Cliffs do not appear freshly broken; they sag into columns, shelves, and long aerodynamic backs, as if the whole highland has spent an epoch learning how not to resist the wind. Loose fragments rarely remain angular. They are rolled without water, nudged for centuries by daily expansion and contraction until they become flat pebbles that migrate a finger’s width each season.

In the brightest hours the plateau seems nearly empty. Distances are deceptive in the thin, clean air. A dark line that looks like nearby moss may be a forest two days away. A solitary tower of rock may stand fifty kilometers across a basin.

Vegetation is abundant, but it does not cover the land continuously. It gathers in widely separated colonies, each dense and extravagant within its own boundaries, with great reaches of bare mineral between. From above, the plateau would resemble pale cloth scattered with green-black islands.

The largest plants are called trees only by analogy. They have no wood and require no water. Their bodies are made of layered mineral fiber bound with flexible resins, and their slow circulation uses a cold hydrocarbon oil synthesized from gases drawn out of the air. Their roots spread close beneath the surface, harvesting salts, carbon compounds, and the faint electrical charge produced where sunlight strikes certain crystals.

Each mature organism supports a broad crown of thousands of narrow blades. The blades are glossy on one side and silver on the other. In full sun they turn edgewise, presenting almost nothing to the glare; in the cool morning and late afternoon they open into layered fans. A thriving grove can appear lush and almost tropical in complexity—fold over fold, frond beneath frond, curtains of black-green tissue—yet the air among them remains dry enough to draw resin into threads.

They do not transpire. They breathe in minute pulses.

At intervals, an entire grove changes orientation together. The movement passes from crown to crown like a thought. Silver surfaces flash across the basin, then darken. From a distance it resembles a sheet of light traveling over an invisible sea.

Smaller life thickens the colonies. There are low cushions with glassy hairs, spiraled rosettes the color of old copper, and branching mats that penetrate cracks in the stone. Some grow in the permanent shade beneath overhangs and possess no pigment at all. They feed on chemical gradients in the rock, slowly bleaching amber veins to ivory. Others store daylight in fluorescent compounds and continue glowing for hours after sunset, so that the groves become dim archipelagos beneath the stars.

Nothing here burns easily. Heat causes most living tissue to seal itself beneath a ceramic crust. After rare lightning strikes, a grove may stand blackened and motionless for years, then split its shells and unfold green blades from within.

The plateau makes its own weather, though almost none of it involves moisture. During the day, sun-warmed gases rise from the central basins and draw cold currents over the escarpments. In the afternoon these currents collide, lifting vast walls of dust so fine that they look solid. The storms travel as ocher fronts hundreds of kilometers long. They seldom roar. The thin atmosphere gives them only a low, papery hiss.

Dust is not merely destructive. It is the principal migration of fertility. Groves turn their crowns into the oncoming storm and strain useful minerals from it. For several days afterward their leaves become edged with gold, and root networks wake beneath the ground. New shoots may appear far downwind where charged dust has settled into cracks.

At night the temperature falls sharply. Gases condense—not as water, but as a faint waxy frost of atmospheric organics—on the coldest stones and leaf margins. Before dawn, plants sweep this deposit inward with tiny muscular ripples. When the sun rises, whatever has not been collected evaporates without ever becoming wet. The frost leaves dark halos on the soil and a smell like pine resin, struck flint, and distant ozone.

Fauna is rare enough that its appearance changes the character of a place.

The most commonly seen creatures are thin, six-legged grazers that cross the open ground alone. Their bodies are no larger than a hand, but their sail-like backs cast long shadows. They eat only the outer skins of old leaves, removing translucent layers without killing the plant. In danger they flatten against the mineral plain and become nearly indistinguishable from wind-polished stones.

Larger animals exist, though one may travel for months without encountering one. They follow the grove chains across the continent: tall, slow forms balanced on three pillar limbs, carrying branching sensory vanes above their bodies. They do not drink. They browse on resin-rich seed cases and can remain dormant through long crossings, walking only at dawn and dusk. Their tracks persist for decades because so little disturbs the ground.

Predators are fewer still. They wait among the rock formations rather than pursue. Their metabolism is measured in seasons; an adult may feed once and then become as still as a boulder until the next migration. Around such a waiting place, the grazers alter their routes, the plants close their lower fronds, and a broad zone of unease takes shape without any visible cause.

Most of the landscape is quiet. There is the granular whisper of moving dust, the occasional dry click of a leaf changing angle, and, beneath a grove at twilight, the soft internal ticking of fibers contracting in the cold. Seeds travel on the wind as hollow shells. When they strike stone they make tiny bell notes, one here, another far away, never enough to become a chorus.

The deepest sound comes from the plateau itself. As night cools the highlands, immense buried layers contract at different rates. A vibration passes through the ground, too low to hear clearly, sensed more in the ribs than in the ears. Groves fold their crowns when it comes. The tall migrating animals stop walking. Pebbles creep downslope.

There are no roads, walls, ruins, or marks of tools. Nothing interrupts the old negotiations between light, stone, gas, and life. Even the richest groves remain shaped by the available cracks and wind shadows. They do not conquer the open mineral country. Their abundance is concentrated, almost disciplined, leaving enormous intervals of brightness and silence between one living stronghold and the next.

From the plateau’s highest ridges, those intervals are the dominant sight: thousands of kilometers of pale, settled land; dark colonies scattered across it; dust fronts moving slowly under a brilliant sun; distant escarpments stepping down toward an unseen world below.

It is a lush place without softness, active without haste, and dry beyond thirst. Its life has never waited for rain.
