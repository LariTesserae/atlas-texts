The land is called the Sump, though it is not a single basin. It extends for many kilometers across a low, newly exposed plain where warm water lies just beneath a skin of black silt. There are marshes, but no broad reed beds; pools, but few open surfaces. Most of the water moves invisibly through porous stone, announcing itself in the soft collapse of banks and in droplets that gather beneath overhangs where no rain can reach.

There is no natural light.

This is not merely night. Flame burns darkly here, consuming fuel and giving heat without illumination. Electric filaments become hot red objects only when touched; from a distance, they cannot be seen. Even animals that elsewhere produce light—fungal gnats, lantern eels, the small signaling worms brought in shipping crates—lose the visible part of their glow. The darkness accepts radiation as the wet ground accepts rain. Sight reaches no farther than the pressure of the eyes.

As a result, the landscape is known through other means.

Underfoot, the plain alternates between resilient mats of mineral foam and young, sharp ridges of stone. The ridges rose recently, perhaps within living memory, extruded in long seams from the saturated earth. Their edges have not yet softened. They cut boots and the feet of animals. Condensation runs down them continuously, making each ridge a weeping wall no higher than a person’s knee in some places and as high as a warehouse in others. Where the water collects, it forms warm trenches with steep, fresh sides. There are no rounded pebbles. Every fragment feels newly broken.

The air is thick without being windy. Warm vapor presses against skin, carrying smells in distinct layers: iron, algae, machine grease, brackish mud, hot insulation, animal musk. Sometimes a colder ribbon of air passes at waist height and continues unchanged for several kilometers. It does not disperse. Travelers use these ribbons as roads, following their chill with bare hands.

Vegetation is sparse. Pale cordgrass grows in isolated knots, its blades stiff and nearly leafless. Low bladder-plants anchor themselves along cracks, feeding more on mineral seepage than sunlight. Their buried sacs inflate over months, lifting plates of stone by a finger’s width, then collapse all at once and leave shallow chambers in which rainwater and small animals gather. There are also columnar fungi, dry to the touch despite the humidity. They may be mistaken for rotted fence posts until they lean toward a passing engine.

The scarcity of plants does not make the Sump empty. Animal life crowds every available layer.

Warm pools contain blind fish packed tightly enough that the water seems to have a grain. Mudskippers slap across wet clay, pausing to listen through their jaws. Soft-shelled crabs inhabit the mineral foam and replace their carapaces with flakes of iron or ceramic scavenged from machinery. Above them move long-legged marsh animals called feelers, each with a body no larger than a fist and limbs spanning several meters. They step slowly over channels, touching stone, water, pipe, and one another. When hundreds travel together, the only sound is a dispersed ticking as their feet make contact.

Winged animals are abundant but rarely fly. The darkness has no horizon, and open flight easily becomes endless falling. Instead, moths and skin-bats crawl along tension cables, pipelines, and the undersides of rock shelves. Their wings serve as blankets, resonators, and sails for detecting distant pressure changes. Dense colonies hang around the scattered industrial works, drinking condensation from rivets.

The works are remnants of an extraction industry whose purpose is no longer certain. They stand kilometers apart: pumping houses, narrow derricks, concrete caissons, clusters of tanks sunk to their shoulders in mud. No town grew around them. There are only occasional dormitory shells, tool lockers, sections of elevated walkway, and doorframes erected where no wall was ever built.

Many machines continue to operate.

Their engines turn at extremely low speeds. A piston may take six hours to complete a stroke. Gear teeth meet with soft, widely separated knocks. Pumps draw warm water from one layer of the ground and press it into another, though the pipes sometimes lead back into the same reservoir. Huge flywheels rotate too slowly to feel mechanical; their motion resembles the turning of weather. Animals nest safely on their rims and are carried around over the course of a day.

The machines are electrified, but electricity behaves strangely in the Sump. Current does not always follow conductors. It can remain pooled in disconnected metal for weeks, then migrate through wet air to a structure that did not exist when the charge was generated. Workers once laid ceramic markers to warn of these reservoirs. The markers now stand scattered across the plain, and crabs collect around them, waiting for the brief muscular contractions that pass through the mud when a charge relocates.

Sound travels poorly in the air and remarkably well through water and stone. At the surface the Sump is mostly quiet: dripping, the remote sigh of pressure valves, a wing brushing a cable, the occasional collapse of a saturated bank. Beneath that quiet is a crowded seismic world. Every pump stroke can be felt through the soles from kilometers away. Fish change direction when buried gates open. Colonies synchronize their breeding to the tremor of particular engines. A person lying with an ear against a ridge may hear machinery that has not yet been built.

This is one of the region’s ordinary failures of sequence.

Distances and durations are unreliable, but only slowly. A trench crossed in ten minutes may require half a day on the return journey. Rain sometimes reaches the ground several hours before it falls, appearing first as beads on stone, then rising as a cool mist until the clouds receive it. Tracks deepen before the animal that makes them arrives. None of this happens abruptly. The land gives warning: pressure in the teeth, a smell of hot copper, the gradual loss of echoes.

The darkness itself has depth. In some places it is thin, allowing touch and sound to agree about the shape of things. Elsewhere it becomes dense enough to resist movement. These regions are called blackbanks. Entering one feels like wading into soft, dry water. Objects left inside emerge compressed, duplicated, or with their interiors turned outward. The pumps may have been built to drain blackbanks rather than marshes. Their intake pipes sometimes exhale folds of darkness that slide across the wet plain and settle in low ground.

No creature is known to see, yet many behave as though watched. Herds halt around empty patches. Moths arrange themselves in concentric rings facing a point in the air. The feelers lift all their feet but one and remain balanced for days. When this occurs near a pumping station, the machinery slows further, until motion can only be inferred from changes in rust.

Human habitation persists in small, scattered forms. A family may occupy a raised control room, navigating by ropes and textured walls. Mechanics live in cylindrical tanks converted into dry chambers. Ferrymen pole along narrow channels, striking the banks with tuning forks to identify them. Buildings have no windows. Doors are padded to prevent echoes from escaping and returning altered.

Maps are embossed in wax and must be remade frequently. They show not only channels and ridges but repetitions: the same pump house occurring three times, a road that crosses itself without turning, a pool that is smaller than the animal living in it. The most trusted maps include blank areas labeled with dates rather than names.

Change in the Sump is patient. Water rises by the thickness of a fingernail each season. Stone seams continue to emerge, fresh-edged and sweating. Engines turn. Blackbanks gather in hollows. Animal colonies shift from one abandoned structure to another in migrations that take generations.

Nothing seems hurried, even when it is dangerous.

Across the open plain, beyond the reach of any hand or instrument, one can sometimes feel all the pumps complete a stroke at once. The mud tightens. The hidden water becomes still. Every crawling and swimming thing pauses in its layer.

Then, from somewhere under the young stone, comes a single enormous drop.
