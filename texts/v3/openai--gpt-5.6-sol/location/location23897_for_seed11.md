The land stands very high, but no longer rises.

Whatever mountains once occupied this region have been worn past grandeur into immense, level-backed remnants: long tables of dark stone, shallow domes, isolated shelves, and blunt ridges separated by gulfs of warm air. Their summits lie at nearly the same elevation, as if an ancient world had been planed flat and afterward broken into pieces. From one rim, three or four other plateaus can be seen through the haze, green on top and black beneath, each many days’ travel away.

The valleys between them are less like valleys than absences. Their floors are hidden under suspended vapor, and their sides descend in cliffs so old that erosion has erased every fresh angle. The walls are fluted, hollowed, and scalloped into enormous curves. Some overhang for miles. Others taper inward until a whole plateau appears to balance on a narrow, weather-darkened root.

Nothing here is still.

Warm air rises continuously from the unseen lowlands. It strikes the undersides of the high shelves, divides, accelerates through the ravines, and erupts over the rims. The result is a nearly permanent atmospheric violence. Wind pours across the summits in visible waves, pressing vegetation flat and releasing it. Cloud banks form in minutes, tear open, and re-form elsewhere. Rain may fall hard upon a circle of ground no more than a hundred paces wide while the surrounding heath remains sunlit. Water runs briefly, vanishes into cracks, then reappears hours later as condensation beading from the shaded faces of stone.

The sound is overwhelming.

Every escarpment is riddled with chambers left by ages of weathering. Wind enters these hollows and turns the whole country into an instrument. Low passages produce a continuous bass concussion that can be felt through the feet and teeth. Narrow vents scream. Deep cavities pulse at intervals, making loose stones jump. When clouds push through the chasms, thunder passes between the plateaus and returns altered—slower, deeper, multiplied until it seems to come from inside the rock.

There are calmer sounds beneath the roar, though they are difficult to hear: water ticking from leaf to leaf, stems rubbing together, the soft collapse of sod at a cliff edge. People who travel here learn to read vibration rather than listen. A taut cord tied between two sticks serves as a storm warning. Pebbles placed in shallow bowls dance before the most dangerous gusts arrive. Speech is done close to the ear, with one hand enclosing both mouths, or by broad gestures adapted to the open ground.

The summit vegetation is lush without being tall. Height is punished by the wind. Moss, fern, sedge, creeping vine, and thick-leaved flowering plants knit themselves into dense carpets rarely higher than the ankle. They grow in interlocked colonies, their roots braided through the topmost layer of fractured stone. From a distance these mats appear smooth and richly green; close at hand they are miniature forests, wet beneath, full of translucent stems and cup-shaped leaves holding warm droplets.

In sheltered depressions, vegetation becomes more extravagant. Groves of low, many-trunked trees grow with their crowns flattened sideways, every branch aligned with the prevailing air. Their bark is pale and fibrous, peeling into streamers that hum like reeds. Vines span gaps between trunks in heavy curtains. Broad fungi rise after rain, open for an hour, release dark clouds of spores, and deliquesce before the ground dries.

Water is present everywhere in traces and almost nowhere in abundance.

There are no permanent rivers on the open summits. Instead there are brief silver runnels, shallow rain basins, dripping caves, and deep clefts where moisture persists under mats of root. The largest pools occupy eroded bowls near the centers of plateaus. They may remain for a season, warm and tea-colored, then empty overnight through some reopened fissure. Days later a waterfall may burst from a cliff hundreds of fathoms below, pouring sideways into the wind and dissolving before it reaches the cloud layer.

Travelers dig narrow condensation trenches and line them with broad leaves. By dawn, if the night wind has turned correctly, a trench yields a hand’s depth of water. If it has not, the leaves come up dry and warm.

Though the plant cover is abundant, the country feels almost vacant. There are few upright objects, few animals, and fewer settlements. The openness is severe. A person crossing one of the green tables may remain visible for half a day, a single moving fleck beneath an uninterrupted sky.

Animal life hides from the air. Small six-footed grazers burrow under root mats and emerge in the stillness before storms. Their bodies are broad and low, their ears sealed by folds of skin. Pale gliding creatures sometimes cross between nearby rock spurs, but they do not fly so much as surrender themselves expertly to the currents. They may remain aloft for days, feeding on windborne seeds and insects. Their arrival is abrupt: a shadow races across the ground, a membrane snaps overhead, and then the creature is gone into the gulf.

Larger animals are exceptionally rare. Now and then a plateau bears the winding trail of a solitary shell-backed browser. These animals move slowly enough that moss grows on them, and their tracks become temporary channels for rain. No one agrees on where they breed. Some say each plateau has only one, as old as the stone forest it grazes.

Human structures occur at great intervals. Most are low, round shelters built behind natural lips of rock. Their walls are fitted without mortar from porous blocks, with moss packed into the seams. Roofs are woven from root fiber and pinned down by slabs so heavy that several families are needed to move them. Nothing projects unnecessarily. No chimney rises above a roofline; smoke is led through long ground-level ducts and released among stones, where the wind tears it apart.

Metal is uncommon and poorly suited to this wet, abrasive air. Tools are made from knapped volcanic glass, hardwood, shell, bone, and the dense silicified roots found in ancient soil layers. Rope is the most important technology. Paths are marked by ropes threaded through holes in the rock, because cairns are quickly scattered and painted signs are stripped away. Bridges, where they exist, are little more than parallel lines stretched over narrow clefts: one for the feet, two for the hands, and a fourth carrying small bone chimes whose motion warns whether the crossing is safe.

The largest works are wind courts. These are shallow rings of standing stones, never more than waist-high, arranged around natural vents. Their purpose is partly practical and partly ceremonial. By blocking or opening gaps in the ring, people can alter the pitch of the air moving underground. Certain tones indicate distant rain, failing cliff faces, or changes in the deep pressure of the gulf. Others have no stated use. They are maintained because they have always been maintained.

Many wind courts are abandoned. Their stones lie sunk in moss, still sounding when storms pass over them. A traveler may hear a measured booming from an empty plateau and know that no living hand has adjusted those vents in generations.

The land changes quickly on the surface and almost not at all beneath. A grove can be uprooted in a minute. A pond can appear before noon and be gone by dusk. Whole sheets of flowering ground peel back in cyclonic gusts, exposing pale roots like woven cloth. Lightning ignites dry fibers, rain extinguishes them, and new shoots emerge from the warmed ash within days.

Yet the underlying forms seem to have reached a final age. The plateaus do not look freshly broken. Even their collapses reveal rounded interiors, stone already softened by unimaginable duration. New scars resemble old scars as soon as they appear. Fallen rock disintegrates rapidly into familiar curves. It is as if erosion here is not a process leading somewhere, but a stable condition—a perpetual finishing of forms already finished.

This produces the landscape’s strangest property. Under particular combinations of heat, cloud, and wind, distance becomes unreliable.

A plateau visible across one gulf may seem to draw near, its trees and abandoned shelters suddenly distinct. A traveler may set out toward it along a known ridge, only to find the intervening chasm widening with every step. Conversely, isolated shelves sometimes meet during storms. Their cloud-wrapped rims overlap without impact, joined for minutes or hours by a trembling saddle of wet stone and roots. Plants cross these temporary bridges. Rare animals appear on summits where they were never seen before. Occasionally a roof stone, a tool, or an extinguished cooking fire is found after the plateaus separate.

The inhabitants do not say that the land moves. They say the gulfs breathe.

According to their understanding, stone is fixed, but separation is a kind of weather. Nearness and farness gather, intensify, and pass like rain. For this reason, maps show not distances but tendencies: which places approach during hot winds, which recede under thunder, which have not come near in living memory. A destination is reached not only by walking but by waiting for the world to make the correct shape.

At midday the light is ordinary and strong. The green summit carpets shine, wet rock turns almost black, and cloud shadows race visibly across miles of open ground. There is no supernatural color in the sky, no permanent twilight to soften the exposure. The strangeness lies in how clearly everything can be seen and how little that clarity guarantees.

Toward evening, condensation gathers. The roar deepens as cooling air descends against the warm updrafts. Ribbons of cloud spill over the plateau rims like slow waterfalls, then are seized and thrown upward in white spirals. Leaves fold. Burrowing animals seal their entrances. People lie close to the ground in their stone shelters while the roofs strain against their pins.

Outside, the wind courts begin to answer one another.

One gives a low note, another a higher one from beyond the gulf, then a third sounds from somewhere that should be too distant to hear. The tones pass through the foundations of the plateaus, so loud that they cease to resemble sound and become pressure, weather, law.

By darkness, the entire high country is booming.

In the morning there may be a new pool beside the path, a grove stripped bare, an unfamiliar animal crouched under the eaves, or another green table resting close across a gap that yesterday held only haze. No one expresses surprise. Ropes are checked, water is gathered, and someone climbs to the nearest rim to see what, for the moment, the world has placed within reach.
