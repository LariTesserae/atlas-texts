### The Black Pith City

The city occupies a hollow no larger, perhaps, than a buried room.

That is difficult to believe from within it.

Its distances are made deceptive by darkness and by emptiness. Streets run out across broad, nearly level floors and do not meet another wall for a long time. Towers rise sparsely, each isolated from the next by plazas of cold air. Bridges vanish into the black and remain gone for minutes beneath a walking hand. Yet the whole settlement is enclosed inside the dry heart of a single ancient plant: a subterranean storage organ, seed, gall, or root-knot buried beneath a salt plain.

No one within the city has ever found a useful name for the plant. It is simply the Whole.

There is no natural light. Not dimness, not night under stars, but complete and enduring darkness. Surfaces are known by touch, by echo, by scent, and by the different ways they conduct vibration. The oldest maps are not drawn. They are sequences of taps, knots, ridges, and hollow reeds: maps to be read by fingers or sounded against the floor.

The city’s ground is the flattened pith at the center of the Whole. Once it was soft tissue, swollen with stored moisture, but its water vanished ages ago. What remains is a pale substance no inhabitant has seen: cork-light, cool, fibrous, and dry enough to pull oils from the skin. Under pressure it does not crumble like rotten wood. It separates into patient layers, sheets thinner than bark paper, each preserving the compressed channels of cells that lived before the city began.

All other terrain rises from this floor in low, worn forms. There are ribbed banks where vascular bundles pass beneath the streets, broad steps made from concentric growth rings, and shallow basins once occupied by droplets of sap. The basins have been dry for so long that their edges have lost all sharpness. Generations of hands, roots, settling dust, and minute contractions have polished them into smooth bowls.

Nothing here flows.

Water is not merely scarce; it belongs to a previous condition of the world. The city’s chemistry runs on waxes, resins, oils, dry salts, and slow exchanges of gas. Its living tissues drink vapor so efficiently that the air tastes desiccated. Exposed moisture would be seized at once by a thousand root hairs and carried into sealed organs. A wet surface could not persist.

The Whole is still alive.

Vegetation fills nearly every solid thing in the hollow, though at first the city seems empty. The apparent stone is compressed bark. The ropes are twisted phloem. Doors are overlapping scales that curl shut when touched. Walls have been trained rather than laid, coaxed over centuries into partitions, vaults, stairs, conduits, and buttresses. Buildings stand widely apart because each is not a collection of materials but an individual growth: a columnar bud hollowed into rooms, a folded leaf hardened into a hall, a cluster of root tips braided into a watchtower.

Between them there is almost nothing.

The plazas are vacant. The long streets have no weeds because the streets themselves are plant tissue. There are no insects in the cracks, no worms under the paving, no birds in the upper hollows, no warm bodies sleeping behind the walls. No creature rustles, breathes, calls, hunts, or decays. The ecosystem consists entirely of plants and plant-derived structures, with fungi absent or indistinguishable from specialized filaments of the Whole. Pollination, where it still occurs, is performed by contact: stamens grow across deliberate gaps over the course of decades, touch, exchange dust, and withdraw.

The city may never have had animal inhabitants. Its urban character comes from organization rather than population. There are districts, roads, workshops, reservoirs, public chambers, boundary markers, and immense acoustic avenues, but all have been formed by rooted intelligences. Their thought, if it is thought, moves in gradients of pressure and chemistry. A decision can take a season to cross a courtyard. A civic argument may occupy three hundred years.

Their technology is simple because their patience is not.

Cuts are made with flakes of mineral salt or with thorns hardened in resin. Fibers are spun by torsion. Joints are pegged, wrapped, grafted, or persuaded to scar closed. There is no furnace: open flame would be catastrophic, and there is little free oxygen to sustain one. Metal, where it occurs, is cold native copper pressed into hooks and sounding plates. Most mechanisms are levers of branchwood, wedges of hornlike seed coat, counterweights filled with dry granules, and valves that open when their fibers shrink.

The city’s loudness is therefore startling.

It is never quiet.

The Whole contracts in the cool. Its outer tissues, buried under immeasurable weight, pull against the rigid inner ribs. The strain travels inward and is released as enormous reports: wooden detonations that cross the hollow from wall to wall. A single splitting fiber can sound like a tower collapsing. Afterward, the echoes circle through the streets, return altered by chambers and galleries, and fade only after a long interval.

Beneath those shocks is a continuous communal noise. Dry vessels whistle as gases move through them. Seed-rattle elevators pour hard kernels from one chamber to another. Suspended bridges groan in different tones according to their tension. Broad leaves, reduced by age to parchment membranes, tremble in the faint respiratory currents and produce a ceaseless thunder like distant surf—though nothing in the city knows the sea.

Sound is both weather and illumination.

A resident plant senses the city through its roots. It sends a knock into the floor and receives the shape of nearby streets. Architects tune buildings by thickness: a healthy wall answers with a low, round tone; a dying wall gives a short papery click. Towers are built around resonant shafts so that structural changes announce themselves. When a crack begins several districts away, warning pulses pass through the vascular roads faster than any physical messenger could travel.

The oldest quarter lies close to the center, where erosion has reached its final state. Here every protrusion has been worn down by touch, vibration, settling fibers, and the slow abrasive movement of salt dust. Thresholds have become shallow depressions. Inscriptions are smooth. Staircases are ramps. Columns have thinned into waistless curves and then thickened again where the Whole grew scars around them.

Nothing ruins quickly. Nothing heals quickly. Collapse is a civic event measured across generations.

In one plaza stands—or grows—the city’s most ancient feature: a vast stump scarcely higher than a table. Its top has been eroded perfectly flat. Fine concentric seams run through it, each one a former age of growth. The stump is called the Low Archive. To read it, one presses a stylus into selected seams and listens through the other end. Every ring returns a different resonance. The oldest records are the deepest notes, almost too low to hear, felt instead as an ache in the root or palm.

According to those records, the hollow was once small and wet. Its walls pressed close around a soft center. Then came a long drying, a retreat of sap, and an inward death that created space. The city grew into that absence. Every avenue occupies a place where living tissue withdrew. Every house is built along the frontier between survival and desiccation.

That frontier still moves, but very slowly.

Once in several lifetimes, a wall recedes the width of a finger. Elsewhere, a blind shoot advances into a plaza. Streets must be redirected. Buildings fuse. A district may spend centuries being gently crushed between two thickening ribs, its halls narrowing until no passage remains. Another district opens when ancient pith separates into layers and exposes a new cavity, untouched, cold, and utterly black.

Exploration of such a cavity is done by noise. Stakes are driven at intervals. Lines are stretched from one to the next. Workers strike the floor with resin mallets and wait for the echoes to reveal pits, overhangs, or soft regions. They carry no lamps. The idea of light exists only as an obscure botanical reflex: sealed buds sometimes attempt to turn toward something that is not there.

The temperature remains cool and nearly constant. There are no days. There is only the deep thermal breathing of the world outside, delayed by layers of bark and soil. A slight chill arrives perhaps months after winter begins above. A faint warmth follows long after summer has passed. These changes are enough to tighten bridges, open scale-doors, and alter the pitch of every tower. Thus the city has seasons of sound rather than weather.

In the cold phase, streets crack sharply and distant chambers boom.

In the less-cold phase, the Whole relaxes. Walls creak. Resin softens. The city’s voice lowers.

No wind enters from outside, but pressure migrates through microscopic pores. It moves down the main avenues as a dry exhalation carrying traces of mineral, dormant pollen, old resin, and the bitter odor of defensive compounds. Where the avenues narrow, this breath becomes loud enough to shake the membrane roofs. Where they widen, it disappears into the enormous-feeling pocket of the hollow.

The edges of the city are difficult to define. Urban structures thin into untended rootwork, then return unexpectedly beyond vacant tracts. A lone grafted tower may stand at the far end of a bare plain of pith. A ceremonial road may lead to a wall with no opening. Whole neighborhoods exist as plans impressed chemically into dormant tissue, waiting centuries for conditions that may never come.

There are rules here, though none are written.

Nothing wet may remain exposed.

Nothing may be burned.

No living wall may be cut before its tension has been sounded.

A new structure must be joined to an old root, so that no building stands wholly alone.

Every hollow must be tested for breath.

Every major fracture must be answered by three knocks from each district that survives it.

The last rule is older than the city: when the Whole speaks from below, all other sound must cease.

This happens rarely. The mills stop. Rattles are packed with fiber. Workers hold bridges still. Even the whispering valves are pinched shut. For a moment the city discovers true silence.

Then, from somewhere beneath the polished floor, comes a pulse too deep to be called a noise. The streets lift by an imperceptible amount. Towers flex. Dust rises without being seen. Every root tip in the hollow receives the same slow pressure.

It may be growth. It may be the settling of the dead center. It may be a signal from parts of the plant extending miles beyond the city, under the salt field, into other buried hollows.

The pulse passes. The city resumes its rattling, booming, whistling labor.

Nothing appears to have changed.

Years later, a doorway opens where no doorway was before.
