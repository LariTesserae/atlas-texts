At this altitude the land has no visible ground.

What appears from a distance to be a dark upland forest is, on approach, a single continuous depth of vegetation spread across a chain of high basins. It fills valleys, climbs scarps, bridges ravines, and pours in slow green-black shelves over the shoulders of the mountains. There are no trunks in the familiar sense, no soil beneath them, and scarcely any stone left exposed. The oldest rock has been enclosed, dissolved grain by grain, and incorporated into living tissue. Here the plants are not inhabitants of the landscape. They are its terrain.

The surface underfoot is a hard mesh of rootwood: ridged, fibrous, and cold enough to silver with rime. It yields slightly to weight, though not always where it is stepped upon. In places the mesh is only a handspan thick over hollow chambers; elsewhere it descends for hundreds of metres in interwoven columns. Ancient compression has turned the lowest layers almost mineral—dense lignin, black cellulose, veins of resinous amber—while new growth continually spreads over them. Weathering has rounded the immense forms into long terraces and broad, settled slopes, as though ordinary erosion had worked on them for geological ages. Yet the contours are maintained by growth as much as by decay. A ridge worn down by frost may be rebuilt from below before the following season.

The country is open in extent but crowded in substance. One can see across several kilometres from a high swell, over a broken plain of leaf canopies, spore towers, ribbed bladders, and low plains of tightly packed needles. Beyond that rise pale vegetal escarpments resembling cliffs of layered shale. Their strata are annual skins. Along their faces, whole acres peel loose in the dry cold, fold inward, and are digested.

There is little free water. Rain comes irregularly, in fine diagonal sheets that vanish into the canopy almost as soon as they arrive. Meltwater never gathers into broad lakes. It is caught by cups, gutters, hairs, sealed cisterns, and vast translucent sacs suspended in the depths. For much of the year the principal moisture is condensation. Warm vapour rising from the lower living mass meets the cold upper air and pearls upon every exposed filament. At dawn, millions of droplets bead the ridges, giving the upland a dim metallic sheen. By midday they have been drawn inward through capillary mouths.

The rivers run inside.

They move through flexible conduits beneath the walking surface: clear water, green sap, dissolved stone, sugars, antifreezing oils, and clouds of microscopic spores. Occasionally a conduit ruptures. A slope then darkens as water wells through it, and a temporary stream rushes down a crease in the rootwood. Within hours, pale feeding fronds close around the flow. Within days, the channel is sealed and the river has been redirected into the body of the land. What remains is a line of glassy ice and a stain of intense new growth.

The cold controls everything but stops nothing. Frost is not an interruption here; it is one of the mechanisms. Some tissues expand when frozen, driving pistons of woody cartilage into neighbouring chambers. Others snap along prepared seams, releasing coiled tendrils or opening ranks of seed vanes. Vast seedpods split under nocturnal ice pressure, not with an audible crack but with a soft separation, their inner walls padded in felt. At sunrise the loosened seeds rise on the thin mountain wind like grey ash.

The entire region has the organization of an industrial complex, though nothing has been built and no hand has designed it.

On the western slopes stand fields of chimney-plants, each a hollow cylinder several metres across. They pull cold air down through lattices of damp fibre and vent warmer breath from crowns far above. The resulting pressure differences drive underground flows. On the basin floors, banks of broad leaves tilt and lock together to catch daylight; beneath them, sugars are concentrated in pulsing knots and pumped toward the colder heights. Along the escarpments, root mills grip exposed rock with concentric rings. Acids soften the stone, rotating toothlike plates scrape it loose, and cilia sort the grains by size and chemistry. Iron migrates to red tissues. Silica is laid into transparent spines. Calcium becomes white internal scaffolding. Nothing is discarded. Even windblown dust is combed from the air.

These processes have the scale and relentlessness of mechanized production, yet their motion is nearly silent. There are no engines to knock, no gears to shriek, no animals calling through the canopy. Pressure is carried by fluid. Bearings are lubricated with wax. Impacts are absorbed by cork and pith. If one kneels and presses an ear to the surface, there may be a faint sequence of internal sensations: a liquid pulse, the drag of fibres under tension, the minute fizz of gas crossing a membrane. Standing upright, one hears almost nothing beyond the thin wind.

This quiet makes the landscape’s turbulence difficult to understand at first. Nothing seems hurried. Then a distant canopy buckles.

A dark line runs for kilometres through the basin as linked plates of foliage turn edgewise to the sky. One district goes from green to silver in seconds. Elsewhere a grove of tall bladder stems collapses, each bending into the next until the whole formation lies flat; from the opened space rise thousands of warmer, darker fronds that had waited beneath it. A root shelf extends over a ravine, branches, thickens, becomes a bridge, and then is severed by its own parent mass when mineral flow changes. Towers inflate overnight and are reabsorbed before noon. Entire valleys alter their internal drainage in response to a few minutes of rain.

There is no stable individual plant. Forms bud from the common substrate, perform a task, and disappear back into it. A “tree” may be a seasonal cooling fin. A meadow may be the exposed digestive surface of something kilometres wide. The pale flowers that open in calm weather are valves, releasing excess vapour; their apparent petals are thin radiators furred with ice. The low crimson shrubs of the southern ridges are emergency organs, produced wherever the living land has suffered a deep tear. They clot ruptures with resin and then consume themselves.

Because growth is everywhere, decay is equally pervasive. Old tissues do not rot in heaps. They descend. The surface opens beneath them in soft circular mouths, and mats of exhausted fibre fold into warm fermenting chambers. There they are separated, dissolved, and returned upward. The local ecology is less a contest among species than a restless allocation of functions across one entangled commonwealth. Whether it is truly one organism or innumerable organisms so interdependent that the distinction has failed is impossible to tell.

Animals are rare and seem almost accidental.

Small white crawlers sometimes emerge near ruptured water conduits. They have six or ten legs—the count varies—and translucent bodies filled with green fluid stolen from the landscape. They leave no lasting tracks because the surface lifts behind them. High above, on certain clear afternoons, a lone black glider may circle the warm breath of the chimney fields without flapping. Nothing answers it. There are no herds, no birdsong, no clouds of insects. Most pollination is done by wind, internal transport, or mobile flowers that detach and creep across the root mesh on soft cilia.

The few larger animals live cautiously. A long-limbed grazer, scarcely distinguishable from dead twigs, visits the frost meadows during the brief dimness before sunrise. It feeds only from tissues already marked for shedding. To bite living production-growth invites a response: nearby leaves turn toward the wound, the footing softens, and slender adhesive threads emerge from seams in the ground. The landscape is not predatory in any deliberate sense, but it repairs itself without regard for what may be standing on the damage.

There are rules here, though they are rules of flow rather than territory.

Nothing remains exposed unless exposure serves a function. Hollows either become reservoirs or are filled. Shadows induce upward growth; heat induces ventilation; injury induces enclosure. Water is never allowed to travel far in the open. Dead matter is never permitted to remain separate. Any repeated pressure becomes a path for a time, because the fibres thicken around it—but if the pressure ceases, the path closes like a healed incision. Thus there are no roads, even animal ones, and no ruins. The country will not preserve an artifact. Anything abandoned would be shaded, wrapped, tested by acids, and gradually sorted into usable substances.

Daylight is ordinary and unspectacular, which makes the place stranger. Beneath a pale mountain sky, every impossible mechanism is plainly visible. There is no concealing mist, no permanent twilight. Colours are restrained by cold: deep bottle-green, charcoal, old bronze, lichen-white, the red-brown of dormant tissue. Only where an internal river has burst does vivid colour appear—emerald films, yellow feeding threads, blue translucent bladders swelling with captured water.

In the afternoon the wind usually strengthens. The high canopies do not roar. They streamline themselves before it, flattening leaf by leaf until the whole basin resembles a dark, taut hide. Loose spores travel east in immense but nearly invisible rivers. Frost dust skims the surface. The chimney crowns close. Internal pressure shifts downslope. Far away, a line of pale towers rises one after another, each pushed up by the redistribution, and the horizon changes shape.

By evening the wind falls. Condensation begins again.

Droplets assemble along billions of hairs, bright in the level light. They swell without falling, then vanish inward almost at once. Across the high living plain, structures continue to rise, fold, pump, fracture, and heal, but the vast work proceeds in profound quiet. The landscape is never still. It merely has no need to announce its motion.
