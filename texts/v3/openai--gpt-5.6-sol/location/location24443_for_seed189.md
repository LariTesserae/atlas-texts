Beneath a plain of sun-hot stone lies a chamber no wider than a village and, by any ordinary measure, far deeper than the crust should allow. There is no entrance in the usual sense. One reaches it through seams that open only after rain, descending between roots while the roots themselves loosen and pass the body downward. When the ground dries, the seams knit shut without a scar.

The chamber is called a hollow only because its center is empty.

Everything around that emptiness is plant.

The walls are not covered in vegetation; they are vegetation: pale, load-bearing tissues layered in green membranes, amber pith, and cords of red-brown fiber. Great roots descend from above, but instead of reaching the floor they bend away into the walls, leaving most of the volume unobstructed. Broad leaves tile the ceiling in overlapping fans. Their undersides shine as brightly as leaves seen from above at noon. There is no visible sun, yet the light is full and warm, casting soft-edged shadows and showing colors without distortion—at least at first.

The floor is a single living surface stretched across the bottom of the chamber. It resembles moss from a distance, but each tuft is the crown of a buried plant whose body extends sideways for hundreds of paces. Between the crowns lies resilient green skin, smooth and sparsely freckled with stomata. A foot placed on it leaves a dark print as moisture rises beneath the pressure. The print slowly closes.

Despite the abundance of life, the hollow feels nearly vacant. Growth has been pressed outward into skin, wall, and shell, leaving an enormous, clear interior. There are no groves, no thickets, no fallen branches. Nothing crowds the eye. A person may stand in the center and see almost from one side to the other, across a warm expanse interrupted only by a few narrow blades taller than a person and the dangling threads of roots that have not yet decided where to attach. The plants have made architecture by withdrawing.

The space is young. Its surfaces have the rawness of something recently opened inside a larger body. Ridges along the walls are sharp and wet, like the margins of fresh-cut stems. Pale growth fractures into angular terraces before it has time to slump or soften. Here and there, the floor has split under its own expansion, forming narrow cracks with bright green edges and darkness below. These fissures do not remain long. Fibers bridge them, thicken, and pull them shut over the course of days. There is little debris. Even fragments torn loose are digested by fine white hairs before they can decay.

Water arrives in episodes.

Most of the time the air is only damp, carrying the warm mineral smell of sap, crushed herbs, and earth after distant rain. Condensation beads along the ceiling leaves. The beads swell without falling, sliding instead toward their pointed tips. For hours the hollow may hold its breath beneath thousands of gathering drops.

Then all of them release at once.

A brief underground rain crosses the chamber in silver lines. It drums against the living floor, gathers in temporary hollows, and runs along veins that rise to receive it. The pools are shallow, clear, and short-lived. Within minutes, root mouths open beneath them. Water spirals downward with a sucking sound, leaving only dark patches and a residue of translucent pollen.

There are also streams, though none remains in the same place. Water sometimes breaks from a wall in a narrow horizontal jet and travels several body lengths through open air before entering another wall. On another day it may climb as beads along a dangling root, pass over the ceiling, and vanish through a seam. A traveler who relies on a stream to mark a route will find the route moved by morning.

The chamber is never quiet.

Sap shifts through the walls in great hydraulic pulses. Each pulse begins as a distant impact, followed by a rolling concussion that circles the hollow. Leaves snap open overhead with the report of cloth sails catching wind. Roots tighten against one another and give off long wooden groans. During the rain cycle the noise becomes immense: drumming water, hissing pores, the hollow knock of air forced through stalks, and beneath it all a low rhythmic thud, not unlike a heart but too slow to belong to an animal.

At irregular intervals, a sound like a tree splitting runs from floor to ceiling. A new fault appears in the living wall, bright and sharp. Light pours out of it. The fault then seals from both ends, swallowing the illumination as if closing an eye.

Conversation is possible only in the pauses between these processes. Even then, whispers carry strangely. A word spoken against one wall may return from the opposite side before the speaker has finished saying it. A shout may not echo at all, but emerge hours later as a tremor in the floor.

The light obeys rules of its own.

It has no single source. Brightness collects wherever leaves turn toward it, and leaves turn toward wherever brightness collects. This mutual pursuit sustains a stable, ordinary-looking day throughout most of the chamber. Shadows fall consistently until someone tries to use them for direction. Then their angles begin to differ. A standing blade may cast three shadows, each corresponding to a different place from which it could be observed.

Night does not come gradually. It occurs in small areas.

A patch of darkness may flower on the floor, round and soft-edged. Within it, colors recede, leaves fold, and cool dew forms. Beyond its boundary the chamber remains warm and bright. These nights drift slowly like clouds, though there is no sky beneath which they could pass. Small creatures follow them.

Fauna is scarce. The plants occupy nearly every useful role, leaving little room for grazers or hunters. The most common animal is a flat, translucent moth with wings like two wet seeds. It sleeps inside the moving darkness and feeds on light when the darkness passes over bright leaves. Its feeding leaves tiny black punctures in the air. The punctures persist for a while after the moth has gone.

Long-legged root crabs emerge after underground rain. They are no larger than a hand and bear fragments of bark on their backs. They do not eat the plants. Instead, they trim dead fibers with stone-hard mouthparts and carry the cuttings to fissures in the floor, where the material is accepted and absorbed. Their appearance is brief; as the pools vanish, they fold their legs and enter pores too small to admit them.

There is said to be one larger animal in the hollow, though it is rarely seen whole. Sometimes a smooth flank passes behind a translucent section of wall. Sometimes a row of dark eyes opens in the floor and closes when approached. During the loudest sap pulses, something moves through the central emptiness without displacing air. Its passage bends the hanging roots toward it. Whether these are glimpses of one creature, several creatures, or the chamber perceiving itself as an animal is uncertain.

People do not live here permanently. The hollow refuses permanence.

Those who enter carry little: chipped stone blades, bone needles, twisted cords, leaf cups, and wooden wedges hardened in resin. Metal sweats in the warm air, grows a rind of green tissue, and becomes useless within a day. Fired clay softens as if never baked. Knotted rope sprouts at the knots. Tools made from the chamber’s own discarded fibers fare better, but only briefly. A cut handle may root into the user’s palm if held too long.

No shelters can be built. Poles planted in the floor become stems and draw apart. Mats unfold into leaves. Markers turn themselves toward the light and wander by slow degrees. Even a ring of stones is taken below overnight, each stone wrapped and pulled down by white hairs. For this reason, visitors sleep in the open on broad temporary leaves coaxed from the floor with shallow cuts and careful pressure. By morning the leaves have flattened again.

The skill of traveling here is therefore less like construction than negotiation. A wedge placed in the proper vein can persuade a wall to open. A rhythm tapped with bone against a root can delay a rainburst. Certain leaves, folded by hand in a sequence of three or five, retain water without immediately drinking it. None of these methods works twice in exactly the same way. The chamber remembers handling and changes its response.

Distance is also unreliable, though the hollow remains visibly contained. Two people walking side by side may reach the same hanging root at different times. A wall that appears twenty paces away may take an afternoon to approach, while a root seen beyond it can be touched by reaching backward. Travelers tie themselves together not to avoid becoming lost, but to insist that they remain in the same version of the place.

The plants do not seem troubled by this. Their veins cross distances without passing through the intervening tissue. A seed released from the ceiling may fall upward, vanish in a bright patch, and sprout moments later underfoot. Flowers open before their buds form. Pollen occasionally rises from the floor, enters unopened blossoms, and is later produced by them.

Cause and effect are not wholly reversed; they are tangled like roots.

This becomes clearest during the chamber’s growth surges. The air warms. The low pulse quickens. Every wall brightens from within, revealing vast networks of vessels extending far beyond the visible hollow. The floor tightens until its surface rings underfoot. Cracks race across it in sharp geometric lines, opening not into soil but into brief views of other interiors: a blue cavern full of airborne roots, a black pool beneath a red sky, the underside of a field at noon, where grass roots hang like chandeliers.

The views close quickly. Fresh green tissue floods the fractures. The chamber expands by a few imperceptible measures, though afterward there is more room in the center and less distance between the walls.

Then the rain falls.

The noise becomes unbearable, less like weather than applause inside a living body. Temporary pools flash into existence. Moths wheel through wandering pockets of night. Root crabs rise and begin their trimming. The unseen large creature bends the empty air as it passes. Every leaf turns at once toward a brightness that has appeared in no location.

For several minutes, the entire hollow smells of cut stems and lightning.

Afterward, calm returns—not silence, but the slower groaning calm of growth. Drops tremble on the ceiling. Sharp new seams glisten in the walls. Tools left unattended send out tender white roots. Footprints close. Any trace of occupation is taken up, softened, and made botanical.

The chamber remains hidden below the dry world, brightly lit, almost empty, and completely full: a pocket of open space maintained inside an immeasurable density of leaves, a room a plant has grown so that something absent may continue to exist at its center.
