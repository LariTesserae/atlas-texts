The valley is built from rim to rim.

There is no exposed ground, though at first the slopes appear earthen. Their brown and charcoal surfaces resolve into brick, poured aggregate, corroded iron, and old retaining walls furred with moss. Every incline is terraced. Every terrace carries a conduit, a service road, a row of sheds, or a long bed of planted soil held in riveted troughs. Even the stream at the valley floor runs through a channel of dressed stone, disappearing at intervals beneath turbine halls and emerging white with aeration.

The place lies high enough that clouds routinely enter it. They drag across the upper works, snagging on gantries and chimney braces. Rain is frequent but seldom heavy. It gathers instead: on cable sheaths, window mullions, fern leaves, handrails, the undersides of pipes. Condensation beads on cold machinery and drops steadily into gutters. Walls weep through old mortar. Steps remain dark for days after a shower.

Water is everywhere without dominating the landscape. It moves through narrow races, settling tanks, roof cisterns, culverts, cooling galleries, lock chambers, and open gutters lined with green weed. In the oldest districts, drains have been repaired so many times that they resemble masonry streams, wandering around later foundations and dividing where buildings have settled. Small square reservoirs occupy rooftops. Their surfaces are black beneath the dim sky, disturbed by inflow pipes and by the slow rotation of skimming arms.

The valley has been engineered for centuries. Its present shape is not raw or new. Brick corners are rounded from weather and use. Granite curbs have shallow channels worn into them by cart wheels, then steel tires, then the hard rubber wheels of electric carriers. Iron stairs are polished in the center and orange at the edges. Whole factory fronts lean a few degrees downhill but have been stabilized with buttresses, cross-ties, and neighboring structures built snugly against them. Nothing is abandoned enough to become a ruin. Anything disused is annexed, roofed over, planted, or made into storage.

There is scarcely a gap between one function and the next.

Workshops fill the undersides of bridges. Greenhouses occupy the spaces above boiler houses, warmed by waste heat. Water tanks stand on the roofs of warehouses, and footpaths thread beneath them. Pipes cross streets at shoulder height, forcing pedestrians under low arches. Transformer yards are fitted into courtyards no larger than rooms. Rail spurs vanish through building walls and emerge several blocks farther on, having passed through loading floors, lifts, and covered switchbacks.

Seen from the upper road, the valley looks less like a town than a single enormous apparatus assembled in layers. Yet no central plan is visible. It has accumulated by repair and addition. Mills stand inside later mills. Old aqueduct piers support tram platforms. A row of narrow houses occupies the former bed of an elevated canal, while a new canal runs in steel flumes above their roofs. The whole construction extends beyond the nearest basin, over the saddle, and into adjacent valleys. In mist, the farther districts appear as repeated bands of lights, retaining walls, planted roofs, and vertical stacks.

Vegetation is nearly as dense as the building.

Because there is no natural substrate left exposed, plants grow wherever soil and water have been allowed to collect: deep roof beds, filter marshes, retaining baskets, orchard terraces, drainage seams. Moss coats shaded brick. Liverworts make black-green fans beneath leaking joints. Ferns rise from cable channels. Willow and alder are trained along settling ponds, their roots helping clean the water before it returns to the works. On the sunnier southern walls, which are only intermittently sunny, vines climb mesh screens six and eight stories high.

The planted areas are productive rather than ornamental. There are cabbages with blue leaves, rows of leeks, berry canes, medicinal herbs, and small tough apple trees espaliered against warm walls. Watercress fills the shallower treatment races. Reeds stand in rectangular beds between factories, dense enough to hide the concrete partitions beneath them. Mushrooms are cultivated in old service tunnels where the stone sweats continuously.

Lushness does not soften the construction so much as occupy it completely. Green matter fits into the same tight system as water, freight, electricity, and people. Hedges are clipped clear of signal lamps. Roots are guided away from inspection hatches. Fallen leaves are swept into chutes and carried to digesters. Even spontaneous growth is tolerated according to whether it obstructs a valve or stabilizes a wall.

The air is cool throughout the year. At dawn, mist lies low between the buildings, and the higher terraces may stand above it under a flat silver ceiling. By afternoon the fog often thins without entirely lifting. Light reaches the valley indirectly, reflected from cloud and wet masonry. There are few hard shadows. Colors remain deep and subdued: bottle green, wet red brick, oxidized copper, black water, pale concrete, yellow lichen.

Lamps burn even at midday in enclosed lanes. They are practical, widely spaced, and hooded against rain. Windows contribute muted rectangles of amber or bluish white. The brightest places are the cultivation halls, where rows of growing lights shine behind condensation-streaked glass. From outside they resemble green-lit furnaces.

Despite the extent of the machinery, the valley is mostly quiet.

Much of its industry is enclosed behind thick walls or sunk into the terraces. Motors are electric. Shafts run in oiled housings. Freight moves slowly on rails, and the water systems are designed to fall in stages rather than crash outright. At a distance the entire district produces a low, nearly continuous pressure in the air: transformer hum, ventilator breath, the subdued vibration of turbines underfoot.

Closer sounds stand out clearly. Water ticks from a pipe flange into a metal basin. A relay closes behind a wall. Somewhere uphill, a carrier’s warning bell rings twice. Chains tighten with a brief iron murmur. Rain moves across a greenhouse roof and fades. Voices are rare in the open lanes, not because the valley is empty, but because most activity occurs indoors, beneath canopies, or within the layered structures.

The quiet conceals constant motion.

The valley is always being redistributed. Gates open and send water from one terrace to another. Elevators lift freight through the cores of buildings. Bridges rotate aside for maintenance barges in the lower channel. Trains of short electric wagons emerge from one doorway and disappear into another. Roof panels fold open when sensors detect a break in the cloud, then close as mist returns. Mobile cranes crawl along rails fixed to the upper retaining walls, moving pipes, tanks, entire prefabricated rooms.

Weather adds another kind of turbulence. A passing cloud may condense so heavily on the cold upper works that gutters overflow within minutes. Sluices respond automatically. Storage ponds rise. Pumps reverse. Water is sent downhill through alternate routes, and whole neighborhoods adjust their drainage. Sirens do not sound; colored lamps change at junctions, and barriers settle across low streets with calm mechanical certainty.

Nothing remains still long enough to be neglected, yet little appears hurried. Change is continuous, procedural, and practiced. One factory is dismantled while its replacement is assembled around it. A greenhouse migrates roof by roof as underlying structures are repaired. A canal is drained, relined, and returned to service without stopping the flow, which has been diverted through older pipes buried two levels below. The landscape is stable precisely because it never ceases to alter itself.

Animals are uncommon. The packed construction and controlled planting leave few unmanaged niches. Now and then a small bird crosses the basin, a dark speck passing between chimneys, but flocks do not gather. In the reed filters there may be frogs, heard more often than seen. Pale fish inhabit inspection pools below the treatment works. Cats appear at loading doors and vanish indoors again. There are insects in the greenhouses, mostly pollinators maintained in screened sections, and slender moths around the lamps on damp evenings.

Their rarity makes each animal conspicuous. A heron standing in a settling pond may stop work along an entire gallery as people pause behind windows to look at it.

From the highest terrace, the valley seems both immense and crowded beyond measure. There is no vacant foreground from which to observe it. The lookout itself is the roof of a pumping station, planted with thyme and crossed by ventilation ducts. Below, buildings descend in interlocking shelves. Beyond them rise other built slopes, wet and green, their uppermost towers entering cloud.

The valley gives no impression of wilderness, nor of having conquered wilderness. That distinction is too old to matter here. Rain falls on made stone. Roots bind imported soil. Water follows channels that have existed long enough to feel inevitable. The machinery is weathered, repaired, and ordinary. The vegetation is cultivated but vigorous. Every surface belongs simultaneously to infrastructure, climate, and growth.

After dusk, the mist thickens. Lamps become soft disks. The lower channel disappears, though its movement can still be felt through the soles of the feet. Far across the basin, a line of freight windows travels horizontally, pauses, and begins to climb.

Then the valley closes around its own activity: dim, saturated, almost silent, and changing everywhere at once.
