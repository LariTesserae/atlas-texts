The underland is not dark.

Light pours from the ceiling in broad, colorless sheets, as steady as an overcast noon. Far overhead, the vault is plated with translucent stone: immense deposits of salt, gypsum, and cloudy crystal compressed into a single luminous roof. Heat from below drives vapor upward into hairline fissures. There it condenses, catches whatever radiance exists in the world above, and scatters it through the crystal beds. The result is daylight without a sun—soft in the distance, glaring on wet surfaces, strangely shadowless beneath the largest arches.

The cavern extends for many leagues. It is less a chamber than a buried region, with horizons, weather, and districts of differing stone. Ridges rise from the floor in raw folds. New escarpments have the blunt, torn appearance of clay pulled apart by hand. Basins subside while one watches. Pale columns thrust upward a finger’s width each season, fed by warm mineral seepage. Elsewhere the ground has not decided whether to be solid: shelves of chalk-colored crust drift over mud, and islands of pumice turn slowly in subterranean pools.

Nothing here looks old enough to have been eroded. There are few rounded stones, few patient channels, no broad alluvial plains. Water has only begun its work. It drips, beads, sweats, and gathers in pockets, but seldom travels far. Brief streams leap into existence after pressure changes in the walls, run noisily for an hour, then vanish down fresh cracks. Their beds are sharp and provisional. They carry splinters rather than pebbles.

Warmth rises from countless seams. It has no single center. Kneeling anywhere, one can often find a patch where the stone is hot against the palm, another where condensation cools it, and between them a strip of comfortable dampness crowded with tracks. The air is heavy but breathable, smelling of wet limestone, iron, hot shells, and the sour musk of animals packed too closely together.

There are almost no plants.

No roots split the floor. No leaves soften the ledges. Nothing stands quietly and turns toward the light. A few black microbial varnishes stain the warmest springs, and translucent fungal films sometimes form over drowned carcasses, but even these are temporary. The land belongs to moving life.

Animals cover it.

At first the distant slopes seem clothed in gray moss. Then the gray changes direction. It parts around a vent, bunches at a pool, pours over a low ridge, and reveals itself as millions of six-legged grazers no larger than fingernails. They rasp mineral films from the stone with porcelain mouthparts. Their bodies are soft and nearly transparent except for dense coils of copper-colored gut. In their passage, entire hills appear to shiver.

Among them walk heavier creatures: broad, low-backed things armored in overlapping plates, each plate grown from minerals swallowed in infancy. Their hide may be alabaster, rust-red, blue-black, or glittering with embedded pyrite. They move in crowded files, knocking shoulder against shoulder, breaking newborn crust beneath their feet. Some carry nurseries in chambers along their spines. Others trail clusters of pearl-like eggs glued under their tails.

The air is hardly less occupied. Flocks of membrane-winged animals circulate beneath the luminous roof, so numerous that their passage produces moving dimness across the valley floor. They have no feathers and scarcely any flesh. Warm updrafts hold them aloft while they sieve airborne mites, spores, salt dust, and the tiny floating young of larger species. When a thermal collapses, thousands descend at once, clattering against pillars and one another like dry leaves thrown into a well.

The pools are packed rim to rim. Blind ribbon-swimmers knot themselves into living ropes. Shell-bellied filterers pulse in the shallows. Pale hunters lie disguised as wet stone until something touches their sensory whiskers, then snap shut with a noise like a hand clap. Even individual droplets may contain frantic, wheel-shaped organisms spinning around grains of sulfur.

Life here is not arranged into peaceful layers. It surges.

A spawning migration can divert a stream. A feeding swarm can strip the microbial skin from a square mile of rock and leave the ground chemically altered. Burrowing colonies undermine shelves until they collapse, opening vents that release new heat and poison old nesting grounds. Predators follow prey in such numbers that their bodies become a second terrain—rivers of shell and muscle running over the mineral one.

The cavern is loud with it. There is the continuous abrasion of tiny teeth on stone, a dry whisper multiplied beyond counting. There are wingbeats, shell-clicks, breeding horns, alarm rattles, and the booming of animals that communicate through the floor. Pools slap at their margins though there is no wind. From deep cracks come periodic groans as the young rock settles. When a ceiling plate shifts, the entire underland answers: flocks explode upward, herd-creatures stampede, burrows empty, and distant colonies begin hammering warnings through the ground.

Silence occurs only after catastrophe, and then only for a few breaths.

Across this living pressure, scattered structures stand like hesitant punctuation. They are made by hand, without mortar or forged tools. Low walls of stacked shell divide a few defensible ledges. Rope bridges, braided from shed tendons and strips of cured hide, cross steaming fissures. At the mouths of stable caves are screens made from the rib-fans of enormous animals. Heat has warped them into elegant curves.

The builders live in small groups because large settlements attract too much attention. Their huts crouch beneath overhangs and are rebuilt constantly. Floors are paved with flat plates cut from dead armor. Gut cord binds frames of bone. Water is caught in nested shells, covered quickly before swimmers colonize it. Stone blades are chipped from fresh glass exposed by tremors; older glass is softer, chemically clouded, or already gnawed by creatures seeking trace salts.

Metal exists mostly as color in the walls. Occasionally a native copper lump is hammered cold into a hook, awl, or resonant warning disk. Such objects are precious less for their strength than because many animals dislike their taste.

The people—or whatever upright, many-jointed inhabitants claim that name here—do not cultivate. Cultivation is nearly impossible in a place where nothing remains where it is planted. They herd briefly, trap seasonally, gather eggs during surges, and follow the emergence of warm springs. Their maps are not maps of location but of sequence:

*After the red mites rise, the western floor will split.*  
*When the ceiling flock goes pale, cover the cisterns.*  
*Three nights after the stone begins to sing, abandon low ground.*

Their oldest structures are not houses but listening posts. These are narrow towers, rarely taller than two bodies, built of carefully balanced slabs. Bone needles extend from them into cracks. Shell cups amplify vibrations. A skilled listener can tell whether the trembling beneath the floor is a herd, a flood, a gas pocket, or a new ridge beginning to push itself into the cavern.

The distinction matters because the landscape is still being made.

At irregular intervals, pressure deep below forces warm slurry upward. The floor domes, splits, and unfolds. Whole chains of hills can rise in a month. They emerge wet and pale, unmarked by weather, steaming gently in the constant light. Animals arrive before the stone has cooled. First come the airborne dust-feeders, then the mineral raspers, then egg-layers, scavengers, and hunters. Within days the bare ridge is moving. Within weeks its crevices are inhabited. Within a year it may collapse and become a basin full of cloudy water and drowning nests.

Elsewhere, stone is destroyed almost as quickly as it forms—not by wind or rain, but by appetite. Creatures bore for salts, lick away soft deposits, crack columns to reach warm interiors, and digest certain minerals into stronger shells. Geology passes through bodies. Cliffs become herds; herds die and become reefs; reefs are quarried into walls; walls are eaten by the young of the same species whose ancestors supplied the stone.

Moisture arrives in episodes. The luminous ceiling may sweat for weeks, covering every surface in droplets. At such times the cavern fills with silver dripping, and the dust is pressed flat. Animals breed explosively. Egg masses appear on every upright surface. Shallow hollows become nurseries dense enough to boil.

Then the condensation ceases. Pools shrink. Their occupants crowd inward until water is barely visible beneath backs, mouths, and shells. Predators wait around the edges. Smaller creatures seal themselves into mineral cases or crawl into the damp breathing passages of larger animals. The air grows powdery. Static flickers across wings.

There are also sudden rains, though no cloud produces them. Reservoirs trapped above the crystal roof find new fractures and descend in localized curtains. A column of rain may fall for hours in one place while the ground a hundred paces away remains dry. These downpours are landmarks in motion. Camps uproot to follow them. Herds converge. Temporary waterfalls carve the first shallow inscriptions into newborn cliffs.

The unusual law of this place is that emptiness does not endure. A cleared floor becomes a congregation. An abandoned shelter becomes a nest before its hearth is cold. A new pool clouds with swimmers as if they had been waiting inside the water itself. Even a footprint fills: first with condensation, then with mites, then with something that eats the mites.

Travelers therefore learn to seek not solitude but favorable crowds. A river of harmless shell-grazers may be safer than a quiet corridor. A deafening rookery means few ambush predators. A bridge crawling with tiny red scavengers is sound; if the scavengers flee, its fibers are about to part.

The most dangerous places are the still ones.

There are smooth white basins where no animal crosses and no water settles. Sound dies near their margins. Warm air rises from them without trembling. Around each lies a ring of abandoned shells, intact but empty, all facing inward. Some say these basins are unfinished mouths in the earth. Others say they are places where the cavern’s relentless life has, for unknown reasons, been refused.

They do not stay empty forever.

Fine cracks are already appearing across their floors. Light leaks through from beneath—not the diffused white of the ceiling, but a dense amber glow. The cracks widen by fractions. A moist clicking travels through the stone. Listening towers many miles away have begun to answer with sympathetic notes.

All across the underland, animals pause when those notes sound. Wings hesitate. Armor plates lift. The rasping multitudes fall quiet just long enough for the newborn ground to be heard moving.

Then everything starts again, louder than before.
