At the top of a mountain too narrow to possess a proper summit, there is a hollow.

It is not large. From rim to rim it might contain a chapel, a market court, or a cluster of small houses, though nothing could easily be built there now. The hollow lies nearly enclosed by steep walls of pale stone. Beyond those walls the land drops away with such finality that distant ranges appear less like neighboring mountains than blue sediment suspended in the sky.

The place is called, or might once have been called, the Needle Court.

Thousands of stone spines crowd its interior. They rise shoulder to shoulder from the floor: some no higher than a hand, others tall enough to divide the court into aisles. They are not crystals, though at first they resemble a bed of enormous quartz. Each needle is made of thinly laminated limestone and gray volcanic dust, compressed into alternating bands. Wind, frost, and rare water have cut away the softer layers, leaving blades, fins, hooked shelves, and hollow tubes. Many lean against one another. Some have weathered into lattices dense enough to stop a thrown pebble.

There is scarcely any open ground. Passage requires turning sideways between formations, stepping over ribs of stone, and ducking beneath mineral bridges. From above, the whole hollow looks packed with pale teeth.

The court is cold even in full sun. Its elevation has thinned the air until breath feels insufficient and every exposed surface seems close to the greater cold above. Yet the light is fierce. The sky has a hard, nearly violet clarity; shadows are blue-black and sharply edged. Snow does not often lie here because the wind removes it, lifting it over the rim in white banners, but frost persists wherever direct light cannot reach.

Water comes in brief and secretive forms.

At night, vapor from the lower valleys climbs the mountain and condenses on the needles. By dawn every edge is furred with clear ice. When sunlight enters, it advances slowly down the western wall and touches the formations one rank at a time. The ice loosens. Drops gather beneath ledges, hesitate, then fall into narrow stone cups worn into the floor. For an hour the court is full of tiny lenses of water, each brilliantly lit, each reflecting an upside-down fragment of sky.

Most of these pockets vanish before noon. Some drain through hairline fractures. Others freeze again in shaded cavities and remain for weeks as dark, buried beads. A few collect in deep basins lined with black mineral glaze. The water there tastes of iron, chalk, and old smoke. Nothing drinks it.

There is no vegetation. No lichen colors the rock, no moss softens the channels, no root has ever opened a seam. There are no insects in the sun and no birds crossing the hollow. Even the surrounding heights seem avoided by life. Feathers are absent. Droppings are absent. Bones are absent. The place has the unnerving cleanliness of a world from before appetite.

Nevertheless, it possesses something like an ecology.

Ice inhabits the shaded pores. Salt gathers at the margins of evaporating pools. Dust blown from faraway plains settles on the wet stone and is cemented into thin skins. At dusk those skins contract; at sunrise they split. The fragments are caught by the needles, ground finer by vibration, wetted again, and deposited elsewhere. Iron migrates downward in rust-colored threads. White carbonate blooms around certain cavities, forming brittle rims that last a season or a century depending on their angle to the light.

Nothing grows, but the forms accumulate. Nothing feeds, but each process consumes the results of another.

The hollow is old enough to have reached a rough balance. Its walls are deeply weathered without seeming ruinous. Every exposed face has been worked into a stable curve, every channel rehearsed by many brief thaws. Collapse occurs, but rarely as catastrophe. More often one hears a single grain loosen, then a whispering chain of grit descending through the packed stone. A needle may spend decades tipping toward its neighbor before the two touch. Once joined, they brace each other and become an arch.

The court is therefore active without being noisy. Wind passes above it with a steady remote pressure, but among the formations the air is mostly still. Meltwater ticks into basins. Ice gives occasional dry clicks from within the rock. At the warmest part of the day, trapped air escapes from flooded fissures in small, deliberate bubbles.

A louder sound—a snapped tool handle, a shouted word—does not behave normally here. The crowded blades break it apart. Each narrow corridor returns a different sliver, so that a single cry comes back as several quiet syllables from unrelated directions. Soft noises, by contrast, travel surprisingly far. A fingertip drawn over one hollow fin may produce a thin hum in another fin across the court.

There are signs that people once entered.

Near the northern wall, where a shelf offers enough level space for three sleepers, stands a low shelter made of fitted slabs. It has no roof now. The stones bear no mortar and show marks from blunt hammering rather than metal cutting. In one corner lies a shallow bowl pecked directly into the bedrock. Beside it, a gutter leads to a surface on which dawn frost forms more heavily than elsewhere. Whoever made the shelter understood how to harvest the court’s brief water.

Elsewhere, little wedges of dark stone have been driven into cracks. Some still pin cords made from twisted mineral fiber—perhaps spun from the silky crystals found lower on the mountain, perhaps something less familiar. The cords are rigid with age and cold. They connect several needles in a pattern too deliberate to be accidental, but not obviously structural. They may have supported windbreaks. They may have marked safe paths. They may have been an instrument.

At the center of the hollow is the one feature the erosion does not explain.

Nine upright stones form a close ring around a shaft no wider than a person’s shoulders. The uprights are basalt, nearly black, brought from some other stratum of the mountain. Their tops have been polished by hands or by repeated abrasion. Simple notches run down their inward faces. When meltwater gathers, it follows these notches and drips into the shaft.

No bottom can be seen. A pebble dropped within strikes the sides many times, growing fainter, but no final impact returns.

Cold air rises from it at night.

At noon, when the sun stands high enough to reach between the nine stones, the air reverses. For several minutes the shaft breathes downward. Loose dust streams into it. The thin puddles nearest the ring tremble and creep, against the slope of the floor, toward the opening. Then the sun moves on and the effect ends.

This is the court’s one apparent rule: light determines the direction of depth. In shadow, the mountain exhales. Under direct brilliance, it draws inward.

The old builders seem to have known this. A line of chipped symbols circles the ring, crude and repetitive: a hollow triangle, a cluster of vertical strokes, a cup, a darkened disk. They may record days, water measures, or warnings. There are no remains to clarify them. No abandoned clothing, no hearth ash, no graves. Only the shelter, the bowl, the wedges, and one heavy stone mallet lying beneath a shelf as if set down between blows.

Weather arrives suddenly. A cloud can climb from the abyss and fill the court in moments, erasing the bright world beyond the rim. Condensation pearls on every surface. The needles emerge and disappear at arm’s length, making the small hollow feel immeasurably deep. During these immersions the stone darkens from bone-white to gray, then almost black. Water begins traveling along paths invisible in dry weather. Channels awaken. Cups overflow into other cups. The central shaft draws threads of moisture toward itself.

Then the cloud passes.

Sunlight returns with startling force. Every wet edge ignites. The court becomes a dense field of prisms and silver points, bright enough to hurt the eyes. Steam too faint to be warm lifts from the western faces. Frost remains in the eastern hollows. For a little while, freezing, melting, evaporation, mineral deposition, and the slow settling of stone all occur together within the same enclosed space.

By late afternoon the water is gone from the open basins. The black ring casts nine long bars across the needles. The bars climb the opposite wall, merging as the sun lowers, until the entire court is in blue shade while the distant mountains remain ablaze.

Nothing comes to feed. Nothing comes to nest.

The landscape continues its intricate, lifeless work.
