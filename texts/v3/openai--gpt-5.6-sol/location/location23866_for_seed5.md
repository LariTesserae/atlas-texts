At the bottom of an old floodplain, beneath a crust of pale sedge and wind-combed gravel, there is a city hidden in a hollow no wider than a large house.

From above, nothing announces it. The land is low, cold, and almost level. Frost-polished stones lie among mats of gray grass. A few shallow channels wander across the plain, but they carry water only after rain, and even then reluctantly. The entrance is a slit beneath the roots of a prostrate willow. One descends sideways, brushing past dense leaves, into brightness.

The hollow is a glacial pothole worn into white limestone. Its walls have been rounded over immense time, not freshly cut but settled into their final curves: scoops, ledges, shallow alcoves, and smooth channels that seem less eroded than completed. The stone is chalk-white, threaded with mica and translucent quartz. Daylight enters through a narrow opening above and is caught, divided, and reflected until the whole cavity shines with an even, shadowless radiance. There is no visible sun inside. Roofs, leaves, paving stones, and the undersides of bridges are equally clear, as if light were a substance pooled in the basin.

The cold is constant. Not bitter, not windy, but deep and mineral, the cold of a cellar carved beside buried ice. Breath lingers close to the mouth. Bronze chills the fingers immediately. Nothing steams. Nothing thaws. Yet the place is lush.

Vegetation occupies every surface that is not walked upon, and much that is. Moss grows in upholstery-thick banks over the outer walls of houses. Liverworts tile the gutters in glossy green scales. Ferns rise from cracks no wider than thread, their fronds small, dark, and intricately divided. There are pale mushrooms under stairways, beadlike berries in roof gardens, miniature willows trained along parapets, and dense curtains of clubmoss hanging from bridges. Algae make emerald mirrors of the cisterns. Even the paving has seams of luminous green pressed between its stones.

The abundance is compressed downward. Few plants reach the knee. Instead they overlap in dozens of close layers: moss beneath liverwort, liverwort beneath fern, fern beneath woven vines. A hand laid against any wall touches stone, root, leaf, moisture, and some small living creature all at once.

Water exists here chiefly as beads.

It condenses on the limestone and gathers along carved lips above the streets. Each drop swells for hours or days before slipping into a shell, a cup, or a narrow runnel. Rain from the plain sometimes reaches the opening, but it arrives strained through roots and moss, divided into a thousand clear points. There are no torrents. The city’s reservoirs are shallow dishes hollowed into the floor, some natural and some finished with stone tools. Their surfaces remain perfectly still. Drinking water is lifted with ladles of horn or folded bark. Irrigation is done by touching wet cords to roots.

No pipe is pressurized. No wheel turns. There is little metal, and what exists is simple: small bronze hooks, copper needles, soft blades repeatedly sharpened until they have become thin as leaves. Most of the city is made from limestone chips, antler, fired clay, wicker, hide, and wood gathered from the scrub above. Construction is sophisticated not through machinery but through repetition and fit. Stones are shaped by tapping, rubbing, and patience. Beams are lashed rather than nailed. Roofs are living mats grown over wicker ribs.

The settlement is astonishingly dense. Houses occupy every shelf of the pothole, stacked five and six levels high. Some are no larger than cupboards. Their rounded doors open directly onto stairs; their windows look into neighboring kitchens, gardens, workshops, or courtyards scarcely broad enough to kneel in. Ladders cross above alleys. Footbridges cross above ladders. Storage baskets hang beneath bridges. Public ovens are fitted into warm-colored seams of stone, though even their heat seems subdued by the surrounding cold.

There are no empty lots. A space between two walls becomes a shrine, a pigeon shelf, a drying rack, a bed of medicinal moss, or a stair made from three embedded bones. The city has plazas, but they are plazas in function rather than size: widened steps where twelve people might sit close together, knees touching. Its principal avenue is a smooth channel left by ancient meltwater. Two people can pass there only by turning sideways.

Everything is worn. Thresholds have shallow depressions from generations of feet. Handholds are dark and satin-smooth. Steps sag in their centers. Carved faces on lintels have lost their mouths and eyes, leaving only serene ovals. Repairs are themselves old enough to have been repaired. Walls lean into one another and are accepted in that posture, mutually supported by beams, roots, shelves, and adjacent walls. Nothing appears ruined. Weathering is part of the architecture’s final form.

Despite the crowding, the city is almost completely silent.

The moss absorbs footfalls. Doors are padded with felted fiber. Water does not fall far enough to splash. There is no room for wind to gather speed. Voices, when used, are kept low, for sound behaves strangely in the hollow: it does not echo, and it does not travel. A word spoken at arm’s length arrives as a breath against the skin; beyond a few paces it is gone. Hammering is done with wooden mallets wrapped in hide. Stonecutters work by pressure and abrasion rather than blows.

Much of communication is visual. Pale cords stretched across alleys carry knots, colored feathers, seed shells, or pieces of polished chalk. A blue shell means water is available. A white knot marks a birth. Three dark feathers warn that a passage is closed. Hands speak constantly across windows and bridges.

The animals have adapted to the same hush. White-backed voles move through wall gardens without rustling them. Snails graze the cistern rims, their shells patterned like tiny masonry domes. Long-legged cave hares sleep beneath platforms, nearly indistinguishable from bundles of wool. Small, flightless birds nest on roofs; their plumage is green-gray, and their calls are made below hearing, felt as a faint trembling in wicker railings. Moths with mirrored wings bask in the brightness. Beetles carry spores from garden to garden. Pale salamanders live where condensation gathers behind the lowest houses.

There are many creatures, but few moments of pursuit. Predators are small and patient: thread-thin snakes waiting in fern beds, broad-faced spiders under bridge rails, a single kind of owl no larger than a fist. Even these hunt without suddenness. The ecology seems based on nearness rather than chase. Everything is already beside what it eats.

The city has seasons only by report. Above, snow may cover the floodplain, rain may darken it, and summer grasses may seed. Inside, the temperature changes by less than a degree. The bright shaft broadens slightly and narrows again, but the limestone diffuses it so thoroughly that morning resembles evening. Plants flower without spectacle. Fruit ripens one berry at a time. Birth, growth, decay, and repair occur continuously and in such fine increments that the whole appears motionless.

This constancy is treated as a rule of the world: anything changed too quickly will not remain.

A wall raised in one day cracks. A channel cut too sharply fills with mineral crust. A plant transplanted whole dies, while one moved root by root survives. New houses are built over generations, each family adding a palm’s width of masonry before passing the work onward. Disputes are not decided at once; objects representing each side are placed in a public niche and left until moss begins to grow on one of them. The moss’s choice is considered less arbitrary than urgency.

Nothing large enters the hollow. The entrance seems to resist it—not by narrowing, but by becoming difficult to notice. A person searching hurriedly passes over it. A flood divides around the root-covered opening. Smoke laid heavily upon the air thins before it descends. The city admits only what approaches slowly enough to become part of its balance.

At its center is the oldest feature: a round basin of black stone, perhaps carried here before the surrounding limestone was worn smooth. It holds a finger’s depth of water. The water is bright though the stone beneath it is dark. No one remembers seeing the basin empty, overflow, ripple, freeze, or gather dust.

Around it stand the oldest buildings, low and roofless, their walls buried under centuries of moss. Ferns fill their rooms. Silent birds nest in their doorways. Narrow streets converge there from every level of the city, polished by feet that still arrive one careful step at a time.

Nothing in the hollow is truly still. Drops enlarge. Roots divide. Stone becomes powder. Roofs settle into walls, walls into gardens, gardens into cisterns. Yet these movements cancel one another so perfectly that the city retains the same shape: crowded, cold, brilliant, and green, concealed beneath the sparse plain like a thought the land has been keeping for a very long time.
