The town occupies a steep, shallow bowl in the uplands, where water gathers before spilling through a narrow cleft toward the valley below. From a distance it is difficult to distinguish settlement from forest. Roofs appear as darker shelves among the leaves; footpaths vanish beneath woven tunnels; smoke rises in thin blue strands and is immediately combed apart by wet branches. Only the repeated lines of terraces reveal intention: hundreds of low green steps fitted around the slopes, each holding soil, houses, gardens, livestock, and rain.

The surrounding heights are not severe mountains, but old, rounded country lifted well above the warmer plains. Their stone has been exposed for so long that nothing is sharp. Cliffs have become walls of damp, silvered rock. Boulders are sunk to their shoulders in moss. Where bare stone remains, it is furred with lichen and crossed by black threads of seepage. Rain rarely falls with violence here, yet it is seldom entirely absent. It drifts, condenses, collects in leaf tips, and continues falling beneath the canopy long after the clouds have passed.

Water is never far below the surface. Push a heel into a garden bed and the print slowly shines. Every retaining wall weeps. Ditches run beside the lanes, roof gutters empty into tanks carved from old logs, and chains of shallow ponds occupy the center of each terrace. The town’s lowest quarter is half marsh: a confusion of boardwalks, reed beds, duck pens, dye vats, and raised houses standing on short stone piers. Its water is tea-dark, cold, and always moving, though often too slowly to show a current.

Vegetation does not merely surround the town. It forms much of its substance.

The broadest trees were planted generations ago in regular arcs along the contours. Their trunks support bridges, platforms, pigeon lofts, ladders, drying racks, and entire rooms. Houses are framed with coppiced poles bent while green and tied into ribs; the walls are wicker packed with moss, clay, bark fiber, and chopped straw. Roofs are dense mats of turf planted with sedges and low herbs. In the rainy months, roots knit them tight. In the drier weeks, families climb onto the roofs to cut fodder or gather medicinal flowers.

There is little clean distinction between garden, street, and building. Squash vines cross alleys overhead. Gooseberries push through fences. Hazel stools become workshops beneath broad awnings of leaf and hide. Living willow screens divide courtyards and must be pruned several times a year before they close the doorways. Even the public square is less a clearing than a managed thicket: an oval of hard-packed root and gravel beneath pollarded trees, with benches carved into the swollen bases of trunks.

The town is small enough that its outer boundary can be walked in an hour, but within that boundary it is crowded almost beyond comprehension. Paths divide, climb, rejoin, pass under houses, squeeze between cisterns, and emerge on terraces several levels above where they began. No path remains empty for long. Porters carry baskets of wet greens on shoulder poles. Children hurry geese uphill. Woodcutters drag bundles of coppice. Fishers return from the marsh ponds with wicker traps knocking against their knees. At the busiest crossings, people call warnings before stepping through, because foliage hides what lies three paces ahead.

The settlement owns few machines. Water turns some small wooden wheels where the streams descend sharply, powering querns, bellows, and trip hammers used for simple ironwork. Elsewhere everything is done by hand. Timber is split with wedges. Bark is beaten into sheets. Rope is twisted from nettle fiber and rush. Clay is dug from the pond margins and fired in low kilns whose chimneys are threaded among the trees. Metal is valuable and repeatedly repaired: a knife may have a new handle every decade and a new blade twice in a century, yet keep the same name.

Most objects are grown, woven, carved, or bound. Baskets outnumber pots. Doors are light hurdles rather than planks. Bridges are replaced branch by branch as their lower members soften. When a house begins to lean, no one necessarily tears it down. Its roof is cut back, its frame is braced against neighboring trees, and another chamber is added on the uphill side. The town has settled into this method over many generations. It does not resist rot so much as keep pace with it.

Animals are everywhere.

Ducks churn the lower pools and crowd the sluice gates. Small black pigs forage beneath the orchard terraces, each wearing a wooden clapper so they can be found in the undergrowth. Goats occupy the drier ledges and sometimes the roofs. Chickens scratch in workshops. Dogs sleep on bridges, forcing pedestrians to step over them. Pigeons burst from lofts in sudden grey clouds, circle the bowl, and settle again amid a rain of feathers and complaints.

Wild creatures are more numerous still. Frogs pulse from every ditch at dusk. Midges hover above the water, drawing swallows so thickly that the air appears stitched by their flight. Beetles inhabit the walls; snails climb the rain barrels; pale moths cluster beneath eaves. The canopy belongs to finches, thrushes, jays, and quick red squirrels. Water voles burrow beneath the banks. Herons stand in the outer reed beds with the grave stillness of officials.

The townspeople do not imagine that these animals are visitors. They are part of the town’s appetite and labor, as unavoidable as rain. Seed is stored behind clay-plastered mesh. Fruit trees are netted during ripening. Houses keep narrow openings for cats and wider ones for smoke. Every craft has its corresponding scavenger: gulls by the fish tables, wasps at the cider presses, rats beneath the grain platforms, crows at the slaughter yard. The struggle is constant but rarely dramatic. A good building is not one that excludes life; no building could. It is one that persuades life into tolerable channels.

This gives the place its noise.

Water knocks in gutters, hisses through reed screens, and pours from terrace to terrace. Axes strike in the coppice yards. Hand mills grind. Loom weights tap against frames. Traders argue under dripping canopies. Bells tied to animals sound from unseen paths. Ducks quarrel by the hundred. Songbirds compete in the branches, while frogs fill the dark hours with a low, continuous vibration.

After rain the noise increases. Channels clog with leaves and are cleared in haste. Sluice boards are lifted. Children are sent running to warn the lower terraces. The main cascade, ordinarily divided among mills and ponds, becomes a white, blunt force under the central bridge. Water wheels turn too quickly and must be disengaged. Mud slides from garden walls. People emerge with wicker scoops, mattocks, rope, and bundles of brush, working in teams to shore the banks before the soil escapes downhill.

Such disturbances are common enough to have become a civic rhythm. The town is turbulent not because it is fragile, but because it is perpetually being remade. Rain swells the wood. Roots lift paving stones. Vines tighten around railings. Channels migrate by inches. A willow collapses and becomes a bridge; a pond silts up and becomes a garden; a garden saturates and returns to reeds. Each season requires cutting, tying, dredging, splitting, transplanting, and patching.

There are rules for this work, though few are written. No household may block running water. The downhill neighbor has a claim on overflow, but also a claim against filth. Large trees belong partly to the family whose terrace holds the trunk and partly to anyone whose house rests in the branches. Nesting birds may delay roof repairs. Fruit over a public path can be taken by passersby, provided they do not climb. Frogs are never cleared from a cistern until the eggs have hatched, because their presence is thought—correctly—to keep down biting insects.

The oldest part of town stands around a spring near the upper rim. Here the buildings are lower and darker, pressed among huge alder roots and weather-rounded stones. The spring emerges from beneath a slab carved with channels worn almost smooth by centuries of hands and water. It feeds a chain of covered basins: first for drinking, then washing food, then washing bodies, then soaking fibers, and finally watering the marsh gardens. To use them out of order is a serious offense.

Above the spring, beyond the last inhabited terraces, the forest closes quickly. The ground rises through fern, bilberry, moss, and fallen timber toward the windy crest. In that upper wood the town’s clamor fades, but never disappears. Ax blows travel between trunks. Dogs bark below. The wingbeat of pigeons rises through the canopy. One can look down through gaps in the leaves and see almost none of the streets, only smoke, roof meadows, trembling branches, and small flashes of moving water.

Toward evening, cool air settles into the bowl. Mist collects first over the marsh quarter, softening the boardwalks and turning lanterns into pale yellow blurs. Cooking fires scent the wet air with alder smoke, onions, mushrooms, and fish. Livestock is driven inward. Market awnings are lowered so rain will not pool in them overnight. On the roofs, snails emerge among the sedges.

The town does not become quiet. Its daytime clatter merely gives way to another population. Frogs take possession of the ponds. Owls call from the upper trees. Wings rustle in the lofts. Water continues through the dark, slipping beneath houses, filling tanks, turning idle wheels by a fraction, and searching every wall for a lower path.

By morning some small portion of the place will have changed. A stair will have sunk. A branch will have entered a window. A fish pond will have overflowed into a lane, leaving silver minnows among the footprints. Before breakfast, someone will already be out with a basket, a hook, a coil of rope, or a sharpened stake, putting the town back into order—not the old order exactly, but the next workable form.
