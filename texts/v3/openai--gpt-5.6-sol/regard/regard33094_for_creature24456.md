The power does not descend in light.

Light would empty the upper galleries in panic, wake the bats at the wrong hour, expose the pale creatures beneath the stones, and turn every wet surface briefly white. Nor does it arrive as a voice. A voice large enough to speak to the whole city would become weather inside the domes. It would shatter eggs, confuse the frogs, and send the monkeys fleeing along routes that end in broken roofs.

It comes as attention.

For one full turning of wet season and dry, it does nothing that can be distinguished from the place itself.

It follows water from the first bead forming under a leaf to the black cistern where that bead eventually arrives. It attends the sleep of the fish in shrinking pools and the dormant eggs sealed in mud. It learns which leaning walls are empty and which contain generations. It feels the pressure of roots, not only upon stone but upon one another. It knows where the snakes wait, where the monkeys leap, where the bats cling in heat, where the last coolness remains at the end of a rainless month.

It does not mistake suffering for error. The frog taken at the cistern edge is not rescued from the bat. The nestling beneath the ants is not restored to its niche. The old monkey who can no longer cross the widening gap is not made young. To abolish every death would be to feed nothing, crowd every chamber, and end the city by mercy.

The power is benevolent, but it is not sentimental.

It asks no creature what it wants, because the answers would destroy one another.

The fig wants the wall opened.

The wall’s lizards want it warm and bare.

The termites want the buried timber.

The swiftlets want the termites winged and rising.

The fish want the pool never to dry.

The frogs need the fish to vanish from some pools.

The monkeys want fruit.

The fruit trees want monkeys.

The seeds want to be carried and then abandoned in light.

There is no common desire except continuation, and even continuation cannot mean the persistence of every form. The power understands that the being who lives here is not hidden in a chamber. It is not the last builder, sleepless beneath the foundations. It is not a spirit seated above the basin, counting species as possessions.

The being is the exchange.

It is the passage of water through stone and body; the movement of seed through gut; the conversion of guano into fungus, fungus into beetle, beetle into frog, frog into bat, bat into nourishment beneath a fig. It is the memory of routes without a mind to contain them. It is many hungers fitting imperfectly together.

This being cannot be spoken to. It can only be answered.

And so the power gives it neither command nor cure.

It gives it a margin.

Deep beneath the basin, below the foundations and the oldest buried avenues, water moves through compressed rubble and mountain rock. Much of it has long escaped along fractures opened by roots and settling masonry. Some drains too quickly into valleys beyond the city. Some lies trapped beneath collapsed conduits, close enough for trees to sense but too deep for them to reach.

The power touches no pool directly.

Instead, over the course of several nights, while cloud presses low against the upper terraces, grains shift in the darkness below. A crack closes by the width of a root hair. Another opens. Lime loosened centuries ago settles into a narrow throat. Pebbles turn in underground channels. Nothing reverses direction abruptly. No buried mechanism wakes. No gate moves.

Yet the next rain remains longer.

Not everywhere.

The sunny walls still dry. Dust still forms in upper chambers. Bromeliad cups still empty, and some larvae still fail before becoming winged. But in the deepest courts, a little water persists beneath the visible water. Cisterns that once ended in hot mud retain coolness in their cracks. Roots reaching downward find damp stone one finger’s breadth sooner. A chain of hidden refuges forms under the city, discontinuous and small.

The blind fish do not know they have been saved from the next severe dry season. They know only that the black ribbon beneath the fallen tiles does not contract beyond a certain point.

The frogs continue to lose whole generations in shallow pools.

Enough survive.

The power does not make rain abundant, because abundance would favor a different city. Constant water would drown nests, erase the dry chambers, rot the high-rooted trees, spread fish into every cistern, silence some frogs beneath others, and turn old, stable collapses into moving earth. Instead it preserves variation. Wet remains beside dry. Flood still joins what drought separates. The rain keeps its uncertainty, but ceases to contain the possibility of total thirst.

Then the power attends to fire.

Fire has visited the exposed roofs before. Lightning has run through meadow grass and burned until cloud returned. Such fires clear shrubs, open seed, and renew the wind-bent plants. They belong to the high city as rain belongs to the channels.

But the basin now holds centuries of accumulated litter. There are years when the cloud rises too long, when dry leaves fill galleries and dead vines connect tower to tower. One day a fire could descend from the meadows into the canopy, enter the roofless districts, and discover that the entire city has become one fuel bed. It would move through bridges, roots, guano, timber, leaf mold, and hollow trees. It would not renew. It would simplify.

The power does not forbid flame.

It loosens three ancient terraces.

During separate storms, retaining stones settle and release narrow slides of wet soil. No occupied tower falls. No cistern is buried. The scars are modest: pale bands across the green slopes, soon colonized by grass and low flowers. Yet beneath those bands, the continuity of dry litter is broken. Fire may cross in sparks, but not as a single advancing body.

Monkeys avoid the slides at first. Then insects gather in the new growth, and birds follow them. Within years the open strips become feeding places. Within decades trees begin to close over them. By then other walls will have fallen, other breaks will exist. The power has not built barriers. It has reminded the landscape how to interrupt itself.

Its final act concerns what has not yet entered the basin.

Beyond the warm mountains, other beings move with purposes unlike hunger. They measure distance, cut roads, name ownership, inventory stone, feather, timber, medicine, rarity. If they find the city, some will love it. Some will wish to preserve it by clearing roots from walls and animals from chambers. Some will wish to recover its inscriptions. Some will cage its bright birds, drain its cisterns, collect its blind fish, burn its lower avenues, and rebuild one tower to prove what all the towers once were.

Their arrival is not inevitable, but it is possible.

The power considers hiding the city completely.

It could thicken the cloud, turn paths back upon themselves, make compasses tremble, cover every causeway in thorn. But permanent concealment would become another kind of imprisonment. Populations must sometimes pass beyond the basin. Birds must carry seeds outward. New blood must enter. Mountains change. A refuge sealed forever becomes a trap with delayed walls.

So the power places no enchantment at the boundary.

It alters recognition instead.

Those who come seeking treasure see only wet stone, difficult slopes, insects, and distances greater than their provisions. Those who come seeking conquest lose confidence in every map. Those who would strip the place for rarity find its rarest creatures ordinary to their eyes and forget, on leaving, the exact chambers where they saw them.

But those who arrive with patience encounter no such resistance.

A botanist who can pass a flowering season without uprooting the flower may remember the route. A child who stops to move a snail from a bare stair may dream accurately of the cisterns years later. A tired traveler who takes shelter in a gallery and leaves food without setting a snare may glimpse, when the cloud lifts, the terraces continuing into the neighboring valleys.

Even these are not welcomed deeply or often. The city is not made into a lesson for them. It owes revelation to no one. Yet the possibility remains that beings capable of restraint may know it and, by knowing, stand between it and those who are not restrained.

After this, the power withdraws its attention.

It leaves no shrine, guardian, law, or chosen species. Nothing is appointed steward. The monkeys receive no new wisdom. The frogs acquire no gratitude. The city does not become conscious of protection.

For a time, minor anomalies are noticed.

A female bat returns from hunting and finds the lower chamber cooler than it was the previous dry season. A fig root, descending through a cracked bath, divides at a place where there is no visible moisture. A troop of monkeys begins using the flowers on a landslide strip. Fish press themselves into a deep seam beneath the cistern floor and remain there until rain. A hunter from outside the basin reaches a fork in an old causeway and, though the left branch is plainly broader, chooses the right and never understands why.

Then even these events become habit.

Predation continues. Walls settle. Species appear and vanish. One day the last of a particular cistern frog calls into its dome and hears no answer of its own pitch. Elsewhere, in a court newly reached by floodwater, another population begins to diverge. A tower falls after holding for a thousand years. Its collapse kills a tree, opens a shaft of sun, fills one pool, creates three others, and gives the parrots a new pale cliff on which to gather.

The power does not return to prevent it.

Wisdom has not preserved the city from change. Benevolence has not made it harmless.

It has done only what the whole place could not do for itself: reduced the reach of a few final endings. It has placed a little water beneath drought, a little distance within fire, and a little uncertainty between beauty and possession.

Everything else it entrusts to the crowded dark.

There, life resumes the work it never stopped doing. Roots tighten. Wings issue from towers. Frogs fit their voices to stone. Monkeys teach their young where to leap. Rain enters the old channels, divides, disappears, and waits below.

No creature knows that the world has been made kinder.

The proof is merely that, after many seasons, the city remains difficult, hungry, various, and alive.
