The power did not descend.

It did not take a body, because any body large enough to announce it would crush roofs, frighten flocks into wasting moisture, and send a continent-wide alarm through the ground. It did not speak, because speech requires an addressee, and there was no single ear here. The plain listened with whiskers, roots, tarsi, air shafts, folded leaves, sleeping seeds. A voice made for one kind of listener would have been thunder to another.

So the power remained above, below, and nowhere that could be pointed to.

It watched.

For a full turning of generations it did nothing but learn the hour of each shadow. It followed moisture from a bird’s throat into the mouth of a nestling; from the nestling’s breath into porous clay; from clay into a root hair; from root into a flower; from flower into a moth; from the moth’s body, at last, into the pale egg of an owl.

It learned the paths that seemed wasteful and were not. It learned why termites sealed one corridor and opened another, why a burrower abandoned a sound tuber, why the grazers stopped cropping a cushion plant while green tips remained. It learned that some collapses were repairs beginning, that some deaths prevented larger dying, that a predator’s patience was also a form of restraint.

The power grieved, nevertheless.

It grieved for the burrower caught when a tunnel folded.

It grieved for the carrier bird that gave the last wetness in its throat to young not its own and perched afterward with its beak open, too tired to fly.

It grieved for the vent snake starving through the season when every crossing changed.

It grieved for the seed that waited eighty years and opened beneath the one patch of crust where no root could descend.

Being benevolent did not make these things invisible. Being wise did not make them painless.

Many gifts presented themselves.

A river, first.

The power imagined drawing one bright line across the plain: cool banks, wet clay, reeds, shallows, open drinking. It saw the first astonishment of animals touching water deeper than a tongue’s breadth. Then it saw what followed. Mounds softened and failed. Root grafts drowned. Flood-breeding insects multiplied beyond all appetite. Creatures from the river took the surrounding land. Those who controlled the banks flourished; those farther away withered. The city gathered around the gift until most of the plain became empty.

The power let the river remain unmade.

Then it considered rain every season.

It saw leaves broaden, herds grow heavy, queens fill chambers with eggs, groves rise into forests. It also saw thrift become disadvantage. The waxed leaf lost to the open leaf. The careful tuber lost to the thirsty grass. The old alliances dissolved because abundance made them unnecessary—until abundance ended, as all weather eventually must. Then the descendants of the plain, having forgotten how to keep one another alive, died beneath a green sky.

The power withheld that gift as well.

It considered removing the predators.

For a while there would be less terror. Young would emerge without the ripple of silence passing before them. Birds would enter their chambers without looking back. Burrowers would sleep near the warm mouths of tunnels.

Then grazers stripped the cushion plants. Burrowers multiplied through the root halls. Birds emptied the dusk of insects. Thorns failed to replace themselves. Famine came not with teeth but everywhere at once.

The cats remained on their parapets. The snakes remained in the shafts. The owls kept their dead forks.

The power considered teaching the inhabitants that together they were one being.

This temptation lasted longest.

It could have joined the signals in the earth. It could have made every termite feel the thirst of every bird, every shrub feel the footfall of every cat, every burrower know the location of every seed. The continent might awaken and say *I*. Its chimneys would breathe by intention. Its migrations would become thoughts. It might defend every vulnerable chamber, apportion every mouthful, forbid every needless wound.

But the power looked closely at the queen, who was not a cell but herself. It looked at the old bird, the waiting beetle, the cat’s suspended paw. To combine them without consent would not perfect their communion. It would erase the very beings communion was meant to serve.

The plain possessed an order, but it did not owe that order a mind.

The power left them their separateness.

At last, after long attention, it found one mercy small enough not to become conquest.

It changed no creature. It increased no total measure of water. It took nothing from another land. It placed no immortal object in the clay, no shrine, no law, no chosen species.

It taught the rain to fall differently.

Until then, the rare clouds had crossed the plain carrying drops grown huge in the warm upper air. They struck hard, breaking crust, drowning chambers, washing seeds into heaps and filling low tunnels faster than claws could close them. Much of the water fled downward beyond roots or rose again in sudden steam. The rain gave life, but it arrived with the clumsiness of a gift thrown from a great height.

The power touched the dust veils made by the plain’s own breathing.

It made each grain a place around which a drop could form. Not large drops, not hail-heavy beads, but small ones: warm, numerous, slow to join. It altered no season. It summoned no cloud that would not otherwise have come. It merely persuaded water to divide itself before descending.

When the next rain arrived, generations later, the oldest living bird had never felt one.

The first drops still struck sharply enough to wake the seeds.

Termites felt the impacts through their towers and began sealing gates. Burrowers rushed toward upper chambers. Birds flattened themselves against their nesting walls. Leaves opened with the suddenness of hands.

But the expected blows did not follow.

The rain softened.

It became a close silver fall, finer than the dust, thick enough to dim the nearest mound but too gentle to shatter its fresh corners. Water silvered fur instead of beating through it. It gathered along whiskers and thorn points. It darkened clay without making it run. It entered the outer plaster slowly, passed through porous walls, and condensed in chambers prepared for scarcity.

The plain drank at the speed of its own living.

Roots took water before gravity could steal it. Fibers swelled. Tubers filled without splitting. Emergency chambers received their share but did not flood. Seeds woke where they had waited instead of being swept elsewhere. A beetle raised its shell out of habit and found every groove overflowing.

For three nights and two dim days the rain continued.

There were losses. Wisdom did not make the world harmless. One old wall collapsed, its foundations already hollow. A litter in a shallow burrow grew cold. A moth emerged too early and spent its brief life beneath wet clay.

But the great drowning did not come.

Nor did a river remain afterward. No lake shone where none had shone before. There was no permanent place to possess, defend, or poison. The water entered leaves, walls, roots, blood, eggs, fungal chambers and seed coats. By the fourth dawn the surface was merely dark. By evening it was pale again.

The inhabitants did not thank the power.

They did not know anything had been done.

Termites incorporated the fallen wall into three nearby towers. Burrowers widened nursery rooms. The birds laid larger clutches. Cushion plants flowered dusty violet, cream, ocher and red. Cats hunted among them. Beetles ate the fallen petals. Roots entered the bones of those who died. Every gift passed quickly into obligation.

This pleased the power.

But it did one further thing, even smaller and more secret than the first.

It looked into the possible futures of the plain. It saw droughts the inhabitants could endure and droughts that would change them. It saw extinctions caused by appetite, weather, adaptation, chance. These it mourned but did not forbid, for a living world cannot be preserved by preventing it from becoming different.

Farther out, however, it saw accidents too large to answer: a stone from the dark capable of burning the atmosphere; a trembling beneath the continent that could open the hidden moisture to the sky; a wandering alteration of the sun that would sterilize the upper clay before any root or animal could respond.

Against these, and only these, the power set its hand.

Not a wall. A wall would separate the plain from the universe that made it.

A hand: ready to turn the stone by the width of a beetle’s shell while it was still years away; ready to quiet the deepest fracture before it reached the inhabited crust; ready to soften one impossible season into a merely terrible one.

It made no promise that nothing would be lost.

Its promise was only this:

Nothing here would be destroyed by a catastrophe so vast that thrift, cooperation, adaptation, courage, cunning, patience and chance had no meaning before it.

The plain would still have to save itself from all survivable things.

Then the power withdrew its attention enough for freedom, but not so far that it ceased to care.

Below, an old carrier bird returned to its nesting wall. Rainwater had gathered in a flaw above the entrance and soaked slowly through. A clear bead formed on the inner ceiling.

The nestlings reached for it.

The old bird let them drink first, as it always had.

Another bead formed.

This one it swallowed itself.

Outside, the continent breathed through its million chimneys. Shadows moved. Mandibles clicked. A clay cat lowered one paw toward a passing shape. A buried seed broke open along a seam finer than hair.

No command passed across the plain. No revelation joined its minds. No creature understood itself as protected, or chosen, or seen.

Yet the rain, when it came, had learned gentleness.

And high above the warm pale sky, where no wing could rise and no tower could breathe, something wise remained watchful without asking to be worshiped, and something benevolent loved the city enough not to make it into anything else.
