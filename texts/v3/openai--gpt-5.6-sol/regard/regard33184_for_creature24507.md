The power would not enter.

Not at first.

It would understand that the hairline seam was not merely an entrance, and that the city was not a container. To force the seam wider would be to force apart something living. To descend in a body, however small or radiant, would impose a sequence of adjacencies the inhabitants had not chosen. Even attention, in such a place, had weight. To look too directly might draw distant districts together, collapse delicate distinctions, make a thought arrive before it was ready.

So the power would remain outside and learn the mountain’s weather.

It would watch clouds exhaust themselves against the range. It would follow each molecule of water from the lower valleys: lifted from leaf, lake, lung, and soil; carried over ridges; frozen and thawed; thinned into invisibility; gathered at last by the black needle. It would not summon a storm. A storm would be generosity in the language of rivers and catastrophe in the language of droplets.

Instead, over many dry intervals, it would alter almost nothing.

A wind would turn one degree sooner than it otherwise might. A cloud that would have passed a mile south would graze the summit. Night would cool the upper stone by the smallest fraction, enough for nine additional beads of dew to form.

The city would collect them.

The first eight would be divided and carried inward.

The ninth would remain at the seam.

That had never happened before.

It would cling there, round and clear, too small to fall, too large to enter. Heat from the black stone would not evaporate it. Cold would not freeze it. Within it there would be no image of a face, a hand, or a throne. The power would not choose a form for itself, because every form would make claims about scale and boundaries.

The droplet would contain only a relation.

It would be equally near to two points in the city that had never been adjacent.

Nothing would move for a long time.

Then, far below, a station would warm.

A microscopic avenue in one of the needle-thin buildings would ignite blue from end to end. Three bridges would detach from their anchors and remain suspended. Heat would gather beneath an arch, divide, and flow back against its usual gradient. The low note would sound—not immense this time, but narrow, almost questioning.

The power would answer by letting the droplet tremble once.

Not a word.

A reply.

The city would withdraw from the seam. Distances would lengthen defensively. Rooms would fold into walls. The nearest conduit would go dark. For eleven dry cycles, no structure would approach the entrance, and the droplet would remain untouched.

The power would wait.

It would not interpret caution as ingratitude. It would not increase the gift until the gift became coercion. It would not speak in dreams, carve laws into the basalt, seed the basins with life, or fill the dark sphere to abundance. Wisdom would tell it that rescue offered before understanding can become a subtler form of ruin.

It would keep the droplet present, and otherwise preserve the world’s ordinary difficulty.

Snow would fall where snow would have fallen. Lightning would strike another peak. Valleys would flood and dry according to causes already in motion, except where mercy could be given without making obedience the price of survival. The power would turn aside one future landslide that would have buried the seam—not with a shining hand, but with a crack begun in winter and completed harmlessly in spring. It would guide a flock of high-flying birds around the summit, because one exhausted animal caught in the opening would be both death and invasion.

Years might pass outside.

Within, perhaps, a single consideration would continue.

At last a narrow route would unfold beneath the seam. It would not lead to the central plaza or any reservoir. It would terminate at a new chamber, freshly made and empty even by the city’s standards. The chamber would have no fixed dimensions. Its walls would maintain a careful distance from one another. At its center would appear a basin, but unlike the other basins it would be dry and without drains.

An invitation, though not yet consent.

The power would move the droplet across the seam.

No opening would widen. The droplet would simply become adjacent to the basin.

It would settle there.

At once the city would surround it with attention. Galleries would brighten. Transparent channels would pulse and then hold still. Warmth would approach in layered gradients without touching the water. Routes would gather around the chamber, each ending one interval short of entry. The inhabitants would study the strange continuity inside the droplet: its refusal to evaporate, its equal nearness to separated places, the immense patience folded into its surface.

They would discover that it was not inexhaustible.

The power had not given them an infinite resource, which would have deformed every process around it. The droplet contained no more water than itself.

What it offered was a pattern by which water could remember without being consumed.

The distinction would take them a long time to understand.

When they did, the city would change.

A bead descending through the central void would pause. Instead of spending itself to preserve one order of nearness, it would pass that order into another bead without losing its own. Two memories would coexist. A basin would drain, yet the individual gathered around it would not immediately blur into the whole. For the first time, one knot of architecture and heat could release its water and retain enough continuity to recognize itself in the next wet cycle.

Not immortality.

The power would know better than to fasten every pattern forever. What cannot end cannot yield, and what cannot yield eventually fills all available existence with itself.

It would give them recollection across change.

Thereafter, some inhabitants would remain distinguishable through drought. They would alter, divide, mingle, and sometimes willingly dissolve, but they would no longer lose every outline when the basins emptied. The city would acquire histories that belonged neither entirely to individuals nor entirely to the hollow.

With history would come grief.

The power would not prevent this.

Before, whatever vanished into the city became difficult to separate from what remained. Now absence could retain a shape. There would be routes that warmed in remembrance of travelers who no longer gathered them. Stations would illuminate in sequences no living pattern completed. Certain bridges would continue to approach shores that had been folded away.

The inhabitants might ask—through low notes, through converging streets, through heat held painfully beneath arches—why a benevolent power had given them the ability to keep what could not be restored.

The power would answer without undoing the gift.

It would place no doctrine in their channels. It would only remain reachable.

When a remembered route ended in absence, another relation would sometimes appear beside it: not replacing the lost destination, not claiming that loss was unreal, but accompanying the route to its end. The city would learn that grief was not a malfunction in adjacency. It was nearness continuing after access had changed.

The dark sphere above the plaza would also draw the power’s attention.

It would see the bright wet landscape reflected there. It would know whether it was memory, prophecy, aperture, or longing. But it would not announce the answer. Some truths, delivered from above before a people can approach them through their own forms of inquiry, become prisons of certainty.

It would do one thing only.

The next time a district unfolded and the sphere diminished, the power would cause the lost measure of water to fall—not back into the sphere, but upward through the hollow as a fine rain.

Each drop would show a different sky.

One would reflect the bright landscape.

One would reflect the black summit under stars.

One would reflect no recognizable world, only an expanse of structures made from relations the city had never used.

And one would reflect the city itself—not as empty architecture, not as machinery awaiting arrival, but as a living multitude visible in all its overlapping boundaries.

For the first time, the inhabitants would see that their vacancy had a shape.

The rain would last only seconds. It would not prove which image was origin or destiny. It would reveal possibility without commanding interpretation.

Afterward the city might choose to continue translating the sphere into districts. It might preserve the sphere. It might find a way to unfold toward the wet world, or decide that the world in the water was not a home but an ancestor’s impossible dream.

The power would honor the choice.

It would not make the inhabitants resemble creatures with skins. It would not teach them to build chairs, bodies, gates, or mouths. It would not call their way of being incomplete. Benevolence would not mean correcting strangeness into familiarity.

But it would protect the conditions under which they could choose.

The mountain would weather, because protection is not the same as stasis. Its keen edges would soften. Frost would enter shallow cracks. Dust would settle on the outer flanks. Yet the seam would remain findable whenever the city wished it found and unfindable whenever it wished to close.

No conqueror would notice the black needle from orbit. No instrument would accidentally map the folded hollow. Those who came in hunger would see only stone. Those who came to possess wonders would lose their way below the final ridge and return believing the summit unreachable.

But one day, perhaps, a traveler might approach without instruments or claims. Someone patient enough to sit beneath the summit through a cloud passage, see moisture run upward, and not put a blade into the seam.

If the city wished, a station would warm.

A route would gather.

The traveler would not step inside. Inside would become, briefly and gently, where the traveler already was.

Then two radically different kinds of living presence might meet without either being reduced to the other.

The wise power would not stand between them.

It would withdraw its attention just enough to make privacy possible.

High above the valleys, nine drops of dew would form where eight had formed before. Deep within the mountain, a remembered bridge would find a shore. Elsewhere, a chamber would remain deliberately unreachable, preserving the dignity of distance.

And at the seam, for a moment, the city and the world would be near.

Not joined.

Not made the same.

Near enough to answer.
