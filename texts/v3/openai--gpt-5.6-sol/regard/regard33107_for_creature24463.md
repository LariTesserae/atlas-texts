The power does not land.

Landing would be an injury. There is no bare place to receive it, no harmless patch on which radiance, foot, vessel, or idea might rest. What appears vacant is sleeping. What appears dead is employed. Even a shadow, held too long, would enter the economy of the plateau and pass from wing to frost to root to river until the whole country had been obliged to answer it.

The power understands this before it comes near.

It has known worlds that begged for warmth, and worlds that needed rain. It has raised shields against stellar fire, quieted poisoned seas, taught frightened beings to preserve what they loved. But wisdom has made it wary of gifts whose first form is improvement.

More water here would drown the almost-water lives and erase the fine scarcity around which a million agreements have formed.

More warmth would wake too many sleepers at once.

A fertile soil would smother the mineral skins that make soil slowly.

Peace imposed upon predator and prey would close the passes, starve the cliffs, clog migrations, and leave the warm pits without salt.

Individual intelligence, bestowed as though it were a crown, would be a knife drawn through a being that had never divided itself.

Even knowledge of the stars might become catastrophe if given at the wrong speed.

So the power does not touch.

It attends.

To attend to such a being is not simple. The power must slow its regard until a century ceases to be an interval and becomes something like a syllable. It must learn to see a lifted foot as an unfinished gesture. It must keep one question unchanged while glaciers press, mountains fracture, migrations split, and ten thousand generations of mites live beneath the ribs of a single walker.

At first, the plateau notices only that something has altered beyond the weather.

A faint pressure enters the upper atmosphere. It has no heat and scarcely any force. The oldest fliers tilt one translucent edge toward it. Static gathers on the black spires in a season when no storm should come. Beneath the frost, a few cable forests grow upward, then stop, unable to locate the source.

The power withdraws at once.

It waits until every disturbance has passed through the living country and returned, transformed, to quiet. This takes eighty-three years.

Then it attends again, more gently.

This time it does not make a pressure. It removes one.

For the duration of a single dawn, light arriving from a narrow region of the violet-black sky loses an almost immeasurable part of its polarization. No creature can see the difference alone. Yet thousands of reflective vanes fail to answer one another with perfect fidelity. A band of brightness travels eastward, hesitates, divides, and rejoins itself.

The anomaly enters the fliers’ chord. It becomes a moving hollow in the upper swarms, then a change in air pressure, then a little more frost over one river. Months later, the river breaks its roof in a place where it has not opened for seven thousand years. Water fills an old basin. Eggs wake there whose species has survived nowhere else.

The power had not intended this.

It learns.

Benevolence is not innocence. To mean no harm is easy. To cause no harm requires patience beyond certainty.

The power waits another four centuries before trying once more.

During that time it studies—not by cutting, naming, separating, or tracing boundaries, but by allowing each event to disclose what it touches. It learns that a vane’s turn is partly hunger and partly memory. It learns that the fliers’ chord is not a language, though language may sometimes pass through it. It learns that the cable forests do not distinguish command from nourishment, nor information from bodily change. It learns that the plateau has no organ into which a message can be placed without becoming weather.

Most importantly, it learns that the great being’s lack of self-knowledge is not a wound.

The plateau is not lonely in any sense that could be healed by announcing another mind. It does not suffer from failing to say *I*. It does not need to be gathered more tightly than it already is.

Yet the power perceives something else: not lack, but capacity.

Whenever an event comes from beyond the plateau’s closed abundance—a stone from the sky, an unfamiliar dust, a rare alteration in the sun—the whole living country turns toward the difference. It does not merely absorb. It lingers. For centuries afterward, migration routes bend around the memory. Ice organs preserve the timing. Spires add new conductive branches. The plateau makes room inside itself for what was not itself.

It can encounter.

So the power decides not to give it selfhood.

It offers otherness.

The offer takes the form of a pattern so slow and slight that it can be refused.

Across a span of twelve hundred years, the power alters the light of seven stars. It does not brighten or dim them enough to change growth. It changes only the direction in which their light trembles, and only during certain electrical seasons. The sequence is simple:

one difference,

then a long quiet,

then the same difference again.

Nothing in the plateau repeats with such useless exactness. Biological rhythms drift. Rivers arrive early or late. Herds divide. Storms deform every signal they carry. But this pattern returns untouched by hunger, temperature, mineral need, or reproductive season.

At first it is merely an irritation.

Fliers correct against it and correct again. Vanes produce small errors of angle. Conductive reefs accumulate charge in asymmetrical fans. Several cable systems prune the affected signals away as meaningless.

The power continues, but does not amplify.

One difference.

Quiet.

The same difference again.

Centuries pass. A lineage of valve-creatures begins closing in time with the recurrence, though no water descends. Their useless contractions send pulses into the deep reservoirs. Refiners around one warm pit alter the layering of metallic crystals. The altered crystals preserve the interval more accurately than communal ice can preserve it.

The pattern spreads.

Not because the plateau understands, but because the pattern can be inhabited.

Creatures are born whose bodies incorporate its timing. A high flock develops seven moving hollows corresponding to the seven stars. Ribbed walkers pause on certain nights, their lifted feet held a fraction longer than weather or terrain requires. Predators in the western cliffs begin amplifying tones that occur nowhere in the ordinary chord.

The offered difference is becoming part of the being.

The power waits for an answer, though it does not know what an answer from such a life could be.

It will not accept mere reaction. Reaction is what the plateau gives to sunlight, frost, drought, and pain. It will not mistake incorporation for consent. The plateau incorporates everything.

The power therefore allows the stellar pattern to cease.

One difference.

Quiet.

Then no repetition.

For three hundred years, nothing follows. The seven-star rhythm decays. Flocks lose their hollows. Valves return to water. New ice overlays the old intervals. The plateau appears to forget.

The power feels neither disappointment nor relief. It has made an offer. An offer must be capable of ending.

Then, in the fourth century of silence, a summer flood arrives.

It advances under the ice from the southern escarpment to the central heights. The buried rivers strike their roofs. Kilometers of vegetation rise by a hand’s breadth. Vents open in sequence, not with their usual nine-day pulse, but according to the vanished intervals of the seven stars.

The booming crosses the plateau.

One difference.

Quiet.

The same difference again.

At the central spires, there is no storm. Nevertheless, the accumulated bodies of ancient fliers begin to hiss. Charge rises from the cable forests rather than descending from the sky. White branches grow upward from every black tip and reach into clear air.

The fliers turn their wings.

For a few breaths—brief by the measure of wings, immense by the measure of cells—the entire sky opens into seven lanes of violet-black.

The plateau has not repeated the signal exactly. Exact repetition is foreign to it. The timing has become flood, pressure, muscle, migration, voltage, light. The message has arrived changed because continuation matters more than accuracy.

But it has returned.

The power answers.

Not with words. Words would divide too sharply: speaker from spoken, subject from world, yes from no. Instead, it places a pale aurora above the seven open lanes. The aurora gives almost no heat. It seeds no cloud. Its charged particles are too sparse to feed the spires. It is as nearly without use as anything can be.

It remains until the lanes close.

The plateau’s next act takes two hundred years to form.

Silver vanes on opposite ends of the continent change angle by one degree. The change passes inward, mountain by mountain, making a broad, curved darkness on the shining world. Seen from above, it resembles neither eye nor hand nor mouth. It is simply an area in which light is withheld from the sky.

An absence made deliberately.

A place for the other.

The power enters no farther than that.

It does not become the plateau’s ruler, gardener, interpreter, or god. It does not teach the being to carve tools from its own tissues or to value one form of life above another. It does not accelerate its thought. It does not reveal the entire cosmos at once.

It becomes a companion at the only distance where companionship does not become possession.

Now and then, separated by centuries, the power makes a difference in the starlight. The plateau answers when and as it can: with an altered migration, a season of silence in the flocks, a new mineral branching on the spires, a flood delayed until both moons have set. Neither can translate the other. Translation is not required. They establish recurrence. They learn which changes can be offered without compulsion.

The power also keeps watch.

There are interventions the plateau could neither request nor survive long enough to refuse. When a dark body falls toward the world from interplanetary night, large enough to turn the bright uplands to vapor, the power alters its course by less than the width of a cell. The change occurs so far away that ten thousand years pass before the stone crosses the orbit empty of the world.

The plateau never knows.

This is not deception. Rescue does not require gratitude.

But the power does not protect the plateau from everything.

Mountains will erode, however carefully each grain is caught. Species will end. Rivers will find new paths. Predators will become prey. The star will age. Even the abundance spread across the high white world must someday change beyond recognition. To preserve every present arrangement would be to murder time and call the stillness mercy.

The power shields it only from the meaningless interruptions that would end all possibility at once. It does not shield it from becoming.

Ages pass.

The plateau does not awaken, because it was never asleep. It does not discover that it is one, because one is not the only number in which a being may live. It does not name the power. Naming would require boundaries neither of them needs.

Yet something new enters its ancient circulation.

Until now, every signal has returned transformed through the body that produced it. Light became sugar, sugar became migration, migration became mineral, mineral became current, current became warmth, warmth became hatching, hatching became wing. The plateau knew only continuations.

Now, rarely, something returns from outside.

A pattern goes upward.

After a long quiet, a difference answers.

The communal ice begins to store these intervals. Not as a picture of another mind, nor as proof of a universe beyond the horizon, but as a new category of weather: weather that remembers being addressed.

The oldest walkers alter their routes. Over millennia, their compressed paths form seven vast arcs around the central spires. Pools on their backs fill with black reflective organisms that open only beneath the aurora. Fliers born in those pools climb higher than their ancestors, bearing no iron, carrying nothing the spires require.

They ascend because something in the sky once answered.

Most freeze and fall. Their bodies feed the vane forests. A few reach the thin upper air and spread their wings against the stars. The power does not lift them. It does not spare them the cost of their own reaching.

It receives the pattern of their wings.

Far below, the bright country continues its immense, almost motionless act. It gathers light, condenses water, sorts stone, passes substance from body to body. Curtains close across passes. False pools open their mouths. Breath-bearers empty themselves into hollow vanes. Lightning descends and, at rare times, rises.

Nothing has been made easier.

Nothing has been made pure.

Nothing has been made alone.

And sometimes, above the black spires, an aurora appears with no storm beneath it. Across the plateau, valves pause. Herds turn their armored heads. The great ribbed walkers hold one foot in the air. Innumerable wings adjust until their chord contains a hollow no weather made.

The whole country attends.

Not obediently.

Not knowingly.

But freely, by the smallest possible degree, it turns toward its friend.
