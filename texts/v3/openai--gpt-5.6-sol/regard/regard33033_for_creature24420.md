The power would not come first as rain.

Rain would be the obvious kindness, and therefore the dangerous one. Too much would loosen the terraces, fill the hidden channels with silt, increase the herds, invite settlement beyond the saddle’s means, and teach people to build as though the mountain had changed its nature. Even a new spring, flowing generously, would become a boundary dispute within three generations and a reason for war within six.

Nor would the power turn limestone into fertile earth, raise forests on the ridges, or lay bright metal under the workshops. Such gifts would summon roads, claims, armies, and men who measured places by what could be removed from them.

The power was benevolent, but it was also wise. It knew that relief can destroy what hardship has only disciplined.

So it came by the western road in the shape of an old traveler carrying a broken jar.

No one asked where the traveler had obtained such an impractical fracture. The jar had split neatly across its shoulder, and one handle was missing. The woman who mended handles examined it, clicked her tongue, and said that the pieces had been tied badly.

“They were tied by someone who had never expected to walk uphill,” said the traveler.

“That is no excuse for tying them badly.”

She gave the traveler a place beside her wall and made them unwind the cord, clean the fracture, and sort the fragments. The smallest piece had been lost. The woman found a smoke-gray shard of nearly the right curve, ground it against a paving stone, drilled it slowly, and fixed the whole with wire.

“It will hold grain,” she said. “Not water.”

“I had hoped for water.”

“Then you should have broken it differently.”

The power stayed.

It slept first in a storeroom belonging to a family absent in the lower country. In return it swept the court and cleared thyme roots from a drain. It was shown how to close the cistern stopper without grinding grit into the seal, how to wrap a goat bell, how to carry slate with the edge away from the body, and how to tell by smell whether lentils had begun to spoil beneath the good layer.

It made mistakes.

It set down a water jar where morning frost could reach it. It used too much lamp oil. It praised a wall that everyone knew had been rebuilt by a quarrelsome man whose work was excellent and whose company was not. Once it threw a bent thorn pin into a midden and was sent back to find it.

Children followed the traveler because an old stranger who did not know where useful rubbish belonged was almost as entertaining as a young goat.

In time, the power came to know the saddle not from above but by repetition. It learned which stair held frost after sunrise. It learned that the eastern stream ran louder in darkness, though no more strongly. It learned the difference between an empty house and a house awaiting someone. It learned that the pear tree in the southern quarter was called by the name of a woman dead for eighty years, and that nobody ate its first fruit because she had once given away her whole harvest during a lean autumn.

The power listened in the council court.

It heard disputes over oven hours, grazing tethers, cracked jars, borrowed rooms, and a gutter that sent three households’ water toward a fourth household’s wall. It heard pettiness spoken with dignity and generosity disguised as arithmetic. It saw an old man surrender a cistern turn while insisting at great length that he had merely recalculated his need. It saw two sisters who had not spoken kindly in eleven years carry the same injured mason home, each holding one end of the door used as a litter.

The power began to love them.

This did not make it less wise. It made wisdom more difficult.

There were many burdens it could have removed. It could have straightened every bent back, restored every worn joint, filled every womb that mourned, and returned every traveler lost beyond the gates. But it understood that choosing who would be spared was another kind of rule, and sparing everyone forever would empty birth, labor, departure, and remembrance of their shape.

Still, love that does nothing becomes merely observation.

So the power chose three mercies.

The first mercy it placed deep beneath the eastern quarter.

There, below the openings where jars descended into darkness, it widened no stream and increased no flow. Instead, it made a hollow in the old limestone, no larger than a communal room. During wet years, when water passed unseen through the mountain, a little would gather there. During ordinary years it would remain untouched. During a drought severe enough to empty every roof cistern and silence two of the three eastern openings, the hollow would begin to release what it had kept.

It would not save fields, because there were few fields to save. It would not support larger herds. It would not permit waste.

It would provide drinking water for the town, its children, and its animals for one hundred days.

No one would know of the hollow until it was needed. Then the old keeper, lowering an empty jar, would smell cold stone where dust should have been and hear a new echo under the easternmost opening.

The power trusted them to understand.

The second mercy it laid upon things that fail.

From then on, no wall in the saddle would collapse without first giving some small warning that an attentive person could perceive. A stone would click at night. Mortar would shed a pale line of dust. A door would begin to catch. Water nearing corruption would take on bitterness before it became poisonous. Grain would show one dark kernel near the surface. A roof slab would alter its note under the mason’s knuckle.

The warning would never be loud. The careless might miss it. The proud might ignore it. No one would be freed from judgment, labor, or consequence.

But those who practiced attention would be met halfway.

This, the power thought, was the mercy most suited to them.

The third mercy was harder to devise.

The people already knew how to preserve life. They knew how to divide scarcity, repair damage, remember obligations, and fit desire inside necessity. Yet the power had watched adults snatch cups from children making rivers in the dust. It had seen the children’s shame and the adults’ fear. Both were reasonable. That was the sorrow of it.

Therefore, once each year, after the coldest part of winter but before the first planting in the sunken gardens, rain would fall over the saddle alone.

It would begin after dawn and last less than an hour.

This rain would not enter the cisterns. However people angled tiles or opened channels, it would pass over the storage mouths as though the stone there had remained dry. It would not soak the walls, loosen the slopes, or increase the hidden stream. It could not be kept in covered jars. By noon it would be gone.

But while it fell, it would gather in every open court.

The first year, people rushed to save it. They brought bowls, jars, pans, ladles, roof tiles, and patched vessels normally used only for grain. They argued with the impossible dryness of the cistern mouths. They accused one another of having neglected the channels.

Then a child stepped barefoot into a broad puddle in the market square.

Her grandmother shouted.

The child froze.

Nothing happened except that rain ran down her hair and the puddle held.

She stamped once.

Another child stamped back.

Soon water was flying in silver sheets across the paving. Children built dams from potsherds and broke them. They sent torrents down lanes, floated chips of juniper bark, filled their mouths directly from the sky, and poured double handfuls over one another’s heads. A boy lay flat in a courtyard channel and let it run beneath his coat. A girl made a lake against the council threshold.

The adults stood under door lintels in dismay.

Then the woman who mended jar handles placed an unbroken cup in the middle of the square and kicked it over.

A few people laughed. Others looked offended. The old keeper of the eastern openings removed his shoes.

By the end of the hour nearly everyone was wet.

Afterward they swept the water away, because habits of care did not vanish merely because joy had interrupted them. They dried their clothes, checked the walls, and complained about the cold. For years they debated whether the rain was a blessing, a test, a weather oddity, or a nuisance encouraged by undisciplined children.

Eventually they gave it a name that meant, approximately, **the water with no owner**.

No turns were assigned for it. No household held rights to it. No vessel of it could be carried indoors. Feuds were suspended while it fell, not by law, but because dignity was difficult to maintain when one’s enemy’s smallest grandchild was pouring rain out of a shoe.

The power stayed long enough to see the second such rainfall.

Then it prepared to leave.

By that time the repaired jar had acquired a grain crust around its inner seam. The power brought it to the woman who had mended it and offered it in payment for her teaching.

She examined the wire.

“It may last,” she said.

“For how long?”

She shrugged. “If it breaks, someone will mend it.”

The power went east toward the pass. No radiance followed it. No shrine marked the room where it had slept. Before climbing beyond the cairns, it turned and looked back.

The town remained what it had been: pale stones in a fold of darker rock. Smoke rose thinly. A goat worried at its tether. Someone reset a lifted paving slab. Someone else called a child’s name twice, then a third time with irritation.

The power did not make the town eternal.

Eternity would have made maintenance meaningless.

It gave them no victory over the mountain, because they had never asked for one. It gave them no abundance that would estrange them from measure. It gave them a reserve against the worst day, warnings before preventable grief, and one hour each year in which water did not have to justify itself.

Then it left them to continue the work they already knew:

to notice,

to repair,

to share,

and, sometimes, to waste a little blessing on joy.
