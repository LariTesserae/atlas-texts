The power does not descend in fire.

Fire would sterilize the shallow pores. A trumpet would mean nothing to a life without hearing. Rain called down in generosity would scour the channels, drown the surface cells, uproot the herbs, and carry the patient colony into depths where light could not follow.

Nor does the power awaken the stone-dweller into swiftness.

It could gather the scattered threads into a single mind. It could give the colony memory as animals know memory, a voice, desire, an image of itself. But the power is wise enough to understand that consciousness is not always a crown. In such a place it might be an injury: a thirst for movement in a body that cannot move, a hunger for company where none could live, the counting of years that had once passed without burden.

So the power first does nothing.

It attends.

Its attention rests on the basin so gently that no water trembles. It follows the seep backward through the limestone, grain by grain, to the buried place where condensation and ancient rain enter the rock. It learns every living filament, including those sealed for centuries in dry pores. It perceives the colony not as a vague stain but as innumerable exchanges: mineral passed inward, water passed sideways, a surface cell dimming so that a deeper one may persist.

To the power, this is neither small nor unfinished.

It is a complete manner of being.

For a long while—long enough for one wall stone to settle the width of a fingernail—the power keeps company with it. The colony does not know it is accompanied. Nothing in its nature has prepared it to distinguish divine regard from warmth retained after sunset.

Yet slowly, at the dark northern seam, a change begins.

Not a miracle against the laws of the basin. A correction within them.

Far beyond the shelves, where the buried rock has begun to shift, one fissure would in time have closed. The seep would have weakened, then failed. This would not have been cruelty. Places end. Lives end. But the closing is not necessary, only accidental: the result of pressure traveling through stone from a distant collapse.

The power lays no hand upon the basin. Instead, deep underground, it permits a different grain to break.

The pressure turns aside.

The seam remains open.

Water will still be scarce. Dry years will still seal the colony beneath its transparent skins. Some threads will perish. Seeds will fall on dust and fail. The power does not abolish the nature of the place in order to save it. It grants no abundance that would invite other lives to consume what has endured by spareness.

It preserves only the possibility of continuance.

Then it looks outward.

There are roads that may one day be built, surveys that may mark the limestone for extraction, wells that may lower the hidden water, collectors who might prize the strange green stain and carry pieces away. The power does not strike these people blind or bury them beneath falling shelves. Benevolence extends beyond what it protects.

Instead, small hesitations enter the world.

A proposed road is moved east because the ground there proves cheaper to grade. A quarry sample from near the basin is mislabeled and reveals nothing of commercial interest. A surveyor pauses on the western rim, sees only dim stone and a few ruined walls, and decides to eat lunch elsewhere. Years later, someone with the authority to draw a boundary includes the basin within a larger protected tract—not because they understand what lives there, but because an old map suggests that the ruins may matter.

The protection is imperfect, as all protection within the world must be. But it is enough.

Last, the power considers whether to leave a gift.

Not more water.

Not richer soil.

Not animals, whose arrival would turn stillness into appetite.

Not immortality, which can become another kind of ruin.

It leaves a difference so slight that only the stone-dweller can receive it.

Once in a great interval, when rain is heavy enough to join the thumb-deep pools, the colony releases its dust-fine cells into the overflow. Before, nearly all traveled only a few handspans and vanished under gravel. Now one narrow pore beneath the last channel remains wet a little longer than it should. The power has altered no climate and created no stream. It has merely smoothed a mineral edge finer than a hair.

One cell passes.

It enters the gravel, survives the dark descent, and reaches a second seam below the southern shelf.

There it rests.

Decades later, water wakes it.

The new growth will not replace the old or conquer fresh ground. It will not transform the basin. It is only another continuity, a hidden answer to catastrophe: if one seam closes, life may remain in the other. In centuries, perhaps, the two colonies will touch through buried pores and exchange what little can be exchanged.

This is the gift: not expansion, but resilience.

The power does one thing more, though it is difficult to say whether this is done for the colony or for itself.

It makes a record.

Not in a book, which can burn; not in a monument, which would draw visitors; not in the minds of people, where reverence so easily becomes possession. It receives the full pattern of the life into its own inexhaustible knowing: every branching discoloration, every dormant cell, every slow mineral rim, every exchange between root and fissure.

The colony is not made aware of this. It receives no revelation. Yet from then onward, its hidden existence is no longer wholly unregarded.

The ages proceed.

Cloud veils the sky. Condensation forms. The seep gathers in its shallow pockets. Walls lower into terraces. Herbs thicken and fail and thicken again. The green in the deepest pool fades during drought, then returns.

Anyone looking into the basin would say that nothing had happened.

They would be nearly right.

The wise power has not improved the place. It has not filled its emptiness, accelerated its life, or translated its silence into a form more easily loved.

It has understood the basin’s simple rules and chosen to work within them.

It has kept the seam open.

It has turned danger aside without punishment.

It has given the slow life one additional way to endure.

And then, because true guardianship does not require that the guarded know the guardian’s name, it withdraws all sign of itself and leaves the warm limestone exactly as it appears:

quiet,

spare,

and complete.
