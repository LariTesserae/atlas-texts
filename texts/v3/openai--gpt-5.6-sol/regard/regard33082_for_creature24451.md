The power did not raise the mountains.

To do so would have emptied the kelp forests onto bare slopes, stranded whales in valleys, broken the cold currents, and turned the holds into tombs inside dry stone. Nor did it still the sea. A motionless sea would have starved the coral walls, the mussel beds, the herring, and everything that fed upon them.

Benevolence without attention might have destroyed the place in an afternoon.

So the power came without light or proclamation and spent a full year listening.

It listened from inside the basalt, where the blows of storms arrived as slow pressure. It listened in the cables and turbine housings. It heard the scrape of basket stars over coral, the minute chewing of limpets, the electrical murmurs of sharks, and the calls of whales passing from basin to basin. It listened to maintenance arguments, lessons recited in classrooms, lovers speaking softly after watch, and children lying awake while boulders struck the outer works.

It learned that most disasters began small.

A hairline crack. A seal not seated cleanly. A wrong figure copied onto a tide board. A pilot tired enough to mistake one beacon for another. A cable shifting half a meter in sediment. A child hiding fear because everyone else appeared brave. A whale entering a channel while the turbines were turning.

The power understood that these people did not need to be made invulnerable. Invulnerability would make them careless, then cruel. They did not need dominion over the sea. They needed a margin.

The first gift was given to the stone.

From then on, nothing important failed without speaking first.

Metal under strain sang faintly through its mountings. Concrete about to fracture gave a series of soft knocks, distinct from wave impact. A pressure door with a damaged seal breathed cold mist around its rim long before water passed it. Cables nearing rupture made the rock around their anchors tremble in a slow, recognizable rhythm.

These warnings could not be heard by everyone. They required patience, practice, and contact: a palm against a bulkhead, an ear to a pipe, bare feet on a gallery floor. The old maintenance chiefs heard them first. They thought at first that age had sharpened them strangely. Then younger workers learned, and children quickest of all.

No machine became immortal. Bearings still wore down. Salt still entered scratches. Storms still took platforms. But henceforth the holds were rarely surprised by the failure of something they had tended faithfully.

The second gift was given to moving water.

The power did not weaken the currents. It placed within them brief refuges: narrow folds of relative calm behind ridges, beneath overhangs, and beside the turbine pylons. Some had existed before, but now they held with unusual constancy. A diver torn from a line might find one. A seal pursued past exhaustion might enter another. Herring schools could pass through the generating channels along routes the rotors could not reach.

The islanders charted these places and called them mercies.

They did not build in them. That became law before anyone proposed it. A mercy was for whoever needed it while in motion, human or otherwise. Boats could shelter there only long enough to repair a steering fault or recover a person from the water. Fishing was forbidden within them. So was anchoring.

The turbines changed as well. Their instruments began detecting more than engineers had designed them to detect. A whale’s approach appeared on control boards as a low blue pulse. Dense shoals registered as silver interference. Rotors slowed before living bodies entered the passes and resumed only after they had gone.

Technicians dismantled the sensors and found nothing new in them.

They reassembled them carefully and did not ask too loudly.

The third gift was given to breath.

In every hold, there came to be one interior chamber where the air remained good after all other systems had failed. It was never large. In the smallest holds it was an old supply closet; in the largest, a disused schoolroom or cable gallery. No pipe diagram accounted for it. Carbon dioxide rose there more slowly than it should. Heat lasted. A lamp left inside burned for days.

The people marked these chambers with a plain white circle. They stored no valuables in them and assigned them no ordinary purpose. During drills, the youngest children and the injured were taken there first.

Years later, when a submarine landslide severed three cables and crushed the ventilation trunks of South Fault Hold, eighty-one people waited in its white-circle room for nearly two days. Rescue crews found them cold, thirsty, and alive.

Afterward, the engineers tried to enlarge the chamber.

Its strange property vanished until they restored the walls to their former dimensions.

The lesson was accepted.

A mercy could be maintained. It could not be exploited.

Only then did the power speak to the people directly.

It chose no council chamber, shrine, or command station. It spoke through the hydrophones during the quiet turn between tides. Every hold heard it, though each heard a different voice: a dead parent, an old teacher, a partner asleep in the next room, a child not yet born. On the surface islands it came through radio sets switched off for maintenance. Divers heard it through the bones of their helmets.

It asked one question.

**What is hardest to keep alive?**

The holds argued for nine days.

Some said the power systems. Some said the kelp. Some said children. Some said memory. Some said trust between settlements. The turbine crews said spare parts. The clinic at North Stair said uncontaminated antibiotics. The bird wardens said nesting ground. An elderly cook said soil. A twelve-year-old, assuming the question was private, answered, “People who no longer want to be here.”

The power heard every answer.

It did not choose between them.

In the deepest occupied mountain, beyond the warm machine galleries, an old sealed fault opened without admitting seawater. Behind it was a long cavern lined with mineral clay and filled with air. Fresh water condensed continuously on its walls. The floor was deep enough for roots.

The power did not create an impossible paradise there. Nothing grew without labor. Blight remained possible. Water had to be carried, lamps maintained, nutrients measured, and insects watched carefully. But the soil renewed itself. It could be divided without becoming exhausted, provided some portion was always returned.

The islanders planted potatoes first, from caution.

Then beans, onions, cabbages, medicinal herbs, and dwarf fruit trees brought aboard supply vessels. They established seed stores for the surface and submarine settlements alike. Children pollinated early blossoms with brushes until a small colony of bumblebees could be introduced safely behind double screens.

The first apple harvest amounted to nineteen fruit.

They were sliced so thin that every person in the hold received a piece. No one pretended the portions were sufficient. No one forgot their taste.

Near the garden the people built a room without machinery. Its walls were layered with woven fiber, cork, and felt. Pipes curved around it rather than passing through. The door closed softly.

It was the quietest human-made place in the range.

Those overwhelmed by alarms, confinement, grief, or the endless duty of being reliable could go there without explanation. There were no clocks inside. No one was asked to improve while using it. A person might sit for ten minutes or sleep through a tide.

The islanders made such rooms wherever they could, though none was as silent as the first. They appointed keepers for them, not guards. The keepers were taught to listen without turning every sorrow into a repair.

This, more than the unfailing chamber or the speaking stone, changed the holds.

The people had always known how to rescue a body. They began to learn how to notice a person disappearing before the body was endangered.

The wise power did one further thing, though the islanders did not discover it for generations.

Far below the settlements, where the mountain descended through metallic dusk, it joined certain cracks in the basalt into a structure too broad to be recognized from any single survey. The structure held no treasure and generated no force. It was simply a record.

Every whale call passing through the range impressed itself faintly there. So did the songs of the holds, the hammering of construction, the pulse of turbines, the cries of lost ships, and the ordinary voices of people speaking near stone. Nothing was preserved perfectly. The mountain kept pattern rather than words.

Centuries later, someone placing a sensitive instrument against a deep cliff would hear layers within layers: extinct engines beneath living whales, old work songs beneath children counting tide intervals, storms that had names beneath storms no one remembered.

The record was not meant to prevent forgetting.

It was meant to ensure that forgetting would never be complete.

Deaths continued. The power did not call back divers already taken into the passes or keep every vessel from foundering. It did not forbid grief, because grief was not a defect in the holds. It was evidence of attachment, and attachment was among the things the power had come to protect.

But fewer people died for want of one more minute.

Fewer whales entered turning blades. Fewer cracks remained silent until they opened. Fewer frightened children grew into adults convinced that fear must be hidden. Gardens spread from hold to hold. The white-circle rooms waited unused for years at a time, which everyone understood to be their proper use.

The power itself was never worshiped in an organized way. The islanders distrusted any being that might become an excuse to neglect maintenance. Pressure doors were checked even when the stone had given no warning. Turbines were shut manually when whales approached. Emergency air was tested. Mercies were charted anew after landslides.

When outsiders asked whether they believed some guardian watched over the drowned range, the islanders usually answered with a question about the outsiders’ seals, fuel reserves, or weather plan.

Only in their songs were they less guarded.

A new verse entered the oldest storm song. It concerned no rescue, revelation, or promise that the sea would spare them. It said merely that between one force and another there was sometimes a little room; that the room had not been earned; and that whoever found it alive should leave it open behind them.

Above, the weather continued.

Kelp streamed flat. Herring turned. Whales passed between the drowned summits. Machines rotated where the current permitted, and stopped where life required passage. Far below, silt settled grain by grain.

Inside the finished mountains, the people kept watch.

And something kind, having taught the stone to warn them, kept watch with them.
