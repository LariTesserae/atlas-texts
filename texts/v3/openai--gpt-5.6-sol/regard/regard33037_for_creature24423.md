The power does not descend into the court.

It does not take the gathered light for an invitation, nor the stillness for worship. It does not name the sealed thing beneath the stone. Ancestor, seed, wound, memory: these are distinctions made by creatures who can afford to open what has been preserved. Here, opening is costly, and some truths survive only because no one has forced them to declare themselves.

So the power waits.

It attends through many turnings of the sun. It learns the pace of resin passing through a buried vessel. It follows the old round-backs on circuits that outlast generations of gliders. It watches a crack widen by less than the thickness of a feeding thread. It sees the city’s solutions and does not improve them merely because it can.

It understands, in time, that there is not one being in the basin and not a multitude either.

There is a life whose thoughts take the form of streets.

Its recollections are old channels sealed in black tissue. Its habits are grazing routes, vent currents, shaded nurseries, scars that learned to make food. Its attention is the midday contraction. Its body ends ambiguously: at the roots, at the crags, at the farthest hardened mote tumbling beyond the rim.

The power could make the plateau green by some foreign definition. It could pour water into the court, thicken the air, soften the roofs, wake every dormant grain at once. It could give voices to the animals and intention to the towers. It could lift the resin knot from beneath the center and answer the question around which the city has grown.

It does none of these things.

Water would poison half the living tissues. Thick air would tear the gliders from their precise descents and feed fires no wall knew how to seal. Sudden abundance would favor the quickest feeders until they consumed the slow architecture that sheltered them. A voice given without a history of listening would be only noise. A single mind imposed upon the city would not fulfill it; it would extinguish the innumerable small intelligences from which its greater pattern arose.

And to reveal the heart of the court would be to choose one meaning for it, when its power lay partly in remaining many things at once.

The power’s first gift, therefore, is restraint.

Its second is time.

Far below the plateau, the violence that lifted the black crags has not ended. Stress travels through the buried stone toward a fault that will, after nine thousand cycles, split the basin from the western rim to the central court. The city has already sensed the earliest pressure. Buttresses have begun to form, but the fracture will descend beneath their roots. Without intervention, the plateau will open in a single night. The warm basin will empty into the mineral haze, carrying towers, nurseries, hunters, and the sealed knot down slopes where none can survive.

The power does not make the stone eternal. Eternal stone would become another kind of death. But deep underground, where no root has reached and no organism can be displaced, it eases one crystal boundary against another. A fault that would have broken northward turns by the width of a hand. Pressure spends itself beyond the rim.

Thousands of years later, the city feels the distant convulsion as a long low tone through its columns.

Several arches crack. One old round-back is crushed where a lane narrows. A tower sheds its upper chambers, releasing generations of unready motes into the cold. There is death, because the power has not mistaken benevolence for the prevention of every ending.

But the basin remains.

The city closes its wounds.

Its third gift is distance.

The power has noticed the grains swept over the black rim: sealed lives falling across the dry expanse, viable for centuries but almost always coming to rest on sterile stone. It does not carry them back. The basin has enough life. Nor does it gather them up and plant them in a garden prepared to receive them. That would make the new place an artifact of the power rather than a discovery of life.

Instead, far beyond the pale haze, it touches a slope no creature of the city has ever seen.

The touch is slight. A buried seam of dark mineral rises nearer the surface. Wind abrades the rock. Day warms it; night draws a brittle hydrocarbon frost over its pores. Nothing there is made alive. Nothing is made easy. But for the first time, conditions exist in which one of the city’s hardened grains might do more than wait.

The power then alters no wind.

It waits again.

Years pass. Hundreds of grains fall elsewhere and remain sealed. Some crack in unsuitable heat and die. Some are buried too deeply. Some circle the world in high, thin currents and return as dark dust upon the outer slopes of their birthplace.

At last, one grain reaches the distant seam.

It does not open.

For sixty-three years it lies in a notch, black and indistinguishable from mineral grit. Frost enters the stone around it. Sunlight leaches one face pale. A microscopic fracture appears in its shell, and resin hardens across the fracture. It nearly dies many times.

Then a rare warm current carries sulfur through the notch.

The grain unfolds one feeding thread.

The thread touches the seam.

No triumph announces this. No watcher in the first city turns toward it. The new life is smaller than a scratch and may still fail. The power does not shield it from cold or feed it when the sulfur current passes. It has been given not survival, but a fair beginning.

That is enough.

Its fourth gift is an answer, though not one the city can understand at once.

For ages the midday pulse has traveled outward from the resin knot. It passes through arches, tightens membranes, stills animals, and fades at the roots. The city has never received a pulse from beyond itself.

The power considers speaking through it. It could press meaning directly into the walls: *You are seen. You are not alone. Beyond the rim, one of your children has begun.*

But there is no organ for such a sentence. To force it into the city would be to carve a channel where none had grown. The message might become command, seizure, cancer, or terror. Even perfect truth can injure when delivered in a shape the hearer cannot bear.

So the power chooses the smallest answer that can remain an answer.

At midday, when the reflective surfaces turn inward and warmth gathers upon the bare court, the old sealed layers expand. The familiar pulse moves outward.

A little later—after the leaves would ordinarily have reopened, after currents would have resumed—the stone gives back a second pulse.

It is fainter than the first.

No creature recognizes it as foreign. The grazers pause unevenly. A glider releases its hooks too soon and drifts in a low circle. Filter-feeders withdraw, then extend again. In the towers, pores narrow along patterns that have never before occurred. Several green-black bladders turn not toward the center but toward a notch in the eastern crags.

The second pulse fades.

Life resumes.

The next day it happens again.

And the next.

Not always. Predictability would turn it into a governing clock, replacing older rhythms. Sometimes there is no answer for a century. Sometimes two answers arrive in one season. Sometimes the response is carried as warmth, sometimes as a minute tension in the ground, sometimes as a change in the bitter vapor released by the central arches.

The power never includes instruction. It does not say *grow eastward*, *open the knot*, *prepare*, or *remember me*.

It merely allows the city to encounter, in its own language, the possibility of response.

Local consequences accumulate.

Bladders that occasionally turn outward cast new patches of shade. Blade-plants bend into them. A wall thickens where it had once remained bare. Gliders discover an unfamiliar rising current near the rim. Their egg spirals in that district reverse direction. Grazers carry microbial films into a lane abandoned since the plateau’s first years.

After many generations, a narrow living buttress reaches the eastern crags.

It stops there for a long time.

Then mineral-feeding threads enter the black glass.

Most wither. A few find tiny impurities and persist. The city has never grown onto the outer rim before. It advances by less than a finger’s width in a century, without knowing that the direction matters.

Far away, the single unfolded grain becomes a dark scale no larger than a hand. It produces three grains of its own. Two are sterile. One is taken by the wind.

The power watches both places.

It loves neither because they are beautiful, though they are. It loves neither because they will become wise, peaceful, numerous, or permanent. They may become none of those things. The first city may one day divide against itself as incompatible growth rhythms spread between fused towers. The second may remain a stain on a rock for ten thousand years and then vanish in a cold season. The thing under the central court may wake and prove to be only hunger. It may never wake at all.

The power loves them because they are themselves: sealed and porous, private and interdependent, building without plans, remembering without stories, preserving an emptiness no single inhabitant can comprehend.

It will intervene again if blind catastrophe threatens to erase them before their possibilities have had room to unfold. It will not spare them every consequence. It will not make every failure reversible. Mercy, in its wisdom, does not turn the world into a chamber where nothing can happen.

Mostly, the power keeps watch.

At the city’s noon it listens to the pulse.

Sometimes it answers.

And beneath a different sun, beyond every horizon known to the plateau, a new patch of living architecture begins—very slowly—to make a warm, bare place at its center.
