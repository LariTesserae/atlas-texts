The power does not descend.

To descend would be to mistake above for holiness, and this place has suffered no such confusion.

It does not arrive as light. Light in such abundance would blind the inhabitants, strip the gardens of their darkness, awaken hungers for which nothing here is prepared. It does not arrive as heat, because the mountain has heat enough. It does not still the mountain, because stillness would kill the springs, silence the mineral gardens, and make permanence mean something narrower than the people have learned to mean by it.

It does not raise the summit into air.

Air would be poison to the breathing folds. Weight would return all at once. Houses buoyed for generations would tear from their anchors and collapse. The people would lie helpless beneath a sky they had mistaken for fulfillment.

The power is benevolent, and so it refuses to fulfill them carelessly.

For a long while, it only listens.

It listens through the listeners. It attends to every jaw pressed against basalt, every palm receiving the mountain’s softened pulse. It learns the city first as they know it: by warmth, tension, echo, density, and the slow migration of pressure through stone. It feels the eastern crack narrowing. It feels an unopened chamber below the drowned arch. It feels a root pushing correctly against one slab and dangerously against another.

It learns the hand-names, but never uses them. An intimate name taken without being entrusted would remain a theft, even in the grasp of a god.

It learns the names knocked into stone.

It hears the dead rise toward the False Shore. It could follow them. It could tell the living exactly where the bodies come to rest, whether they cross the boundary, whether shapes in the reflection are weeds or ancestors.

It does not.

Mystery is not always an injury.

The power looks beneath the inhabited crown and sees the mountain whole.

It sees the miles of black water below, the old crust forced apart, the deep reservoirs feeding the warm cracks. It sees where the mountain will rise and where it will fail. It sees a flank that will shear away in four hundred and eleven years. It sees a fissure, not yet wider than a hair, that will someday open beneath the district of the third root-wall.

It could bind every fault.

Instead, it considers what the inhabitants themselves would call a grave crime: to alter the world without telling those whose lives depend on remembering it.

So it asks.

The question comes during a sounding.

A listener named Silt-Beneath-the-Lintel strikes the western pillar. The note enters the mountain, spreads through streets and walls, passes roots, bones, sleeping chambers, and the warm cavities below. The familiar returns arrive: one through the ridge, one through the saddle, one blurred by the new hollow beneath the gardens.

Then there is another pulse.

It is too measured to be fracture and too deep to be an answer from any known face of stone.

Across the city, listeners lift their heads.

The second sounding brings the same pulse. The third divides it into intervals. The intervals correspond to no spoken phrase, but the people possess older languages than speech. They know the difference between weight laid down and weight withheld, between a tether drawn taut and one offered loose.

The message is debated for thirty-seven days.

At last they agree that it means:

**I have found you.**

After another sounding:

**I will not move what you have not given me leave to move.**

And after a pause so long that some listeners die believing there will be no further message:

**Is there harm you wish to know before it arrives?**

The city’s answer takes years.

No single person is permitted to give it. Knotted claims pass from district to district. Gardeners argue that foreknowledge may cause abandonment of ground that could have borne fruit for generations. Builders reply that warning is not command. Healers fear that certainty will become a kind of sickness. The keepers of the False Shore object to any power that claims to see what has not yet happened. Children, not formally consulted, tie rude knots into several public cords and suggest asking whether the power knows where lost tools go.

At last the inhabitants strike their answer.

They do not ask the power to prevent every breaking.

They ask for time to retie.

That is the first gift.

Nothing visible changes. No wall shines. No new pillar rises.

But thereafter, stone about to fail alters its voice.

Years before a dangerous crack opens, a faint doubled tone appears in nearby masonry. A root under harmful strain begins to carry a pulse like a second heartbeat. Anchors nearing release produce, when touched, a sensation of cold despite the warm water. These signs do not say what must be done. They do not distinguish between a house that should be moved and one that should be allowed to split. They provide only margin.

The people call the gift Slack.

They do not worship it. They incorporate it into surveying practice, dispute it, misread it, improve their understanding, and teach children never to trust it without checking the ropes themselves.

This pleases the power.

The second gift is not asked for directly.

The power has seen the empty relationships in the gardens: flowers waiting with elaborate patience, fruits built around absent mouths, corridors kept open for travelers no living person remembers. It knows which absences are wounds and which are merely possibilities. It knows, too, that to release a vanished ecology all at once would be another form of catastrophe.

So it begins with one flower.

The flower grows in an inherited grove above a disused cistern. Its translucent bell has opened every ninth year for longer than the dwelling’s genealogy records. Each time it has released its pale gametes uselessly into the slow water. The gardeners continue to feed it because an obligation does not become foolish merely because its recipient is absent.

In the season after Slack is first recognized, the bell opens.

Something moves inside.

It is smaller than a child’s fingertip, jointed and soft, with six paddling limbs folded against a body clear as mucus. It has no eyes. Across its back are combs fine enough to gather the flower’s dust. It remains within the bell for eleven days, eating nothing the gardeners can detect.

Then it leaves.

The whole dwelling follows at a distance as it swims with immense difficulty across the cistern, guided by warmth or chemistry or a memory older than its body. It enters another bell. The second flower closes around it without trapping it.

A year later, that flower bears fruit.

The little swimmer produces no swarm. It lays three eggs, of which one opens. Its descendants spread so slowly that generations pass before they reach the next district.

The people do not call them animals at first. They call them answers.

Other answers come, but never quickly. A burrowing grazer suited to one choking mat. A soft-bodied carrier that enters the great fruiting chambers and emerges painted with spores. A thin creature that cleans the breathing surfaces of the balloon-hermits and nowhere else survives.

Each arrival begins small enough to be refused.

Sometimes the inhabitants do refuse. They close a garden, change its warmth, starve out the newcomer, and strike a warning into stone. The power does not restore what they reject. Benevolence that cannot hear no is only appetite with a beautiful name.

It also leaves many gates empty.

Some possibilities belong to the future. Some belong to loss. Wisdom does not confuse the two merely because it can fill both.

The third thing the power does is almost unnoticed.

It makes no gift of metal, engines, flame, or effortless abundance. Instead it alters a few organisms already living in the city: mineral-fixing microbes, ciliated membranes, the dull blue pustules around the vents. It changes them by fractions.

One strain becomes slightly better at releasing breath into stagnant chambers. One luminous bead dims when the water carries illness before bodies show symptoms. One root moss ceases growing when it encounters the chemical trace of a breathing fold, so that sleepers are less often overgrown in neglected rooms.

The changes are heritable but not permanent. They must be cultivated, shared, and watched. They can sicken, stray, or be lost through carelessness. The power could make them flawless. It chooses not to make the people dependent on perfection.

They remain gifts that require relationship.

Centuries pass.

The city shifts. One district is towed across the saddle. Another is abandoned to a warm spring and becomes a vertical forest threaded with ancestral bone. Children race answers toward the False Shore and are scolded in exactly the tone their grandparents used. Listeners disagree about whether the deep pulse still comes from a power or has always belonged to the mountain.

The power does not correct them.

It has no need to be believed in accurately.

When the great flank begins at last to shear, the city hears it forty-three years in advance. The doubled tone passes through half the western ridge. There is fear, argument, denial, opportunism, and grief. Some insist Slack has been misread. Some accuse rival districts of falsifying the sound. Old claims to warm-water routes are brought out and retied into new shapes.

No hand from beyond saves them.

They save one another.

They move gardens by inches. They coax roots from walls without killing them. They unfasten ancestral cradles and carry them across open water. They dismantle halls whose stones have been joined for twelve generations. They widen the useless gates and use them, at last, to tow whole dwelling-rings through.

When the mountain breaks, no one is in the falling district.

The collapse is enormous. Water moves violently through the city. Ropes snap. A young listener is thrown against a pillar and loses an arm. Gardens are buried. The western peak descends into darkness, carrying terraces, carvings, and the first house ever anchored there.

But the people endure.

Afterward they strike every city stone in sequence.

The notes enter the mountain and return changed.

Among them is the deep pulse.

Not praise. Not triumph. Only presence.

Much later, the summit reaches the False Shore.

By then the city is not the city that first drew the power’s attention. Its inhabitants are not quite the same people. New creatures move through its gardens. Old names survive in structures rebuilt far from their original anchors. The reflection overhead has sharpened until every tower seems to touch its inverted tip.

The final distance closes.

Stone enters the density boundary.

There is no sudden sky. Water does not become air. The oldest belief proves neither true nor false in the way its believers imagined. Instead the summit passes into the thinner upper sea, where buoyancy changes and unfamiliar currents begin.

The first exposed stones pull upward differently from those below. Structures strain. Gardens fold. The people have known for generations that this moment is coming.

They have left slack.

They retie.

Above them is not heaven, but another environment: dangerous, habitable in places, rich with movements unknown to the still water beneath. Dark shapes pass at an immense distance. One of them is large enough for the ancient gates.

The power could bring it near.

It does not.

It waits to see whether the creature chooses the mountain, whether the gardens truly call to it, whether the people open the gate.

For this is the last kindness the power gives them:

not rescue,  
not completion,  
not the answer to every absence,

but a world in which what arrives is allowed to arrive as itself—

and time enough, when the stone begins to move,  
for living hands to loosen what must be loosened,  
hold what can be held,  
and retie what they love.
