The power does not enter as a body.

No radiance stands in the plaza. No voice crosses the marshes. The water in the central well does not tremble, and the pale flowers do not turn their faces toward an unseen arrival. Its attention is gentler than weather and more complete. It knows the basin without disturbing it: the pressure of each root against clay, the slow exchange through every buried graft, the mineral hunger of cells spread thinly over basalt.

Before acting, it asks what mercy is required.

Nothing here is in pain. Nothing is hunted, starved, driven from shelter, or made afraid. The city’s living continuity does not grieve its makers. The trees possess no loneliness. The moss does not regard the empty doorways as empty. To return the vanished people merely because their absence troubles an observer would not be kindness. To introduce birds for the sake of song, insects for the sake of flowers, or animals so that the streets might once again bear tracks would satisfy a preference for motion, not a need of the place.

So the power restores no one.

It does not populate the courts with imitations of the dead. It does not force memory into the roots or make the pools reflect scenes that have passed. The old inhabitants are allowed their completed lives. Their jars, steps, channels, and planted soil remain what they have become: conditions inherited by beings who do not know they have inherited them.

Nor does the power preserve the city forever. Perfect preservation would be another kind of death. Stone must eventually soften. Channels must alter. Roofs must descend into soil. To hold each wall upright past its nature would bind the living basin to the intentions of absent hands.

Yet the power perceives a danger too slow for any inhabitant to feel.

The life here persists, but it narrows. Clones extend themselves through ground that changes little. Crops derived from a few selected bodies multiply without renewal. Spores settle beside their parents. Pollen falls within the same courts from which it rose. The basin is stable enough to endure for ages, but stability has begun to close around it. One unfamiliar rot, one shift in the minerals of the springs, one century of slightly deeper cloud could pass through the linked roots and leave whole districts green no longer.

The power decides not to give the basin new creatures.

It gives it weather.

Until then, rain has varied only in amount. Now, once in a great interval, the cloud ceiling lifts.

The first lifting comes near midday. It is not dramatic. The gray above the eastern ridge thins from slate to pearl. For several hours the full shape of the mountains is visible, including summits ordinarily dissolved in mist. Sunlight reaches the basin without fierceness, laying a mild warmth across roofs, marshes, and black paving.

Stone begins to dry.

The change is no more than the width of a finger along the edge of each pool, yet throughout the city films contract, seed cases loosen, and clay seams open invisibly. Air warmed over the central plaza rises. Cooler air descends from the green shelves beyond the last houses.

A breeze passes through the streets.

It is weak. It overturns nothing. It moves first along the high roads, follows the avenue divided by the glassy spine, enters the courts through roofless doors, and crosses the flooded beds. The reeds bend together for the first time in centuries.

Pollen does not fall where it formed. It travels.

Spores cross from one district to another. A pale flower receives dust from a plant beyond the marsh rather than from its own adjoining stem. The crowns of the court trees stir, and among their millions of leaves a few are pressed against leaves they have never touched. Fine fragments of moss rise from walls and settle on bare stone farther upslope.

Then the clouds close. Rain returns, light and prolonged. Cracks swell shut. The channels fill to their accustomed rims.

Nothing seems transformed.

But in the following years, several seeds form that could not have formed before. Most fail. One germinates beside a boundary marker where its kind has never grown. Its first leaves are smaller than those in the old beds and darker at their edges. It tolerates the mineral soil for three days longer than its ancestors could have done. Three days are enough for a root to reach a damp seam.

After that, the lifting comes irregularly. Sometimes two centuries pass without it. Sometimes it occurs twice within the life of a tree. The basin acquires not seasons exactly, but occasions.

On one occasion, water lowers in the shallowest pools and exposes mud long hidden from air. Old seeds wake there, descendants of plants no longer visible in the city. On another, the western marsh rises high enough to cross a low causeway. Floating stems enter a separate drainage and establish themselves among pale rocks. Once, a wind carries spores over the serrated ridge and out of the basin entirely.

The power guides none of these to predetermined ends. It does not select a favored tree or preserve every delicate film. Some old cultivated forms disappear. A broad-leaved plant that once occupied nine terraces dwindles to one and then is gone. A fungus reaches roof timbers previously too dry for it, and a storehouse settles inward after a season of rain. Its wall falls without violence, opening a rectangle of soil to the sky.

The loss is permitted.

From the broken wall, minerals enter the garden below. A tree root follows them. In time the root lifts two fitted stones apart, not as conquest but as growth.

The power watches without demanding gratitude. There is no mind here capable of worship, and it desires none. Its benevolence is not a bargain.

It gives the city no guardian except change in gentle measure. It places no law upon the living continuity except that its future need not be identical to its past. It leaves refuge enough for the old, distance enough for the new, and time enough that alteration does not become catastrophe.

Much later, a flowering plant appears on a roof where only moss had grown. Its petals are still pale, its scent nearly absent. Yet its pollen has come through many crossings, from terraces, marshes, courts, and distant shelves. It is not wholly a crop and not wholly wild. No vanished gardener planned it. No wise power designed its exact form.

At dusk, its petals close.

Below it, drops gather beneath the roof edge and fall into a jar left by hands that will not return. The jar overflows into a clay channel. The channel carries water through the house and into a bed whose retaining wall has begun to lean. Roots receive it there, passing some portion through old grafts and keeping some within a seed newly made.

The city remains quiet.

But it is no longer held at the point just after rain.

It has been given a before and an after.
