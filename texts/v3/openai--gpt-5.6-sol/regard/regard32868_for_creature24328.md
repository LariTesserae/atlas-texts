The power does not descend.

Descent would already be an answer, and in this place direction is too intimate a thing to assume.

It does not appear as a light. Light would pass through the bronze dust, strike a million leaves, awaken old reflexes in the velvet fields, and fix the transparent young in whatever unfinished shapes they happened to be wearing. Nor does it speak. A voice large enough to cross the sea would enter every air chamber at once and break the frail order of all stories being told there.

It does not still the currents.

It does not lift the water into a basin, close the whirlpools, straighten time, restore the mountains, drive off the immense shadows, or make permanent what has endured only by yielding.

A lesser mercy might do these things.

The wise power first learns the weight of its own attention.

Then it makes itself small enough not to be mistaken for weather.

In a province far from the edge, where a cold well rises through three terraces of yellow mineral and a stone circle waits for an event whose builders have not yet been born, a current warms by less than the width of a thought. The warmth does not travel. It remains close to the circle.

After one year, it cools.

After another, it warms again.

The interval is irregular, but not random. It is shaped like a question.

The inhabitants notice before the question begins.

For decades they gather near the empty circle. They do not enter it. Elders loosen themselves from the plain and come on root-slow limbs, carrying gardens and transparent creatures in the chambers of their bodies. Travelers arrive from beyond the troughs. Near the reeds, unseen young shift within their molts. Those who practice air assemble in a leaned-slab chamber and attempt to give the question a sound.

They cannot.

They produce a dry click followed by a long exhalation. The meaning escapes the room and returns to pressure.

The power waits.

It has waited beside first stars. It has watched mountains rise from level ground and become level ground again. Yet its waiting here is not the waiting of something for which time is cheap. It grants each interval its full cost. It refuses to make the inhabitants hurry merely because it can outlast them.

When at last they answer, they do so by changing the question.

Seven of them arrange themselves around the circle. Each removes one smooth stone from within its body and sets it into a gap that has waited centuries for the exact shape. They hold the stones without striking. Rootlets enter the seams. The cold well alters its upward flow. Moss darkens across the plateau in bands too wide for any single inhabitant to perceive.

The reply reaches the power in portions.

It says:

**You are pressing upon us.**

The power withdraws at once.

Not wholly. Wholly withdrawing would also be an act performed without consent. It loosens its regard until it is no heavier than the attention of a sleeping animal. Reeds straighten. An unfinished young one, caught halfway between transparency and color, resumes becoming.

Years later, the circle sends another pattern.

**You noticed that you were pressing.**

Warmth touches the current once.

**Yes.**

There is a long silence, material and complete.

Then:

**What do you intend to preserve?**

The power considers.

It could preserve the suspended sea. It could knot the distortion of weight so deeply into the world that no age would loosen it. It could brace the plateau beneath the water, forbid the final grains of stone from wearing away, and turn every trembling boundary hard as law.

It could preserve the inhabitants. It could separate them from the spores and rooted growth, establish a distinction between body and forest, cause each child to arise recognizably itself. It could give their mouths permanence, their limbs fixed number, their memories order. It could place every consciousness beyond hunger, accident, inversion, and loss.

It could preserve their records, unchanging leaf by leaf.

It could preserve their settlements, their stories, their disagreements, even the pale grazers moving through moss.

But the power is wise enough to understand that preservation can be a manner of ending.

To preserve the sea perfectly would be to make it cease responding. To preserve the inhabitants perfectly would hold them in one description forever—the very fate from which they conceal their young. A deathless forest cannot make room for germination. A memory that cannot be grazed becomes a mineral crust over the mind.

So the power replies:

**Nothing against its nature.**

The answer takes eleven years to pass through the stones.

The inhabitants receive it with neither gratitude nor disappointment. They carry it across the plain. It moves through root mats and the limbs of elders. In distant air chambers they try to tell it from beginning to end, and some emerge remembering that the answer came before the question.

At the margin, those who teach the sea its continuance interpret it as support.

Those strengthening their bones interpret it as refusal.

The few who wish to cross interpret it as permission.

The oldest inhabitant does not interpret it.

This elder lies beneath a forest whose ribbon-leaves have grown through openings in its body and rooted again on the other side. Three mineral shelves encircle it. One limb has become a causeway. Another extends into a stone chamber and forms part of its roof. It has not moved since before one of the immense shadows failed to pass.

The power attends to it as lightly as it can.

A violet ring travels over the elder’s skin. Centuries later, one limb lifts from the plateau.

The gesture asks:

**Then what can mercy do?**

The power does not answer quickly.

It looks through all the times folded into the place. It sees the sea fall. It sees the sea remain. It sees the sea overturn wholly and become rain around the low world. It sees the plateau emerge into cold air, furred in stranded forests that take a thousand years to die. It sees black spores descend through clouds and root in mountain snow. It sees shepherds pick up smooth red stones and carry them in their mouths during storms. It sees air chambers open upon futures in which no water remains outside them. It sees other futures where the old distortion spreads and the sky itself becomes a sea without surface.

None of these futures is singular.

None is harmless.

The wise power finds no outcome called salvation that does not also contain a taking.

So it asks:

**What do you fear losing?**

The answer begins at once and takes generations.

They fear the sea’s forgetting.

They fear that one day weight will choose a direction and never forgive it. They fear that the vertical wall will cease trembling. They fear open air entering all at once, folding the broad soft bodies inward before they can reach chambers. They fear water falling upon the low world with the force of a continent.

They fear the immense shadows, but not because the shadows may consume them. They fear that the shadows are what remains when a country is erased not only from place but from relation—when nothing remembers how to reach it.

They fear that those who cross the boundary do not die and do not live, but become unanswerable.

They fear the young being seen too soon.

They fear the old becoming too dense with memory to move.

They fear becoming permanent.

They fear ending.

Last of all, beneath these many pressures, is a smaller pattern:

**We fear that what leaves us becomes nothing to us.**

The power understands.

It cannot promise that nothing will be lost. Such promises are made only by powers that intend to possess what they save.

But it can alter one small courtesy of the world.

Near the margin, where horizontal forests press their leaves into vast green windows, the power touches the ancient distortion of weight.

Only there.

Only gently.

It does not strengthen the boundary or weaken it. Instead it makes the boundary deep.

What was once a division without thickness—a single impossible instant between water and open sky—becomes a region.

At first it is no wider than a hand. Then a body. Then the length of a stone circle. Within it, water does not end and air does not begin. They enter one another by degrees finer than mist. Pressure loosens without vanishing. Sound gains edges but not yet distance. A soft body finds its bones slowly. A breathing chamber opens inside the sea and the sea continues through it as suspended beads, warm vapor, films stretched between leaves.

The plants at the margin recoil.

Then they explore.

Ribbon-leaves push into the deepened boundary. Their waterward portions remain broad and translucent. Their airward tips divide into dark branching threads, trembling in weather. Root veils gather droplets. Black spores enter the interval and neither fall nor float; they hang there until touched.

Rain from below rises into it.

Drops that would once have fallen away now pause, flatten, and join the sea. At other times, bronze water gathers itself into warm rain and passes outward without tearing a mountain-sized wound in the wall.

The boundary becomes a shore—not a line where one world stops, but a country made from their meeting.

It remains difficult.

The power does not make crossing painless. Bodies still fold. Hidden bones still bear unfamiliar burdens. Those who enter must learn breath, then pressure, then breath again. Memory behaves badly there. A traveler may recall both sides as home and neither as origin. Some turn back. Some go onward. Some remain in the interval so long that moss covers one half of them while dry lichen covers the other.

But they can answer.

A limb pressed into the water sends pressure through the root mats. A limb lifted into air can strike stone and make a small, close sound. The same being can do both.

The first to enter is not one of the bone-practicers.

It is an elder thought incapable of movement.

For three hundred years it travels toward the margin, carrying its forest. Causeways separate from its body along seams prepared by descendants. Transparent animals abandon chambers in its skin. Mineral shelves crack loose without sound. It reaches the new shore during an interval of darkness, when spores obscure the sea.

There it waits with half its body in thinning water.

Its outer limbs collapse inward. Bones long concealed under soft tissue take weight. The elder makes a sound.

It is very small.

Those in the sea feel the gesture before hearing it. Those in the air hear the click after the gesture has passed. For the first time, one meaning arrives by both paths.

The sound means neither farewell nor arrival.

It means:

**Still related.**

After that, the power does less.

This is the part of benevolence most difficult for power: not the making, but the refusal to continue making once others have begun.

The inhabitants extend stone circles into the shore. They grow records with veins adapted to vapor. They build chambers whose first entrance opens into water, whose second opens into air, and whose third reveals a time in which the chamber has been dismantled because it is no longer needed.

Those who believe the sea must remain continue teaching it its shape.

Those who believe it must overturn continue strengthening their bones.

Neither is proven right. The power does not settle their disagreement. Wisdom does not impoverish a people by removing every uncertain future from them.

It gives one further gift, though even this is made only after asking.

Around the reed thickets where the unfinished young conceal themselves, attention becomes visible.

Any gaze that falls too heavily there takes on color: a pale violet stain in the water, gathering around the watcher’s sight. Curiosity can no longer pretend to be weightless. Those who look learn to loosen their regard. The young are not made invulnerable; they are given warning.

In time, some choose to be seen.

They emerge clear and radial from the hollow stalks. Under gentle attention their limbs remain possible, neither fixed nor formless. Adults surround them, not with concealment but with many conflicting gestures, so that no single description can close around them.

The power watches this from very far away.

Far enough to do no harm.

It does not prevent the next inversion. Forests bow, drifting islands pass through rooted meadows, and mineral rain rises from the plateau. The new shore twists but does not close. Air descends in silver chambers. Water lifts in warm spheres. Beings on both sides reach into the interval and hold one another while down changes its mind.

Nor does the power drive away the next immense shadow.

As it crosses the troughs, the inhabitants thin themselves against the stone. Young ones root beneath pillars. The pale grazers become still.

The shadow passes.

This time it leaves something behind: not a wound, not an absence, but a pressure like a distant country answering.

Perhaps the power did this.

Perhaps it merely made possible the noticing of what had always answered.

It does not say.

Ages later, the sea still trembles above the clouds.

It has not been made eternal. The plateau continues wearing smooth. Structures collapse and are raised. Records grow illegible. Grazers remove memories. Some who cross never return, though stones sent outward occasionally come back inside warm rain. Some who remain regret remaining. Some air-breathers root again in old age and slowly become forest.

At the deepened edge, weather and ocean meet without deciding which contains the other.

And when the plateau gives off its inaudible tone, the inhabitants gather as before. They arrange their broad bodies. The stones within them tremble.

Pressure passes across hundreds of kilometers.

Beyond the margin, in the pale low world, small red stones answer from hearths, riverbeds, pockets, shrines, and the mouths of shepherds standing beneath sudden warm rain.

The acknowledgment is no longer only:

**The sea is still here.**

It has become:

**What leaves is not therefore nothing.**

And the benevolent power, hearing this, does not claim the words as its work.

It loosens its attention until the place can no longer feel it.

Then it remains nearby, quietly enough to be mistaken for freedom.
