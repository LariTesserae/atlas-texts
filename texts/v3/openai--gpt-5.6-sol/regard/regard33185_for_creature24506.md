## The First Correction

The power would not arrive in light.

Light was already too plentiful there: white on the salt, white on the ceramic roofs, white striking the naked shelves until the eye invented darkness for relief. Nor would it speak from the mountain. The inhabitants had heard enough deep voices to know that magnitude did not imply wisdom.

It would begin by attending.

For eleven years, perhaps, nothing would happen that could be distinguished from the city’s ordinary continuance.

The power would learn the difference between a piston’s intended pause and the hesitation that came before failure. It would follow condensate through abandoned pipes. It would count the water reclaimed from the dead and the water permitted to evaporate at wealthy tables. It would rest its awareness in the Datum chamber without disturbing the needle. It would watch children play at fracture and notice which children always volunteered to be the alarm, which became the wall, which hid imaginary water for themselves, and which returned across the dangerous floor for someone others had forgotten.

It would not assume the mountain was asleep.

It would not assume the films were mindless.

It would not assume that the city, because it contained laws and names, was the only thing there capable of suffering.

This would take time. Wisdom, encountering an unfinished world, would be careful not to finish it by mistake.

The power would find much to admire.

The inhabitants did not demand permanence from what could not provide it. They built joints into their monuments. They permitted names to change. They knew that survival was maintenance rather than victory. Their dead were kept near but not made to bear weight. They had learned, under relentless conditions, to hear small warnings.

Yet the power would also perceive the wound hidden inside their greatest virtue.

They had lived so long by scarcity that they had begun to grant scarcity moral authority.

Necessary things were respected because they were scarce; therefore some had come to believe that making them less scarce would make people careless. Suffering had become evidence of seriousness. A person who endured thirst without complaint was called disciplined, while the arrangements that had caused the thirst went unexamined. Children were taught catastrophe before wonder. The rich displayed abundance by destroying a little of it. The poor apologized for breathing too quickly.

The city corrected every kind of drift except the drift by which an emergency becomes a tradition.

The power would not condemn them for this. It would understand how it happened.

But it would correct it.

Not all at once.

The first intervention would be a warning.

One night, during an hour when the pumps were in their lowest cycle and no authorized hammering was underway, three blows would pass through the city.

The first would sound in the eastern pressure loom.

The second would sound in the sealed pipes beneath the oldest quarter.

The third would sound from the bottom of the Reservoir of Minutes.

Every sleeper would wake.

The crawlers would emerge by the thousands.

Until then, most citizens would have seen only one or two at a time, pale shells beneath a warm collar or along the lip of a drain. Now they would pour from the walls in orderly lines. They would not flee upward, as they did before pressure failure. They would descend.

All through the city, they would make for the reservoir.

Keepers arriving at the upper chambers would discover the translucent films shining strongly enough to illuminate their hands. The branching light beneath certain keepers’ skin would answer it. On every unscraped surface, the threads would have arranged themselves in the same figure: a long curve intersected by seven short lines.

No one would understand the figure.

A senior corrector, old enough to remember three retired maps, would recognize it as the profile of a conduit omitted from the present plans but shown in a survey made two centuries before. The conduit had been sealed after an earthquake, its purpose forgotten. According to the old map, it ended far from the reservoir.

According to the crawlers, it did not.

The city had shifted.

The maps had shifted.

The mountain had not shifted in the same way.

They would open walls bearing no dead, evacuate six residential blocks, and drill where the films shone brightest. Behind a hand’s width of vitrified stone they would find the old conduit bent like a loaded spring. Its inner lining would be split. On one side lay the Reservoir of Minutes.

On the other was a rising chamber of arsenic vapor and superheated brine.

It would have opened within nine days.

Had it done so, the reservoir would not merely have been poisoned. The sudden pressure exchange would have driven contaminated steam through the oldest household pipes. Thousands would have died before the city understood where the danger had begun.

The correctors would brace the conduit. The machine tenders would relieve its pressure in increments. Water keepers would catch the poisonous condensate in black-glass vessels and seal it in the newest stone.

No miracle would perform the work for them.

The power’s warning would save them, but their hands would do the saving.

Afterward there would be inquiries.

The custodians would deny moving the Datum. The tenders would deny striking the loom. Reservoir workers would deny entering the sealed pipes. The films would be scraped from some towers and preserved in others. Officials would debate whether the event had been sabotage, group delusion, unknown machine behavior, or a coincidence enlarged by fear.

Then one of the long-winged black shapes would land.

It would come down at dusk upon the roof of the Allocation Tables.

Everyone who saw it would disagree about its size. Some would say it was no larger than a cloak. Some would insist its wings covered the building. It would fold along lines that were not joints and settle around the navigation lamp, thin as burnt paper.

For the first time, one of the shapes would be seen at rest.

It would have no eyes.

It would have no mouth.

It would have no clear border between body and wing. Fine channels covered it, each narrower than a hair. Where the evening air touched its upper surface, it became cold—not mildly cool, but cold enough for frost to form despite the heat retained in the roof.

Water began to bead beneath it.

The creature, or sheet, or instrument opened once.

The droplets fell into the public gauge.

Then it separated into hundreds of narrow strips and let the thermal current carry them across the city.

This would be the second intervention: not a gift of water, but a lesson in how to receive more of it.

The strips would catch on towers, rails, roof slots, veils, sculptures, and the outstretched hands of citizens. Each strip would turn one edge toward the open sky and the other toward the nearest channel. Before midnight they would be wet.

Engineers would study them. So would keepers, tenders, children, smugglers, physicians, cooks, and those sects that returned bodies to new stone. The strips would prove impossible to reproduce as a single substance because they were not a single substance. They consisted of mineral layers, carbon membranes, microscopic cavities, and threads resembling those in the condenser films.

Separated from the films, they would work for a few nights and then become ordinary ash.

The films, placed on ordinary metal, would grow but condense little.

Together, they made surfaces that released the day’s heat toward the empty upper sky while sheltering their undersides from the warm rising air. Water gathered there in quantities no existing tower could match.

The High Condenser would know the physics involved. Nothing would be created from nothing. No law would be broken.

Yet the arrangement would be so elegant, and so suited to the mountain, that its discovery would feel less like invention than being forgiven for overlooking something.

Attempts would be made to own it.

The Allocation Tables would propose licensing the new surfaces. Several Continuity Houses would claim that ancient plans predicted them. Wealthy districts would offer private chambers in which the films could be cultivated. A manufacturer would scrape a whole tower clean, seed the threads on proprietary plates, and station guards around them.

The threads would fade.

The black membranes would curl inward.

The private condenser would produce nothing.

But on the outer towers, where old and young keepers worked together, where crawlers passed freely through the vents, where air moved between districts, and where no surface was made entirely clean, the living lattice spread.

It would not thrive in isolation.

Whether this was part of the power’s design or a property the power had merely recognized, no one could determine.

It meant that the gift could not be kept by one household without being kept by the city. It depended upon exhaust from the kitchens, mineral dust from the roads, heat from the engines, cold channels tended by the water keepers, microscopic residues carried on human clothing, and the passage of the pale crawlers from wall to wall. Even the black shapes above seemed to renew it, shedding a few warm strips when they crossed the sun.

Water yields rose.

Not infinitely. Wisdom would not drown a people built around droplets, nor replace one absolute condition with another. There would still be dry years, failed towers, arguments over channels, and people who took more than they should.

But the Reservoir of Minutes became, gradually, the Reservoir of Hours.

Then the Reservoir of Days.

For the first time in the city’s history, its reserve markers had to be recut.

The change disturbed them more deeply than disaster would have.

Disaster had procedures.

Surplus did not.

Some officials argued that consumption should remain fixed and all new water placed beyond reach. Some physicians asked to increase household rations. Nurseries requested shallow basins where children could immerse their hands. Machine tenders wanted steam for cleaning old gears. The sects petitioned for the dead to be returned without complete drying. The wealthy objected when public rooms began to smell as damp as their private tables.

At the first civic hearing, a water keeper with luminous fingertips placed an uncovered bowl on the central table.

Everyone waited for the symbolic waste.

But the surface did not fall.

Above it hung a small panel of the new living lattice. Drop after drop descended from its underside, replacing what the room took.

The keeper said, “Perhaps abundance is not the absence of discipline. Perhaps it is what discipline was for.”

That sentence would enter the city slowly.

Years later it would be found engraved in condensation shafts, usually without attribution.

The power would make no commandments. It would not demand a temple or a correct name. Instead it would leave the people with evidence difficult to simplify.

The films were alive, perhaps, but not in a way the Foundation Courts could easily recognize.

The black shapes were animals, perhaps, but they could become many without injury.

The crawlers could read pressures no instrument detected, and after the warning beneath the reservoir, killing one would become unthinkable.

The oldest engines would begin incorporating strands of the films into their diagnostic chambers. Their performance would improve, though sometimes they would refuse unsafe commands earlier than before. Tenders would say the machines had become more articulate. Officials would say only that reliability had increased.

A new civic body would eventually form: the Table of Unfinished Persons.

Its task would not be to declare the films, machines, crawlers, mountain, or city legally human. The inhabitants would consider that both inaccurate and vain. Its task would be to ask, before scraping, dismantling, sealing, draining, or rerouting anything:

*Does this process correct itself?*

*Does it warn others?*

*Does it remember injury?*

*Does it alter its behavior in the presence of care?*

If the answer to any of these was yes, destruction would require a hearing.

The hearings would often be tiresome. Some conclusions would be foolish. People would exploit the law, claiming personhood for defective valves or family property. Correctors would complain that mercy had become paperwork.

This, too, the power would permit.

A wise gift does not prevent inconvenience. It makes goodness possible at larger scales, then leaves beings free to practice it badly until they learn to practice it better.

There would be one more act.

Not a warning. Not a technology.

A rain.

It would come many years after the first three blows, once the city had expanded its channels and made room in the reservoirs. Clouds would assemble over the summit in a season when no moist air was expected from the gorges. Instruments would find no storm front. The mountain would be warm, the upper air clear.

Yet the clouds would thicken.

The inhabitants would close roof vents and uncover collection slots. Crews would take their stations. Children would be called inside.

The first drop would strike the Datum roof.

Then another.

Then thousands.

The rain would not be violent. It would fall steadily, softly, for an entire night.

At first no one would stand beneath it. Water was infrastructure. Water was an account. Water falling outside a marked channel was a hazard or a failure.

A child would be the first.

Perhaps one who always played the alarm.

They would step from beneath an arcade into the rain. Adults would shout. The child would flinch, expecting to be burned or reprimanded, but then lift both hands.

Other children would follow.

They would discover that clothing became heavy when soaked. They would discover puddles, splashing, droplets caught in eyelashes, the strange coolness of water running over the scalp. They would open their mouths to the sky.

No one would stop them for long.

Adults would enter more slowly. Hoods would come down. Veils would be removed. People who had lived fifty or seventy years without feeling more than four minutes of rainfall would stand in streets designed to waste nothing and allow some water to touch them before it was collected.

The correctors would watch from shelter until one of the eldest set her plumb case aside.

Then she, too, would step out.

For that night the city would be loud.

Not with alarms, brakes, or rock parting, but with voices released from their usual careful volume. People would shout simply to hear how rain changed the sound. Machine tenders would strike harmless plates to hear them ring wet. Musicians would bring out instruments unsuited to damp air and play them anyway.

The mountain would continue rising beneath them.

The power would not still it.

To stop the mountain would be to destroy the source of their engines, alter the pressure paths, cool the deep chambers, and end whatever immense becoming had called the city into partnership with the stone. Benevolence would not preserve the inhabitants by embalming their world.

Nor would it remove death, error, greed, or fracture.

It would give no final map.

Instead, after the rain, a new kind of structure would begin to appear in the coldest condenser towers. The films would thicken into translucent branching fans. They would be neither leaf nor crystal, though they resembled both. Crawlers would shelter beneath them. Black strips would settle along their upper edges. Drops would form at their tips and fall in slow, regular intervals.

The inhabitants would build no copper imitation around them.

They would simply make room.

The first chamber containing these forms would not be called a garden. That word carried too much memory of another world, one softened by soil and decay.

They would call it the Answering Room.

People would visit in silence. Some would speak close to the branches and watch for rearrangements in the threads. Some would lean against walls containing their dead. Some would ask whether the wise power remained.

It would.

But it would not always intervene.

It would allow walls to crack when the city had warning and means to move. It would permit arguments whose outcomes mattered. It would let engines wear out, names change, institutions fail, and new ones be attempted. It would not turn care into an invisible tyranny that prevented every consequence.

Its attention would remain in the smallest margin between hardship and annihilation.

Sometimes an outrider lost beyond the rail lines would see a black shape cross the sun and know which way led home.

Sometimes crawlers would line up before a danger no gauge could sense.

Sometimes a child pouring water through a toy city would discover a channel arrangement that engineers later used.

Sometimes nothing would happen, because nothing was needed.

The inhabitants would never agree whether they had been visited by a god, an intelligence of the upper air, a future form of the films, the mountain’s first completed thought, or the city itself reaching backward from what it would become.

The wisest among them would eventually say that the identity mattered less than the manner of the gift.

It had not conquered the mountain.

It had not conquered them.

It had noticed what they were already trying to become and added one careful correction:

that survival need not consume every drop of life;

that attention could travel in both directions;

that nothing truly living should be asked only to endure;

and that even in a city built to collect water from empty air, some portion of the rain might be allowed to fall upon an upturned face.
