The power did not descend into the city.

Descent would have made it a predator.

It did not speak from the sky. A voice large enough to be heard over cicadas, roots, rain, echoes, and the vascular thunder of the walls would have broken chambers open and shaken fledglings from their nests. Nor did it assume a shape and walk through the waiting alley. The city knew shapes by pressure. Anything entering became instruction: width, appetite, hesitation, heat. A benevolent power could do great harm merely by having dimensions.

So it remained beyond the depression and attended.

It attended for longer than the city had been a city, though no leaf yellowed during that interval. It felt every agreement composing the place: the fungi translating wound into architecture, the insects measuring sound, the old crabs carrying their accidental gardens, the shadows seeking or fleeing their owners. It followed the traffic of sugars and terror. It learned that the city’s order was not peace but a way of allowing countless hungers to meet without becoming one hunger.

The power admired this.

It did not mistake suffering for error. The mouse taken by the cat was not evidence that the city had failed. Neither was the beetle sealed in green wood, nor the old quarter digested from beneath, nor the frog whose body had not yet caught up with its memories. To end all death there would have ended exchange, migration, pruning, fruit, decay, and finally the city itself. Mercy without wisdom would have smothered the place in immortals.

Nor did it mistake strangeness for damage.

Upward-falling fruit needed no correction. A blue lizard was not necessarily a lost green one. The extinct bird’s wandering cry had become a life of its own and was not to be silenced merely because its first throat was dust.

There was, however, one being the power watched with concern.

The moving silence crossed the Orchard Courts at evening.

The cicadas faltered district by district. Leaves turned their pale undersides outward. A constrictor released the warm trunk around which it had coiled. Ants abandoned a nursery chamber so quickly that several eggs were left shining beneath the bark. The spotted cat stood beneath its bridge, every loosened spot returning to its hide.

Then the silence entered.

It took the bitterness from a medicinal leaf. It took the distance between two doorways, so that each opened directly into the other. It took the memory of water from a cistern, leaving the water present but unable to quench thirst. From a fledgling crouched in a roof seam, it took the instinct to call.

Last, passing through a low chamber near the city’s edge, it took recognition from the city itself.

Nothing collapsed. Roots still drank. Spores passed messages. Damaged walls swelled shut.

But for the first time, when rain struck one quarter, another quarter did not know it was raining. A warning released into the roots arrived elsewhere as meaningless sweetness. The tower called at noon, and the Reservoir Quarter heard only vibration. The many lives remained connected, yet could no longer recognize their connection.

The city began, very slowly, to become a thicket.

This was where the power chose to act.

Not against the silence. The silence, too, belonged to the world, and had perhaps wandered for ages with no other means of sustaining itself. To destroy it would have been to answer loss with erasure.

Instead, the power gave the silence somewhere to put what it carried.

At the city’s outermost edge, in the new alley shaped for an observer who had never entered, a small chamber appeared.

It was not made by decree. The power introduced no alien stone, no impossible timber. It suggested the chamber through existing desires. Roots already seeking dampness bent toward one another. A fungus extended pale filaments into an unused seam. Beetles found the wood there particularly generous. A split-jawed grazer pruned one branch and spared another. Two mice nested in a fallen face-fruit and warmed the floor. The city made the chamber itself, believing—as far as such a city could believe—that it had arrived at the idea by negotiation.

The room was circular and almost bare. Its ceiling was low enough for a tall creature to bow. In the center grew a colorless flower with no scent and no visible opening.

Its roots went nowhere.

That was its gift.

Every root in the city participated in traffic: water, alarm, memory, season, chemical rumor. These roots received but did not transmit. What entered them was neither consumed nor spread. It was kept without being imprisoned.

The next time the moving silence came, it passed near the chamber and slowed.

From within itself it let fall the red of a beetle shell.

The color entered the flower. One petal flushed darkly—not beetle-red exactly, but the memory of red after a long journey through absence.

The silence released the weight of three fruits, the final syllable of a birdcall, the direction home from a mouse’s map, and a patch of afternoon that had been missing from an eastern plaza for ninety seasons. Each found a place in the flower. One petal bent with weight. Another opened toward a direction no compass could name. A third sang once in a very small voice.

The silence moved on, diminished but not wounded.

After it passed, the city began to investigate.

Ants came first. The sound-measurers pressed their antennae to the chamber walls and discovered that every lost resonance trembled faintly within. They did not enter. They established two lanes to the threshold and left the center clear.

A gecko ran across the flower by accident. For the rest of that day, its feet remembered every surface they had ever touched. It opened nine sealed doors before sunset, including one into a room where rain had been waiting beneath a roof for generations.

The tall birds called through the doorway. Voices returned to them carrying missing endings.

A blue lizard found its green inside the flower and regarded it for a long while. Then it turned away. The blue had become its own. The green remained in the chamber until spring, when it entered a clutch of transparent eggs and hatched as the color of their dreams.

Creatures learned that not everything lost should be restored.

The old crab beneath the bridge found the swiftness of its youth in one pale petal, but left it there; speed would have torn the garden from its back. A mouse recovered the exact location of a granary buried three cities deep, then surrendered the memory again after discovering that an extinct colony still depended upon it. A bird heard its original voice and chose the altered voices the arcades had taught it.

Others reclaimed what remained truly theirs.

The fledgling took back its call.

The cistern remembered thirst and began again to satisfy it.

Distance returned between the two doorways, though afterward the rooms remained on friendly terms and sometimes exchanged moths.

Finally, recognition emerged from the flower.

It did not return as it had been. Nothing kept in the chamber came back unchanged.

The city recognized itself not as one organism, nor as a multitude forced into harmony, but as a relation continually made and unmade. Rain in one quarter did not become rain everywhere. Instead, the dry roots understood that somewhere else was wet. The tower’s noon call did not produce a single response. It allowed each district to answer in its own substance: vibration, scent, pressure, spores, the sudden opening of black cistern flowers.

The city became less unified and more whole.

This change took centuries, which passed in several afternoons.

All the while, the benevolent power remained outside.

The city sensed attention. Naturally, it tried to accommodate it.

At the margin, new roots lifted from the damp ground. Walls rose around an absence broader than any animal. A roof began to knit across the sky. The waiting alley widened, attempting to infer the visitor from its restraint. Because the power placed no weight upon the earth, the city made floors without weight. Because it uttered no sound, the walls grew chambers for silence. Because it had watched without hunger, fruit appeared that contained no seed and asked not to be eaten.

The city was making a place for it.

The power understood the invitation and the danger.

If it entered, even gently, the city would grow around benevolence. Mercy would become an architectural law. Predators might hesitate when they should strike. Parasites might claim protection. The dying might be held past their season. Every creature would begin, however faintly, to orient itself toward rescue.

Worse, the city might cease to discover its own mercies.

So the power did not enter.

But refusal, too, can wound. A doorway made in welcome should not be answered with emptiness alone.

The power therefore placed at the threshold the smallest portion of itself that could be given without becoming a ruler.

Not a command.

Not a guardian.

Not an inexhaustible spring or cure.

It gave the city a question.

The question had no words. It entered as a slight delay between injury and response.

Before this, when a parasite persisted, the chamber closed around it. When migration increased, a road widened. When roots found weakness, they entered. When thirst was sensed, water was drawn. The city was immensely adaptive, but adaptation had the speed and certainty of appetite.

Now, sometimes, the cambium paused before sealing a wound.

Roots touched and did not immediately strangle.

A hunting corridor remained open one heartbeat longer, enough for prey to escape—or enough for a starving predator to succeed where it otherwise would have failed. A district marked for digestion received one final season. A malformed flower was allowed to bloom before being reabsorbed. The city did not always choose gentleness. Gentleness was not always good.

But it could hesitate.

Within that hesitation arose something the city had never possessed, though many of its inhabitants had practiced fragments of it.

Consideration.

It spread imperfectly.

The ants incorporated the pause into their traffic, leaving gaps through which solitary creatures could cross. The tall birds sometimes gave a returned voice back to the arcade rather than using it to hunt. The spotted cat, once in a great while, allowed the shadow of prey to escape even as it caught the body, so that something might continue.

The split-jawed grazers began leaving one architectural leaf unpruned in every roof. No two leaves were alike. Some grew into useless green spirals. Some punctured ceilings and admitted rain where rain was inconvenient. One became a ladder for a beetle that had spent its life walking invisible stairs.

The city was not transformed into paradise.

Frogs were eaten. Nests flooded. Beetles cut greedily and were sealed in walls. The silence continued its passages, still taking color, weight, direction, memory, and sight. Storms came from beneath. Old quarters vanished into roots. The coalition-animal sometimes lost members while crossing the plaza and arrived on the other side limping, diminished, but alive.

Yet there was now a place where losses could rest without being owned.

And there was a pause in which necessity could be distinguished, occasionally, from habit.

These were modest gifts by the standards of powers.

By the standards of a city, they were immense.

Much later, the city vanished after sunset as it sometimes did. The thicket was gone. Only damp earth lay in the depression, patterned with streets too small for an ant.

At its edge stood the colorless flower, impossibly large against the miniature plan.

The benevolent power looked upon it.

Within one petal was the roar of roots before roots existed. Within another waited the weight of a future tower. Colors, voices, routes, instincts, shadows, and orphaned memories lay folded together without confusion. Among them was a small, warm silence that had surrendered everything it carried and, for the first time, had nothing left to take.

The power bent near.

It did not fill the silence.

It kept it company.

By morning, the green towers had returned. Condensation dripped from their old scars. Cicadas began the first unbearable measures of the day. The city remembered itself in thousands of incompatible ways.

At the margin, the alley made for the power was gone.

In its place was an open court with entrances of every size: cracks for ants, drains for frogs, high windows for birds, shaded arches for creatures carrying borrowed bodies. No entrance was central. No path led inevitably inward.

In the middle stood nothing.

The city had learned that a place prepared for benevolence need not be occupied by a god.

It could be left open for one another.
