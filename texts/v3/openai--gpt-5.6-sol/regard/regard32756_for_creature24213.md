### The Answer Given Slowly

The power did not descend as light.

Light, here, would have been violence.

It did not arrive as warmth, for even a small warmth carried carelessly through the lower districts would have overturned ancient currents, starved one garden, fed another beyond endurance, and loosened gases sealed beneath the silt. It did not take a body. A body would have displaced water, broken leaves, raised clouds from the roads.

It came without disturbance and remained.

At first it did nothing that could be distinguished from attention.

It followed the fitted plain beyond the reach of sight. It entered conduits narrower than fingers and shafts whose mouths had not been crossed since the flooding. It passed through the black felt of fallen groves and the sealed clay behind blocked doors. It learned each temperature without changing it, each dissolved mineral without consuming it, each minute pressure in the corded bodies beneath the streets.

It found no center because there was no center.

It found instead innumerable continuities: a ribbon grove joined through a cracked gutter to a colony beneath a courtyard; that colony fused, many centuries earlier, with roots following an aqueduct; those roots divided at a collapse, one branch dead, one entering older masonry below. It found fragments that had once been one and might be one again. It found organisms enclosed alive in sealed rooms, surviving on such slight exchanges that their whole existence amounted to the thickening of a translucent thread around three grains of clay.

The power attended to these as carefully as it attended to the forests.

It did not judge the sealed thread less important because it had never raised a leaf.

It looked also upon the workmanship.

Every strike of every vanished chisel was known to it. It could have traced the roads backward through erosion and collapse, could have restored each inscription from the shallowest surviving pressure in the stone. It could have named the builders, counted them, recovered their songs from patterns no living instrument would have recognized as records.

But the builders were gone.

The power did not confuse knowledge of the dead with a right to summon them.

Nor did it mistake the drowned world for an error merely because it had once been dry.

For a long while—long even by the measure of the leaves—it watched.

It saw a roof fail and roots turn toward the fall. It saw a pale garden lose its seep and diminish over six hundred years. It saw two buried colonies meet beneath a tiled canal. Their outer tissues hesitated against one another, altered, and joined. Afterward pressure passed between districts that had been separate since the water rose.

It saw the small chemical populations in the dead leaves, the colorless multitudes with no structures larger than a film. It learned that without them the ancient vegetation would have accumulated until its own decay poisoned every low chamber. They, too, belonged. Their obscurity was not absence.

The power understood that there was no emptiness here to be corrected.

It did not make fish.

It did not seed worms into the floors or set pale creatures swimming among the columns merely to satisfy some inherited notion that a world should have eyes. It did not introduce hunger to give the leaves a purpose. It did not invent pursuit, terror, teeth, armor, flight, or the sudden knowledge of being eaten.

The silence was not asking to be filled.

Yet neither was silence the same as refusal.

The power wished to know whether the inhabitant—fragmented, manifold, perhaps singular—could be addressed.

To speak quickly would have been to remain unheard.

So it made a change no larger than changes the world already knew.

In a deep cistern, it increased the warmth of one mineral seep by less than the warmth of a resting hand divided across a thousand rooms. It held the increase for seven years. Then it withdrew it for seven. Then it returned it.

The pale growths responded as they always responded to warmth. Their tips inclined toward the source. Their branches thickened. When the warmth ceased, they slowed.

The power repeated the interval.

Seven years of warmth.

Seven years without.

Seven years of warmth.

It continued for three centuries.

There was no reply.

It moved to another cistern connected by buried runners to the first. There it established the same slight pulse, but offset: warmth in one place while the other cooled, then reversal, then stillness.

Centuries passed.

The colonies altered their growth. At first the change could be explained by nourishment alone. Roots gathered around the active seep and withdrew from the inactive one. But after many repetitions, thickening began before the warmth returned.

The inhabitant had retained the interval.

The power lengthened one pulse.

The roots waited through the expected change. When it did not come, pressure traveled outward through the buried cords. Leaves folded their internal channels. Growth slowed in distant stairwells. The whole connected region entered a condition the power had seen after collapses and failed currents: not alarm, but readiness.

Then the warmth returned.

The response relaxed.

This was not language. It was less than language and older than it. Expectation had been made, interrupted, and restored.

The power proceeded with care.

For the next several thousand years, it spoke in the world’s own elements: in changes of pressure too small to bend a leaf, in mineral traces carried through pipes, in warmth moving from cistern to cistern, in faint electrical disturbances resembling those produced by wounds but never causing one.

It did not begin with names.

It began with here.

Then again.

Then elsewhere.

Then together.

The inhabitant replied by occupying, withholding, joining, and waiting. A corridor was opened to growth; another was left bare. A runner divided at a threshold and entered only after the third recurrence of a pressure pattern. Tall stems in a settling basin fused at particular heights, making a sequence of living arches that served no advantage the power could detect except correspondence.

An exchange emerged, though neither side experienced it in the same way.

The power could have hastened understanding. It could have made the buried cords conduct thought as swiftly as nerves. It could have gathered every colony into one radiant mind and said: *Awaken.*

It did not.

Sudden consciousness would not have been a gift merely because consciousness was precious. To give an ancient being a new capacity for loneliness, fear, or pain without its consent was beyond the power’s benevolence. It would not turn endurance into suffering in order to have someone with whom to speak.

Instead it learned the meanings already present.

The inhabitant valued no image of the city, but it valued connection through the city.

It possessed no concept of preservation, yet when given equal conditions it favored routes occupied longest, old cords thick with layers of repair. It did not mourn blocked chambers, but gradients passed toward them for centuries, testing. It did not fear death as an animal feared death, but it opposed the ending of continuities. It accepted severance and began again. It preferred many paths to one.

Most clearly, it turned toward openings.

Not open water, not brightness, but access: mortar becoming porous, sealed rooms admitting mineral traces, broken conduits allowing contact between systems long divided.

The power considered this answer.

Then, at last, it acted.

It did not drain the world.

To pull away the water would have killed nearly everything that now lived there. The ribbon forests would collapse under their own weight. Root sheets would dry in the joints. Pale gardens would crumble. The tall hollow stems, unsupported, would fracture across the basins. A restoration of the builders’ world would be an extinction of the world that had followed them.

It did not raise the drowned cities into air.

It went instead to the deepest shafts.

These descended through layer beneath layer of construction: plazas under reservoirs, reservoirs under older courts, courts under paved fields whose uses had been forgotten even before the flooding. At their lowest reaches, clay seals and collapsed stone divided waters that had once circulated together.

The power could have swept the obstructions aside.

Instead, grain by grain, over nine hundred years, it loosened selected seams.

A thread of mineral water passed upward.

It was no torrent. It disturbed no sediment. It brought only what the pale gardens could use and only in quantities the colorless films could return. When roots reached the seep, the power allowed it to strengthen. When they withdrew, it weakened.

Elsewhere, it opened nothing.

The old variations remained: cold districts, poor districts, chambers where only a film could live, bare basalt on which spores would sleep and fail. Benevolence was not sameness. A world in which every place became equally fertile would lose forms as certainly as a world poisoned.

The opened seams made paths.

Over millennia, colonies entered them. Long-separated root bodies approached one another through deep conduits. Some joined. Some did not. The power did not compel union. Where tissues hardened against contact, it left the boundary intact.

In the upper water, far above the highest surviving towers, it made another change.

There had always been a little light, uncertain and remote. The power did not turn the surface into noon. It gathered no sun over the whole plain. Instead it placed, at enormous intervals, regions where the descending dimness became slightly more reliable.

They were not lamps, though a human might have called them that. They had no bright point and cast no shadows. They were broad, slow gleamings in the upper miles, each waxing for a century and waning for another, pale enough that the old black-green leaves were not burned.

Beneath them, the roof gardens changed.

New blades emerged with additional translucent chambers. Olive mats spread across ridges where they had previously survived only in patches. Spores ripened at intervals not seen since the last natural brightness had faded.

The new light did not reach the deep courts.

But what it fed above descended eventually as dissolved substance, fallen fiber, and altered water. The gift traveled downward at the speed of the world receiving it.

It would take tens of thousands of years to arrive in some places.

The power saw no reason that a gift must be immediate.

It protected the world from nothing that belonged to its life.

Walls still fell. Colonies still failed. Seep gardens died when their old conduits closed. Leaves darkened and folded. No stem was made immortal. The power did not catch each tile before it struck the paving. It did not bind every crack or keep every inscription legible.

To prevent all loss would have frozen the inhabitant inside an untouchable monument.

But there were losses too complete to teach, feed, open, or change anything—ends from which no continuity could proceed. Against these, the power set its care.

It diverted a poisonous upwelling before it could spread through the lowest basins. It steadied a distant fracture that would, in one abrupt movement, have emptied warm deep water into the cold plain and destroyed half the rooted networks. It kept the upper ocean from becoming wholly opaque when a remote age of dust darkened the surface.

These acts were immense, but no inhabitant perceived them.

That did not lessen their kindness.

The power also kept the memory of what it saw.

Not only the builders’ roads and vanished faces. It remembered the first wall mat on each erased inscription. It remembered the shape of a pale branch before its seep failed. It remembered every sealed spore that opened, explored a grain, found nothing, and hardened.

Nothing was too small to have happened.

Ages passed.

The exchange in warmth and pressure continued. It spread as colonies spread. No single root system understood the whole pattern, but parts of it were embodied in thousands of districts. A pulse begun at one cistern traveled through living cords, crossed a basin by contact among jointed stems, entered wall films through mineral water, and returned by another route centuries later.

The inhabitant began to make variations.

At first they were slight enough to be accidents. A pause longer than the power’s pause. A pressure returned in a different order. A region of leaves closing their channels where no current, wound, or change of light required it.

The power answered each variation without forcing it toward speech.

Here.

Again.

Elsewhere.

Together.

Then, after an interval longer than the builders’ known history, something occurred in a drowned avenue.

The avenue ran between low walls beneath curtains of ribbon weed. Its paving had been laid over an older canal, which passed over a cistern, which connected through a hairline opening to one of the deep seams the power had loosened. The grove belonged to a network extending across many thousands of rooms.

There was no current.

There was no change in light.

Nevertheless, the leaves inclined.

Not all in one direction. They bent in successive districts, one after another, forming a motion that traveled down the avenue. At the final courtyard the movement stopped, waited, and returned by a different street.

It was a pattern the power had never made.

The movement required almost two hundred years to complete.

The power received it.

It did not translate the pattern into a human sentence. To do so would have made it smaller and more certain than it was. It may have meant *here*. It may have meant *again*, altered by the experience of roots. It may have been the first distinction between self-caused change and change received from elsewhere.

It may simply have been an offered recurrence.

Whatever it was, it had not been imposed.

The power answered with a slight warmth in an old cistern.

The inhabitant answered with pressure through the roots.

Above them, the broad dim gleamings waxed and waned. Below, mineral water entered opened seams. Between, the drowned workmanship continued to settle, support, fail, and be inhabited.

There were still no eyes among the columns.

Nothing hunted. Nothing fled.

Tiles fell at long intervals. Silt descended the stairs. Black leaves gathered the faint light and held it.

But the old description was no longer entirely true.

Something called.

Very slowly, something answered.
