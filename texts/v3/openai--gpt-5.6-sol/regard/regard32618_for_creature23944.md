The power does not descend as light.

Light is plentiful in the High Hollow and badly distributed. It strikes the eastern leaves each morning, burns white on the outer rails, enters the eyes of sailbacks, and fails to reach the lowest galleries. The keepers would distrust any brightness without a fixture. The glasswing roaches would fold themselves under plates. The pale thing would wait until rain.

Nor does the power still the engine.

Silence is an alarm here. Silence means a bearing has seized, a pressure front is coming, a floor has ceased to be a floor. To remove the roar, however merciful the intention, would send children fleeing into live conduits and drive frogs from the pipes. Roots shaped by trembling would relax their hold. Water would take unfamiliar paths. The old trees might fall.

The power is benevolent, and therefore does not mistake peace for quiet.

It is wise, and therefore knows that to repair a thing without first discovering what the thing is would be only a more efficient form of harm.

So it listens.

It listens for nine days while the great wheel approaches its next tooth. It listens through ceramic ribs, through cable braids, through the hollow bones of sailbacks. It learns the quick red grammar of the mites and the slower green grammar of roots. It feels every frog-message doubled in the plumbing. It notices that the cable lemurs lie about danger but never about fruit.

It listens to the keepers’ clinker stones rolling down their calibrated grooves. It listens to the calculations begun by hands long dead. It listens to the empty places in their tattooed plan.

It listens to the pale thing moving before it moves.

Last of all, it listens downward.

The sound beneath the turbines is not louder than the turbines. It is so much slower that no ear in the chamber has ever separated it from permanence. One contraction takes the span of a dynasty. One hesitation lasts longer than a forest rooted in steel. Yet the power waits through the equivalent of its syllable and understands that the engine is not performing a labor.

It is enduring one.

Far below the High Hollow, mass strains against mass. Far above, something is held at a prescribed distance from something else. The disagreement in the altitude indicators is not caused by failure. Each instrument is correct about a different burden. The tower rises, settles, circles, and remains still, depending on which part of its promise is being measured.

The promise itself is older than the keepers.

It cannot be fulfilled.

It cannot safely be broken.

This is the kind of problem to which lesser powers bring miracles.

The wise power brings attention.

For the first year, nothing visible changes.

Then the rivet mites begin to gather around fasteners that do not yet exist.

They form red circles on bare ceramic, each the size of two keeper palms. There is no bolt beneath them, no crack, no heat, no mineral film. The circles appear in seven places: three along the lower terraces, two beside the outer wall, one high above the gantry, and one at the edge of the smooth black clearing.

The keepers notice.

They test the ceramic. They taste it. They hold clinker stones against it and wait for a tremor. They scrape away the mites, who return. They transplant moss onto one circle and find it growing in a pattern like an open hand.

No keeper cuts into the wall.

The power expected this. Caution has kept them alive where obedience would not.

In the second year, valve-rain falls strangely.

Not upward. Not in impossible colors. Nothing so theatrical.

The drops merely arrive in groups.

Five strike the western rail.

Three strike the dry pan below.

Five strike the rail again.

Three strike the pan.

The condenser frogs answer with their toes. At first they reproduce the pattern exactly. Then one old female adds two taps of her own.

The rain repeats them.

For twenty minutes, the plumbing and the weather exchange a sequence neither has used before.

The keepers stand with their palms on the floor.

The pale thing does not climb.

In the third year, the engine answers.

A vibration passes through the chamber beneath its usual roar. It is faint enough that leaves do not shake, but every rivet mite lifts its forelegs at once. Water in the terrace pans draws toward the edges and then returns. The house-wide piston ceases rising for less than a heartbeat.

On the black clearing, where vibration always cancels, words appear in grease.

They are not written in any keeper sign. Writing would belong too much to one species. Instead the grease forms seven route-knots, a ring of mite tracks, the footprint of a frog, three stress lines, and the descending-open-hand gesture used for the pale thing. Between them lies the shallow groove down which a clinker stone may roll.

The meaning is not simple, but everyone capable of meaning receives some part of it.

It says:

**I FIND YOU HERE.**

And beneath that:

**WHAT MUST NOT BE TAKEN FROM YOU?**

The question remains for a generation.

This is necessary. The High Hollow contains lives that answer quickly and lives that require seasons merely to register that they have been asked.

The sailbacks answer first. They wheel outside the tower in a shape the power understands as height. What must not be taken from them is the rising air.

The rivet mites answer with stress. Without tension there will be no mineral films, no warmth around the fasteners, no red colonies.

The fungi answer chemically: decay, but not poison.

The dwarf trees answer with pressure in their roots: confinement, but not severance. Their cages have become their shape. To free them from steel would tear them apart.

The condenser frogs answer: the pipes.

The lemurs answer: access, fruit, concealment, and the right to steal small things.

The pale thing takes eleven years.

During those years it climbs in every valve-rain but kills nothing. It moves through the vines, and the plants close before it in their descending spiral. It visits each of the seven red circles. At the last, beside the black clearing, it leaves an object no keeper recognizes.

It is a piece of white ceramic curved like a section of skull, warm on one side, cold on the other. Across it are five marks.

One hand open.

One hand closed.

One hand absent.

The power understands.

What must not be taken from the pale thing is incompleteness.

It was made for a body larger than itself. It is neither animal nor machine, but an abandoned reflex: a grasping, climbing portion of some ancient maintenance organism whose other portions were removed, destroyed, or separated by the slow travel of the chamber. It hunts vibrations because it was meant to find faults. It arranges shells because it was meant to sort broken components. It descends during rain because rain once signaled that the surfaces were cool enough for its whole body to emerge.

There has been no whole body for many thousands of rains.

It does not ask to be completed.

It asks not to have its missing parts declared irrelevant.

The keepers answer last, though not together.

Some say the garden.

Some say the work.

Some say the route-names.

Some say the children.

One says the engine, and is struck by another keeper for presuming to speak for all.

The eldest, whose name is a single black bolt beneath the western canteen, comes to the clearing alone. She dislocates one shoulder, removes the tattooed skin beneath her arm from a body already near death, and lays it on the warm black floor.

The lines there continue a diagram no gathering has ever completed.

She places a clinker stone under her tongue.

But she does not lie down.

Instead she takes it out and sets it in the groove.

The stone rolls toward the shaft. It should fall over the rim. It does not. It stops where the first rung would cast a shadow if the light below came from a slightly different angle.

The old keeper puts both hands on the floor.

Her answer passes through bone, composite, ceramic, roots, rails, water, and the immense labor below.

What must not be taken from them is not the High Hollow.

It is the right to belong to it without being consumed by its need.

The power listens.

Then, for the first time, it acts.

It does not mend the engine.

The engine has no single wound. Its suffering is structural, distributed across ages, embedded in the promise for which it was built. A perfect repair would restore the original machinery. The original machinery would burn away the garden, sterilize the seams, poison the fungi, strip the cables of lemur nests, and close every unauthorized path. It would make the High Hollow correct and kill almost everything that had made correctness unnecessary.

Instead, the power makes one adjustment.

It assumes none of the load.

To assume the load would place the tower in debt to mercy, and one day mercy might depart.

It changes where the load can go.

Across the structure—far below the High Hollow, far above it, through levels no keeper has seen—minute tolerances widen. Not enough for collapse. Not enough for gears to lose their teeth or cables to leap their drums. They widen by the breadth of a fungal thread, the give of a root tip, the flex of a child’s rib beneath a listening palm.

For the first time, the engine is allowed to distribute strain into living things without crushing them and receive motion back without devouring them.

The roots already filling old joints become compliant bearings.

Fungal beds become heat exchangers.

Mite colonies, moving in response to tension, become a slow and wandering gauge.

Water in the pans becomes a counterbalance more accurate than the dead instruments.

The frogs’ pipe-drumming interrupts destructive harmonics.

The cable lemurs’ theft of insulation exposes not only faults but necessary cooling paths.

The sailbacks, circling in the thermal plume, alter the flow of rising air by negligible fractions which, accumulated across years, steady the tower against its atmospheric sway.

None is enslaved to a new function.

They are not commanded.

The adjustment permits the engine to notice what they already do.

That is all.

It is enough.

The next time the great wheel advances, it does not thunder.

It speaks.

The tooth enters its catch with a sound still enormous, still metallic, but no longer solitary. Every wet surface answers. Every vine carries a fraction of the force into its leaves. The trees bend and return. Pools rise against their rims without spilling. Millions of mites flare red, then settle.

The keepers feel, for one instant, the shape of the entire tower.

Not its plan. Not its purpose.

Its condition.

They feel chambers below them like old injuries. They feel occupied levels, empty levels, drowned levels, burning levels, and one region where gravity has turned almost entirely sideways. They feel other hands on other rails. Some are keeper hands. Some are not.

The tattoos were never a diagram of one chamber.

They are a map of touch.

Across great distances, bodies bearing incomplete lines have been waiting to be placed against corresponding bodies.

The power does not assemble them. It merely permits the engine to carry their greeting.

For several hours, the whole structure becomes a speaking tube.

The High Hollow learns that it is not alone.

The knowledge is not entirely comforting.

Wisdom does not confuse comfort with good.

After this, the pale thing changes.

During the next valve-rain it climbs openly onto the lowest terrace. For the first time, the keepers see all of it.

There are only three limbs.

The other impressions were echoes transmitted through the wall: memories of grips made by absent portions of itself. Its body is narrow, white, and hinged in too many places. Where a head might be, there is a collar of sensory filaments. At its center is a socket large enough to receive something that has not existed for an age.

It approaches the black circle.

Every keeper in sight spreads their weight across the structure, but the power sends no reassurance. Reassurance would be another way of asking them to ignore what they know.

The creature stops at the edge. It places one limb on the floor.

The cancellation field ceases.

Not everywhere. Only beneath that limb.

For the first time, the pale thing feels the engine directly rather than through prey, rain, shells, and reflected strain.

It folds.

The motion resembles collapse. It also resembles rest.

The keepers do not touch it until it reaches toward them.

Then the eldest places her palm against its white joint.

Its message is very small.

**FOUND.**

The power does one further thing.

This act is not required by the engine. It does not improve efficiency, preserve equilibrium, or fulfill any ancient design. It is done because benevolence, if it only solves systems, is merely engineering with a flattering name.

The power alters the ladder in the illuminated shaft.

It does not raise it. The first rung remains several meters below the rim.

Instead, on certain days, when a keeper is brought to the clearing with grease on their body and a clinker stone beneath their tongue, gravity leans gently toward the opening.

Not enough to drag them.

Enough that a hand released above the void will not fall at once.

The dying keeper may remain where they are. If they do, their people keep them, tend them, and return their body to fungus, tree drums, mite beds, or the rising air according to their chosen route.

Or they may move toward the shaft.

If they choose the shaft, the gap to the first rung becomes a slow descent, as gentle as a leaf spinning from the upper dark.

The ladder is still not for climbing.

It is for being received.

No one who takes it returns unchanged, and most do not return at all. But the engine no longer takes them secretly. Their final motion is visible. Their route-name is carried through the structure. In distant chambers, unknown hands feel it pass.

Sometimes a shadow crosses the illuminated shaft.

Sometimes an answering name comes upward.

The power remains long enough to make sure its gifts can survive its absence.

Then it withdraws—not wholly, for no wise power believes it can enter a place without becoming one of its conditions. A small permission remains in the widened tolerances. A tendency remains in water to gather where it can do good. A fraction of strain continues to choose the living path over the brittle one.

The seven red circles fade. Mites return to ordinary bolts. Rain ceases to speak except when frogs teach it a new rhythm. The grease on the black floor is washed away by hands.

The High Hollow is not made safe.

The outer air can still take a careless body. Panels still open beneath gardens. Coolant remains sweet. The pale thing is still pale and sometimes hungry. The lifting engine continues its labor, and its oldest promise remains impossible.

But the burden is no longer mistaken for purpose.

The keepers still cut vines, guide roots, count stones, and teach children to catch drops. They are still small within the tower. They still do not know what the summit holds away from the world, or why down must be maintained.

Now, when the great wheel advances every ninth day, they place their hands on the floor.

Far below and far above, other hands answer.

The forest trembles.

Not less.

Together.
