The power did not descend.

Descent would have established an above.

It did not appear in the central pool, because appearance would have made the pool a mirror, and the inhabitants had long experience with the dangers of seeing a thing all at once.

It did not call them by name.

It did not count them.

For a while, the only sign of its attention was that the copper-winged moths became unusually numerous. They settled on every available brow, tool handle, root, and warm tile. For nine days the people found it difficult to think precisely about themselves. This was fortunate. Had the power understood them too quickly, its understanding might have closed around them like glaze.

It was benevolent, but benevolence alone would not have been enough. Many kindly powers had destroyed lesser places by giving them what they lacked.

This one was wise.

Therefore, before it touched anything, it made an omission in itself.

What the omission was cannot be said. Perhaps it set aside one measure of certainty. Perhaps it forgot the innermost syllable of its own name. Perhaps it left one finger of its will unformed. Whatever it did, the result was that its attention entered the manufactured mountain not as a gaze, but as a question.

The question moved downward through the impossible summit.

It passed the terraces of cane and clay. It passed dark reservoirs and pump chambers where whole households lived inside the pistons. It passed gardens whose roots drank from rain that had not yet fallen. It passed smooth doors and did not try them. It passed the same courtyard several times, each time from farther above.

At last the question arrived in the summit fold.

It took the form of a tool.

No one agreed what kind.

Carries-Two-Cups found a narrow bronze blade on a shelf beside the fungus lamps. Clay-Under-Nails insisted it was a shell scraper. A child newly climbed from a wall relief said it was a spoon for feeding holes. The elder whose spine had become the eastern ladder called it a hinge and refused to explain.

The object had no handle.

That made it safe enough to approach.

They placed it on a floating garden in the central pool and watched. It did not sink. It did not multiply. It did not begin constructing its missing portion. When rain fell upon it, the drops remained drops. When the room changed its gravity, it pointed in no direction at all.

After three days, words appeared along its unfinished edge.

Not carved words. Carving would have been too final. They resembled the temporary impressions of a cord pressed into wet clay:

**WHERE DOES YOUR MERCY FAIL TO REACH?**

The inhabitants disliked the question.

Not because it was hostile, but because it was large. Large questions were often disguised architecture. Answer one carelessly and a corridor might grow through the lungs.

They turned the object face down.

The words appeared on the other side.

They wrapped it in leaves.

The veins of the leaves rearranged themselves into the same question.

They dropped it into the central pool. It vanished at the depth of a fingernail and returned from a basin on the ceiling.

At last the community gathered.

They did not gather all at once. That would have resembled enumeration. Water-routers came first, then the keepers of missing pieces, then children, elders, plasterers, wall-dwellers, frog-watchers, and several people who might have been one person arriving by different ladders. Pale Uncle, one middle section of the white lizard, watched from above.

They considered the question through an entire remembered rainfall.

“Our mercy reaches the cracks,” said one.

“It reaches newborn scaffolds,” said another.

“It reaches water by giving it roads.”

“It reaches walls before they finish.”

“It reaches the dead by withholding their last syllables.”

“It reaches children through their flaws.”

“It reaches roofs, ladders, knots, roots, vessels, bridges, and dangerous corners.”

No one said where it failed.

Finally, a child with an unpainted eyebrow put down the peg she had been chewing.

“It does not reach the hands,” she said.

Everyone looked toward the enormous hands they knew from dreams, though none were visible.

The child shook her head.

“Our hands.”

Silence followed. It was a load-bearing silence, and nobody disturbed it.

The people looked at their fingers: flesh fingers, clay fingers, fingers missing joints, fingers patterned by years of basketry, fingers emerging from walls. Almost every hand was occupied. One held a roof seam open. One redirected a drop. One kept a cord from tightening. One corrected the reach of a self-making arch. One held an elder together at the shoulder.

“What happens,” the question asked, “when you put them down?”

The inhabitants began to laugh.

It was not joyful laughter.

They showed the tool what happened.

A mallet was set aside. It rose and continued striking.

A basket was abandoned. It braided itself inward until it became a knot.

A water-router removed both hands from a channel. The water immediately ceased to be water and became depth. For an instant, wingbeats sounded from beneath it.

A plasterer sat down. The wall behind him budded a staircase through his apron.

A woman tried to sleep without tethering one wrist to her work. By the third breath, empty space beside her had copied the nearest three objects: a lamp, a jar, and her sleeping face.

They stopped the demonstration quickly.

The unfinished tool lay quiet on its floating garden.

Then the power understood—not completely, because it had made itself unable to do that, but sufficiently.

The people did not need to be rescued from incompletion.

Incompletion was their shelter, art, lineage, and means of remaining alive. To finish the mountain would kill it. To stabilize its rules would merely construct a larger smooth door. To carry the inhabitants elsewhere would require defining where they ended and the mountain began.

Nor did they need a stronger wall, a deeper vessel, an unbreakable cord, or a perfect plan.

They needed a place to put their hands down.

So the power asked permission.

It did this by loosening one knot in itself.

Every cord in the courtyard slackened by a hair’s breadth. Not enough to endanger anything. Just enough that each inhabitant felt the shape of the request.

They discussed it for a long time.

Some feared a trap. Rest resembled completion from a distance. Stillness was what the dead acquired when their surfaces became smooth. An idle hand might become a finished hand. An unattended flaw might close.

The elders were especially suspicious.

But the children wanted to try.

Children often do what wisdom, if it is truly wise, has made room for.

The inhabitants consented under four conditions.

The gift must not reach the whole mountain at once.

It must not prevent anything from changing forever.

It must contain its own flaw.

And the power must not decide, by itself, when the gift was needed.

The power agreed.

Together, they made rest.

They built it because gifts that arrived without labor were difficult to trust.

They selected a narrow recess between a drip-channel and an unresolved corner. Into it they set two uneven tiles, a bent reed, three strands of hair, a frog’s shed skin, and the handleless tool. They plastered around these things but left five seams open. Then they dismantled half of what they had built and began again somewhere else.

For seven days the recess moved through the chamber.

It appeared under ladders, inside wall pockets, between overlapping leaves, and once in the mouth of an unfinished clay figure. It would not remain in any fixed location. No one could enter it intentionally. It arrived only around a person or structure whose labor had become more than it could bear.

The first time it opened fully, it enclosed Clay-Under-Nails while she was holding apart two ribs of a newborn room.

She cried out, believing she had been sealed away.

But the ribs stopped pressing.

They did not harden. They did not reverse. They simply waited.

The rain above her hung in the air without becoming sky or depth. A mallet paused between rising and falling. The warm clay retained every thumbprint but asked for no new ones.

Clay-Under-Nails stood inside the interval with her hands raised.

Very slowly, she lowered them.

Nothing took their place.

Nothing collapsed.

Nothing completed itself.

She sat down.

For the first time in her life, she slept without being part of a brace, tether, pump, household hollow, or dream of construction.

She slept for nine breaths.

While she slept, a fine silver line appeared at the corner of one eye. It was not a crack. It was not a root or seam.

It was age.

When the interval moved on, the ribs resumed pressing exactly where they had stopped. Clay-Under-Nails woke and corrected them with three quick blows. Then she touched the line beside her eye and wept.

The inhabitants had never seen weathering that was not damage.

Until then, time had been allowed to fabricate, soften, split, bud, return, or seal—but not to pass gently across a surface and leave evidence of use.

The wise power offered that too, though cautiously.

Not erosion everywhere. The manufactured mountain could not have endured it. Instead, wear was allowed only where touch had been freely given many times.

A rung developed a shallow polish beneath generations of gripping toes.

A cup acquired a dark crescent where mouths met its rim.

A floor beside the central pool kept the faint hollows of kneeling bodies.

Tool handles learned the shapes of hands.

These marks did not smooth the inhabitants toward death. They made history without completing it.

The people had possessed memory before, but memory had always been architectural and dangerous. It became doors, earlier rooms, returned adolescents, rain released weeks late. Now some memory could remain merely as tenderness in a surface.

The moving interval spread.

Never across the whole courtyard. Never to everyone at once.

It traveled like a shadow cast by no object. Wherever it passed, one task was permitted to wait. A pump paused for the length of a heartbeat while water remained patiently at both ends. A root ceased swallowing. A child’s flaw stopped producing experimental freckles for an afternoon. One named portion of the white lizard slept while the others continued eating knots.

The residents devised ways to invite the interval without commanding it.

They sang songs with a missing silence.

They tied soft black fibers around overworked tools.

They placed empty, leaking bowls near those too proud to ask for relief.

They made a new occupation: keepers of the almost-resting. Their task was not to send the interval anywhere, but to notice where it might be welcomed.

Authority changed accordingly.

Someone who said, “I can continue,” was still respected.

Someone who said, “This can wait,” became trusted.

The phrase **enough for now** entered their language. It had to be spoken while neither facing down nor toward the central pool. During altitude spasms, when directions multiplied, it meant:

**No danger has been defeated, but none shall own this moment.**

The power did one other thing.

It went to the smooth doors.

It did not open them. Even benevolence must sometimes refuse the grandeur of forcing what cannot consent.

Instead, it sat outside each one.

A bent nail appeared at one threshold. A cracked cup at another. A question with no answer was written beside a third. The power listened to the workers whose every action had already occurred.

Then, in each perfect door, it placed the smallest possible flaw:

not a hole,

not a broken hinge,

but uncertainty.

Behind one door, a flawless hand paused before making the gesture it had always made.

Behind another, a worker turned their head by less than the width of a hair.

Behind a third came a sound no one in the courtyard had ever heard from a completed place.

A mistake.

The inhabitants rushed toward the sound, carrying wedges, fibers, warm mud, leaves, ladders with changing numbers of rungs, and all the merciful tools of revision.

The doors did not open that day.

Perhaps they never would.

But from behind them came tapping that no longer repeated exactly.

That was enough for hope, provided hope was not required to finish.

As for the benevolent power, it did not remain as a ruler or guardian. A permanent guardian would eventually become a roof, and a perfect roof would remember too much rain.

It withdrew most of itself.

The handleless tool stayed behind, though it was never found in the same form twice. Sometimes it was a needle, sometimes a spoon, sometimes a hinge, sometimes merely a cool weight in a tired palm.

And the omission the power had made in itself remained open.

Through that opening, the inhabitants occasionally dreamed not of the two enormous hands assembling the mountain, but of a third hand lying nearby, relaxed, palm upward.

It did not build.

It did not seize.

It did not repair anyone without permission.

It waited to hold what could, for a little while, be put down.

The mountain continued growing.

The people continued hindering it with skill and love. They left seams, withheld pegs, redirected rain, welcomed impossible children, fed softened knots to Cup-Guest, and carried the smooth dead toward distance.

But now, moving endlessly through the summit, there was also a small unfinished rest.

It never reached everyone.

It never lasted.

It was never complete.

Therefore it endured.
