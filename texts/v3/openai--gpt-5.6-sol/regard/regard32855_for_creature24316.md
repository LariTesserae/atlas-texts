The power did not bring a river.

It considered one.

Far below the tableland, water moved through the dark: old water, iron-tasting and blind, pressed between strata laid down before the plateau had risen. The power could have lifted it. A gesture would have opened springs along every basin. Channels would have brightened. Reeds would have gathered, then trees. The empty-looking country would have become green enough to be praised from a distance.

But the power saw farther than greenness.

It saw roots drowned in their own chambers. Mud towers slumped by constant rain. Lichens buried beneath moss. Frogs called awake too often, spending in a few abundant generations what long scarcity had taught them to preserve. It saw quick animals arrive from the valleys: coursing hunters, flocking seed-eaters, wasps that searched rather than waited. It saw the small tortoises displaced from their ancestral hollows by larger, thirstier mouths. It saw forests take hold, burn, and leave behind a country less able to endure than before.

So the river remained below.

The power considered removing death.

That, too, it declined.

Death here was wall, chamber, soil, instruction. An empty shell shaded a hatchling. A dead chimney housed thirty lineages. The body of a moth became the only meal a waiting beetle would find that season. Nothing departed cleanly. To prevent death would be to interrupt the passage by which one guarded life became another.

It considered making the creatures larger, swifter, more wakeful.

It considered giving the birds uninterrupted songs.

It considered teaching the tortoises to travel beyond the plateau and the insects to build monuments visible from the sky.

All these gifts it withheld.

For a long while, the only thing the power did was attend.

Its attention entered no mind as a voice. It cast no additional shadow. Yet it followed every hidden life with a patience equal to its own.

It knew the oldest tortoise, whose shell bore a black lichen continent divided by a pale river-shaped scar. It knew the mites crossing that continent and the minute hunter following the lichen boundary. It knew the folded frog beneath the tortoise’s customary resting place, asleep through nine failed rainy seasons. It knew which drop of condensation would descend the underside of which stone, divide around a grain of grit, and reach the frog’s sealed nostril without waking it.

It listened to the interval between one bird’s chirp and another’s reply.

It felt the wars advancing partition by partition beneath neighboring mud towers.

It kept company with a moth that emerged after seven years, failed to find a mate, and died before morning beneath a stone where nothing witnessed it.

Nothing, except the power.

The plateau had no word for being seen. Nevertheless, something eased in the world around it. Not in the animals, whose caution remained necessary, but in the solitude of their necessity. Their restraint was no longer mistaken, anywhere that mattered, for emptiness.

Then the power looked into the future.

Scarcity by itself was not a wound here. It was measure, rhythm, architecture. But the future held scarcities without rhythm.

There would come a sequence of years in which the warm air from the valleys rose too hot to bead against the undersides of stones. Curtains of rain would appear beyond the horizon and fail to cross it. The deep mantle would give back its last pockets. Eggs would wait beyond the span for which waiting had shaped them. Thorn roots would follow ancient seams and find only mineral dust.

The plateau could survive severity. It could not survive the loss of return.

The power understood that rescue must not feel like rescue. A conspicuous mercy would summon hunger, alter custom, reward waste. The gift had to enter the old arrangements without replacing them. It had to be small enough for small mouths and slow enough for patient bodies.

So the power went beneath the plateau, into the layer where vanished floods had left their polished stones.

It did not create water. It changed one inclination.

Deep underground, a shelf of close-grained rock tilted by less than the thickness of a mite’s skin across the length of a basin. Water that would have descended beyond the reach of roots now traveled sideways. Not much. No spring broke the surface. No pool persisted beneath the sun.

The water moved toward the heart of the oldest field of recumbent stones.

There the power opened no fountain. It made a flaw.

A hairline seam rose through the bedrock, branching as it climbed. At its highest reaches it was narrower than a root filament. Moisture entered it reluctantly. During cool darkness, the seam breathed a trace of dampness into the rubble above.

One boulder began to gather a bead on its underside every nineteen nights.

Then another, three ridges away, every thirty-one.

A mud chimney abandoned for centuries became faintly cool in its lowest cell. The colony occupying the neighboring tower discovered nothing unusual, but one exploratory passage gradually curved toward that coolness.

A thorn root, advancing the width of a fingernail in a year, found the seam after twelve years. It thickened there. Pearl-colored grubs fed upon its outer fibers but did not kill it. A beetle laid eggs among the grubs. A lizard took possession of the tunnel mouth.

The gift entered the food of the country disguised as the country itself.

The old tortoise found the first altered stone by no wisdom of her own. She was pushed beneath it when a younger animal climbed onto the congregation at dawn. A droplet touched the chipped margin of her shell, moved through its dusty groove, and hung beside her mouth.

She drank.

It was not enough water to wake the frogs. Not enough to open the flowers. Not enough to draw the long-legged birds down from the sky.

It was enough.

The power worked elsewhere with the same restraint.

Where a future storm would have broken a shell wall and emptied a breeding pool too quickly, it settled three grains into a crack. Around those grains, silt would gather. The wall would hold one more night.

Where a lightning fire would one day cross the copper grass and consume the last thorn-cushion of an ancient kind, the power did not forbid lightning. It caused a stone, loosened by ordinary cooling, to roll into the future path of the flame. In its lee, a handspan of living root would remain.

Where a boulder continent would split under heat, extinguishing the species that lived only upon its northern black crust, the power allowed it to split—but one flake fell crust-side downward, preserving a cool colony beneath. Centuries later, mites would emerge from its edge and recolonize both halves.

Where the folded frogs lay too deep for the weakened rain to reach them, the power taught no new instinct and altered no body. It encouraged the scale-backed worms already tunneling above. Their passages carried dampness downward. Most collapsed. One did not.

The interventions were nearly indistinguishable from luck.

This was deliberate.

The power did not want gratitude. Gratitude would have been another expense, another behavior the creatures must sustain. Nor did it wish to become a thing anticipated whenever clouds gathered. Hope, in such a place, could be wasteful if fastened to the wrong promise.

So it promised nothing to any single life.

The moth might still fail to find its mate.

The tortoise might overturn between stones after rain.

A chimney might cool forever, every inhabitant within it dying while the empty structure endured.

The frogs might wake to a pool that vanished an hour before their eggs could hatch.

Benevolence did not mean that each small reserve would be spared its spending. Wisdom did not confuse a living world with a painless one.

What the power guarded was continuance: not the immortality of inhabitants, but the survival of their innumerable answers.

There would remain somewhere a throat that remembered how to hold water.

Somewhere a shell angled correctly toward condensation.

Somewhere an egg that did not open at the first false dampness.

Somewhere a bird patient enough to wait minutes for a reply.

Somewhere a root following the obsolete map of a vanished stream would encounter, impossibly, a trace of moisture.

The power placed a second protection at the plateau’s margins.

It foresaw minds that might someday look upon the high country and call it barren. Such minds would measure life by height, water by shine, value by what could be removed. They would see dark pebbles and imagine roads beneath them; mineralized towers and imagine ore; broad empty basins and imagine convenient ground for engines, pits, reservoirs, or war.

The power could have raised an impassable wall.

Instead it altered perception by the smallest necessary degree.

Those who came intending possession found distances difficult to judge. Useful seams appeared to dwindle beneath examination. Routes that had looked straight from afar proved costly, circuitous, and without advantage. Instruments disagreed by margins too minor to seem miraculous but large enough to discourage certainty.

Those who came in curiosity experienced something else.

They knelt.

They noticed a moth where they had thought there was a stain. They lifted a hand before turning a stone because they sensed, without knowing why, that the shade beneath it was already occupied. Silence ceased to feel vacant. The longer they remained, the more crowded the country became.

A few departed without taking anything.

A few returned and taught others how not to step.

The wisest among them understood that protection did not require revealing every secret. They drew boundaries around places whose contents they could not inventory. They left blank spaces in their maps, not from ignorance but respect. When asked what lived there, they answered:

More than appears.

This, too, was the power’s work, though never compulsion. It did not make the cruel gentle or the greedy content. It only ensured that wonder remained available before appetite completed its thought.

Ages passed.

The tilted shelf continued its hidden labor. Some centuries it bore almost nothing. In others, after rare rains, it stored a darkness of water in the stone and relinquished it grain by grain.

The old tortoise died beside a seasonal pool. Her shell moved outward over many years, pushed by living bodies. At last it lodged in a branch of the white wall, black lichen facing upward. Rain filled the pale river-shaped scar. A young frog, newly changed and smaller than a beetle, rested there until nightfall.

The boulder continent split.

The sheltered crust survived.

The mud chimney fell, but its cool lowest chamber became a pit lined with clay. Condensation gathered there. Roots entered. Birds nested in the roots.

Nothing remained as it had been. Everything remained itself.

And still, at noon, the plateau disappeared.

Membranes closed over eyes. Wings flattened. Threshold hunters waited. The copper grasses held motionless above the pavements. Heat pressed every visible creature back into likeness with stone.

No sign in the sky announced guardianship.

No river crossed the old channels.

The benevolent power was present only in margins: one night longer, one root deeper, three grains holding, a path made slightly harder for destruction and slightly easier for care.

If one could have asked the power why it did not do more, it might have answered:

Because this place is not a failed garden.

Because abundance has already happened here, but learned to occupy less room.

Because the lives beneath these stones do not need to be transformed into lives I would more easily recognize.

Because mercy must sometimes be a flood, and sometimes the prevention of one.

Because the smallest beings are not waiting to become greater beings.

Because what looks like almost nothing may be exactly enough—and when it is not, the kindest addition is almost nothing more.

Then it would return to its watching.

And beneath the oldest stone, in darkness no eye had entered, a clear bead would slowly gather.

It would swell without hurry.

It would hold.

And when at last some minute, dry mouth found it, the drop would fall.
