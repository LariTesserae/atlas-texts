The power did not enter by the white sky.

To enter there would have been to become a sun, and the basin already possessed light enough to make every surface painful. Nor did it rise from beneath the courtyards, for anything coming from below would have been taken for a maker, and the power did not know who the makers were.

It did not approach the outer wall. To reach what had never been reached would have converted a mystery into a border, perhaps a prison.

It entered in the manner of the inhabitants.

First it attended.

For a long while nothing changed. The power watched the shell bowls moving water between full pools. It learned the difference between work that sustained the basin and work that sustained the worker. It watched an inhabitant touch every droplet on the eastern ridges in sequence. It followed a child who had appeared with a bone needle and no memories. It sat, without shape, beside a returned woman while her family arranged reeds to describe how they had carried her dead body across three plazas.

It listened to the wet clicks of their language. It learned which silences were peace and which were despair.

Most importantly, it did not mistake restoration for healing.

After a time, one of the oldest inhabitants noticed it.

Her name then was Doorway-Holding-Three-Monkeys. The grove in her chest had become dim from the number of events layered through her body. Behind her present eyes, six older pairs watched in slightly different directions. She had died eleven times that she remembered and perhaps more that she did not. She had mourned companions who returned without knowledge of her mourning. She had crossed one of the branch frames and lived for a season in a country where mud hardened around footprints.

She had come back carrying a fired clay bead.

The bead was the only object she knew that the basin had failed to restore, because it had not originated there. For many years she had kept it beneath her tongue. It was red, uneven, and cracked along one side. In the other country a child had made it for her and then grown older.

Doorway-Holding-Three-Monkeys stood beneath a roof of woven leaves. Her shadow left her feet, crossed the wet pavement, and knelt where the power was attending.

The woman laid three reeds across an empty threshold.

“What do you remember,” she asked, “that this place denies?”

The power considered its answer.

It could have said: I remember the first brick.

But it did not.

It could have said: I know what lies beneath the lowest courtyard.

But knowledge and wisdom were not the same, and the power knew that an answer given too early could become another architecture from which no one could escape.

It could have claimed to remember the hands whose prints marked every inhabitant. Yet those hands were not within its knowledge. Benevolence did not permit invention merely because invention would soothe.

So the power answered only:

“I remember that you asked me.”

Doorway-Holding-Three-Monkeys waited.

The basin resumed its quiet idea of water moving.

The woman removed the cracked bead from beneath her tongue and placed it on a tile between them.

“Will you remember this?”

“Yes.”

“After it is lost?”

“Yes.”

“After I am restored and do not remember giving it?”

“Yes.”

“After everyone here has forgotten what a bead is?”

“If you wish.”

The woman turned her upright cup over.

It was an apology, though she did not know precisely for what. Perhaps for testing. Perhaps for wanting to believe.

When she lifted the cup again, the bead remained.

That was the first thing the power did.

It remembered.

Not as the inhabitants remembered, inside bodies that grew dense with layers. Not as the basin represented, turning death into painted fish and broken tools into tiny models. The power did not flatten events into images or bind them into rules. It held each memory with its uncertainty intact.

It remembered contradictory journeys without forcing one to become false. It remembered the wall both dismantled and standing. It remembered that two people had met even when only one of them recalled it. It remembered the mourning the dead had never witnessed. It remembered every name an inhabitant had worn, without deciding that any one of those names was the true one.

Nothing in the basin changed because of this.

At first, that was mercy enough.

The oldest came privately. They placed cups, fruits, cords, beetles, and tools in arrangements before the unseen listener. Some spoke in the clicks of walls and gutters. Some let their shadows speak. Some pressed a palm to the tile and remained still until their pupils filled entirely with white.

They did not surrender memories. The power would not take them.

They shared them.

To be remembered elsewhere did not make their bodies less layered, but it made the layers easier to bear. A burden is altered when another being knows its exact weight.

Children came also, though they had little to recount. They brought questions.

“Did I exist before the mat was woven?”

“I do not know.”

“Who made the handprints?”

“I do not know.”

“Will I become the person who remembers me?”

“Not entirely.”

“What is night?”

The power could have shown them.

It did not.

Instead, it asked whether they wanted an answer described, an answer pictured, or an answer lived. The children argued for a long while. One wanted darkness immediately. One feared that if the white sky went away it might not return. Another had seen night through the doorway in an old woman’s chest and said that it was full of teeth.

Thus the power learned not merely what they lacked, but which lacks were wounds and which were openings.

After many meetings, the inhabitants made an arrangement on the central field. Seven figures stood around one of the empty branch frames. Because no more than seven had ever gathered, the basin accepted the number without resistance.

They laid before the frame:

an upright cup filled with leaves,

a severed branch held continuously in three pairs of hands,

a fragment of broken tile that had not yet shrunk,

a child’s unworn garment,

the red bead,

a reed from the summit pool,

and the bodyless shadow of a bird.

Their shadows approached one another and joined hands.

Their bodies remained apart.

Doorway-Holding-Three-Monkeys spoke for them.

“We do not ask you to make this place die.”

The power listened.

“We do not ask you to uncover the makers.”

It listened.

“We do not ask you to turn every road straight, or make every distance submit to walking.”

It listened.

“We ask for somewhere that can be changed.”

The power understood.

It did not grant the request immediately.

First it asked what they meant by changed.

They answered for a very long time.

Some wanted a field where seeds could fail. Some wanted scars. Some wanted houses that showed who had lived in them. Some wanted the dead to remain absent long enough for absence to be believed. Some wanted food that did not return whole. One asked for a wall on which a name could be written without making the wall watertight. One wished to break a cup in anger and have the broken cup remain, so that forgiveness, if it came, would not pretend the anger had never happened.

Others were frightened.

A permanent wound might become infection. A fallen tree might never rise. A dead child might not step from a mosaic. A path might close forever behind someone who chose poorly. In a world where consequences had always lived chiefly inside memory, consequence made physical seemed less like freedom than a predator.

The power did not resolve the disagreement by choosing the bravest argument.

It made a place small enough to leave.

Beyond the branch frame, where jungles and enormous suns had previously appeared at certain angles, there appeared a courtyard.

It was not vast. A person could cross it in forty-seven ordinary steps. Four low walls surrounded it, though from inside those walls the basin remained visible. It held a patch of dark soil, a shallow pool, a fruit tree, an unfired clay bench, and a roofed room with one doorway. The sky above it was still white, but its light changed gently according to no fixed schedule.

Nothing entered the courtyard except willingly.

Nothing taken there became permanent merely by crossing the threshold. At the entrance stood two bowls. In the first was white lime. In the second was charcoal.

To offer an object or act to restoration, one marked it with lime.

To offer it to consequence, one marked it with charcoal.

An unmarked thing remained under the basin’s old law.

Living bodies could not be marked by anyone except themselves. Children could enter, but until they had acquired enough memory to understand loss, charcoal would not hold on their skin. The dead returned as before unless, in life and without coercion, they had renewed another choice many times.

No decision made in despair remained binding after sleep.

For the courtyard had sleep.

That was the second thing the power did.

It did not make night across the whole basin. It allowed darkness to gather in the roofed room, softly at first, no darker than the shade beneath giant leaves. Those who entered could lie on mats. Their eyes closed. Insects did not use their bodies as roads. Their pupils showed no white sky. Their shadows rested beneath them and dreamed their own dreams.

No one emerged elsewhere.

No one grew suddenly older.

They woke where they had lain, after a duration that could be felt passing but did not need to be counted.

The first to sleep there was the child with the bone needle. Seven inhabitants kept watch outside. The power remained inside, though it took no form.

When the child woke, she began to weep.

Those waiting were terrified. They thought sleep had wounded her.

But the child said she had dreamed of rain falling all the way to the ground.

After that, others entered.

Some slept for an hour. Some slept through the courtyard’s first darkness and its first gentle brightening. Some could not bear the sensation of time moving without their attention and ran out before closing their eyes. No one was shamed.

Doorway-Holding-Three-Monkeys slept for three days.

While she slept, one of the flowers in her throat closed. An old painted eye faded from beneath the skin of her wrist. Not because the memory was erased, but because she was no longer required to hold every remembered event at the surface of her body.

When she woke, the power asked what name she would use.

She touched the little door in her chest. Beyond it, the three monkeys were sleeping in a pile, their hands curled around one another.

She answered, “For now, Woman-Who-Woke-Where-She-Lay.”

In the courtyard, they planted.

The first seed did not sprout.

The inhabitants gathered around the bare patch of soil in wonder and grief. In the basin, luxuriance followed every prepared bed. Here, the seed had been planted too deeply. Water had pooled above it. It softened, split, and died without becoming a painted seed elsewhere.

They held a mourning for it.

Then they planted another.

The second seed grew crooked. Beetles ate two leaves. A storm no larger than the courtyard tore a third. The sapling leaned toward the changing light and thickened on only one side. Each imperfection was watched with reverence.

Years later, it became the first tree in the basin’s reach whose shape revealed everything that had happened to it.

The power did not make the courtyard immune to suffering. Such immunity would have made consequence counterfeit. But it prevented suffering from becoming law.

No injury in the courtyard taught the rest of the basin to injure. No death spread. No blight crossed the branch frame. The little place could fail without all creation inheriting the failure.

And anyone could leave.

The third thing the power did concerned death.

This required the greatest care.

The returned had always awakened innocent of their own mourning. Their companions bore memories of washing bodies, carrying them, waiting beside mosaics, and speaking love into painted surfaces. To place all those memories suddenly into a restored mind would have been another violence. Love could crush as surely as grief.

So the power made no universal repair.

Instead, in the roofed room, it set a low table. On it stood two unglazed cups.

A mourner could sit at one side and pour into the first cup the memory of what had happened after another’s death. The memory left no one. It became shareable, not removed.

The returned person sat opposite.

If both consented, they drank water from the second cup.

Then the returned person did not receive the events as if they had personally lived them. They received them as they had occurred in the mourner: the weight of the body, the arrangement of reeds, the anger, the patience, the moments of resentment, the ridiculous detail of a dropped fruit rolling through a funeral, the tenderness that had gone unwitnessed.

They knew these things as gifts, not stolen experience.

Sometimes they declined.

Sometimes the mourner declined.

Sometimes they waited many sleeps.

But mourning no longer had to prove itself solely through arrangements the basin would erase.

The first pair to use the cups were sisters by shared recollection. One had died beneath a falling lintel after a stranger counted the reed columns. She returned from a mosaic with water shining on her skin and remembered only looking up.

Her sister remembered the soundless fall, the carrying, the flattening of the body into blue ceramic, and eighty-three visits to the pool where the image remained.

They drank.

The returned sister wept for a love she had not experienced and could nevertheless believe.

Her tears fell.

They struck the table.

They did not hang perpetually on her cheeks, nor vanish when unwatched. They darkened the unfired clay and dried slowly into pale-edged marks.

No one polished them away.

The power did less than many hoped and more than some desired.

It did not tell them whether they had built the basin.

It did not reveal the original owners of the three handprints.

It did not force the outer wall to draw nearer.

It did not make the bodyless birds whole, because the bird-shadows did not appear to consider themselves incomplete. Instead it made a dark eave in the courtyard where they could roost without borrowing strips of shadow from other objects.

It did not silence the summit pool’s visions. It placed a handrail along the path, made of ordinary wood. The handrail could weather if marked with charcoal or remain new if marked with lime. Most sections became silver and smooth beneath generations of palms. One short length was kept forever fresh by an inhabitant who needed to remember that restoration, too, could be chosen.

It did not turn the inhabitants into ordinary flesh.

Clay faces remained clay. Root-ribs flowered. Flat arms acquired thickness only when extended. The door in Woman-Who-Woke-Where-She-Lay’s chest remained open to its impossible grove. Difference was not a disease.

Nor did it make all things age.

The basin’s unending wet brightness continued. Water still flowed without lowering cisterns. Fish matched their mosaics. Fruit returned whole when no mouth touched it. The reed columns still resisted counting. Black paths still descended while sloping upward.

But now, here and there, charcoal marks appeared.

A chipped cup kept its chip.

A child measured her height against the courtyard wall and returned later to find the old line below her shoulder.

Two lovers scratched their temporary names into the unfired bench. The names did not hold up a lintel or waterproof anything. When the lovers changed names, the old ones remained without binding them.

A woman broke a shell adze and did not wrap it in leaves. She placed the pieces on a shelf. Years afterward, she could point to them and say, “I was angry that day.” The broken tool did not accuse her. It simply agreed that the day had been.

The inhabitants still traveled through the green branch doorways. Some left forever. The power did not call them back.

For those who wished to return, it altered one thing: beside the doorway, each traveler could leave a cord tied in a knot of their own invention. Wherever they went, if they freely desired the basin again, they could feel the knot beneath their fingers.

It did not pull.

It only allowed return to be found.

Over time, the courtyard acquired a past.

The soil darkened with fallen leaves. The pool gathered silt. One wall cracked in a hot season and was repaired with mortar of a slightly different color. Children asked who had repaired it. Someone could answer. Someone else could disagree. The crack remained beneath both accounts.

The inhabitants began to understand that evidence did not end uncertainty.

This relieved them.

They had feared that a world capable of keeping marks would become a world in which marks dictated truth. Instead they learned that even a scar could be misremembered, even an inscription could lie, even a broken cup could not explain why it had been broken.

Memory remained living.

Evidence merely spoke beside it.

The power stayed, but not as a ruler.

It became one more witness among witnesses, distinguishable only by the patience of its attention. It accepted names when names were offered and released them when conditions changed. It permitted questions to remain unanswered. It never called the courtyard a cure.

When asked what it should be called, the power refused every title that meant Maker, Keeper, Judge, or Healer.

At last the child who had first dreamed of falling rain placed an upright cup before it. The cup was empty, which in the new courtyard had come to mean: there is room, but no demand.

She named the power Remembering-Without-Binding.

It accepted that name.

Much later, Woman-Who-Woke-Where-She-Lay died.

She had marked herself with charcoal many times over many years, renewing the choice whenever she was rested, joyful, and free from fear. She died beneath the courtyard’s crooked tree with the red bead in her hand. Her companions sat beside her. The power attended.

Her body did not flatten into a mosaic.

No identical woman stepped from a painted pool.

The little doorway in her chest remained open for three days. Through it, the three bright monkeys could be seen waiting in the humid grove. On the third evening—the courtyard had evenings by then—they climbed through one by one and sat beside her body.

When darkness came, her companions buried her beneath the tree.

The basin did not open.

The white sky did not protest.

The next morning the grave remained.

This was terrible.

This was what she had chosen.

Her companions mourned her absence, but they did not mourn alone, and they did not need to persuade a restored innocence that their love had occurred. The power remembered with them. The tree remembered in the new growth of a root bent around the grave. The clay bench remembered in names scratched and crossed out. The monkeys remembered in whatever way monkeys do.

Years afterward, a child who had never met her found the cracked red bead among the tree’s roots.

The child asked whose it was.

Seven inhabitants gathered, though only three knew the same stories. They disagreed over which name to use. They arranged fruits, reeds, cups, shadows, and broken tools. They told the child of a woman who had carried a foreign object beneath her tongue; a woman with a grove inside her; a woman who had tested a listening presence; a woman who had slept three days and awakened where she lay.

No single account contained her.

The child listened with complete attention.

Then she pressed the bead into wet clay beside the doorway of the roofed room. When she removed it, an uneven round impression remained, crossed by the line of the crack.

The mark resembled no rule, no handprint, no picture of a mountain.

It held nothing aloft.

It made no wall watertight.

It was only the trace of something that had been there.

The power looked upon it and did not improve it.

That was the wisest thing it did.
