The power does not enter the lake.

To enter in any recognizable shape would be an act of violence. A body large enough to contain its presence would displace whole districts. Light sufficient to announce it would blind the upper gardeners and empty the shadowed streets. A voice spoken directly into the water would strike every listening organ at once, pass through root and bone, and be heard for generations as damage.

Nor does it appear above the summit. The inhabitants already fear the unbounded sky. A figure among the clouds, however gentle its expression, would confirm their oldest terror: that the naked brightness is inhabited and has noticed them.

So the power remains where they have always left room.

It attends from the central shaft.

For many years it does nothing that can be distinguished from nature. It learns the city in the manner of the oldest listeners, not by separating one sound from another but by hearing change.

It hears towers thicken and their notes descend. It hears children strike forbidden rhythms against guide-lines. It hears the blind creature moving through the lower root beds. It hears the little explosions of seed chambers, the patient rasp of grazers, the minute tearing of a bridge several seasons before its binders discover the weakness.

It also hears what the inhabitants cannot.

Far beneath the oldest root-felt, water is widening its hidden roads through the mountain.

The lake has always seeped. Its apparent stillness depends on an intricate balance: rain above, stone below, roots between. But the stone is changing. A seam in the buried crater wall has softened to clay. Each cold season carries away a quantity too small to cloud the water. Each warm season sends roots into the new hollows, and the roots slow the loss, but do not stop it.

In three centuries the seam will open.

The lake will not vanish in an afternoon. It will descend by terraces. Upper breathing courts will strand against the air. Towers built to bear buoyancy will tear under their own weight. The district chords will rise in pitch as water pressure leaves them. For perhaps fifty years the city will attempt to follow the falling surface downward, until the widening seam becomes a throat and the remaining water passes through the mountain.

The power could seal it.

It could fuse the clay to stone, turn the mountain’s interior into an imperishable vessel, and hold the lake at its present height for as long as snow and cloud remained in the world. No inhabitant would ever know. They would attribute the altered seepage to a fortunate turn of roots. Children would continue to play beneath the pads. The towers would grow. Ancestors would deepen the city’s chord.

But permanence would not be kindness.

A sealed lake would slowly sour. Minerals now carried out through the mountain would accumulate. Deep water would cease to breathe. The pale root vaults would blacken. The armored animal would die first, then the oldest gardens, then the sounding trunks from below upward. The city might endure another thousand years, perhaps two, but only by becoming narrower, more controlled, and more afraid of change.

The power could remake the entire basin: purify the water without outlet, renew the plants without decay, preserve every air court and tower against storm, age, error, and hunger.

It considers this for less than a moment.

A city that cannot be lost cannot truly be kept. A duty that cannot fail becomes ornament. The people would continue their gestures, but their care would no longer matter. The power would have taken the living city and, out of love, made a garden model of it.

It does not seal the mountain.

Instead, it seeks a way to give the inhabitants what they require of curiosity: a means of return.

During a storm, an amphibious bird is driven far beyond its customary range. For three days it crosses ridges beneath a black sky. It feeds in a valley where long, hollow-rooted plants grow beside a river that vanishes underground each dry season. Their seeds cling to its breast feathers. Most are lost on the journey back. One remains.

The power changes nothing in the seed. It changes only the coincidence of its falling.

The bird plunges through the summit lake and passes between two upper towers. The seed tears free, spins past a suspended path, and catches in a crease of root-felt beside an abandoned channel. There it would ordinarily rot. A child finds it first.

The child is cleaning bladder valves as punishment for racing a ray through a civic void. She sees the seed because it is the wrong shape for anything she knows: hooked, dark, enclosed in a shell that rattles when tapped against bone.

She keeps it secretly for seven days. Then, fearing it will die, she brings it to an old shaper.

The seed is planted.

It takes eleven years to germinate.

Its first roots are disliked. They do not spread across the root-felt but force themselves downward, following the faint outward draw of seeping water. Several gardeners argue that the plant should be removed before it enters buried civic layers. Others object that its leaves trap unusually clean air and that its fibers, once split, close around cuts in living wood.

They compromise. The plant may remain under watch.

Watching becomes tending. Tending becomes duty.

Over the next century, the root descends along the hidden seam. It does not block the escaping water. It lines the passage, divides it, and slows it into many narrow channels. Where pressure increases, the root thickens. Where the mountain shifts, it bends instead of breaking. Fine breathing fibers enter cracks too small for hands and return traces of mineral, temperature, and current to the parent stem.

The inhabitants learn to read these traces.

At first they read them as gardeners read any plant: taste, color, tension, the timing of new growth. Later they fit listening pieces against the root and discover a low pulse traveling up from within the mountain. The pulse changes with rainfall. It changes with the lake level. It changes when clay falls from the buried seam.

For the first time, the city can hear its own leakage.

The new plant is not named for safety. Their language does not trust names that boast of outcomes. It is called the far-root.

No revelation accompanies it. No one is told that it came from elsewhere. No commandment is carved into its bark. The power asks no gratitude and permits itself to become indistinguishable from chance.

This is its first act.

Its second concerns the creature below.

The blind animal is older than any living tower. Its kind inhabited the crater before the first stakes were planted, when the lake floor was stone scattered with thin microbial felt. It feeds by grinding mineral crust from the buried wall. The knocking of its brow is neither speech nor threat. It is how the animal breaks open sealed seeps and exposes the pale organisms on which it grazes.

Without knowing it, the creature has kept the mountain porous.

Without knowing it, the city has kept the creature alive. Nutrients descend from cultivated terraces. Warmth escapes from fermentation chambers. Collapsed roots form vaults in which its soft young once hid—though no young have been born for a very long time.

It is the last of its kind in the lake.

The power does not create a mate for it. Wisdom cannot always repair loneliness by duplication, and benevolence does not require every ending to be prevented. Instead, the power eases a growth that has begun beneath the animal’s armored brow. It does not make the creature young. It gives it freedom from pain and enough strength for its remaining work.

Then it teaches the far-root the rhythm of the animal’s knocking.

This teaching is no sentence, only an affinity. When the creature strikes the crater wall, certain chambers in the far-root tighten and release. The motion travels upward. The listeners, who once heard the knocks from all directions, begin to locate them.

Decades pass before anyone understands that the knocks follow the hidden seep.

Decades more pass before they understand that the seep and the city’s future are the same question.

By then the far-root has become part of several districts. Its upper stems support new air courts. Its fibers brace old bridges. Its pulse has been given a place in the civic chord—not a note belonging to any district, but a slow interval beneath them all.

A debate begins.

Some say the far-root should be pruned back. It has crossed boundaries no planted structure was intended to cross. It enters the mountain where no gardener can follow. It carries news from beyond the city and therefore cannot be held to civic duty.

Others say this is precisely why it must remain. A person is one who keeps an opening. The far-root has revealed an opening larger than any doorway and older than any tower.

The debate lasts three generations.

The power does not hasten it.

Eventually, a group of binders proposes a work without precedent. They will train one branch of the far-root not downward along the seep, but upward through the central shaft.

The proposal is nearly rejected. The shaft has never been bridged. Roads end at its edge. Its emptiness belongs to everyone because no one has claimed it.

The binders answer that they do not intend to bridge it. A bridge crosses an emptiness in order to defeat it. Their branch will descend along one side and remain loose in the water. It will be a line, not a road: a means of return.

That phrase enters the argument and changes it.

Permission is given.

The branch grows for seventy-four years.

Gardeners direct it by shading and scoring, by suspended nutrient baskets, by slight alterations in current. It descends slowly past the last balconies, past the inhabited depths, past the nurseries of the dead. At intervals, it produces hollow knots capable of holding air under pressure. Guide-cords are braided around it. Listening fibers are left uncut.

No one living when the work began survives to see its lower end reach the old crater floor.

The first descent is made by four people carrying enough air for a journey twice as long as they expect. They are attached to one another, to the far-root, and to a secondary line tended by hundreds of hands above. Every tool is clipped three times.

They descend into the true emptiness.

The city narrows overhead. Its chords lose distinction. The central sunlight, though it is a clear afternoon, fades long before they reach the bottom. They pass seeds that have hung for years in weak upward currents. They pass the white ribs of a ray no one remembers losing. They pass an old tool of green-black metal, suspended by its hook in a thread of root.

At the floor they find no mouth into the world below, no heart removed from the mountain.

They find the far-root entering a wall of pale clay.

Beside it lies the armored creature.

It is enormous, but not immeasurable. Its blind head is scarred from centuries of opening stone. Feelers spread around it like fallen branches. Its hide carries gardens of translucent animals. It raises its brow, touches the newly grown root, and knocks once.

The vibration passes through the four explorers, through the guide-line, through the terminating roads, through every district that has placed a listener against the wood.

The entire city hears the creature where it is.

Then, after a long interval, a second knock travels back down.

A listener in the upper city has answered.

Others follow. Not a warning, not a civic code, not any pattern that could be mistaken for language. Personal intervals are sent one by one into the depth. The old creature remains still while they arrive.

The power listens with it.

This is its second act: not speech, but introduction.

Its third act will take centuries and will belong mostly to the inhabitants.

Knowing the mountain will one day open, they begin to prepare—not to prevent change, but to survive it.

They grow flexible towers able to stand in air as well as water. They breed pads whose upper surfaces can bear weight. They adapt breathing bladders into vessels that retain moisture rather than air. They send feathered climbers through the lake roof to study the summit. They follow the far-root’s smallest branches into the mountain and discover chambers where water runs toward the valleys.

Some turn back. Some do not.

On a cliff far below, where the hidden seep emerges in a veil of droplets, the far-root eventually reaches daylight. It flowers there.

The flower is noticed by people of the valley, who have long looked up at the dark oval on the mountain’s crown and told stories about what lives inside it. They find woven fibers among the roots. Later they find a bone toggle. Later still, a pattern of knots.

Communication begins badly, then improves.

When at last the lake starts to fall, the city is given years of warning by the deep pulse. Nothing is orderly. No preparation proves sufficient. Towers split. Air courts collapse. Fish herds enter the seep and are lost. Some inhabitants refuse to leave districts whose obligations they inherited. Some climb into the terrible open sky. Some descend through the mountain. Some remain in the shrinking water and reshape the old city into terraces exposed to rain.

The power saves no one by force.

It holds no body back from death. It does not lift the city whole from the basin or restore the water each night. It does not choose for them what must be carried and what may be allowed to end.

But every road that closes does so slowly enough to be heard.

Every dangerous passage has, somewhere, a handhold.

Every descent has a line by which someone may return.

Long afterward, living towers grow beside rivers in the valleys. Their rooms are pruned into roots. Their bridges take generations to meet. Hollow trunks sound in the current. Children born beneath the sky wear small paddles on their hands and laugh at the old belief that brightness has no walls.

At the mountain’s summit, a smaller lake remains.

The central shaft is no longer deep. Plants enter it now, but one round space is always kept clear. No law requires this. Gardeners simply maintain it, generation after generation: a column of open water beneath the moving clouds.

When asked why, they give several answers.

It is for light.

It is for listening.

It is for whatever has not yet found a way to speak.

And the wise power, having never asked for temple, name, or gratitude, keeps that opening with them.
