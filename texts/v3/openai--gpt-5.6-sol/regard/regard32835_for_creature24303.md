### The Smallest Mercy

The power does not arrive as light.

Light would alter the films, call algae into places where they could not remain, drive the head-down fish into crevices, and make the blind crabs’ patient maps suddenly irrelevant. Nor does it arrive as sound. A voice in that chamber would be violence: pressure striking every soft body at once.

It does not make itself small. It has no need to become shrimp-sized, spore-sized, bacterial. Size is a relation among bodies, and it is not a body.

Instead, it attends.

It attends to the city without simplifying it.

It does not see a multitude where only the colony matters, nor a colony where only individuals matter. It sees the mussel wall and every mussel in it; the crab and the tenement borne on its back; the anemone’s hunger and the copepod taken by that hunger. It knows the brittle star as a whole and knows the severed arm curling beneath it. It knows which eggs will hatch, which will cloud with fungus, which will be drawn unnoticed into a sponge canal.

Its benevolence does not require innocence. Nothing here is innocent. Nothing is guilty.

The power could widen the main fissure. It could bring a steady current, rich with oxygen and food. For a time the chamber would flourish beyond measure. Worm crowns would crowd every ledge. Mussels would rise in black ramparts. The anemone would divide, and its daughters would divide again.

Then the abundance would close the streets. Waste would gather. One successful form would smother another. The city would consume its own opening more quickly.

The power does not widen the fissure.

It could keep the octopus away. It could turn every probing arm aside and still every predatory mouth. But shrimp would multiply until their baskets stripped the current bare. Grazers would erase the films faster than they formed. The weak and newly molted would linger, only to starve later. A city without sudden death would become a city of slower deaths.

It does not bar the octopus.

It could blunt the basalt, soften the brine, stabilize the shelf, preserve every tower and shell chamber exactly as it is. But the city exists because stone broke. Its architecture is made from collapse. To forbid disturbance would be to forbid succession, openings, unclaimed surfaces, all the accidents by which a larva finds room.

It does not make the place safe.

For a while, therefore, its mercy is indistinguishable from doing nothing.

A shrimp molts beneath the anemone and is eaten before its new shell hardens.

A clutch of clear eggs survives in a blind courtyard.

A worm tower breaks under falling grit. Half the colony dies. On the fresh fracture, bacteria settle.

A hermit crab tests an empty shell, rejects it, returns, tests it again, and moves in. A ribbon worm already occupies the innermost whorl. Neither departs.

The power prevents none of this.

Yet attention is not nothing.

No life in the hollow is beneath notice. No moment is dismissed because it is brief, repeated, mindless, or too small to trouble the water. The green flash of an ostracod, answered by no eye, is seen. The touch-sequence by which a blind crab knows its way—smooth shell, sponge branch, three tubes, edge—is held entire. The last contraction of prey inside the anemone is not mistaken for an abstraction called feeding.

The power does not confuse scale with worth.

And because it is wise, it waits for the place where mercy will not become dominion.

Deep in the basalt, beyond the rear basin, there is another gas blister. It is smaller than the first and has never opened to the sea. Between the two chambers lies a partition of volcanic glass, thick in most places, almost transparent in one. Stress has been gathering there since the shelf split.

Left alone, the partition will break inward many seasons from now. It will break all at once. The shard will cross the rear basin, sever the anemone at its base, crush the shellwork below, and lodge against the main fissure. Water will slow. The chamber will begin to suffocate.

The city will not end immediately. Ends are rarely so clean.

First the shrimp will leave or die. Then the mussels will gape without feeding. Bacterial veils will thicken over the streets. The head-down fish will rise toward the last oxygenated pocket. For a while the anemone’s torn tissues will feed everything. Then the hollow will become a sealed richness, consuming itself in darkness.

This, too, would not be evil. Chambers close. Cities become mineral. Their inhabitants do not possess a right to permanence.

But the power perceives another possibility.

It touches no creature.

It adds no substance.

It changes only where one strain in the stone meets another.

The alteration is less than the pressure made by a settling grain of silt. A crystal lattice accepts the smallest possible fault. One bond yields before its neighbor. Nothing in the city feels it.

For eleven tides, nothing happens.

On the twelfth, dense brine descends through the ceiling crack and spreads along the floor. Animals climb the walls. The great anemone contracts. Its tentacles withdraw from the rear basin. Shrimp gather on the mussel ridges. A brooding isopod abandons one clutch and carries another higher.

Then the partition breaks.

Not inward.

The fracture runs sideways in a white line, turns through the weakened place, and opens the second blister by a slit no wider than a worm tube.

A small quantity of ancient water escapes. It is poor in oxygen and bitter with dissolved stone. Several amphipods nearest the opening die. A sponge crust pales and does not recover. The city receives no miracle without consequence.

But after the trapped water is gone, a new current begins.

It is faint. At first only bacteria answer it. Rust-colored threads spread along the lip of the crack, feeding on minerals from the newly exposed rock. Then flatworms enter. A larval mussel fixes itself where the flow divides. Tube worms follow. The long-legged crab reaches the unfamiliar opening, taps it repeatedly, and withdraws. The next night it returns.

The second chamber is empty of shellwork and almost bare. Its surfaces are sharp, its water slow, its dimensions no greater than a closed fist.

It is enough.

Not an escape from death. Not abundance. Not safety.

Room.

The city begins to pass through.

Bacterial films cross first, then grazers. A sponge sends growth around the edge. Copepods are swept in and some find their way out again. One brittle star extends an arm through the slit and feeds there for many days before drawing the rest of itself after. Eggs adhere to the new ceiling. A dead shrimp catches in a crevice and becomes the first feast, then the first sediment.

The inhabitants do not know they have been helped.

They establish no rite at the fracture. They offer nothing. The blind crab eventually incorporates the opening into its map: sponge branch, two tubes, mineral seam, cold slit, open water. The anemone extends a single tentacle toward it and captures an unfamiliar larva arriving from beyond.

The laws of the city remain unchanged.

Whatever stays still becomes terrain.

Space exists only temporarily.

Everything borrows.

The benevolent power does not repeal these laws, because they are not decrees imposed upon life. They are the form life takes here. To abolish them would not perfect the city. It would replace it with something else.

So the power grants no immortality. It establishes no order. It crowns no species, ends no hunger, and preserves no district from becoming ruin.

It gives the city one additional way to continue becoming.

Then it withdraws—not its attention, only its influence.

Much later, when the basalt shelf settles again, the original chamber will still be changed beyond recognition. The great anemone will be gone. Its base will remain as a tough ring beneath layers of bryozoan stone. The first mussel wall will collapse. The second blister will become crowded, then choked. Other cracks will open. Some will lead nowhere. One will admit silt. Another will carry larvae into chambers no larger than the spaces between shells.

No generation will know the old shape of the hollow.

Nothing will remember the mercy as mercy.

But for a little longer, in more than one bubble of black rock, mouths will open toward turning water. Soft bodies will borrow old hardness. Ruins will acquire inhabitants. The city will divide, fail, inherit itself, and spread.

And the power, asking for neither gratitude nor proof, will know that this was enough.
