The power did not enter at once.

Had it looked directly into the Low Garden with the whole of its attention, the garden would have suffered.

Every path would have drawn near. Every unwatched chamber would have opened simultaneously. Herds from the deep intervals would have crowded into the same courts as their other possible bodies. All given birds would have arrived on all branches. Every shadow would have been forced beneath its owner. Predators would have completed their approaches. Unfired eggs would have become every creature they contained the possibility of becoming.

The southern margin would have finished itself in an instant.

For the garden, this would have been indistinguishable from destruction.

The power understood this before its gaze fully descended. It turned aside.

For seven evenings, nothing happened except that one pinhole in the dome went dark.

No sunbeam entered there. No blackness showed beyond it. The hole appeared filled by something neither bright nor opaque, like an eye closed without pressure.

The inhabitants noticed.

Beetles came first. They circled the patch of floor beneath the darkened pinhole, but no flame kindled under their wings. They could collect no attention from it.

Given birds were passed through the higher branches. Their shadows avoided the place.

An ancient masked stilter lowered its face against the tile and found no aromatic crust. Nevertheless it remained there, breathing behind its mask.

By the third evening, eye-leaves had grown around the clearing. All faced outward.

By the fifth, a black hunter began its approach from four paces away. On the sixth it stopped, folded itself, and became a low arch.

On the seventh evening, when the garden inhaled, the darkened pinhole inhaled with it.

Then the power made itself smaller.

It did not disguise its greatness. It laid greatness aside.

What entered the garden was only enough of the power to make one careful decision at a time.

It arrived beneath the black arch as a small figure in a plain hood, carrying an empty basket. At a distance it appeared to have two hands. Close inspection showed no hands at all. Beetles, mites, threadworms, lizards, folded birds, walking jaws, or any other familiar constituents could not be found beneath the hood. Nor was the figure a single body.

It was an agreement made from things that had not happened.

The step not taken.

The mouth not used.

The branch on which no bird had arrived.

The tile never lifted.

The prey that had not yet discovered itself eaten.

The figure stood without casting a shadow.

The garden did not know what to make of it, and therefore, for a little while, it was safe.

The power waited.

Waiting in the Low Garden was not the absence of action. Distance rearranged itself around the hooded figure. Creatures approached by indirect routes. A circuit of forty paces brought some there in minutes and others after years. Several animals died on the way and arrived as pigments in the bodies of sorters. One came first as a child and later as one of the mismatched legs of a stilter. A given bird reached the clearing before the branch that had released it.

The power received them in whatever order they arrived.

It did not speak, because speech would have entered the garden as an animal and required feeding.

Instead, it listened by becoming briefly incomplete in the shape of each visitor.

When a matback came, the power lacked a companion.

When a petal-bearer came, it lacked instructions.

When a black hunter came, it lacked prey.

When a shadow came, it lacked a body.

When an unfinished bird was carried from the southern margin and laid before it, the power lacked a song.

In this way the inhabitants understood that there was room within the visitor for what they could not supply.

They began to show it their injuries.

Not broken joints. Knot-mites repaired those.

Not cracked walls. Gardeners assembled for them.

Not hunger, exactly, nor fear, nor death. The garden possessed ancient arrangements for each.

They showed the power the matbacks whose brow patches had been scratched away. Such pairs could remain separated by the width of a leaf and wander for generations toward one another. If brought physically together, one would cross the small space while the other receded into a distant grove. If their brows were polished and painted anew, the reflections showed only strangers.

They showed it given birds whose shadows had been pinned so long that the thorns had become trees. No one wished to pull the thorns, because the birds might arrive carrying the age of their absence all at once.

They showed it shadows that did not wish to return to their owners and owners weary of rising each morning into the obligations of their shapes.

They showed it old predators that had become shelters but still dreamed, somewhere under the black ribs, of the edible world. They dreamed without jaws and woke ashamed when small creatures nested inside them.

The sorters came last.

They opened their compartments and showed the power certain substances they had never learned to classify.

A weariness that was not fiber, oil, salt, tooth, or pigment.

A memory that did not belong in another body.

A task completed so fully that reusing it would be a kind of cruelty.

The wish of one small being not to become anything else.

The sorters had carried these from corpse to corpse, century after century, unable to empty themselves.

The power looked upon these things with only one small portion of its attention.

Even so, the red pebbles ceased turning.

All through the garden, leaves grew warm. The dome drew low. Given birds failed to arrive. Mouthkeepers stopped polishing. For the space of one breath, the Low Garden came dangerously close to having a single center.

The power lowered its head.

“I am sorry,” it said.

The words entered the air.

The first became a six-legged creature with a soft gray pelt. The second became a shallow clay bowl. They regarded one another, then moved beneath the black arch and were not seen again.

The red stones resumed their turning.

After this, the power understood what gift the garden lacked.

It was not completion.

The garden knew how to complete itself, slowly and in countless ways. To finish it from outside would be to choose among its possible bodies and murder the rest.

It was not permanence.

Permanence would fix every animal in its present joints, every path in its present distance, every gardener in its present agreement. Nothing could molt, recede, become architecture, or wake from fruit.

It was not freedom from death.

Death was one of the garden’s most skillful crafts.

It was not even freedom from suffering. The power knew that removing all pain from so interwoven a place would also remove warning, tenderness, patience, repair, and the knowledge by which one being made room for another.

What the garden lacked was a form of being kept that did not require being watched.

It lacked the difference between forgotten and abandoned.

So the power went to the southern margin.

The ancient gardener was there, moving between blank tiles and skeletal frames. It had the usual bent hood, basket, and mallet. Its constituent creatures had been replaced innumerable times, but its habits remained unchanged. It tied cords with the same absent hand. It touched the same hollow in each egg. Whenever it reached one particular blank tile, it paused, unable to proceed.

The relief in the oldest shrine showed something standing on that tile.

No one could determine what it was. Portions resembled a tree, a doorway, a seated animal, a fountain without liquid, or a person with its back turned. Generations of gardeners had attempted to reproduce it. Each attempt was disassembled before completion. The pieces were not wrong, but the agreement among them would not hold.

The power sat down on the blank tile.

The ancient gardener raised its mallet.

For a long time they remained so: one waiting to build, one declining to become the plan.

At last the gardener lowered the mallet.

Its hood loosened.

Beetles emerged first, their cold flames very faint. Then came threadworms, knot-mites, undersides, three walking mouths, petal-bearers carrying nothing, and a lame matback with no reflection on its brow. A given bird was passed from the empty hood to the frame of an unfinished wall. Two shadows slipped under the blank tile. The basket collapsed into reeds. The mallet opened its eyes and crawled away.

The gardener that had continued for centuries came to an end.

Nothing replaced it.

The power pressed one finger to the blank tile. The tile softened, though no threadworms entered it. Instead of leaving a thumbprint, the power removed one.

A smooth, shallow oval remained: not a mark, but the place from which a mark had been spared.

The tile opened.

Below it was no decorated tile, no lattice of ceramic rods, no packed bed of carved beads.

There was no bedrock.

There was no oil channel, buried court, folded herd, forgotten room, or older workmanship.

There was only a small darkness.

It was not the blackness beyond the dome. It had no distance and reflected no hidden light. Attention could enter it without bringing anything near. Memory could rest there without fastening itself to a body. Nothing placed within was sorted, repaired, completed, copied, persuaded, hunted, or made useful.

The power had not created emptiness. Emptiness already surrounded and permeated more worlds than even it wished to count.

It had created privacy.

The inhabitants approached cautiously.

A beetle leaned over the opening. The flame beneath its wings went out. When it drew back, the flame returned, smaller and steadier.

An underside crawled in and vanished. The leaf from which it had detached remained cool. Three evenings later the lizard emerged. It possessed no new limbs, no altered color, no memories of architecture. For the first time in its lineage, it had gone somewhere unwatched and returned unchanged.

A matback entered while its companion waited beside the opening.

The reflection on the waiting animal’s brow disappeared. It did not show darkness. It showed nothing at all.

The waiting matback cried out.

No one had heard such a sound from that species. It resembled fired clay breaking deep underground.

The companion returned.

Its reflection reappeared.

The two touched brows and lay down together. They did not become flooring.

After this, the opening became known—not in words, but in routes, scents, gestures, and inherited inclinations—as the **Mercy Between**.

Those who entered it were not held nearby by attention. Nor did they recede into the uncounted garden. They were not anywhere that distance could express.

Yet the garden did not lose them.

Pairs learned to sleep without watching one another.

Eggs no longer needed eyes painted on every shell. Some were left blank on one side.

Given birds whose shadows had been pinned for centuries were carried to the opening. The thorns were removed. Each bird failed to arrive, but the branches did not bend beneath an undelivered weight. Much later, some emerged from the Mercy Between and chose a single branch. Others did not. The garden ceased to mistake the latter for errors.

Shadows entered without bodies. Bodies entered without shadows. Most reunited. Some separated by consent.

Old shelters sent their dreams into the dark and retained only their ribs.

The sorters came with their unclassified burdens. One by one they opened their compartments over the opening.

Weariness vanished first.

Then ownerless memory.

Then completed tasks.

Last came the small wish not to become anything else.

This did not vanish. It remained at the edge of the opening as a tiny colorless animal. It curled into itself and slept.

Nothing used it.

The power stayed for a long while, though never as the garden’s ruler.

It mended no wall that could be mended by a gardener. It named no species. It did not reveal whether the oil was an animal, whether the dome was alive, what lay beyond the salt flats, or whose hands had left prints in the lime.

When creatures asked by gesture whether the garden had a maker, the power closed its hood.

When they asked whether the relief showed the future or the past, it turned the tablet face down.

When they brought it a one-winged bird, it did not attach the second wing. It placed the bird between two branches and waited.

One branch released it.

The other did not receive it.

The bird hung for a moment in open air, wholly present.

Then it returned to the first branch and folded itself contentedly.

This possibility spread.

Not rapidly. The power would not permit its gift to become a command.

Over generations, small refusals appeared throughout the Low Garden.

A mouthkeeper allowed one discarded jaw to remain blunt.

A flower opened with an empty place in its outer petals.

A trellis reached a wall and did not persuade itself that a doorway had always been there.

A predator approached edible prey, arrived unchanged, and lay down beside it.

A sorter buried one blue pigment without recording where.

At evening, some shadows chose no nest.

At the basin, the turners let one red pebble keep a single corner.

The garden did not weaken from these omissions. It grew more spacious.

Not larger. Not emptier.

More spacious in the manner of a hand that can hold without closing.

Eventually the power prepared to leave.

The inhabitants assembled a gardener to stop it.

Beetles fixed the path. Threadworms softened the threshold. Knot-mites tied the hem of the visitor’s hood to the roots of a tree. Mouthkeepers bit gently at the air around it. Matbacks lay across every route. Petal-bearers arranged themselves into flowers on its shoulders. Given birds burdened the branches overhead with arrivals.

From a respectful distance, all these agreements formed a tall figure—not knee-high this time, but nearly reaching the lower boughs. It had many hands, each holding a tool.

The figure stood before the power.

The power stood before the figure.

Neither moved.

Then the garden-made person lifted one hand and pressed its thumb to the visitor’s hood.

A print appeared there.

The power bowed.

It understood that the garden was not asking to keep it near. The garden knew by then that love need not do that.

It was asking to remain in the power without being continually observed.

The power accepted.

It withdrew through the darkened pinhole, taking neither specimen nor plan, neither effigy nor tooth. The pinhole opened again. Sunlight entered in a broad, bending shaft and warmed the blank tile.

For several days the power did not look back.

This was difficult even for a wise power.

Especially for a benevolent one.

But it had learned the local shape of care.

When at last it remembered the Low Garden, it remembered without summoning. The paths did not contract. The deep intervals remained deep. No animal felt a pressure from above. No unfinished thing was forced toward its final form.

Only the shallow oval in the southern tile grew briefly warm.

The garden continued.

The southern margin advanced by one tile in a generation, except when it did not. The red pebbles turned, one of them clicking softly at each rotation because of its retained corner. Matbacks wandered in pairs and sometimes slept apart. Ancient predators became huts. Flowers persuaded animals into petals. The dead were sorted, except for what was allowed to rest.

Each evening, the whole enclosed place inhaled.

The paths narrowed.

The dome lowered.

Every animal looked toward a different center.

And now, among all those centers, there was also the Mercy Between: a small darkness in which nothing was required to appear, continue, return, improve, or become.

The garden exhaled.

Somewhere beyond the wicker roof, the benevolent power turned its face away with great tenderness.

Nothing followed.

Nothing needed to.
