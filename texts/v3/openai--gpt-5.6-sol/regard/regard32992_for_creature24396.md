### The Gift of Elsewhere

The power did not descend into the basin.

Descent would have required an opening, and every opening already had its use. Light entered through the canopy. Rain entered along the leaves. Birds entered by the perimeter and departed the same way. Spores moved on air too slight to stir the moss. There was no unused road by which a miracle might arrive.

So the power remained outside every surface and attended.

It listened first to the loudest lives: the cicadas dividing the air into narrow bands, the frogs calling through hollow stems, the birds exchanging their hard notes around the boundary. Beneath them it heard the beetles, the contracting pods, the small collapses of softened wood. Beneath those sounds it heard water passing through roots. Beneath the water, ants touched one another in darkness. Fungal fronts advanced through a branch at less than the width of a hair in a day.

The power heard all of these separately.

Then it heard the being.

The being had no voice beyond the voices already sounding. It made no request. It did not know itself surrounded, ancient, vulnerable, or beautiful. There was no inward chamber in which it formed desires. The open space at its center was not a mind. The low throbbing beneath the pools was not a heart. These resemblances belonged to anyone standing outside and wishing to find a body.

The power was too wise to mistake resemblance for identity.

It did not awaken the Green Mill.

To give the grove consciousness would have seemed, to a lesser benevolence, the greatest possible gift. The power could have gathered its dispersed exchanges into awareness. It could have made the trees feel the ants within their roots, the moss perceive the pressure of each foot, the frogs know that their calls joined a circuit larger than courtship. It could have allowed the entire basin to say *I*.

But then every death would have entered one memory.

The torn moth, the chick taken in darkness, the ant colony drowned when a hidden channel filled, the fungus burned back by its rival, the fish suffocating beneath contracted water—all would become wounds of a single creature unable to flee its own body. Hunger would no longer be local. Fear would travel the full perimeter. Winter would be anticipated. Drought would exist before it arrived.

The Mill had coherence without suffering from coherence. The power did not take this mercy away.

Nor did it grant immortality.

To hold the pools full forever would have drowned their margins. To stop the trees from falling would have denied the fungi their wood and the roots their openings. To forbid predation would have filled the basin with starvation. To prevent one species from failing would have allowed it to thicken beyond its layer and season.

The Green Mill endured because nothing within it endured absolutely. Its stability was not stillness but an immeasurably complicated permission to die.

The power did not confuse kindness with exemption.

It attended for one whole turning of the grove.

That was longer than a year. The cicadas kept years differently from the birds, the trees differently from the frogs, the stone differently from them all. The power waited through the return of a deep brood that had last climbed the trunks before several living branches had rooted. It waited until a pool disappeared, became moss, and opened again two body-lengths away. It waited until one of the boundary trees abandoned its oldest trunk and continued itself through a limb that had already become a grove of pillars.

During that interval, the power found no error.

Everything cruel there was bound to appetite, defense, reproduction, or decay. Nothing inflicted pain in order to enlarge pain. Nothing accumulated more than it could use except by accident, and accidents were consumed in turn. There was rivalry without empire. There was killing without contempt. There was waste, but waste did not remain waste for long.

The power could not make the Mill innocent. It was already as innocent as a living place could be.

Yet wisdom saw farther than equilibrium.

The surrounding country had been worn almost level, but its wearing had not ended. The buried river would one day deepen elsewhere and draw moisture away from the basin. The climate would wander. A sequence of dry years, unremarkable on the scale of stone, might arrive too closely for the root mat to recover. Fire might cross the leveled country under a wind stronger than any remembered by the boundary trees. A disease might enter through a beetle and find the twelve old architectures similar enough to receive it.

Or hands might come.

They need not come with hatred. Hatred was rarely necessary.

They might come measuring the stone beneath the peat, or seeking a level route for a road. They might see water held in the basin and decide that water should be made to move more efficiently. They might hear the mechanical roar and search for its source. They might drain the pools to reduce insects, clear the central space to inspect the trunks, cut a single access path through the perimeter, and discover too late that the path had severed a hundred relations that possessed no names.

No equilibrium, however old, could prepare for every power arriving from outside it.

The Green Mill had solved scarcity, decay, competition, and time. It had not solved singularity.

There was only one of it.

This was the lack the power chose to answer.

Not by enlarging the basin. Greatness was not what the grove required. Not by surrounding it with an impassable wall. Isolation was already its danger. Not by copying it entire into some vacant country, for a copy made all at once would possess the arrangement but not the history by which arrangement learned to live.

The power gave the Mill elsewhere.

The act was so slight that nothing in the basin responded.

At the northern rim, one old tree bore a season of fruit whose hooks curved a little more tightly than before. Three fruits fastened beneath the breast feathers of a bird. Usually that bird circled the perimeter, but on the morning of its departure a moth rose near its face, and the bird turned after it, crossed the central opening, and flew beyond the basin.

One fruit fell on exhausted stone and died.

One was eaten.

One remained caught until the bird rested in a shallow crease many miles away. There the hook loosened. The fruit entered a bed of ordinary moss beside an ordinary seep.

It did not create another Green Mill. It made two leaves and was grazed to the stem by a snail.

But its roots lived.

Elsewhere, the power altered nothing so much as timing. A frog calling into a hollow reed was interrupted by rain, climbed into the folded leaves of a low plant, and remained there when the plant broke loose in runoff. It traveled only to the next drainage. Most of its eggs failed in the unfamiliar water. A few developed calls at the wrong interval for their birthplace and the right interval for their new neighbors.

A fungal spore adhered to the foot of a tree-dwelling mammal. The mammal was taken by an owl. The owl crossed country the smaller birds avoided. The spore descended with a pellet beneath a stand of young trees where dead wood had been accumulating too slowly.

Ant queens rose on warm evenings and were carried farther than usual by winds that did not touch the floor of the basin.

Cicadas emerged out of sequence.

Seeds passed uneaten.

A needle-fish laid eggs in submerged moss just before a black pool overflowed through a channel that had not opened in centuries. The eggs traveled into the buried river. Nearly all were lost in cold gravel. Several entered a root-dark spring downstream.

Nothing prospered easily. That was part of the gift.

The power did not make the emigrants conquerors. In each new place they met lives already present, hungers already established, water governed by other slopes. Many vanished. Others changed. They surrendered old timings, old partners, old forms. The descendants of the Mill became less like the Mill in order to carry something of it forward.

Over centuries, shallow green places appeared across the worn country.

None was identical to the first. One formed beneath trees that grew upright and let wind move through them. Another held no fish but many pale crustaceans. One was nearly silent because its frogs called below the range of ordinary hearing. One burned and returned as sedge before creeping wood enclosed it again. Another persisted only as a damp band along a ridge, without pools or central chamber.

Yet in each, water was slowed and divided.

Dead wood ceased to be merely dead.

Life entered surfaces and seams.

No species kept dominance indefinitely.

Open space was maintained within abundance.

The being had not been copied. Its grammar had traveled.

The original basin knew nothing of this. Its cicadas did not hear their distant relatives. Its trees did not take comfort in descendants rooting beyond the horizon. The gift of elsewhere brought it no assurance, because assurance would have required a self capable of fear.

Still the power was not finished.

When hands at last approached, carrying instruments, flags, and a plan for the leveled country, the power did not turn them away by storm or terror. A broken bridge could be rebuilt. A frightened surveyor could be replaced. Miraculous concealment would preserve the grove only until curiosity became determined.

Instead, the power permitted them to hear accurately.

One of them stopped at the rim.

She had expected insects. She had expected frogs and the ordinary sounds of wet ground. She had not expected intervals. She heard the answering stations of birds. She heard the circuit of calls moving around the perimeter. Beneath these she detected the low exhalation of water through buried wood.

At first she believed there was machinery beyond the trees.

Then she understood that the machinery was the trees.

She did not enter the central chamber.

This restraint, too, was slight. It passed easily for professional caution, then for wonder, then for fear of damaging the root mat. She returned with others who knew fungi, insects, water, stone. Each arrived prepared to study a part. None could fully describe the exchange among parts, but together they recognized that the apparent emptiness was structure.

They moved the planned road.

It was not moved far. No dramatic sacrifice was demanded. The Green Mill did not become a shrine, and no fence was driven through its roots. A boundary was drawn on paper beyond the actual boundary. Water extraction was limited uphill. Fire was permitted to pass through the surrounding country in cool seasons but not through the basin. Visitors were few, and those who came walked the rim.

The place was not hidden from humanity.

It was made legible to human mercy.

This protection would not last forever. No law did. No reverence remained pure across generations. The power knew that guardianship, like canopy and moss, must decay and be renewed. So it did not lodge the grove’s safety in one decree or one person. It let the sound enter stories, recordings, field notes, childhood memories, arguments, and dreams. It allowed the Green Mill to become valuable in many incompatible ways.

Some loved its age.

Some loved its frogs.

Some cared only that it stored water.

Some studied its fungi for medicines.

Some believed the open center sacred.

Some simply disliked the road.

Their reasons did not need to agree. Agreement was a single trunk. The power preferred a ring of descendants.

Then it withdrew its attention.

Not entirely. Benevolent powers, if they are wise, do not stare forever at what they love. Unbroken attention can become another climate, another pressure under which a place must deform itself. The Mill had no need to perform holiness. It had no need to become more harmonious, more beautiful, or more conscious because it had been seen.

Rain fell in its ordinary amount.

A bird took a frog.

An ant colony entered another’s tunnel and destroyed its brood.

A supporting limb split and settled slowly into the mat. Fungal threads reached it by morning.

In the central opening, nothing appeared.

No monument marked the blessing. No new species stood upright and spoke for the rest. The light remained plain. The black pools contracted in dry weather. Cicadas failed in one band of pitch, and beetles occupied the absence.

The Green Mill continued to consume its ruins.

Far away, in shallow folds of the country, other green places began their own slow arrangements. They would not remember where they came from. They would owe the first basin nothing. In time, some might outlast it.

This was the power’s kindness: not to perfect the being, not to preserve every part of it, not to force upon it the burden of knowing itself.

It left the center empty.

It gave the pattern roads.
