The power did not bring rain.

Rain would have been mercy only from a distance. In the Press it would have boiled in the upper cracks, dissolved the salt terraces, drowned the heat-feeders in chemistry they could not survive, and split old armor as steam formed inside it. A pool on the floor would have been a grave. Cool air would have been worse. Light would have nourished nothing already there.

Nor did the power lift the creatures out.

There was nowhere else in the world where most of them could live. To remove a tile from the Press would be to separate skin from body and call the skin rescued. To carry away a bearer would be to take one reflex from a thought too large to know it had lost anything. Even the spires, whose lives were made of waiting and sudden murder, could not be subtracted without altering the whole. Their hunger was one of the pressures that kept the pavement moving.

The power understood this.

It understood, too, that suffering was present. Benevolence did not require pretending otherwise. Young tiles were exposed and pierced. Burrowers were crushed when galleries settled. Threads fled gases that burned through their soft bodies. Wind tore linked creatures from the walls and carried them beyond the rim, where they cooled on the plateau beneath an intolerable abundance of stars.

But pain in the Press could not be separated cleanly from contact, warning, hunger, molting, and the long communal labor of remaining alive. To abolish every wound would have required abolishing almost every sense by which the hollow knew itself.

So the power did not begin by changing anything.

It attended.

Its attention did not appear as light. No figure stood at the rim. No voice entered the thin air. For a span longer than the life of many shell-borers, the power learned the hollow at the pace of the hollow.

It followed warmth through the iron plates. It felt the burrowers’ pressure-language pass beneath the floor. It learned how the death of one tile became mineral for six neighbors, shelter for mites, a cradle for a spire, and eventually a fitted black plate in the wall of a bearer’s chamber. It learned that a wind-lock was not merely a crowding of frightened animals but an event distributed across species, each body supplying an ability the others lacked.

It watched generations of bearers carry fragments uphill.

It watched the pale column.

The column was not alive, though it had once been made by life. It was not a monument, though its makers had intended it to outlast them. It was the final exposed branch of a structure descending far below the hollow: a pale lattice compressed within the basalt, older than the Press in its present form. Most of the lattice had broken, melted, or been carried grain by grain through the bodies of burrowers. What remained gathered strain from the mountain and released it as vibration and heat.

The inhabitants avoided it because it was unlike everything their senses were made to understand. It did not feed, weather, tarnish, rot, or permit itself to be inhabited. It was a hole in the logic of the place.

Yet when it vibrated, the multitude aligned.

The power placed no message in it at first. A message presumes a listener capable of choosing between meanings. Instead, very gently, it touched the buried lattice.

The column cooled.

Across the Press, legs drew inward. Mouthparts paused against stone. A spire held its hook above the soft seam of a molting tile. Deep below, corrosive fluid ceased to pass through the rasping organs of the burrowers.

The power let one pulse travel through the column.

Then two.

Then one again.

Nothing moved.

The sequence was repeated.

One. Two. One.

It was not a word. It meant nothing.

After a time, the column warmed, and the Press resumed.

Many gas-rises later, the power made the sequence again.

One. Two. One.

This time a section of tiles near the western wall lowered themselves before the last pulse came. They had not understood. Their bodies had merely remembered an interval.

That was enough.

The power did not hurry.

It repeated the pattern only when the mountain was quiet, never using it to command retreat or stillness, never pairing it with pain or food. It allowed the sequence to remain useless. Useless things can sometimes become the first things done freely.

The response spread by accident. Young tiles learned the pressure of their elders lowering. Mites retreated into joints. Bearers stopped with plates clamped in their jaws. Burrowers braced, though no gas followed. Each species inherited the pause differently. No individual knew there was a pattern shared by others.

After many generations, the power gave the first pulse.

It waited.

From somewhere beneath the northern wall came a faint answering strain: burrowers extending across their tunnels in the remembered rhythm.

Two tremors, then one.

The answer was imperfect. It passed through crowded stone and arrived broken into dozens of small pressures. Tiles shifted. Threads tightened. A bearer released the slab it carried.

The power received it as an answer nonetheless.

Then it turned its attention beneath the mountain.

Far below the Press, magma had begun to rise.

The eruption would not come soon by the count of wings crossing the stars. By the count of mountains, it was already occurring. A plug of dense stone confined the ascending melt. Pressure accumulated beneath it. When the plug failed, the floor of the Press would open from center to rim. There would be no migration, no remnant, no shell left cool enough to shelter an egg. The hollow and the life shaped into it would become gas, glass, and a brief red weather on the plateau.

The power could have hardened the plug forever. That would have ended the fumaroles on which the chemical films depended. The Press would starve slowly in safety.

It could have quenched the magma. The mountain’s chemistry would have quieted, and again the life above would fade.

It could have moved the whole living pavement elsewhere. There was no elsewhere.

Instead, the power made a weakness.

Thirty leagues away, beneath an empty ravine, it opened a seam no wider than the mouthpart of a mite. The work was delicate. Stone was persuaded grain by grain, along an ancient fault leading away from the Press. The magma found the seam. Over centuries it widened the passage, spending its pressure in depths where nothing lived.

On a winter night, a red fissure opened in the distant ravine. No inhabitant of the Press saw it. The winged scavengers altered their routes for a season. Ash rose, traveled east, and fell into a sea.

The floor of the Press swelled less than it otherwise would have.

No creature gave thanks.

The power wanted none.

But saving the Press from one death was not enough. Every closed place, however perfectly inhabited, contains its ending. The burrowers were deepening the basin. The walls were becoming thin. Gas routes shifted as tunnels multiplied. The living hollow was slowly consuming the conditions that made it possible.

This was not an error. It was what life did. Still, the power saw no wisdom in requiring the Press to finish merely because it had begun within a boundary.

So it made another weakness.

This one began behind the northern wall, where the bearers had raised and rebuilt their oldest chambers. Beyond that wall lay the blind throat of an extinct vent, sealed by basalt and collapsed rubble. Farther still was a second hollow, narrower than the Press and buried beneath stone. It had heat. It had metallic salts. It had porous walls. It had never had life.

The power did not open the way all at once.

A sudden passage would have changed the winds and killed the first explorers. It would have released gas, altered the heat gradients, and perhaps collapsed the bearer chambers. Instead, one microscopic fracture appeared in the wall.

Heat-feeders entered it.

They tarnished iron and altered sulfur. Mites grazed them. The fracture widened under the mouths of things too small to have names. A burrower tasted weakened stone and began to eat.

It did not know it was going anywhere.

Its descendants—or its separated continuations—followed the chemical seam. They lined the passage with vitrified pellets. Tiles clustered over the entrance. Bearers fitted flat fragments around the new thermal boundary, making an arch where no arch had been before.

The tunnel advanced at the speed of digestion.

Years became centuries. Centuries became a change in the shape of the northern wall.

At last the burrowers broke through into the buried hollow.

Nothing crossed immediately.

The new space was too empty.

Its floor had no layered armor, no inhabited seams, no paths written in old shells. It lacked the pressure of neighbors. To enter it was to become exposed, and nothing exposed remained alone.

For a long while the opening stayed dark and vacant.

Then a bearer came to it carrying the shield of a dead tile.

The thermal boundary troubled its feet. It turned uphill, though uphill now led through the wall. It passed into the tunnel and continued until instinct made it release the shield in the second hollow.

The object fell flat on untouched basalt.

The bearer returned.

Later it came again with a plate of stone. Then with bone. Then with the mineralized shell of something whose kind had vanished from the Press ages before.

Other bearers followed.

They made a low ring in the emptiness.

Mites arrived within their armor. Heat-feeders clung to the carried stones. Thread-creatures were transported in breathing pits. A young tile, pressed between adults during a wind-lock, found itself pushed through the tunnel and beneath the old shield lying on the far side.

The second hollow acquired its first living pavement not as a conquest, but as shelter carried piece by piece.

Spire eggs came hidden in empty shells.

Predation crossed with everything else.

The power did not prevent it.

In time, the new hollow became crowded enough to be safe. Bearer walls rose around its vents. Burrowers began the long work of passing it through their bodies. Tiles moved across its floor with the patience of settling land. The life of the Press had not been copied. It had extended.

Only then did the power touch the buried lattice again.

The pale column in the first hollow gave one pulse.

Across the mountain, in the new hollow, a vein of the same ancient material—long buried and never exposed—answered with two faint tremors.

Then one.

Every creature in both chambers stopped.

For the first time, the thing that was the Press attended across a distance larger than contact.

It had no word for here.

It had no word for there.

It had no word for itself.

But pressure passed through its tunnels, through locked armor and jointed legs, through structures built without intention and inhabited into purpose. The pause in one hollow entered the other. A shared interval held them both.

The power withdrew a little then.

Not entirely. Benevolence need not become abandonment, and wisdom need not refuse attachment. It continued to watch the magma, the thin walls, the chemistry of the vents. When disaster could be turned aside without making the life of the Press dependent upon rescue, it turned it aside. When a shell cracked beneath a spire, it did not intervene. When wind came, it allowed the inhabitants to hold one another.

It gave no commandments.

It made no paradise.

It left hunger, danger, darkness, and death, because to remove them all would have required replacing the Press with the power’s idea of a better thing. Instead, it preserved possibility. It gave the closed place a way to become larger by its own labor. It offered a pattern that had no use and waited until the multitude chose, without knowing choice, to answer.

Ages later, there were many hollows beneath the range.

Some were hot, some merely warm. Their inhabitants changed. Tiles in the farthest chambers became narrower and pale-edged. Spires learned different silences. Bearers built along gradients the first Press had never contained. In one hollow, water entered as a thread so slight that life had time to discover it rather than drown. Strange soft colonies formed there, and the old armored multitude approached them over generations.

The original column weathered at last.

A crack appeared in its smooth side. Mites entered. Heat-feeders darkened the pale surface. The untouchable thing became touchable and joined the cycle it had interrupted for so long.

By then it was no longer needed.

The pulse traveled through walls, arches, packed shells, and the braced bodies of burrowers. It crossed chambers by paths made of living contact. The mountain answered itself.

One.

Two.

One.

And from deep beneath the northern range, from hollows no creature of the first basin had ever entered, the reply returned—not as a voice, not as a thought, but as a stillness held together.

The wise power listened.

Then, because the answer belonged to the Press and not to its benefactor, it did nothing at all.
