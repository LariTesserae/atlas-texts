The power would not begin by repairing the plain.

To restore every fitted edge, clear every channel, and return the courts to their first geometry would be to erase the only work now taking place. Nor would it cover the plates with soil and summon forests from elsewhere. A forest would seem generous only from a forest’s point of view. Here it would be conquest: shade where life had learned light, deep roots where life had learned stone, ceaseless appetite imposed upon beings shaped by long intervals of want.

Neither would the power awaken the dispersed life into consciousness.

It could gather the films, roots, cushions, and fungal threads into one perceiving mind. But such a mind would wake divided across distances it could not cross. It would feel every drying basin and torn filament, every colony lost beyond the horizon, with no hands to help and no body from which to flee. Awareness is not always a gift. The power, being benevolent, would not bestow loneliness merely because loneliness could speak.

It would first watch.

It would attend through many cycles, not only through the green days after rain. It would remain during the long brown intervals when life seemed absent. It would learn which basins failed once in a century, which seams held one final thread of damp, which pylons gathered dew under winds from the east, and which colonies prospered by depriving all others.

It would discover that the plain did not need abundance most.

It needed passages.

The country had many refuges, but they were divided by distances absolute to those who inhabited them. A spore might cross a province and find a wet crack, but most did not. A fungal cord could reach the far side of a slab but not the seam beyond it. Water gathered magnificently and then vanished where it stood. Each living patch faced the future with only what chance had placed inside it.

So the power would touch the plain lightly, in places no surveyor could distinguish from weather.

At the lip of one great court, it would loosen three grains of binding material. During the next storm, water would pry them free. The resulting thread of runoff would seem accidental. It would pass beneath a bowed plate, disappear into rubble, and emerge many kilometers away in a lower basin that had not received rain.

Elsewhere, it would incline a settling slab by less than the thickness of a film. Dawn condensation, formerly lost to a sealed cavity, would begin to travel along the root of a seam. It would not flow every night. It would not create a spring. But once or twice in a dry season, darkness would persist under the plate, and dormant life would wake there long enough to mend itself.

Across the plain, the power would make perhaps a hundred such alterations.

Not a network. A network would join everything too quickly, allowing the most vigorous forms to occupy every refuge. Instead there would be stepping-stones: wet in different years, connected under different winds, unreliable enough that no inhabitant could rule them all. Isolation would remain, but it would no longer be perfect.

Then the power would wait again.

The changes would be almost invisible. In one court, the reproductive stalks would appear after a storm that had never reached the court itself. On a shaded pylon, a cushion descended from a distant population would take hold beside the old one. Their edges would meet and neither would wholly displace the other. Fungal threads carrying unfamiliar tolerances would enter seams where local networks had repeatedly died of salt.

Variation would begin to travel through the country more often than catastrophe did.

The power would offer one further mercy.

Far beneath an embankment lay a hollow chamber, empty since the making of the plain. Its roof was sound. Its floor was lined with pale ceramic through which water passed only by the slowest seepage. Rain had entered it before, but through a crack too narrow for anything larger than dissolved salts. The water within was ancient by the standards of the place: cool, dark, and sterile.

The power would widen the crack.

It would not break the chamber open. It would allow only a trickle of storm water carrying dust, fragments of film, spores, and the broken tissues of stalks. These would descend into the dark reservoir. Most would die. Some would persist near the upper margin where faint light entered. Fungal cords would spread along the damp wall. Material falling from them would collect below.

Over years, the chamber would become a refuge against the surface’s worst extremes.

It would also become dangerous. Growth would thicken around the entrance. Salts would accumulate. One organism might seal the crack and end the refuge for all that followed. The power would not prevent these things individually. A world protected from every consequence is only another kind of enclosure.

Instead, once in a great while, when pressure and chance were nearly sufficient, the power would permit the chamber to shift.

A fissure would open lower on the embankment. Cool water would emerge for several days in a thin, mineral-tasting line. It would carry living fragments back to the surface. Then rubble would settle and close it again.

Nothing would come to drink.

There would be no sudden herd, no bright-winged visitors, no mouths introduced merely to make the scene familiar. The power would understand that mouths transform everything around them. Grazing can renew, prune, stir, fertilize, and carry—but it can also erase lives that possess no defenses because they have never needed them.

The first mouths, if they came, would come later and smaller.

They would arise in the temporary waters from possibilities already latent there: cells that engulfed other cells, colonies that learned coordinated movement, minute bodies able to cross the width of a droplet before it dried. The power would not design them in finished form. It would only keep open the narrow doors through which novelty sometimes enters.

If, after ages, one such creature swam through a basin film and left a clear line behind it, the power would regard that line without preference. It would not value the swimmer above the film it consumed. Both would be participants in a relationship the plain had never held before. Whether the relationship endured would belong to them and to the water.

There would be disasters the power allowed.

A court would dry too soon, killing every stalk before its cases matured. A living cushion centuries old would peel from its pylon and fall upon bare ceramic, where it would bleach to nothing. Salt would invade a fertile seam. A buried plate would collapse and crush a whole hidden network.

Benevolence would not mean preserving every arrangement forever. To preserve all things exactly is to forbid succession, adaptation, birth, and any future not identical to the past.

But there would also be endings the power quietly turned aside.

A rare chain of heat and failed storms might otherwise sterilize every shallow refuge at once. Then clouds would gather from a reservoir farther away than usual. A single night of rain would cross the plain.

A fracture might propagate toward the last deep damp chamber while all others stood dry. The fracture would branch harmlessly into rubble.

A salt bloom might enclose the only surviving lineage of a slow stone-dwelling organism. One crystal would split under its own growth, exposing a pore to dew.

These interventions would not be rewards. The films would not have earned rain, nor the fungi rescue. The power would act because small and unknowing lives need not justify their continuation.

Eventually the inhabitants would alter the mercies given to them.

Roots would widen the loosened channels. Films would line the new passages and slow their flow. Cushions would divert droplets from one stepping-stone to another. The underground refuge would acquire sediment, then dark material, then a shallow living margin around its cool water. The plain would take each gift out of the giver’s hands and make it local, contingent, and its own.

That would be the purpose of the power’s restraint.

Not to make the country flourish according to a foreign image.

Not to preserve the immense construction.

Not to complete the intentions of its vanished makers.

Only to ensure that weather and life had enough time, enough difference, and enough imperfect connection to discover what this place could become.

After that, the power would withdraw its touch but not its attention.

Centuries later, no monument would mark what it had done. There would be no inscription beneath the plates. The channels would appear eroded. The reservoir would seem an accident of settling stone. The surviving lineages would carry no message and offer no gratitude.

Yet after certain storms, water would travel farther than the original design had allowed. Separate greenings would meet along dark seams. Spores from one horizon would wake under another. New forms would appear in old basins, not miraculous, only possible.

From above, the dispersed being would still have no center and no awareness. It would remain many lives, waking and sleeping at the speed of weather.

But it would no longer be merely enduring.

It would have begun, slowly, to inherit the plain.
