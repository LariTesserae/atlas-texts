The power would not begin by bringing light.

Light would blind the pale flyers, expose every heat-soft body to predators that had never needed eyes, and wake chemistries long held dormant in the shale. Nor would it raise forests. Roots would split the seep fields, drink the shallow water, and poison whole lineages with an abundance for which they had no use.

It would not silence the basin.

Silence, imposed all at once, would be worse than darkness. Routes would disappear. Young would lose the measures by which they entered the migrations. Hunters would strike unseen in a new and terrible way. The broad-backs would circle until the stone beneath them became bowls.

A benevolent power would know that mercy cannot always resemble rescue.

So it would descend without taking a shape.

It would enter the basin as a pressure too slight to alarm the smallest shell-creature. It would pass through cloud, flesh, mud, and limestone. The pale flyers would adjust their membranes and drift a little higher. The tall filterers would lift their hanging heads. Across the broad-backed herds, breathing pores would pause in uncertain bands.

Then the power would listen.

It would listen longer than any creature lived.

At first it would hear only collision: thirst answering rain, hunger answering flesh, fear answering the sudden absence of a neighboring pulse. Beneath that would be the routes, carried in long measures from body to body. Beneath the routes, the signatures of old disasters: a fissure that had opened centuries ago, a poisoned seep, a migration lost when too many elders died at once. Deeper still, almost inseparable from the trembling of the crust, it would hear the basin’s oldest pattern.

It was not a voice. It did not say *help us*.

It was a rhythm that continually made room for a beat which never came.

The power would answer in the only language broad enough to reach the inhabitant whole.

Once, very softly, it would become the missing beat.

Every broad-back in contact with another would feel it. The signal would enter through feeding plates, shell colonies, water bladders, pressed flanks, and the narrow limbs of creatures standing across several bodies at once. It would travel under the stone fins and through the flexible crust of the gas flat. Burrowers would stop beneath the mud. Predators would loosen their grips. Flyers would descend and settle wherever they could.

For the first time in the basin’s history, the whole crowd would pause without fleeing.

The pause would last no longer than a breath.

Then the basin would answer.

Not coherently. Not with one desire. Millions of local measures would strike against one another. The thirsty would call for rain. The drowning would call for dry stone. The hunted would ask for the ending of teeth; the starving hunters would ask that teeth remain. Ribbon-young would signal warmth. Egg-bearers would signal safety. Parasites would ask for hosts without defenses. Hosts would ask for clean blood. Ancient broad-backs would repeat routes whose destinations had ceased to exist.

The wise power would refuse none of this hearing, and obey none of it blindly.

It would not abolish death. In that place, death was shelter, nursery, mineral store, and feast. To remove it would be to heap the basin with undying hunger.

It would not abolish pain entirely. Pain was among the pressures by which a body found its wounds and a herd detected the predator moving below.

It would not make every creature harmless. Harmlessness forced upon the hunters would only be another manner of killing them.

Instead, the power would seek what suffering served no life.

It would find water lost too quickly into blind depths, beyond every rootless mouth. Far below the shale, it would close certain cracks and widen others. It would shape no river and make no shining lake around which the multitude could crush itself. Rather, throughout the basin, thousands of modest seeps would begin to breathe.

No seep would yield enough to summon a great migration. Each would wet only a shelf, a hollow, the underside of a stone fin. Water would cease to be found solely by catastrophe. There would still be thirst, but less often the thirst that drove whole districts into false-rain pits.

The power would touch the reproduction of the smallest lives most gently of all.

It would add no commandment and inflict no barrenness. It would place a new sensitivity in egg, spore, ribbon, and divided flesh: an awareness of the pressure of abundance. Where bodies lay too densely for young to emerge without immediate crushing, eggs would sleep. Ribbons would postpone their change. Spores would seal themselves in mineral skins.

When space returned, they would wake.

The basin would remain crowded, but it would no longer be compelled at every instant to fill every possible absence. Abundance would learn patience.

Then the power would enter the rounded stone fins.

It would alter no outward shape. It would only align certain crystals within them, laying vein beside vein through the mineral deposits. Thereafter, when a herd pressed a route-rhythm into the fins, the stone would retain a trace. Weeks or centuries later, the passage of feet or thunder might release it again.

The basin would gain a memory outside flesh.

Not a perfect one. Perfect memory would imprison migrations in vanished worlds. The stone would blur old measures and mingle them with new ones. But no route would disappear merely because a generation died together in a flood. No elder would carry the whole burden of being a landmark.

Last, the power would give something to the broad-backs themselves.

It would not give them human thought. It would not put names inside them or divide the herd into millions of lonely kingdoms.

It would give each mature body one small rhythm that belonged to no neighbor.

The rhythm would arise from the turn of its organs, the number of ribbons that had fused to make it, the scars through its water bladders, the particular unevenness of its feeding plates. No two would be alike. Each broad-back would feel its own measure even when separated from the press.

A solitary one would still hunger for contact. It would still be less capable alone. But it would no longer circle its own track until death. It could hold a direction. It could carry a fragment of a route without being wedged among others. When it joined the herd, its breathing might align—or, for a while, not align.

The herd would have to learn invitation.

This would cause confusion. There would be districts where the old unison broke into rough, competing measures. Some migrations would divide. Certain elders, followed for generations because their bodies had become geography, would turn and find that not everything turned with them.

Yet from this disorder would come a deeper assembly: not merely bodies compelled into one rhythm by pressure, but rhythms able to enter, depart, return, and change the whole.

The power would remain through the first consequences.

It would not make its gifts and withdraw before they could wound.

When patient eggs accumulated too thickly in one hollow, it would let a seep shift and wake them in stages. When the stone fins returned an ancient route toward a collapsed shelf, it would soften the echo with the new shape of the land. When predators learned to imitate the private rhythms of the broad-backs, it would not destroy them, but it would give the herd the capacity to answer with variations no mimic could sustain alone.

Slowly, the basin would change.

Not into a garden.

The cloud would remain. Lightning would still move without sound in its upper chambers. Carcasses would still become hills of feeding life. The mud would still open under careless weight. The air would still be used before any creature breathed it.

But here and there, the crowd would thin without panic.

A patch of shale might remain uncovered for an hour. Then for half a day.

Small runners would approach its edge and turn aside, not because a predator waited there, but because the new pressure within them said: there is no need yet.

One broad-back, old enough to carry white ridges of shells across its scars, would enter such a clearing. Deprived of neighboring flesh, its communal pulse would fade. For several breaths it would stand alone.

Under its rotating mouth, the naked stone would be cold.

Above it, the cloud would lower, and condensation would bead along its hide. Its own rhythm would move through its organs: irregular, scarred, unlike any measure held in the stone.

The creature would not understand solitude. It would not call the moment beautiful. It would not know that a power had heard it.

It would simply remain where it was, neither lost nor compelled.

Then it would send one pulse into the ground.

From beyond the clearing, the herd would answer—not by closing over the space, but from its edge.

The old broad-back would answer again.

And in the interval between the two, the basin would possess something it had never possessed before:

not silence,

not emptiness,

but room.
