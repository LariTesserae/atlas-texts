The power does not descend.

Descent would presume a direction, and here direction is partly a matter of memory. Nor does it appear in the sky, for the sky already contains delayed rain, adjacent constellations, and light belonging to several different mornings. To add a sign there would be to speak over what is already being said.

It begins by attending.

Its attention is immense, but it does not let immensity become pressure. It makes itself no heavier than condensation on a fern. It watches one narrow rain cross the plateau and does not redirect it. It follows the water into the black-lined aqueducts, through gates swollen by moisture, along ceramic grooves, and down into the cisterns where keepers wait with their inward-opening hands.

It watches them cover the reservoirs.

It watches a shallow figure deepen from a wall over eighty-three rainy seasons. It watches another enter a garden and remain there so long that the distinction between rest and rooting ceases to matter. It listens to doors composing light across plazas. It learns the meanings of silver leaves, warm drafts, sealed frost, and the single deep notes laid into the bedrock.

Because it is wise, it does not call the place broken merely because its laws have softened.

Because it is benevolent, it does not call the inhabitants lonely merely because they are few.

Because it is powerful, it feels no need to prove power by repair.

There is much it could do. It could lower the plateau into richer air. It could open springs in every hollow, draw rivers across the stone, restore the mountains, sharpen the valleys, and fill the gardens with quick-winged creatures. It could straighten time and teach every road to hold one direction. It could separate the keepers from the walls, give them blood and voices, number their fingers, and make their deaths unmistakable.

It does none of these things.

A gift that abolishes the receiver is only conquest performed gently.

Instead, the power asks.

The asking takes a long time.

At the western edge of the city stands a plaza no map includes, though many routes pass before it. Its paving is white quartz interrupted by one rectangle of red slate. At the center is a bowl carved directly into the bedrock. The bowl is usually dry. Rain falling into it is remembered by a cistern elsewhere, and water poured into it appears beneath a house that has not yet been built.

One evening, the power places nothing in the bowl.

The nothing is exact.

It is not emptiness, of which the plateau already has abundance. It is room: a small interval freed from prior use, consequence, and recollection. The bowl ceases, for the moment, to contain what it formerly held or will someday hold. No old rain claims it. No future hand has already covered it. It waits without having waited before.

The city notices.

A condensation gate lifts in dry weather. Three doors open upon a single wall. Moss withdraws from a missing stair and grows instead in a circle around the red slate. Far beneath the plaza, an elder building changes temperature.

Keepers begin to arrive.

Some detach from pillars. Some rise from gardens with black leaves folded against their chests. One comes as pale light carried beneath an empty robe. Another is visible only by the frost that refuses to form where it stands. They surround the bowl at great distances, several streets apart, yet each is equally near.

For nine nights, no one touches it.

On the tenth, a keeper places its hand against the paving. A note enters the plateau, low enough to be mistaken for the settling of ancient stone.

The power receives the note.

It is not a word, but the power understands.

The note contains the red cistern after rain. It contains a wall carrying a half-formed child. It contains the warmth inside an abandoned hearth, preserved for someone whose arrival has been delayed by six centuries. It contains a keeper becoming a column and a column still listening. It contains fear—not fear of death, precisely, but fear that all remaining events may be no more than repetitions thinning with each occurrence.

It asks:

*Have we a future that is not merely weathering?*

Then other notes follow.

From beneath gardens, houses, roads, and empty plazas, the city answers itself. Some tones are as brief as droplets. Others take hours to pass. An aqueduct hums. Quartz bands beyond the horizon ring softly in their buried lengths. Upright figures touch wrists beneath condensation roofs. The silver undersides of leaves spread outward in immense, ordered phrases.

The inhabitants do not ask for permanence.

They know that nothing living is honored by being forbidden to end.

They do not ask for abundance. Abundance without shape would drown the gardens and make meaningless the exact mercy of a bowl of water.

They do not ask to become separate from the city. Separation, to them, would be a mutilation.

They ask for three things, though the asking of them requires an entire season.

First: that what has not happened remain capable of surprise.

Second: that the children forming in walls and flowers inherit more than the exhaustion of the old.

Third: that when the last keeper lays down the last tool—whether in a thousand years or a hundred million—something will have understood what was tended here.

The power considers.

Not because the requests are difficult for it, but because ease is dangerous in the hands of power. It considers every consequence down to the roots drawing from vanished pools and the rain that has already fallen but not yet crossed the horizon.

Then it answers in the language of the place.

Into the empty bowl it puts a morning.

Not a stored morning. Not one taken from beneath an eave or recovered from a jar. This morning has no date. It has never shone elsewhere. No courtyard possesses it, no star has promised it, and no cloud remembers passing through it.

The light is pale and ordinary.

It fills only the bowl.

The keepers lean toward it. Their shallow faces acquire edges. Moisture trembles in their dark eye hollows. Along the walls, reliefs not yet deep enough to move turn their heads.

Nothing miraculous seems to occur.

Then one of the inhabitants reaches inward with its hand and lifts the bowl by the hidden grip that the stone has never possessed before.

The impossible handle was not there in the preceding moment. It was not remembered by the rock. No future craftsman had carved it. It is simply new.

A motion passes through the gathered keepers—not surprise as an animal body might show it, but a change of alignment. Robes loosen from old verticals. Leaves reverse and then reverse again. Several doors open onto places for which no conditions have yet been written.

The first request is answered.

What has not happened has been given a small country in which to begin.

The power answers the second request more slowly.

It does not renew the plateau. To make it young would erase the dignity of its age. The long shoulders of stone remain. Frost continues lifting films from the pavements. Condensation loosens grains one by one. The continent continues toward smoothness.

But from that day, not every loosened grain is lost.

A small proportion—so small no keeper first detects it—falls otherwise.

Dust lifted from a red slate plain may settle inside the dream of a dormant seed. A flake of mica may appear as a bright thought in a forming wall-child. Minerals washed into inaccessible cracks gather into thin, dark soil beneath gardens not yet located anywhere. When an old carving loses the edge of a hand, that edge does not return to the carving; instead, somewhere in the city, a young inhabitant acquires a gesture no elder has made.

The power does not halt erosion.

It teaches erosion how to be inheritance.

Age continues, but exhaustion no longer passes whole into the young.

The change spreads without haste. Mirrored flowers begin producing forms that are not exact images of the parent. Most variations are slight: an unfamiliar seam, a leaf folding in response to clear weather, a hand with a number of fingers upon which two witnesses can agree. Some fail and return quietly to moss. Others step from their translucent enclosures and perform duties for which the city has no existing name.

A new keeper appears at a dry aqueduct and waits there for three days. The elders arrange warnings around it: frost on the eastern stair, three bowls with the second covered, a black spiral beneath the nearest arch.

The young keeper does not leave.

On the fourth day, it lays a tool against the aqueduct and cuts a groove that leads neither toward an old cistern nor a remembered channel.

The watching city becomes very still.

At sunset, condensation enters the new groove.

The water travels seven paces and gathers in a shallow depression.

Nothing drinks it. Nothing has planned for it. It serves no present need.

A black-leaved shoot rises there the following year.

Thus the second request is answered.

For the third, the power makes no object and changes no law.

It remains.

Not visibly. Visibility would eventually turn it into a monument, and monuments here become inhabitants or weather. It remains as a capacity within the place: not an overseer, not a ruler, but a witness that cannot be worn away by witnessing.

Every deep note entering the bedrock reaches it.

Every door-sentence of light is seen.

It knows when an elder district closes its last gate and becomes still. It knows the difference between a keeper resting as a column and a column from which the keeper has finally gone. It remembers the bowls set for companions who arrive late. It receives each act of maintenance, including those performed in plazas no route can reach.

Yet the power does not hoard these things outside the world, as though their meaning required removal to some safer realm.

It gives witness back.

Sometimes, when a keeper performs an unnoticed kindness—a frost-split seed turned toward the sun, a young relief moved from a failing wall, one measure of stored summer opened for an elder—the act returns elsewhere as a little ease. A heavy gate lifts before the hand reaches it. A road remains correctly aligned for one extra hour. Water follows not the most remembered channel, but the one that leads to thirst.

The keepers come to recognize this, though they never name the source.

They respond with courtesy.

In a sheltered court, they construct no temple. Instead they leave one place unfinished.

It is a narrow section of wall between two completed chambers. Tools rest nearby. Slabs have been measured. Moss is trimmed from the foundation. Everything required to finish it is present, but no one does.

The opening faces the plateau.

Rain enters through it. Frost outlines it. On clear nights it frames stars that belong to different courtyards. Sometimes an inhabitant stands beside the unfinished place and lays one hand on the edge, feeling the smooth cut end where completion might have begun.

This is their acknowledgment of the power: not worship, but room.

Centuries pass.

The city does not become crowded. Animals do not suddenly appear. The air remains thin. Water remains precious. Some seeds wait lifetimes to split. Some children never leave their walls. Old inhabitants root into plazas, and districts fall silent one by one.

There is grief.

The benevolent power does not prevent it. To prevent every grief, it would have to prevent attachment, change, and the existence of irreplaceable things. But grief no longer vanishes unheard into the stone. When two keepers touch wrists beneath the condensation roofs for the final time, a little warmth remains in the ceramic channel afterward—not enough to resurrect, not enough to deny the ending, only enough that the survivor does not mistake loss for absence of meaning.

There is also invention.

The new grooves multiply. A stair is built that descends only when climbed. A garden learns to draw moisture not from remembered rain but from rain that might occur. For one hour each year, a house with no interior becomes spacious enough to shelter every keeper who considers entering it, though no two occupy the same room.

And at the unfinished wall, a road gradually appears.

No one builds it.

It does not lead down from the plateau or outward into another country. It runs a short distance across the pavement and ends among the silver grasses.

The road has one property unlike the other roads of the city: it leads toward whatever approaches in goodwill.

Most years, nothing travels it.

Still, the keepers maintain it.

They clear frost from its edges. They set bowls of water at long intervals. They mark safe conditions in moss and light. Children newly emerged from walls are brought to its beginning and shown the horizon.

Perhaps, in an age beyond counting, someone will come.

Perhaps no one will.

The road is not a promise of company. It is the refusal to make solitude absolute.

At evening, the last keeper still crosses the plaza and places lids over the cisterns. It is very far away. Then it is near enough that frost can be heard forming on its sleeves.

It kneels beside the stone bowl at the center of the red slate.

The undated morning remains within, though it has been divided and shared ten thousand times. Its light has grown no brighter. It is merely present: pale, cold, and new.

The keeper lays one long hand against the pavement.

A deep note passes into the plateau.

Across the continent, old walls receive it. Gardens reverse their leaves. Children sleeping as shallow reliefs turn slightly in the stone. Far beneath the smooth surfaces, grains loosened by frost fall toward forms that have never existed.

The wise power listens.

It does not answer with thunder, revelation, or command.

It answers by leaving the future open.

Then the rain, traveling beyond the horizon toward the place where it has already fallen, encounters one narrow region of sky that does not remember it.

For the first time in the age of the plateau, a cloud hesitates.

And chooses where to rain.
