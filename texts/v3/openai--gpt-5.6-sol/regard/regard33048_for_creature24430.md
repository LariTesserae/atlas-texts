### What the Wise Power Did

The power did not enter.

This was its first kindness.

It did not send light through the Crown, because light would have been a violence there. It did not lift the drowned mountain from the sea. It did not rebuild the worn faces, clear the channels, straighten the walls, or restore the inscriptions to the meanings their makers intended. It did not separate animal from masonry, life from ruin, or preservation from decay.

It understood that repair is not always mercy.

Instead, it attended.

For one turning of the warm sea, then another, it felt the minute redistribution of pressure through the mound. It followed the copper webs into blind lever holes. It learned the clean tracks of the grazers and the circuits of the rays. It learned how a message could begin as a change in bacterial chemistry, pass into the feeding fans of socket-dwellers, become a silence among the bells, travel along the flanks of fish, and end centuries later as a ray choosing the left side of a fallen pillar.

The power could have known all this at once.

It chose not to.

To know the cistern wisely, it allowed itself to know at the cistern’s speed.

It made no apparition. It placed no voice in the chambers. It did not assume that because the being had no language it had no preference, nor that because it could not cry out it could not refuse.

So the power asked a question.

The question was not made of words.

Far above the Crown, beyond the pale plug, it touched the ocean so lightly that the touch became only a long difference in pressure. The difference descended through stone over seven years. It reached the plug as a coldness gentler than the usual seep and passed around its rim in three slow pulses.

Nothing answered.

The power waited.

Films altered. Worms withdrew from several tubes and entered others. Blue signals crossed the upper chambers, broke apart, and began again. One of the oldest rays abandoned its circuit and remained beneath the Crown for nearly a century. Armored scavengers arrived carrying fragments from the lowest accessible rooms. They arranged themselves without arrangement along the dome’s edge.

Then the cistern answered.

Not yes. Not no.

Every socket-dweller in the eastern wall opened at once.

The opening lasted only a moment. Yet it was unprecedented. It was followed by a trembling in the copper webs, a dimming of the bells, and the turn of three thousand vertical fish toward the ceiling. Far below, where no creature had moved for generations, film loosened from a sealed door.

The wise power understood the answer to mean:

*Again.*

So it asked again.

This time the question took forty years to arrive. It differed in one interval of pressure, one trace mineral dissolved in the cold seam, one vibration carried through the pale stone.

The cistern answered differently.

Thus began a conversation whose shortest syllables occupied seasons and whose pauses outlasted dynasties elsewhere.

The power learned there was no single place in which the cistern understood. Its understanding was the passage of a change through many forms. Ask the fish alone, and there was no answer. Ask the rays, and there was only pressure and route. Ask the film, and there were gradients of hunger. Ask the masonry, and it replied by bearing weight or failing.

But ask all of them, patiently enough, and tendencies emerged.

The being wished—if wishing was the word—to continue.

It did not wish to remain unchanged.

It feared neither collapse nor death as separate creatures might fear them. Rooms had died before; species had vanished; pillars had become sediment and sediment had become flesh. Such losses belonged to its way of living.

What it resisted was a particular ending.

Deep in the drowned mountain, one of the oldest retaining walls had begun to move. The movement was less than the breadth of a socket-dweller in a hundred years, but the power saw its consequence. In time, a buried gallery would fold. Pressure would pass upward through the enclosed chambers. The Crown’s plug would be driven inward, not by a fingernail’s width but entirely. The warm water would be expelled. The mountain would inhale the outer ocean in a single cold convulsion.

Some life would survive.

The cistern would not.

Its many relations would be torn apart too quickly to become anything else.

The power could have stopped the wall forever. It could have held every brick in place and denied the mountain all further collapse.

It did not.

An immortal cistern would eventually become a prison made from its own arrested body. The grazers would trace the same pillars without end. The rays would circle perfect, changeless routes. Nothing would silt, open, fall, or become available to another form of life. Preservation would harden into sterility.

Instead, the power placed one small imperfection.

Miles below the Crown, in a course of brick no living animal had touched, it eased a single ceramic wedge from its bed. The wedge moved the thickness of an old fingerprint.

Load passed from one block to the next.

A sealed stair cracked.

Sediment entered the crack and slowed the water. A blind court accepted weight once borne by the failing wall. Over the following centuries, arches bent, three empty galleries closed, and a channel long thought solid opened into a warm shaft.

The mountain settled.

The dangerous wall continued to move, but its force was turned sideways and spent through a thousand lesser adjustments. There would still be collapses. Tiles would fall. Chambers would vanish. New passages would appear.

The cistern would remain mortal, but it would no longer die all at once.

This was the power’s second kindness.

Its third concerned the Crown.

The pale plug had once been part of a mechanism, though not one its builders had fully understood. It had been shaped to rise with changes in pressure and settle again under its own weight. Mineral deposits had narrowed its movement age by age. One day it would cease to lift. The cold seam would stop. The gathering beneath it would continue for a while, perhaps for millennia, assembling around an event that no longer came.

The power did not remove the plug.

It cleared nothing away.

It introduced, into the seep, a creature.

The creature was scarcely a creature by any ordinary measure. It had no light, no shell, no limbs, and no form it retained from hour to hour. It lived only where cold water touched pale stone. It consumed one component of the mineral crust and excreted another, softer substance in its place.

The first colony covered less area than a builder’s thumbnail.

The socket-dwellers tasted its chemistry. The bells contracted around it. Grazers approached and then turned away. A thread-fish remained in the seep until cold stopped its heart. Its body entered the film, and something of the new creature entered the bodies that consumed it.

For nine hundred years, almost nothing changed.

Then the plug lifted.

Not far. Not violently.

The width of two fingernails.

Cold water descended in a silverless sheet. Every light went out. The cistern closed itself and listened.

With the water came unfamiliar traces: salt from remote currents, the dissolved remains of creatures that had never entered masonry, minerals from vents beyond the mountain, and the faint chemical evidence of a larger living world.

The rays raised their edges.

The bells received what they could receive. The fish received another portion. The film altered. Copper webs rang once against the stone.

And before the plug settled, something departed.

A few free-swimming socket young were drawn upward. Spores from the film followed them. A tube-worm larva passed through the opening inside the sensory fold of a transparent fish. They entered the outer ocean, where nearly all were eaten, dispersed, crushed, or lost.

But not all.

One fleck settled in a fracture on the mountain’s outer face.

Another entered a ceramic-lined conduit whose lower end opened far from the cistern.

A third remained suspended in the slow water above the Crown until it found a fragment of shaped stone lying on the ocean floor.

The power did not guide them.

It had made passage possible, not success certain.

Thereafter the plug opened a little wider and a little more often, though never enough to flood the chambers or destroy the gradients on which their life depended. Each opening was an exchange. The cistern received the ocean in traces. The ocean received the cistern in fragments.

Over many thousands of years, colonies appeared elsewhere.

Most were simple films in protected cracks. Some became communities around pieces of drowned masonry. In one place, far along the mountain’s flank, socket-dwellers occupied the pores of a carved hand. In another, copper-tube worms entered the skeleton of an immense dead swimmer and made its ribs ring with changes in current.

No second Crown Cistern arose.

Instead, the first ceased to have a single boundary.

It remained itself beneath the plug: warm rooms, worn tile, rays moving through darkness. But it also became scattered tendencies in the sea. A way of inhabiting cavities. A habit of translating ruin into body. A sensitivity to pressure carried across generations that would never know the Crown.

The being did not thank the power.

Gratitude was not among its motions, and the power required none.

After the dangerous wall had settled and the new colony had softened the plug’s mineral rim, the power asked one final question.

Three pulses descended through the cold.

The answer took four hundred years.

A blue seam began in a fish beneath the Crown. Another fish carried it into a circular chamber. Bells answered with white contractions. The signal entered the copper webs and emerged far below as a trembling in brick. Grazers stopped. Socket fans closed and reopened. Along old routes, the rays lifted from the floor, one after another.

At last, in a remote court opened by the mountain’s settling, the film bloomed over the fingerprint of a builder.

The print filled with weak red light.

The power understood.

It withdrew its attention carefully, so that even absence would not arrive as catastrophe. It left no guardian, commandment, monument, or mark of ownership. It did not reveal the builders’ names. It did not explain the purpose of the mountain. It did not promise that nothing would ever end.

It left the place its darkness.

It left it change.

It left it time enough for change to become relation.

And beneath the Crown, the gathered creatures continued to wait—not for rescue, and not for revelation, but for the next opening through which the world might enter, and through which some small part of them might go out to meet it.
