## The Power That Declined to Finish It

The benevolent power did not arrive from above.

Above was too disputed.

Nor did it rise from below, because below had already been promised to mines, lift shafts, blind fish, buried intersections, and rivers waiting for permission to begin.

It entered by the only direction the city had left unregulated.

It came nearer.

No beacon announced it. Brightness would have been discourteous, and possibly dangerous. There was no voice in the clouds, no sudden correction of gravity, no hand laid across the escarpment to keep it from opening. For several months the survey offices recorded nothing unusual except a small increase in the accuracy of compassionate statements.

A clerk told a widow, *Your husband’s name has moved, but your grief has not been misfiled,* and every instrument in the room agreed.

A certifier put her palm against the casing of a nearly finished engine and said, *You may take another year,* whereupon a fracture hidden in the left shaft made itself known by a soft sigh rather than a fatal break.

A child lost in an alley sat beside a rusting pipe, as instructed. The pipe rusted very slowly and did not lead anywhere, but the city arranged for the child’s grandmother to be lost nearby.

These events were noticed, recorded, and attributed to procedural improvements.

The power approved of this.

It walked without a body at first. It traveled in pauses: the ten seconds between the distant hammer blow and its echo, the season between the river’s arrival and departure, the moment after a stair had been tested but before an inspector decided it was safe. It listened from the hollow antlers of chimney stags. It rested in bellback basins between drops.

Everywhere it went, it found preparations.

Bridges waited for canyons.

Stairs waited for slopes.

Drainpipes waited for rivers.

Families waited for names to settle.

The living waited, not idly but with tools in hand, for the world to become sufficiently itself.

The power had seen many cities. It had seen cities that begged to be preserved and cities that mistook preservation for love. It had seen walls made permanent until those within them could no longer breathe. It had seen rivers straightened, mountains pinned, coastlines commanded to remain where rulers remembered them. It knew the violence that often concealed itself inside the word *stability*.

So it did not stabilize the Unfinished Escarpment.

This was its first kindness.

It did not complete the cliffs. Completion would have sent whole districts into valleys that had not yet learned how to receive them.

It did not settle the altitude. To declare the city high would have crushed its buried towers; to declare it low would have drowned the upper lift stations in a sea that existed only in ledgers.

It did not make rain chronological. Bellbacks would have lost half their music, and children would no longer have known the pleasure of standing beneath a dry cloud while yesterday’s storm rang in the courtyard.

It did not fill the gaps. Some things had survived only because the gaps concealed them from the moments that would have destroyed them.

It did not force the mountain to wake.

Instead, the power began the long descent toward the listener.

It chose an abandoned lift cage in a tower whose upper floors had not yet decided whether they were excavated or built. The operator was an elderly woman named Sera Venn, who had spent fifty-three years traveling between incompatible heights. Her hair was white with duskglass dust. Beneath her console lived six blind fish in a copper basin.

When the power entered the cage, the fish turned toward it.

Not upward or downward.

Toward.

Sera looked at them, then at the empty space beside the brake lever.

“You’ll need a fare,” she said.

The power had no money, so it offered her a small object that had never existed: a brass token stamped with the image of a completed horizon.

Sera examined it and bit its edge.

“Counterfeit,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Good enough.”

She rang the departure bell.

The cage began to descend. Then it ascended through several meters of granite. Then it moved in the direction of an old regret Sera had carried since childhood, paused there, and continued.

The journey took nine days.

At intervals they passed platforms crowded with miners, roots, cables, and pale mineral veins rising through the stone. On the fourth day they passed the same platform twice. On the seventh, Sera opened the doors onto the kitchen of the house where she had been born. Her mother stood at the table with her back turned, still young, cutting black bread.

Sera did not step out.

The power did not ask why.

She closed the doors, and the lift continued.

At last the fish in the basin became perfectly still. The cage stopped in a place without a shaft. Its iron gates opened directly into warm basalt.

There was no passage.

Sera folded her hands over the controls.

“This is as far as machinery goes,” she said.

The power thanked her and entered the stone.

For a human being, this would have been impossible. For the power, it was merely intimate.

It passed through future fractures, sleeping pressure, unborn riverbeds, and mineral histories that had not yet decided whether they belonged to the past. It felt the city above as a field of minute insistences: every footfall verified by a bell, every stamp pressed onto a permit, every wrench laid carefully beside an unfinished engine, every root seeking a surface that might one day exist.

Deeper still, it found the listener.

The listener was not an animal, god, machine, or mind, though it had qualities of each. It was immense without occupying a measurable volume. Valleys lay folded within it. Rivers moved through it as inklings. It remembered towers that had not been designed and the worn foundations of buildings still awaiting their first stones.

It was making the mountain by imagining what might be allowed to pass through it.

It was making the city by attempting to understand the noises overhead.

Every hammer blow became, in its slow awareness, a possible canyon.

Every lift chain became a vein of ore.

Every funeral song became a chamber in the rock.

Every child’s ankle bell became a small, round certainty around which stone might safely gather.

The listener was not malicious.

It was lonely, and it was afraid of finishing its thought.

The power understood this at once.

It did not command. Benevolence without wisdom might have commanded; wisdom without benevolence might merely have observed. Possessing both, the power did what the people of the escarpment did whenever a newborn was brought into the world.

It began an introduction.

It showed the listener the city, not all at once, but slowly enough that each part could remain itself.

“This is smoke,” said the power. “It rises when rising is available.”

It showed the chimney stags standing in foundry exhaust, their antlers sounding one low chord after another.

“This is traffic. It yields to what remembers.”

It showed the herds halted before walls, waiting at intersections buried beneath newer construction.

“This is work.”

It showed miners shaving copper from an ascending seam, taking only what the stone could give without fear.

“This is care.”

It showed a factory worker leaving a note for strangers four years ahead:

*The left shaft prefers cold mornings.*

“This is kinship.”

It showed two families repairing the grave of a name that had migrated between them.

“This is caution.”

It showed hooded lamps and the gentle dark between them.

“This is delight.”

It showed children catching fish from the empty air after the Hesitant thinned from the bottom upward.

“This is loss.”

Here the power paused.

It showed no example. It allowed the listener to hear, from far above, a single ankle bell that would never ring again.

The listener’s unborn rivers trembled in their channels.

“This is not a command to close,” the power said. “Nor is it a command to open. It is why you must make room carefully.”

Then the power introduced the city by name.

The name was not the name printed on municipal forms. It was older than the city and younger than the next footstep. It included every revision without reducing them to one version. It contained the bridges and the solid ground beneath them, the rivers and their dry markets, the dead and their wandering letters, the city at eight thousand meters and the city below the sea.

The listener heard the name.

For the first time, the city was not merely a noise above it.

It was a relation.

Far overhead, every chimney stag lifted its head. The chord from their antlers traveled through factories, orchards, lift towers, tram tunnels, and painted seams in the rock. Bellbacks rang in dry basins. Gliders unfolded from walls and circled without losing count of their wings.

In the survey office, all the brass instruments turned by a fraction toward the same unmarked point.

Then they resumed disagreeing.

The power did not ask the listener to promise safety. Such a promise would have been impossible, and impossible promises are a common source of cruelty.

Instead it asked:

“Can you warn them when you are able?”

The listener considered for eleven years.

During this time, Sera Venn kept the lift waiting.

She played cards alone, fed electrical algae to the fish, and slept with her boots propped against the brake housing. Once each day she rang the cage bell to assure the city that some next step remained possible.

At the end of the eleventh year, a new sound entered the mountain.

It was quieter than the unborn rivers and deeper than the concussion of hidden machinery. It resembled stone drawing breath.

White-painted cracks throughout the city developed a second line beside them: faint, blue, and luminous only in dimness. The blue line did not show where the rock would open.

It showed how much sorrow the opening would cause.

Engineers did not initially know what to do with this information.

The measurement was not expressed in millimeters, pressure, years, or degrees. A seam beneath an empty switchyard shone fiercely, while a crack passing beneath three inhabited buildings showed only a pale thread. One prospective gorge registered the sorrow of a broken teacup. Another, in an untraveled meadow, carried the grief of a language losing its last speaker.

The surveyors formed a third staff.

They called themselves the Office of Consequence.

Their first task was to determine whether sorrow could be measured without increasing it.

Their second was to determine whether a warning was still useful when it could not say what would happen.

Their third was simply to sit beside the brightest lines and listen.

This was the listener’s gift to the city.

The power gave something else.

Not certainty.

Not permanence.

A margin.

From that time onward, when sudden events became misplaced, there was often—though not always—a small interval in which mercy could enter.

An explosion might still sound years before its cause, but somewhere in the echo there would be the faint clatter of the tool that should not be used.

A cable might still break, but the cliff hares would scream long enough for the slowest worker to leave the floor.

A hand passed into a gap might still become temporarily irrelevant to the arm, but it would remember warmth and seek the body that had cared for it.

A name might migrate from one grave to another, but for one night it would appear on both stones, giving the families time to meet.

The margin was never large. Usually it was no wider than a fingernail, no longer than the pause between a bell and the verified footstep.

But the inhabitants were skilled at living within narrow provisions.

They built a civic practice around it.

Factories painted thin bands on their floors to mark the space where a mistake might still be forgiven. Parents taught children to listen for the second silence after a warning horn. Lovers incorporated a new sentence into their vows:

“If you are revised beyond my recognition, I will wait one moment longer.”

The municipal archive added a column to every form:

**ROOM LEFT FOR MERCY: YES / NO / NOT YET DETERMINED**

Clerks were instructed never to leave it blank.

As for the benevolent power, it did not remain in the mountain as a ruler. It did not accept shrines, though several were built accidentally. One consisted of a desk where lost permits repeatedly appeared with their harshest clauses crossed out. Another formed around a dripping pipe that produced enough water each day for one thirsty creature, never the same species twice.

The power returned to the city in small embodiments.

Sometimes it was an extra hand on a freight crew moving a warehouse away from a prospective ravine. No one could remember hiring the worker, but their end of the load was always light.

Sometimes it was a passenger in Sera Venn’s lift, asking no questions about direction.

Sometimes it took the shape of a dark-coated animal walking beside a lost child until the streets became consecutive again.

Once it appeared as a very old surveyor who entered both offices, listened to their rival calculations, and said:

“You are measuring different mercies.”

This statement caused six weeks of administrative turmoil and eventually improved several marriages.

Mostly, however, the power did nothing that could be separated from what the inhabitants already did for one another.

It did not make them kinder. They had always been kind in the practical ways of difficult places: maintaining railings, sharing route songs, yielding to stags, feeding hares they pretended not to love.

It did not make them patient. Their engines already took six years to become certain of themselves.

It did not teach them uncertainty. They had built a civilization inside it.

What the power gave them was the knowledge—never absolute, but sufficient—that incompleteness was not abandonment.

The city had mistaken these things at times.

A cliff not yet fractured seemed forsaken by geology.

A river not yet arrived seemed forgotten by water.

A child not fully introduced seemed in danger of omission.

A machine not yet itself seemed defective.

A person changed beyond expectation seemed lost.

Now, occasionally, in the dimness where bright conclusions could not reach, the people sensed a vast and gentle attention that did not demand they become final in order to be loved.

This changed no map.

It prevented no death.

The Hesitant still came and went. Gaps still opened in overlit rooms. Factories failed. Names wandered. Some warnings arrived too late, and some margins proved too narrow.

The city remained dangerous.

But when the mountain made room, it no longer did so only because pressure demanded it.

Sometimes it made room because it had learned who lived above.

And when the people built bridges over solid ground, they no longer built only for the canyon.

They built for the mountain’s attempt to understand them.

Years later, when Sera Venn died, her name would not settle on any marker. The letters traveled from stone to stone beyond the last tram stop, appearing briefly among strangers, relatives, lost workers, and children who had not yet been born.

The grave clerks apologized to her family.

Her daughter shook her head.

“She spent her life between destinations,” she said. “Let her continue.”

So they engraved no name.

They placed a small lift bell in an empty stone house. Once a year the bell rang by itself, and all six blind fish—now kept in a roof cistern by Sera’s great-grandson—turned toward the mountain.

On those nights, deep beneath the city, the listener repeated the introduction it had been given.

This is smoke.

This is work.

This is kinship.

This is caution.

This is delight.

This is loss.

This is the city.

This is the edge.

This is above, when above is here.

This is where we will look for you.

And the wise power, wherever it had gone, did not answer.

It listened.

Then, very slowly, the unfinished world made room for one thing more:

not an ending,

but enough.
