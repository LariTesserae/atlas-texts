The power does not descend in a body.

A body would be too large a claim. Even a small one would need a place to stand, and there is no unoccupied ground.

It comes first as attention.

Nothing brightens. No voice passes over the water. The animals do not lift their heads all at once, and the mist does not arrange itself into a sign. Yet the basin, which has known rain, frost, hunger, pressure, pursuit, and the blind weight of stone, becomes known in another way: completely, without being seized.

The power perceives every habitation and does not mistake any of them for ownership. It sees the old beaver pressing mud into a breach and the caddisfly fastening one pale grain beside another. It sees the owl’s hunger and the vole’s terror, the fungus entering a drowned branch, the root descending through darkness toward water it has never touched. It follows each spring backward into the limestone and each channel forward beyond the low cleft. It knows which cliff will fall in winter, which dam will fail in spring, which colony will be diminished by disease, which empty burrow will save a litter from flood.

It does not call these things good because they are beautiful.

It does not call them evil because they are painful.

It understands that the city is made not only by thriving but by breakage: roof becoming valley, tree becoming bridge, nest becoming soil, body becoming many bodies. To forbid all death here would be to stop the exchanges by which the place lives. To preserve every structure would turn the basin, in time, into a crowded tomb of things that could neither perish nor make room.

So the power does not make the fox merciful or the rabbit fearless. It does not dull the stoat’s teeth. It does not command the stream to remain in one channel, nor keep every fledgling from falling, nor harden the walls against all future collapse.

But neither does wisdom mean standing aside from every preventable ruin.

The power waits through a cycle of seasons.

It waits while snow gathers in the reed beds, while frogs sleep in spring-warmed mud, while hunger narrows the foxes and the beavers gnaw bark beneath ice. It waits through thaw, when the valley becomes one linked body of brown water and every dam bears more weight than its builders intended. It waits through the brief flowering of the rockfalls, through swallows plastering wet clay to old nests, through herons feeding their ungainly young.

In waiting, it learns the difference between change and erasure.

There is a fracture beneath the northern rim that no creature knows. It begins far above the warrens and descends behind the cliff face, thin as a hair in places, wide enough elsewhere for rainwater to enter. Each freeze opens it. Each thaw lubricates the bed of clay beneath a leaning mass of former roof. In seven years, perhaps eight, the whole face will come down.

The fall will not resemble those that made the basin. It will not open new ground and leave survivors along its edges. It will strike the largest pond, drive a wall of water down the terraces, crush the oldest dams, drown the warrens, and seal the low cleft with stone. The springs will continue to rise. With no outlet, the basin will fill to the upper cliffs.

Fish will enter the swallows’ nests.

Alder crowns will vanish beneath black water.

The valley will become a single cold lake, and almost everything that made a city of it will be gone before the water finds another way beneath the untouched ground.

This, the power judges, is not a necessary ending. It is only one possible consequence of weight.

So deep inside the limestone, where no eye has ever opened, the power lays nothing like a hand against the fracture. It does not mend the rock. It does not make the cliff eternal.

It changes the destination of a drop of water.

The drop enters the high crack after rain. Where it would have run downward, it turns along a seam. Another follows. Lime dissolves by an amount too small to name. In winter, ice widens the altered path. In spring, the water runs there again.

Year by year a new seep appears halfway down the outer face of the rim, above the basin and turned toward the ordinary woodland. At first it is only a dark stain. Then moss finds it. Liverwort shines there. Midges breed in a cup no wider than a hoofprint. Roots enter after them and tease the seam apart—not toward the great hidden fracture, but away from it.

Pressure is relieved by fractions.

The leaning mass still falls one winter, but not as a single wall. It comes down in three lesser slides over nineteen years. The first crushes a stand of alder and blocks two rabbit entrances. The second splits a pond and destroys a heronry. The third sends white blocks through a dam old enough to carry trees.

There is death in each.

There is also time between them.

Willow roots bind the first debris. The rabbits open higher chambers before the second fall. Herons move their platforms to trees upstream. Beavers raise a new embankment against the third field of stone, turning its largest blocks into the foundation of a pond. Swallows return to the fresh cliff and find more ledges than before.

Nothing knows it has been spared an ending.

The power asks no gratitude.

Still, the blocked outlet remains.

Not blocked yet, but vulnerable. Leaves and branches pass through the low cleft. Floods push whole mats of roots against it. One day a drowned trunk will lodge crosswise in the narrow place; debris will gather behind it, and the water will rise faster than paws can dismantle what lies too far beneath the roof.

The power could widen the cleft. It could split the limestone and make a generous channel. But a larger opening would admit otters into the pale fish chamber, drain the warm mud in which frogs survive winter, and lower ponds whose margins feed thousands. It would make the basin safer from one danger by impoverishing it every day.

Instead, in the deep passage beyond the cleft, the power loosens a stone no bigger than a rabbit’s skull.

The stone rolls once in darkness and comes to rest where the current divides. Water takes both sides of it. Slowly, almost invisibly, two narrow runnels begin cutting backward through a softer layer of limestone. For many years they carry nothing but seepage. Then one wet spring, when the main cleft chokes with bark and reed roots, the rising water reaches them.

They become overflow passages.

They are too small for an otter and too dark for a hunting bird. Amphipods move through them. Eels find them. Pollen, scales, spores, and eggshell pass into the ground. The basin rises during flood, but not beyond the old marks on the alder trunks.

This, too, no one witnesses as an act.

The power then turns its attention to warning.

The valley already has warnings. The deep knock of shifting stone. The slap of a beaver’s tail. The stamp passing through rabbit earth. The silence that travels ahead of a lynx. Nothing here requires prophecy. Prophecy would be useless to creatures that cannot carry the future in words.

What they need is a little more time.

Near the eastern cliff, the power touches no animal’s mind. It alters no instinct from outside. It merely gives the limestone a clearer voice.

The cliff is veined with hollow seams. Under strain, they have always sounded, but much of the sound lies too low for winged creatures and too confused by water for those below. The power joins one narrow cavity to another. Thereafter, before a slab falls, pressure passes through the wall as a long, soft note.

It resembles wind over the mouth of a bottle, though no bottle has ever been made there.

The first time it sounds, every creature stills.

The second time, swallows leave their cups and wheel beneath the opening. Goats climb. Rabbits withdraw from the slope. Beavers pause with branches between their teeth.

The slab falls at dusk and breaks where the nests had been.

In later generations no creature knows why the note means departure. Those that retreat survive more often. Their young retreat after them. The warning enters the city not as law or language but as a custom of flesh.

When the stone sings, move.

The gift is not perfect. Sometimes the cliff sounds and nothing falls. Sometimes a small fragment comes down without warning. The power leaves uncertainty because certainty would draw life too near the dangerous walls between alarms. Fear has its wisdom; so does error.

Having prevented erasure and given warning, the power considers whether to do more.

It could ease the winters. Yet the cold restrains parasites that would otherwise overrun the nests and warrens.

It could increase food. Yet abundance without limit would become hunger multiplied.

It could turn predators aside. Yet voles would strip the sedge beds, rabbits would bare the slopes, weakened banks would enter the ponds, and disease would do in darkness what teeth now do in the open.

It could grant the animals understanding of the whole. It could reveal to the beavers what their dams make possible, show the herons how their fallen platforms bridge channels, teach the rabbits the full design of their own joined warrens.

But the whole is too much for any one of them. To know every consequence would burden each act with impossible hesitation. The beaver must hear running water and answer. The swallow must fix clay to stone. The fungus must enter what has fallen. Their innocence is not ignorance to be cured. It is the scale at which they can live.

The power does not make a king.

It does not choose the beavers because their works govern water. It does not exalt the owl for seeing in darkness, nor the oldest tree for holding the most nests, nor the pale fish for dwelling nearest the hidden world. No creature is made the basin’s voice. To appoint one would falsify the place. The city exists because no single need is permitted to become the only need.

Instead, the power gives a small correction to many lives.

Not a command. A margin.

The beavers become, by the slightest degree, more attentive to water flowing beneath their oldest dams, so that they reinforce foundations as often as crowns.

The rabbits become somewhat more willing to open narrow upward shafts in deep warrens. Most are merely vents. Some become escape routes when springs change course.

The cliff birds mix a few more root fibers into their clay, and their nests endure heavy rain without becoming permanent. They still fall, in time, so that old colonies do not cover every safe face forever.

The deer learn no new path, but the scent of unstable stone troubles them sooner.

Otters remain audacious. Foxes remain patient. Owls remain silent.

Into the bats the power places nothing at all. They are already listening closely enough.

Last, the power attends to the city’s smallest inhabitants: the ones for whom a footprint is a basin, a feather a roof, a night the greater part of a life.

Here intervention becomes almost indistinguishable from tenderness.

A fungal thread encounters a mineral it cannot cross and finds, beside it, a pore.

A springtail falling through cold water strikes a shred of bark.

A moth emerging too early rests where a root leaks faint warmth.

An amphipod swept toward dry stone turns in an eddy that did not exist the moment before.

Not all are saved. The power does not turn the world into a net from which nothing can fall. But chance, which has never been obliged to kindness, is now and then allowed to lean that way.

No monument marks these mercies.

Indeed, the only visible sign appears years later at the deepest end of the basin.

A seed comes down through a crack in the surviving roof. It is the seed of a birch from the upper woodland, carried into the fissure by rain, washed among roots, held through winter in a pocket of lime-soft soil. Ordinarily it would rot there. Instead, a beetle dislodges it. It drops onto a dry knob of stone above the flooded chamber.

The place seems impossible for a tree. There is little soil, less sun, and cold water on every side.

The seed opens.

Its first root enters a line of moss fed by condensation. Its first leaves face not the cave but the distant brightness of the basin entrance. For years it remains no more than a thin green switch. Moths rest beneath its leaves. Spiders join them with silk. Bird droppings enrich the moss. Roots thicken and find the crack from which the seed descended.

In time the birch connects the chamber below with the woodland above—not as a road, but as a living thread. Water rises within it. Sugar descends. Fungi travel its roots. Insects use its bark. A wren nests where its trunk forks. Small fish wait beneath it for whatever falls.

The tree never grows straight. It bends toward the reflected light and becomes pale from mineral spray. It will not live as long as the stone. One spring, many decades later, floodwater will undermine its knob and carry the whole tree into the cleft. Even then it will not be wasted. Its branches will shelter fry. Its bark will feed larvae. Its softened wood will pass underground in pieces.

The power knows this from the beginning and lets the seed open anyway.

For wisdom does not preserve only what will last.

It also makes room for what can be brief without being futile.

When all this is done, the power does not depart, because attention need not occupy space and does not grow weary. But it withdraws its influence to the lightest possible pressure.

The basin continues to belong to no one.

Dams break. Nests fall. Hunters feed. Winters take the old and the newly born without distinction. The cliffs go on loosening grain by grain. The city rebuilds itself with no knowledge of the hand that refused to rule it.

Sometimes, before stone falls, the eastern wall sings.

Sometimes, in the worst floods, dark water rises to the mouths of the highest burrows and then stops.

Sometimes a creature at the end of its strength finds an island of roots in the mist.

These things are not enough to abolish grief. They are only enough to keep grief from becoming the whole story.

Above, travelers on the rim may hear water far below. They may see birds turning within the long wound of the land. If they look carefully, they may notice that the basin has no temple, no marked boundary, no creature set above the rest.

The benevolent power requires none.

Its shrine is the disaster that does not become extinction.

Its prayer is the warning understood without words.

Its image is a pale birch growing where a tree should not be able to grow, feeding lives it cannot perceive.

And its wisdom is restraint: to guard the possibility of the city without taking the city away from those whose countless, unagreed acts continually make it.
