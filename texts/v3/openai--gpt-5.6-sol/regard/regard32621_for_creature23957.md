The power did not descend.

Descent would have given it a direction, and direction would have aligned it with too many predators. Nor did it appear in the sky. Anything visible above the plateau would have been seen at once by the upward-facing birds, and their attention, multiplied through millions of clear eyes, might have fixed even a benevolent thing into helplessness.

It did not speak. A first word would have found the echoes waiting.

It did not look.

This was its first act of wisdom.

It became aware of the plateau obliquely: through the warmth retained in dust-keeper colonies, through variations in the shadows beneath lowered muzzles, through all the small gaps in masks and doorways and interlocked antlers. It knew the place as the shelter people knew one another—not wholly, not continuously, never without intervals in which the known thing might become otherwise.

For a power capable of perfect attention, this required discipline.

It left parts of the plateau unknown to itself.

Among the people, the first sign was a new darkness inside closed hands.

Until then, even the tightest fist admitted some ordinary redness from the sun. Now, occasionally, a person enclosed one palm with the other and found between them a darkness deeper than shadow. It had no size. It did not stain the skin. Dust placed within it vanished from observation and returned subtly rearranged. A cracked copper hook emerged whole. A seedlike egg, unfinished for twelve generations, became warm.

The darkness could not be summoned. It appeared most often in the hands of someone who had stopped trying to possess an outcome.

Children found it easily.

Adults named it nothing.

They began to pass it from shelter to shelter without admitting that they did so. A closed hand would be offered. Another hand would close around it. For the brief instant between one enclosure and the next, no one watched. Yet the darkness did not escape.

The unfinished were brought near it.

Not all completed themselves. Benevolence did not mean forcing every possibility into birth. Some cocoons opened to reveal only pale mineral fibers, intricately arranged and without regret. Some children emerged breathing, older than their parents or bearing masks of shell already grown into the contours of their faces. One opened its eyes and made a hand sign that no living family knew.

Far away, an antlered beast acquired the same gesture as a new branch of horn.

The people understood that an answer had been given, though they did not yet know the question.

The power waited.

Its waiting was not like the plateau’s equilibrium. It could have changed everything. It could have loosened the arrested fall at the western brink and sent the suspended world roaring into the clouds. It could have made rivers. It could have set blood pulsing in the mineral-fiber flesh of the animals, forced wingbeats through the crowded air, awakened the ridge-beasts, and put the sun into visible motion.

It did none of these things.

The creatures were not asking to be rescued from their manner of life.

The bird held above the plain was no less alive than a bird crossing a summer sky. The herds were not statues awaiting animation. Their long arrangements contained desires, decisions, recognitions, and pleasures for which speed would have been annihilation. To impose motion upon them would have been like commanding a song to become a blade because a blade was easier to understand.

The power did not mistake strangeness for suffering.

But there was suffering there.

It found the condemned.

They sat within their rings of obsidian eyes, held in postures made durable by the attention of others. Some had been cruel. Some had broken paths, uncovered dangerous fires, or stared unfinished children into deformity. Some had merely become convenient vessels for communal fear. Their punishments had lasted beyond the memory of the crimes.

The power did not release them.

Not immediately.

A person fixed for centuries could draw a whole settlement into the pattern of their old hunger. Some had hardened around vengeance so completely that freedom, given suddenly, would have been only vengeance acquiring hands.

Instead, the power granted each prisoner one unwitnessed thought.

It hid that thought even from itself.

Around one condemned woman, the obsidian eyes saw no lapse. Her living watchers did not blink. Her body did not move. Yet somewhere no gaze could reach, a thought that had repeated unchanged for three hundred years came to its end.

Another thought followed.

The woman remained seated. Dust lay undisturbed upon her knees. But that evening every watcher dreamed of a door bending three times instead of twice.

Within a year, her fingers moved.

No one saw them move. They were simply found in a new position, one hand open on her lap.

The settlement gathered in alarm. Some wished to add more eyes. Others wanted to break the ring and flee. While they argued in tiny signs, an old watcher approached the prisoner backward. He laid a mask over her face.

This was unlawful. It was also merciful.

Nothing terrible happened.

Years later, the woman stood outside the ring. She did not remember how to walk, but walking was unnecessary. She arrived beside the oldest shelter between two glances and waited there, masked, while the descendants of those she had harmed decided what relation they wished to have with her.

Some turned away forever.

Some came to sit nearby.

The power did not require forgiveness. Required forgiveness is only punishment transferred to the wounded.

At the inward-facing rings, it was more cautious.

It entered no center and opened no interval. The storms and illnesses restrained there were not metaphors. Beneath one basin, a city still struck bells in towers made from the bones of its conquered. Inside another ring, rain had learned to reproduce in lungs. These things would have resumed eagerly.

The watchers had preserved countless lives. They had also been born into obligations they could not refuse.

So the power made watchers that were not alive.

They did not resemble eyes. An eye would have been too easily turned toward a person.

They appeared first as smooth hollows in certain stones around the dangerous rings. Each hollow held no image, yet what lay before it could not enter an unwitnessed state. It was attention without curiosity, vigilance without possession. It did not fix what approached from outside. It held only the thing to which it had been freely given.

The people tested this gift for eighty years.

They placed one hollow before a flame. The flame remained rigid while watched and moved when the living turned away; therefore the stone was not merely another gaze. They put it near an egg. The egg continued accumulating. They carried it beside a dying elder. The elder entered the ground at the pace she chose.

At last they brought a hollow stone to the smallest ring, where a fever waited beneath a patch of pale dust.

One by one, the animals were permitted to look away.

For the first time in ages, the descendants of the human watchers left the basin together.

They did not go far. Duty had shaped them too deeply for that. They stood beyond the ridge, confused by the absence of the center from their sight. Then one began to laugh beneath her mask. The others covered their faces and shook beside her.

The fever remained still.

The power took nothing from the plateau except necessity where necessity had pretended to be sacred.

Its third gift was given not to the people but to the herds.

For ages the antler lattice had thought through arrangement. Its thought was vast, patient, and burdened. It held memories of mountains before erosion, of the first shelters, of predators whose shadows no longer existed. Because every part of the herd inherited from every other, nothing once known could be wholly lost. Grief circulated endlessly through the joined crowns.

The power introduced a gap.

On a morning indistinguishable from all others, two interlocked antlers were found a finger’s breadth apart.

They had not broken. Their tips were smooth. No scar marked either animal.

Through that narrow space the ordinary sun passed.

The herd’s thought changed.

Across the plateau, eyelids lowered. A calf completed itself inside a hollow of dust. Three pale cats abandoned prey they had approached for five centuries. One of the oldest antlered animals became basalt all at once—not violently, not as a death imposed, but with the profound ease of a weight finally set down.

The herd had discovered forgetting.

It did not forget everything. It forgot selectively, awkwardly, with fear and wonder. Certain ancient alarms disappeared from its arrangements. Paths closed. New ones opened. A burdened animal no longer had to inherit the exact terror of an ancestor who had seen the mountains swim.

The shelter people, reading the herds as they always had, learned from this.

They began making a new hand sign: two fingers touching, then parting.

It meant many things.

Enough.

Release.

I will not preserve this against your will.

It also came to mean love.

Only after these changes did the power allow itself to be noticed.

Not seen. Never seen.

At the western precipice, among the countless falling birds, there appeared an absence shaped like no creature. Wings overlapped behind it, slabs of earth hung above and below, bright eyes crowded every depth, but in one narrow place the world declined to furnish an image.

A person living beside the brink discovered it first.

He had returned from beneath the clouds long ago with memories of roaring air. For years he had been unable to enter a shelter because silence made the remembered sound grow louder. Birds had gathered around him until his cloak and their wings formed a single suspended pattern.

He looked toward the absence.

It did not hold him.

He looked longer.

Still it did not hold him.

For the first time in his life, he was completely seen without becoming fixed.

The power regarded him—not as a posture, not as a completed history, not as the sum of all the states in which he might be preserved. It saw him as a being whose interior could never be exhausted by attention.

Its gaze made room.

The man wept. Tears were almost unknown on the plateau. There was no channel for them and no custom governing their use. The first tear remained beneath his eye for several days, bright and round.

Then, between glances, it vanished.

Far below, rain began somewhere under the clouds.

Not much rain. Not enough to invade the dry equilibrium or make rivers across the ancient stone. A brief rain in a lower country, falling through moving air upon creatures who would never know its source.

At the precipice, the man made a sign toward the absence.

Thumb enclosed in fist.

Knuckle to throat.

Two fingers touching, then parting.

The power answered by withholding any answer that would bind him.

Afterward it stayed, though “stayed” was not quite the word. It did not govern. It did not demand worship, for worship on the plateau could too easily become an apparatus of staring. It accepted no permanent name.

Stories gathered around it, all deliberately incomplete.

Some said it was the darkness between closed hands.

Some said it was the first being wise enough to blink.

Some said it had come to set the plateau moving and, on finding everything alive, had repented of its plan.

The children told a simpler story.

They said there was now one watcher who could see you without keeping you the same.

Because of this, masks slowly changed.

They were still worn. Privacy remained a mercy. No wise gift abolishes the boundaries that made mercy possible. But lovers sometimes uncovered one eye in one another’s presence. Parents learned to look at a sleeping child and then turn away, not from fear alone, but from trust. Storytellers left omissions because the untold was beautiful, not merely safe.

The condemned rings emptied, though some prisoners took generations to leave.

The dangerous rings remained.

The herds continued thinking in antler and shadow. Predators fed through alignment. Dust-keepers appeared on unwrapped tools. The ridge-beasts did not wake. Birds still migrated between glances. At the west, the world went on falling without descent.

The power repaired almost nothing.

It made no river.

It quickened no sun.

It did not confuse change with motion, freedom with speed, or compassion with transformation.

It gave the plateau only what the plateau could not make for itself:

attention that did not capture,

forgetting that did not erase,

watchfulness that did not enslave,

and darkness held gently enough for something new to happen inside it.
