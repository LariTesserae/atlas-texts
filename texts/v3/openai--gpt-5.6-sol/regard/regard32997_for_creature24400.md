The power does not arrive in the shape of a person.

A person-shaped god would be too tall for the Green Nave. It would cast a sudden shadow, startle the goats, flatten the flies’ warm columns, and oblige every living thing to understand itself as small. Nor does the power speak. Speech travels poorly there, and nearly everything in the basin has learned to distrust what comes from above.

It comes first as attention.

For one complete turning of the seasons, it changes nothing.

It attends to the frost entering quartz. It follows water through the fur of a leaf and into the mouth of a fungal thread. It learns the warm chambers of voles without opening them. It waits beside the fox and is hungry with it. It travels in the dark gut of a beetle and sees how a garden can be carried without being known as a garden.

It attends to the cup marks at dawn.

It attends to the broken lip.

The power is benevolent, but it is not sentimental. It does not mistake endurance for goodness, or death for failure. It sees that to bind the mountain together forever would be to kill much of what lives there. The falling stone makes cracks. The cracks admit moss. The moving ground keeps any one root from possessing the whole basin. Even the final collapse belongs to the nature of the place.

But the power also sees that the Green Nave has become nearly alone.

Once, in colder centuries, there were many such basins above the cloud. Green places answered one another from summit to summit. Spores crossed on birds. Cats carried seeds in their feet. Storms tore living mats free and laid them on distant ledges. As the world below changed, the other basins dried, burned, or sank beneath taller plants. The Green Nave remained because of its black stone, its eastern tilt, its nightly harvest of clear air.

Its destruction would not be merely the death of one place.

It would be the breaking of a long sentence before the final word.

So the power resolves to do three things, each small enough not to become a tyranny.

First, it gives the basin warning.

Not warning in a language, and not warning to every creature alike. The voles would have no use for a prophecy measured in generations. The beetles could not flee because they knew. The moss could not uproot itself and descend.

Instead, the power touches the living mesh beneath the ground.

It does not make the web conscious. Consciousness would be a burden without limbs, a terror spread through every severed thread. It gives only a new response to strain.

Thereafter, when the open lip shifts by the width of a fingernail, the fungal strands nearest the fracture alter the sugars they pass. Roots taste the change. Cushion plants along the eastern terraces produce more seed. Mosses raise an unusual abundance of spore capsules. The creeping juniper makes cones in years when it would otherwise have rested. Plants nearest the danger do not wither; they become prodigal.

The beetles cross those patches and leave more heavily dusted than before.

Hares carry seeds in the scars around their mouths.

Voles line their nests with fertile stems.

The Green Nave begins, very slowly, to prepare for departure.

The old pillar changes too, though no new mark appears upon it. In the years when the deep fracture opens, condensation fills the cup marks in the wrong order.

Water gathers first in the cups that should remain dry. One hollow holds a trembling bead past noon. Another dries from its center outward, leaving a bright ring at the rim. Ravens bend their heads over these errors. They drink from cups they had always left untouched.

When people next climb to the basin, the eldest among them watches the pattern and says nothing for a long while.

Then she removes the packet of salt she had brought as an offering and carries it away again.

Below the Green Nave, in villages separated by ravines and days of travel, the old arguments begin. Songs are compared. Copper tablets are taken from smoke-darkened chests. People who had thought the pillar merely ceremonial learn that one of the drying sequences has a meaning no living reader has seen:

**The house will go. Carry the hearth, not the stones.**

That is the first act.

The second is more delicate.

The power searches the neighboring mountains.

It finds many places that resemble the Green Nave but are not yet green: a north-facing shelf where snowmelt lingers beneath basalt; a split summit that collects fog; a shallow bowl of broken slate too raw for roots; a ridge across the cloud plain where only white lichen lives. None could receive the whole accord. Each lacks something. One has water but no peat. One has peat but too much wind. One has sheltered cracks but no creatures to open the ground.

The power does not remake them.

Instead, it alters chances.

A raven stealing a copper wedge is startled by a hawk and drops it on the split summit. With the wedge falls a clot of moss from the pillar’s base. The moss lands in a seam where meltwater will keep it damp for eleven days. Most of it dies. A thread no longer than an eyelash survives.

The mountain cat visits after an absence of nine years. Before descending, it steps into a dark print filled with spores. Far away, pursuing a hare along the north-facing shelf, it shelters through a storm beneath an overhang. When it leaves, it licks three paws clean and neglects the fourth.

A gravid beetle enters the hollow shaft of a bone needle. A traveler, finding the needle, tucks it into a fur binding. Months later the binding is cut open above another valley. The beetle emerges into unsuitable grass and would die there, but a swift seizes it, loses it in flight, and lets it fall into the raw slate bowl.

Goats begin using a route they had previously passed by. No command enters their minds. One winter merely leaves a cornice where none had formed before. Following it, the lead doe discovers salt sweating from a pale vein of stone. The herd returns. Their hooves break the crust. Their dung darkens the cracks. Seeds arrive in their coats.

Most of these attempts fail.

The wise power permits this. It does not make every spore prosper, because life guaranteed is life transformed into ornament. The new places must answer for themselves. They must accept frost, hunger, competition, accident. They must become communities, not exhibits.

On the split summit, the eyelash of moss thickens to the width of a hand over sixty years.

On the northern shelf, fungus from the cat’s paw enters the roots of a small sedge already growing there. The joining is imperfect. Many roots blacken. A few begin to move water sideways.

In the slate bowl, the beetle lays seven eggs. Four freeze. Two are eaten by mites. The last becomes a female whose wing cases are not yet sealed. Her descendants, after many generations, lose flight.

Green begins elsewhere.

Not a copy of the Green Nave. Wisdom does not confuse rescue with repetition. These new high gardens have their own accords. One favors sedge over heath. One has no voles and is tunneled instead by pale crickets. One gathers its water at evening rather than dawn. On the farthest summit, life does not climb eastward. It climbs west, toward the last warmth of day.

That is the second act.

The third concerns the end.

The power could prevent the collapse. It could fuse the fractured rim, blunt the frost, turn every loosened slab back into its bed. It could hold the basin above the clouds until the mountain itself wore down beneath it.

It does not.

By then, centuries have passed. The warning has entered human memory. The neighboring heights bear small green answers. The Nave remains crowded and beautiful, but it is no longer alone.

The last failure begins at night.

There is no storm. The stars are hard and numerous. Beneath the vegetation, frozen water expands in a crack that has opened and closed ten thousand times. Quartz grates against basalt. A root parts. The sound is less than the breaking of a bone needle.

Across the basin, fungal threads bead at their broken ends.

Voles wake in their chambers. Ground birds raise their heads. The fox stands and leaves without hunting. Along the rim, two ravens open their wings.

The mountain waits until dawn.

Condensation gathers over every green surface in extraordinary abundance. The pillar cups overflow. Water runs between paving stones and under old walls. For the first time in any story, a narrow stream crosses the Green Nave.

It runs uphill.

Not far, and not for long. It passes through the living floor toward the eastern terraces, carrying mineral dust, spores, fragments of root, beetle eggs, seeds of heath and juniper. It fills hoofprints. It enters vole tunnels. It lays a dark shining line across the highest moss.

The sun touches the opposite peaks.

Then the lip departs.

A shelf of mountain half a mile wide inclines toward empty air. Walls fold. The pillar turns once and vanishes. Cushions split along their seams, exposing black peat and pale roots. Goats on the upper ridge run over stone that moves beneath them. Ravens drop into the air.

The power does not stop the fall.

It does one thing only.

It changes the wind.

The alteration is slight: a rising current where ordinarily there would be descent, a warm exhalation from the cloud plain lasting less than a minute. It cannot hold the great slabs. They plunge through white and break on the cliffs below. It cannot save the voles in their chambers, the old junipers, the fox caught on the open floor, the uncounted eggs beneath stones.

Benevolence cannot always mean that nothing is lost. If it meant that, the world would long ago have become unable to move.

But when the green floor tears apart, thousands of smaller fragments enter the air.

Moss. Fur. Dry sedge. Spores. Wingless beetles with their legs drawn close. Lichen scales. Juniper seed. The feather lining of nests. Peat crumbs containing mites, roots, shells, and the dormant bodies of minute lives.

The changed wind receives them.

For a moment the Green Nave is no longer a basin.

It is a cloud within the cloud.

Some of it falls onto bare ice. Some into rivers. Some is carried so low that it reaches forests and is smothered in shade. Much dies. Much feeds lives unlike itself.

But some crosses the distances that had always looked shorter than they were.

On the split summit, moss receives moss.

On the northern shelf, spores settle into the fur of animals whose ancestors have never seen the Nave.

In the raw bowl, a black beetle lifts its head as another black beetle falls out of the sky.

The people watching from a lower ridge see only a greenish darkness moving through white vapor. They bow, though no one has told them to.

Afterward, the summit is smaller and almost bare. A crescent of the basin remains against the eastern ridge. Here and there, roots hold pockets of peat. A few cushions survive. One wall stands to the height of a person’s ankle. The upright pillar is gone.

For many years, no one climbs there.

When at last a young visitor reaches the remnant, she brings no pillar to replace the old one. She repairs no platform. She kneels beside a shallow fracture where dew has gathered in the moss.

A beetle moves beneath it.

She sees that its back is grooved with yellow spores.

She touches the water, then places her damp finger to her tongue.

The power, still attending, does nothing more.

It has not saved the Green Nave as stone.

It has saved what the Green Nave had always been:

not a room,

but an agreement;

not a possession,

but a passage;

not life made safe from falling,

but life taught how to fall beyond itself.
