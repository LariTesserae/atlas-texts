The first thing the power would do is wait.

Not the waiting of indecision. It would wait as the mountain waits: attentively, without requiring the place to become legible at once.

It would not descend in brightness. Brightness would close the pores of the moss, startle the tender from its fern, alter the chemistry of the pool. It would not take a body among the pale trees, for any body large enough to declare itself would become the basin’s central fact, and the basin had prospered by having no center.

So the power would enter only as attention.

It would follow one thread of water until that thread disappeared beneath a root. It would follow the root into fungal darkness, the fungus into softened wood, the wood into mineral film, the film into a fissure too narrow for anything but warmth. It would learn the place through continuities rather than names.

In time it would understand that there was no single being to bless.

The gray tender was not the sovereign of the basin. The buried web was not its hidden god. The pool was not a mind seated beneath the wall. What lived there lived between them: in the passing of pressure into tissue, tissue into gesture, gesture into altered growth, altered growth into the future route of water. Its body was partly made of consequences. Its memory stood rooted in separate places. Its thoughts, if thoughts they were, sometimes required generations to finish.

A lesser power might try to awaken it.

A cruel power might awaken only one part.

The wise power would do neither.

It would listen long enough to distinguish distress from strangeness. The uphill water was strange, but not suffering. The solitary young were strange, but not abandoned. The old entering the fissure were strange, but not necessarily lost. The patient shaping of pod, branch, leaf, and stone did not ask to be accelerated. Nothing there desired a quicker world.

There were wounds, as there are wherever life feeds upon life. Spores failed. Young tenders vanished in cracks. Trees leaned too far and tore their roots from the shelves. Once in many seasons, a dry wind entered the notch and the moss contracted until it was almost black. The power would feel each loss. Benevolence would not require it to prevent them all.

To prevent every ending would fill the basin beyond endurance.

To preserve every arrangement would turn memory into paralysis.

Thus it would permit the ordinary griefs of the place. It would not keep each pale tree upright, nor guide every gliding body safely through mist. It would not stop stone from becoming soil. Wisdom would know that mercy is not the abolition of change.

But the power would perceive a danger the basin could not yet perceive.

Deep below the warm fissures, a shelf of mountain had begun to alter. No collapse was near by the measure of wings or roots. By the measure of stone, however, it had already begun. Water entered a buried seam each rainy season and returned bearing a little more of its walls. In perhaps eighty years, perhaps two hundred, the tallest rock face would open. The dark pool would be lost beneath fallen stone. Several shelves would tip toward the ledge. Much of the buried web would be severed from its warmest chambers.

The place had survived changes before. It might survive this one. Yet the power saw that the basin’s long craft had become too dependent upon a single exposed pool. What was distributed in body had, over centuries, narrowed in passage. The pool was not a heart, exactly, but too much now passed through it.

The power would not hold up the wall forever.

Instead, it would introduce redundancy.

Far along the mountain shoulder lay another shallow notch, colder and nearly bare, where water moved beneath stone but seldom surfaced. Between the two basins were cliffs no tender crossed, even in cloud. Yet the fissures below approached one another within the length of a pale tree.

There the power would make its smallest intervention.

It would not split the rock. It would warm, by less than the warmth of a resting foot, one mineral seam. It would do this only during rain, only for a few breaths at a time. The expansion would be minute. Water would enter. Microbial films would follow. After several years, a fungal cord would find the dampness and grow along it.

After several decades, the two buried systems would touch.

They would not merge completely. The power would not erase their difference. A narrow exchange would begin: salts one way, spores the other, pressures passing in both directions. The second notch would slowly green. Moss would gather around one seep. A fern would establish itself, then die; another would take its place. Much later, a hooked vine might grow against a slab where no pod had ever struck.

The tenders would discover the passage last.

One evening, an old individual standing beside the dark pool would feel a faint vibration not produced by weather, water, or any known hollow trunk. It would place one forefoot down, lift another, and remain so until night. The vibration would return after rain. It would recur over seasons. Eventually a young tender, following no instruction except the invitation embodied in changed roots and altered mineral taste, would climb higher than usual in the mist.

It would glide outward.

It might reach the second notch. It might not.

The power would not carry it.

A gift that removes the need for courage is sometimes only a gentler captivity.

There would be another thing the power would do, though it would hesitate longer over this.

The basin already knocked and answered. Whether this was language did not matter. It was a form of openness: an act sent into obscurity with enough patience left afterward for reply.

The power would wish to answer.

But any answer unmistakably beyond the basin could overwhelm it. A mountain that has known only pressure and rain should not be forced, in one moment, to comprehend the stars. The tender should not be made priest over the moss merely because it has ears in its feet. No species should receive a private revelation that separates it from the household that made it.

So the power would answer in a way no part could possess alone.

On a night of deep mist, after the tender struck a hollow trunk, the power would wait through the customary interval.

Then, under the pool, a small pressure would pass into the stone.

It would be no louder than a seed cracking.

The pool would tremble. Pollen would divide into branching forms. Sideways water would hesitate in its channels. A blade-edged leaf would touch deadwood. Fungal threads around a drowned branch would tighten with moisture. The pale roots would receive the disturbance, and the fern trunk beneath the tender’s feet would carry back one soft pulse.

Not a word.

Not a command.

Only a pattern unlike accident, but made entirely from things the basin already knew.

The tender would remain motionless until dawn.

In the following days it might strike many trunks. Most would answer only with their own hollowness. It might return to the pool and wait. The pattern would not come again. The power would not train the creature into dependence, nor establish a ritual by rewarding repetition.

Years later, when several conditions coincided—rain in the upper seams, mature pods on the exposed slab, young roots crossing a mineral band—the basin itself would produce something close to that pattern without intervention.

This would be the true answer.

Not proof that a power watched.

Proof that what had once arrived from beyond could be taken up, transformed, and made native.

When the wall finally fell, whether in eighty years or three hundred, the power would grieve with the lives extinguished beneath it. It would not call the destruction necessary merely because new things followed. It would not pretend that survival elsewhere restored what was lost here.

But the second notch would by then be wet and warm. Pale saplings might stand with broad spaces between them. Water might travel sideways along fresh mineral bands. The descendants of the basin—roots, films, spores, perhaps winged tenders—would carry fragments of the old arrangements into new stone.

The original room would change beyond recognition. Its ledge might break away. Its tallest wall might become a slope of moss-covered blocks. For a time there might be no dark pool at all.

Still, beneath the fallen rock, water would search.

That is what the benevolent power would do: not save the place from time, not crown its strangest creature, not translate its life into a lesser, quicker intelligence.

It would learn the shape of the relationship.

It would relieve the one danger that had become too narrow.

It would open a way without compelling passage.

It would answer once, quietly enough that the answer could someday belong to the world that received it.

And then it would remain—not above the basin, nor outside it, but attentive to every unfinished instruction—while the mountain continued its slow work.
