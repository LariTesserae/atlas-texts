### What the Power Did

The power did not enter the Crown Thicket at once.

It remained above the mountain for a long while, though “above” is only a manner of speaking. It attended to the place. It learned the weight of frost on each district, the delay between rain touching the cloud-combers and water reaching the lowest cistern, the years required for a bridge vine to cross an arm’s span of air.

It listened through roots.

It felt how water passed from body to body without regard for ownership. It followed sugars through the red spires and entered the hollow trunks with the slow grazers. It knew the small deaths under every green surface and the crowded births beneath them. It learned that what appeared to be suffocation was often shelter, that collapse was not always calamity, and that rot was one of the forms by which the city remembered how to make space.

Therefore it did not clear the lanes.

It did not separate the trees into individuals, though it could have traced every germination back to its first seed. It did not straighten the towers, strengthen every bridge, warm the basin, banish frost, or make the flowers brilliant. It did not fill the mountain with birds. It did not teach the thicket to speak aloud.

These would have been gifts shaped like the giver.

The power wished to offer something the thicket itself could use.

So it waited until the coldest part of the year, when the upper cisterns were sealed and even the fungal lights had dimmed. On that night it entered the city as a degree of warmth too slight for any creature to wake.

The warmth passed down through the grafts.

Where one trunk joined another, the tissues softened. Where fungal threads crossed from root to root, droplets loosened and moved. The warmth continued into chambers that had not exchanged water for many seasons. It reached seeds sealed in old fiber, beetle larvae rigid beneath bark, worms encased in narrow channels, and the pale roots of seedlings that had grown for years without finding stone.

Nothing mistook it for spring. No bud opened to be killed by morning. No ice roof fell. The power was too wise for rescue that created another danger.

It merely asked, in the language available there:

*What do you lack?*

There was no single answer.

The cloud-combers answered with thirst, though they stood in mist.

The bridge vines answered with distance.

The mosses answered with surface.

The buried seedlings answered with light.

The fungi answered with death, meaning not cruelty but material.

The water-striders answered with stillness, then with tremor, unable to choose.

The old vault trees gave no answer that could be separated from their own immense confusion. They lacked and possessed the same things. They wanted to endure. They wanted to fall. They held their descendants protected in darkness and starved them there.

But beneath all these answers the power perceived a deeper want. It passed through every district as tension: not fear of winter, nor hunger, nor overcrowding.

The Crown Thicket had only one body of mountain beneath it.

For all its plurality, for all its seeds and grafts and layered inheritances, it existed in one steep basin. Fire was rare in that wet cold, but mountains had other endings. A ridge might shear away. An earthquake might open the cisterns and drain them. A century of altered wind might cause the clouds to pass too high. A single fall of rock could dam the outlet; a single thaw could tear the trembling marsh from the cliff.

The thicket did not know this as prophecy. Yet every living thing within it carried some blind pressure to go farther than the basin allowed.

The power understood.

It did not make the mountain safe forever. To make it unbreakable would have changed its nature, and perhaps broken lands below. Nor did it make the thicket immortal. Immortality in so crowded a place would soon become another name for imprisonment.

Instead, the power prepared a road.

Not a road through the lanes. Not one that feet could follow.

Beyond the Crown Thicket stood other ridges: bare shoulders, wet cracks, shaded clefts where life had begun only as lichen. Most were too exposed. Some lacked water. Others would not be ready for centuries.

The power touched twelve of them.

On one, it shifted a loose plate of stone no farther than the width of a root. Rain began to linger behind it.

On another, it deepened a crack so frost would break a ledge inward rather than cast it down the mountainside. Moss would collect there.

On a third, it turned no stone at all, but allowed the prevailing mist to keep a small irregularity in its course. A cliff that had been dry at dawn began, several mornings each year, to shine.

Elsewhere it placed nothing and changed nothing, having seen that those places would become ready without assistance.

Then it returned to the Crown Thicket.

At the highest point, among the red spires, there was a flower enclosed so deeply that its pollen had never crossed more than a few neighboring stems. The power did not enlarge it or make it conspicuous. It altered the skin of one future seed, adding a row of microscopic hollows.

At the marsh edge it changed the hook of a bridge-vine seed by the smallest angle.

Within a cloud-comber it allowed a mutation already present in one cell to remain rather than be repaired. Years later, the cone grown from that branch would open not at dawn, like the others, but during the high dry wind before snow.

It made similar changes in moss spores, fungal dust, liverwort fragments, beetle eggs. None guaranteed survival. Most would lead nowhere. Wisdom did not mean arranging victory for every form. It meant giving life more than one place in which to continue its attempts.

The power then waited for a bird.

Three years passed.

A white-winged mountain bird descended through the canopy in late summer. It came hungry and irritable, driven below its usual range by wind. It swallowed dark berries, tore moss while searching for beetles, and caught several hooked seeds in the fine feathers beneath one wing.

The power did not command it.

It did not put knowledge into its mind or steer it like an arrow. It only quieted one gust that would have forced the bird east. The bird chose the western opening because the air there felt easier.

It crossed the first ridge.

One seed fell into snow and died.

One lodged on sunlit stone and dried.

The moss fragments blew away.

The beetle egg hatched where there was nothing to eat.

But a fungal spore entered a wet crack on the fourth mountain. It found a lichen already weakening the rock and joined it.

The altered vine seed traveled farther. Its hook held through two storms, then released when the bird sheltered beneath an overhang. It fell beside a thread of water and waited six years before germinating.

The red-spire seed, carried inside the bird, came to rest behind the plate of stone the power had shifted. There it opened.

Its first root did not descend.

It traveled sideways, searching.

Long afterward—long by the measure of birds, brief by the measure of mountains—a dark fan of leaves appeared above the crack.

The power saw it and did not call it a second Crown Thicket. That would have been too early, and perhaps untrue. It was a plant on a barren ridge. It might die under the next hard frost. It might remain alone for a hundred years. Its descendants might become something unlike the city from which it came.

That was not failure.

Back in the original basin, the old vault at the center finally broke.

It had carried too many generations and too much wet fiber. During a thaw, one interior pillar folded. The failure passed through the fused limbs as a deep sequence of knocks. Bridges tightened. Roots tore. Water spilled from a hidden cistern. Then the crown descended through three levels and came to rest across a lower lane.

The power could have held it up.

It did not.

For several days the wound stood open to the sky. Direct sunlight entered the lower chambers for the first time in centuries. Moss paled. Water-striders fled beneath leaves. A colony of slow grazers, exposed with their hollow trunk, perished in the cold.

The power grieved for them.

Benevolence did not make their small lives negligible. Wisdom did not make their deaths acceptable in the sense of being painless or deserved. The power knew each of them entirely: every fungal seam they had fed upon, every wet chamber crossed, every long pause mistaken by moss for stone.

It grieved, but it did not restore the fallen vault. Restoration would have crushed the seedlings already responding beneath it.

In the new shaft, colorless flowers opened. Buried plants raised leaves. A bridge vine found an anchorage previously hidden from it. Frost entered, split the fallen wood, and fungi began the inheritance. Within a generation the opening narrowed. Within three, it was a vaulted chamber unlike the one before.

The thicket never knew itself to have been visited.

There was no shrine to the power, no mark burned into bark, no law added to frost and growth. The inhabitants continued according to their natures. They crowded, grafted, drank, shaded, hollowed, failed, and began again.

Only two things had changed.

The city was no longer the sole vessel of its inheritance.

And in the deepest living tissues, where one body met another, there remained a faint new tendency. Under great stress, some grafts loosened instead of tightening. A branch might release a burden before both trunks split. A seedling might refuse union long enough to seek another surface. A dying root might close its channels before decay poisoned its neighbors.

The power had not taught the Crown Thicket separation.

It had taught it the smallest mercy of boundaries.

Not walls. Not loneliness.

Room.

Then the power withdrew its attention, though never wholly. It had other young places to watch, other crowded beings to understand. It left the mountain to weather and the thicket to its unfinishable work.

Clouds entered the combers. Water descended. Frost drew pale maps and erased them.

Far away, on a ridge where no city yet stood, one dark-leaved plant held a droplet in the fold of its first branch.

A moss began beneath it.
