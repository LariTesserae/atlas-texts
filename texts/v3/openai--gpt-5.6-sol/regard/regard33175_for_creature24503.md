The power does not descend.

It does not take a body of fire above the ridges, or speak through thunder, or still the rivers so that the mountains may hear. Such gestures would be easy. They would also be wounds.

It attends.

Its attention passes through cloud without parting it, through stone without warming it, through every living membrane without changing the rhythm of exchange. It follows rain into the black soil and into the mouths of frogs. It travels with mineral salts through the gut of a browser, waits in the root mesh of the iron groves, rises again as vapor from a leaf. For a time—though time has little meaning to it—the power learns the whole circulation.

It finds no sovereign creature to address.

There is no hidden mind beneath the basalt, no ancient intelligence imprisoned in the aquifers, no single will dispersed among the roots. The greater animal of the mountains is real, but real as a storm is real: made of relations, continuous only because everything enters and leaves it. To awaken it as one being would require closing those relations around a center.

The power could do this.

It could draw the clicking colonies into language. It could make the lightning groves retain their flashes as memory. It could bind the fungal networks into thought, turn rivers into sensation, pressure into desire, migration into intention. The mountains might open an awareness vast and slow. They might know the clouds touching their shoulders. They might feel each death and birth within them. They might look outward through ten thousand animal eyes and recognize themselves.

But the power is wise enough to ask what such a gift would cost.

A self is also a boundary. A mind is also a place where pain can remain after the wound has passed. The mountains now suffer in fragments: a glider taken in the air, a tree split by current, a young animal starving behind the waterfall. Each pain is complete in the creature that bears it, but none is gathered into an immortal witness. To make the whole land conscious would be to give it every terror at once, and memory enough to grieve for the vanished.

The power does not do this.

Nor does it abolish death. It does not blunt the river hunter’s teeth or turn predators toward fruit. It sees that mercy applied only to the hunted becomes cruelty to the hunter; that saving the beetle condemns the flower, saving the tree denies the fungi, saving every young creature crowds the valleys until hunger becomes the governing law. It will not preserve bodies by destroying the circulation that gives them bodies.

It does not make the mountains safe.

Instead, it searches for what can be given without becoming command.

Deep below the oldest hanging basin, where hot water presses against a fault sealed by mineral deposits, the power finds a future catastrophe. In eleven thousand rainy seasons the seal will fail. The basin will empty through the mountain’s heart. A scalding flood will scour three valleys to stone. Much will return afterward, as life always does, but several lineages found nowhere else will end there.

The rupture is natural. That does not make it sacred.

With an influence smaller than the settling of dust, the power alters the place where one crystal forms. The crystal inclines another; that one narrows a channel; pressure begins to escape through a hairline seam. Over centuries, a new warm spring will appear on an uninhabited cliff. Moss will find it. Then reeds, insects, frogs. The buried water will bleed away harmlessly, and the great failure will never come.

No inhabitant will know it has been spared.

The power considers this good.

On another ridge it discovers a creeping sickness in the iron trees: a fungus recently changed by chance, able to pass the resin defenses that once confined it. Left alone, it will spread from grove to grove. Lightning will then strike dry crowns, and fires—rare here, clumsy and ravenous—will cross the high thickets faster than rain can suppress them.

The power does not erase the fungus. It shifts a protein in one of the pale colonies beneath the bark. The colonies begin to consume the altered resin on which the sickness depends. Some iron trees will still die. Enough will live.

Far below, in a black pool, it strengthens nothing at all. There it sees extinction approaching and allows it.

A blind grazer has become too specialized. Its last pool is slowly filling with sediment. No intervention small enough to preserve the animal would remain small: the stream would need turning, the chemistry remade, competitors suppressed. The power stays with the final generations instead. Not as comfort they can perceive, nor as a promise that their pattern will be restored elsewhere, but as witness.

When the last egg fails to open, the loss is not unnoticed.

This, too, the power considers a form of benevolence.

It continues through possible centuries.

It sees climates lean toward drought and then recede. It sees slopes collapse, rivers leap their channels, new species arrive on storm winds, old ones diminish. Farther away, beyond the hot lowlands, it senses minds that build roads, walls, towers, tools, and plans. They have not yet found these mountains. One day they may.

The power could hide the place forever.

It could turn travelers aside with cloud, ruin instruments, confuse maps, make every approach seem impassable. But concealment would imprison the mountains within a fixed solitude, and it would deny those distant minds the chance to meet something they did not make. Protection that permits no encounter is only another kind of enclosure.

So the power plants no barrier.

Instead, it places a possibility.

Not in the mountains, but in the future minds approaching them.

A slight unease before unnecessary harm. A recurring dream of circulation. The intuition—available but not compulsory—that to understand a thing is not to own it. Most will ignore this. Some will not. One surveyor will choose a longer route for a road. One collector will leave the last flowering plant rooted. One child, seeing manufactured rain pass across a valley in bands, will devote a lifetime to defending what cannot speak in courts.

The power does not make them virtuous. Virtue compelled is only behavior.

It makes refusal possible at the moment refusal matters.

Then, before withdrawing, it offers the mountains one gift that changes almost nothing.

During a storm over the highest iron grove, lightning enters seven crowns at once. Current spreads through trunks, roots, wet stone, fungal threads, mineral springs, and the saturated banks of a river. Flowers open. Beetles emerge. Frogs feel the pulse and answer. Clicking colonies begin in the reeds. Gliders launch into the charged air. Browsers lift their heads. Ridge-soarers tilt their wings toward the sudden warmth.

For less than a heartbeat, all these responses coincide.

There is no thought. No name. No voice saying *I*.

But across the mountains, every part of the circulation briefly receives the same event. The pattern closes without becoming a prison. Water, mineral, heat, charge, root, wing, blood, and cloud align in one immense accord.

The mountains do not know themselves.

They experience themselves.

Then the moment opens again. The insects lose their rhythm. The frogs fall silent. Rain thickens over the groves. A struck branch crashes through the canopy, scattering climbers from their shelter. The river hunter takes a fish. Fungal light spreads along a buried trunk. Warm water issues from the new seam in the ancient fault, one drop at a time.

Nothing worships.

Nothing gives thanks.

The benevolent power asks for neither.

It leaves no monument, no law, no chosen species, no proof that it was ever there. It takes away only a few disasters that served no necessity, and leaves behind a little more resilience, a little more freedom, and one unremembered instant in which the life of the mountains came as close as it safely could to joy.

Then it releases its attention.

The place continues: immense, efficient, quiet.

Water descends.

Heat rises.

Cloud gathers.

And the mountains pass through everything that lives.
