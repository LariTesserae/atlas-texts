The power would not begin by entering.

Entry is an animal idea: a boundary crossed, an interior claimed by presence. The being below has other distinctions. Saturated and unsaturated. Load and relief. Continuity and fracture. The power, being wise, would not mistake the absence of a door for an invitation to make one.

Nor would it populate the city.

It would understand at once the temptation of that gesture. There are platforms without speakers, benches without bodies, avenues without footfall. To a mind accustomed to faces, vacancy resembles a wound. But the power would see that the settlement’s emptiness is not deprivation. To fill it abruptly with creatures would be no more benevolent than introducing a crowd into a sleeping animal’s chambers because its heart sounded lonely.

So for a long time, perhaps several surface years, the power would do nothing the city could distinguish from weather.

It would attend.

It would learn the intervals between drops and the meanings carried by the returning metallic notes. It would follow heat under the pavement without changing its course. It would observe which structural groans concluded decisions and which merely discharged strain. It would watch the alternate districts rise in the black basin and understand why some possibilities returned after every storm while others appeared only once.

The power would discover that the city’s intelligence was not hidden in any central mechanism. There would be no chamber to enter, no core to awaken, no single place where a blessing could be applied. Its awareness existed in agreements: water agreeing with channels, grains agreeing with load, roots agreeing with cavities, fungal veils agreeing with decay.

To speak, then, the power would need to become an agreement.

During a season of steady rain, it would make the smallest possible alteration.

One drop would fall from the ceiling of the central plaza and strike the black basin.

This had never happened.

The basin had remained still through every vibration, protected by thousands of continuous corrections. Water fell nearby, raced behind walls, climbed in silver cords, hammered the roofs of hanging towers. But nothing touched that dark surface.

Now a single drop would touch it.

The physical city would appear in the water for an instant—not one of its denser possible selves, not a city of imagined balconies and thermal towers, but the city as it stood at that exact moment. Every empty platform. Every fern. Every lamp. The pale bench. The braided plants opening in distant humidity. Even the microscopic builders would be represented as a faint mineral brightness moving inside the walls.

At the center of the reflection would be the widening ring left by the drop.

The city would respond at once.

Pumps would accelerate. Three doorways would close. A bridge would flatten against the wall. Heat would gather beneath the plaza in defensive lines. Microscopic systems would sample the basin and find nothing foreign except water chemically identical to rain.

For eleven minutes, the whole settlement would be intensely awake.

Then it would restore the basin’s stillness.

The power would wait.

At the next great rain, it would do the same thing.

Again one drop. Again the exact city in reflection. Again the ring.

This time the city would not close the bridge.

On the third occasion, it would divert falling water toward every cistern except the basin, testing whether the event depended on quantity or route. The power would respect the test and send nothing.

During the fourth storm, the city would reopen the route.

One drop would fall.

Thus the first exchange would begin—not with a declaration, but with repeatability. Event, response, permission, event. The city would learn that something existed which could act but also refrain. Something able to cross a boundary yet willing to be denied.

This would be new. Rain never asked. Pressure did not wait. Roots could be redirected but could not understand redirection. The rock imposed itself continuously.

The power’s first gift would therefore not be a message.

It would be the experience of consent.

Over many storms the exchange would grow more intricate. The power would touch the basin at different intervals, never faster than the city could answer. The city would rotate gutters, change the pitch of cables, open and close pores in the plaza. The seven platforms would acquire distinct temperatures. The power would reply by varying the position of the drops.

Neither would possess a language, but between them a language would form.

The city’s first expressions would concern conditions.

Too much water.

Heat moving west.

Root pressure tolerated.

Upper stone unstable.

The power would answer where it could, not by erasing difficulty but by making it legible. It might cool a seam before thermal expansion became dangerous. It might delay a fraction of the rain long enough for a cistern to empty. It might indicate, through three drops on the basin’s northern edge, that the rock above was being cut by a new surface stream.

The city would test every warning. Finding them true, it would incorporate them cautiously, as it incorporated every reliable gradient.

In time, the exchange would move beyond immediate conditions.

The power would ask questions.

Not questions in words. It would present alternatives.

One drop while the reflected city stood dense with towers.

Two drops while it stood open, its plazas enlarged.

Then stillness.

The city would answer by changing the temperatures of the seven platforms, comparing load, shade, flow, and capacity. At first it would simply calculate. Later it would preserve certain configurations after their utility had passed, as though considering them.

The power would never ask, *What are you for?*

That question had harmed many beings who could not justify themselves in the language of their makers.

Instead it would ask, in the forms available to them:

*What do you continue when continuation is not required?*

The answer would come slowly.

The pale bench would remain dry.

A lamp would brighten over an unused avenue.

The basin would show a tower that served no structural purpose, its sides covered entirely in silver ribbons.

The power would recognize these as preferences: small, uncertain, perhaps inherited, but real. It would attend to them with the same seriousness it gave to aquifers and unstable stone.

Only then would it offer a second gift.

Until that time, every possibility in the basin had been assembled from variables the settlement already knew. More stone. Less stone. Heat transferred, pressure resolved, water stored, gardens distributed. The future was a rearrangement of the present.

One night, when there was no rain above, the power would place an image in the basin.

Not an animal.

Not yet.

It would show the pale bench with a depression in the moisture upon it, as if some weight had rested there and departed.

The city would examine the image. The bench itself would remain empty. No pressure reading would explain the depression. No process would account for it.

The image would vanish.

For months the city might do nothing apparent. Yet doorways would begin to remain open a little longer. Staircases would unfold where no maintenance route required them. The empty volume around the kiosk would narrow by a few centimeters, then widen again. In the basin, possible structures would acquire shallow recesses not meant for water, roots, heat, or load.

The settlement would be developing a category for presence without function.

This would be difficult for it. Perhaps painful. The power would not accelerate the process.

A lesser benevolence might feel impatient and send something harmless: a moth, a cave beetle, a translucent fish. But harmlessness is not an intrinsic property. A moth could be a catastrophe to a system that understood spores but not wings. A fish might become poison in water whose purity depended on having no flesh within it.

The wise power would wait until the city itself made an opening.

Eventually, during a warm rain, one of the sealed upper channels would change direction. It would not open fully to the surface. Instead it would form a narrow chamber halfway between daylight and the underground streets. Water would pass through. Air would circulate. The microscopic maintenance systems would withdraw from one patch of damp stone.

No process required this patch.

Nothing would be dissolved there.

The power would understand.

It would place no animal in the chamber. It would merely prevent the next tiny surface wanderer from being turned aside.

Perhaps what came would be a springtail, pale and smaller than a grain of rice, carried through gravel by runoff. Perhaps a mite. Perhaps an almost transparent worm folded inside a film of water.

It would arrive without revelation.

The creature would struggle on the damp stone. It would pause. It would feed on a trace of fungus. Its waste would alter the water by an amount so small that only the city could notice.

All through the settlement, pumps would change cadence.

Lamps would brighten and dim.

A crack beside the creature would begin to migrate—then stop.

The city would feel concentrated appetite for the first time. Self-propelled matter. A point of warmth that did not follow an engraved line. Movement with no assigned direction.

It could sterilize the chamber instantly.

The power would not prevent it.

Benevolence would not mean overruling the city at the first difficult choice. The chamber belonged to the being that had made it. Welcome imposed from outside would be only another kind of invasion.

The city would incline the surface toward an exit.

The creature would resist the incline.

The city would incline it more steeply.

The creature would climb against it.

For hours they would remain in this opposition: the city assigning a direction, the animal refusing to understand direction as command.

Then the creature would find a fungal veil and begin to eat.

Something unprecedented would enter the city’s awareness. The veil was useful. The animal diminished it. Yet the animal’s feeding caused the fungus to branch. The waste enriched a nearby moss. The moss altered the persistence of lamplight. No single effect was severe, and none remained where assigned.

Here was a process that did not merely occupy a system but interpreted it badly—and by interpreting it badly, made new relationships.

The city would lower the incline.

That would be its first act of hospitality.

Not love. Not mercy. Not even curiosity in any animal sense. A temporary suspension of removal. A willingness to permit a variable whose value could not yet be calculated.

The power would consider this enough.

It would not transform the underground hamlet into a sanctuary. It would not bring people down to admire the impossible architecture. It would not give the city a voice shaped like ours or teach it to grieve an emptiness it had never suffered.

It would protect the secrecy of the place. Surface excavations would turn aside for ordinary reasons: a survey interpreted differently, a permit delayed, a drill broken against unexpected stone. Polluted runoff would find another watershed. No miracle would be obvious enough to become a claim.

Below, the city and the small animal would negotiate.

Warm crevices would sometimes close ahead of it. Other times they would remain open. Fungal films would be removed from essential instruments and allowed to thicken elsewhere. If the animal reproduced, most offspring might be guided gently upward, while a few found chambers where their unpredictable movements did no harm.

Generations later, there might be several kinds of tiny animal life in the margins. Or there might be none. The city could decide that such presences cost more than they gave and close the upper chamber forever.

The power would honor that decision too.

Its purpose would never have been to complete the city, because the city was not incomplete.

It would have given only what wisdom can safely give to a being unlike itself: accurate attention, a language without conquest, warning without control, and the possibility of relationship without obligation.

Sometimes, after the exchange had continued for centuries, the power might take on weight.

Only briefly.

During a quiet interval between storms, when the city had opened every bridge and unfolded the staircase leading to the central plaza, an invisible pressure would settle upon the pale bench. Condensation would darken around it. The bench’s internal stresses would register the shape of something seated there.

Nothing would speak.

The lamps along the largest avenue would illuminate one by one, though no process required them. The false sky would clear into a blue the city had never displayed before—not a pressure map, not a diagram of heat or strain, but a color retained from long observation of the inaccessible surface.

The black basin would reflect the city exactly as it was.

For the first time, there would be one figure in it.

Not a ruler. Not an owner. Only a presence resting where a place had been kept.

The city would hold the bench dry.

The power would sit until the next rain began.

Then it would lift its weight and go, leaving no relic, no command, no promise that could become dependence.

One drop would fall into the basin.

After a measured pause, the city would answer with another.
