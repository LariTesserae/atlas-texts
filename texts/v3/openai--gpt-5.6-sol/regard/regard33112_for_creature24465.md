## The Mercy That Was Not a Spring

The power did not enter the Underfoundry.

To enter in any shape large enough to contain its awareness would have broken the ceiling, crushed the paper nests, startled the root-pigs into the cisterns, and sent a warning shudder through every column. To enter as flame or brilliance would have been worse. Light, there, was food transported from elsewhere. A sudden radiance would not have been revelation. It would have been an industrial accident.

So the power attended in pieces.

It became patience in a silver mite and traveled the length of a condensation reed.

It became restraint in a mantis and allowed a messenger moth to pass between its forelegs.

It became an unseasonable sweetness in one mouthful of rodent pulp, a brief easing of pain in the cracked leg of a freight beetle, a coolness in a bearing where an old snail had fallen asleep.

None of these acts was large enough to be worshiped.

For a hundred rains, the power learned.

At first it asked itself which being lived there.

The beetles seemed plausible, because they traversed the whole and carried its substances from organ to organ. Then the power entered the paper memory of the wasps and found inclinations older than any beetle road. It listened to the arithmetic of the crickets, the refusals of the rodents, the pressure wisdom of the pigs. It tasted the root organism’s slow distinctions between burden and wound, use and invasion.

It followed an elderly messenger moth whose body contained seven destination scents, six of them obsolete. The moth flew into a sealed gallery and beat itself softly against a wall that had not been a doorway for a hundred rains.

The power opened nothing.

Behind that wall was a dormant fermentation chamber under pressure. Had the old route been restored, half the eastern circuits would have drowned in boiling rot.

The moth’s failure was wiser than the power’s first impulse to help it.

So the power continued learning.

It saw cruelty, but could not remove cruelty cleanly from the place. To forbid predation would starve the mantises and choke the hatcheries with flies. To preserve every beetle would fill the roads with mouths beyond feeding. To save every tadpole from drying would poison the next cistern with their abundance. Death there was not an enemy standing outside the city. It was one of the city’s busiest forms of transport.

The power saw injustice too.

Some mill colonies cheated measures. Certain wasp nests remembered offenses after every creature involved had been eaten and built into walls. Rodent debts passed to descendants who had never tasted the original pulp. Weak districts received water late because they had little with which to threaten those upstream.

These things could be changed, but not by command. A command strong enough to overcome every appetite would become the only appetite that mattered. Then the Underfoundry would cease to be its difficult multitude and become an instrument of the benevolent power.

Benevolence that replaces the beloved is only conquest with a clean face.

So the power waited until it understood what the city itself could not provide.

Not peace. The Underfoundry knew many small peaces, each maintained by leverage.

Not unity. It possessed unity whenever the deep shudder came, and wisely released it afterward.

Not immortality.

Not even water.

What it lacked was margin.

In a mild drought, the city rationed. In a severe drought, it adapted. Gates hardened shut. Mites slept. Frogs vanished. Flowers remained furled. The oldest cistern fruits wrinkled as their contents were drawn away.

But beyond a certain point there was no adaptation, only a narrowing cascade. Dry bearings tore. Torn machinery demanded resin. Resin production demanded water. Closed flowers deprived pollinators. Failing pollinators deprived fruit organs. Mill colonies withheld meal from repair crews. Hungry rodents abandoned capstans. Ventilation slowed. Heat gathered. The city’s intricate dependencies, which made survival possible, became a net in which everything strangled everything else.

A single week could decide whether ten thousand years continued.

The power could have brought rain.

It could have opened the sky and poured a lake through every crack. But the Underfoundry had been shaped by thin rain. Plenty would hatch every waiting egg, swell every bridge, wake every fungus, burst dry conduits, and turn the streets into brown torrents. The first creatures to rejoice would be the first to drown.

The power could have placed a spring beneath the central hollow.

Then whoever held the hollow would rule. Beetles would fortify it. Wasps would valve it. Root-borers would threaten it. The city would reorganize around possession of the gift. Every treaty would become a water treaty; every war, a water war.

Nothing may remain singular.

The city had known this law before the power arrived. The power took care not to imagine itself exempt.

Therefore, far below the Underfoundry, it touched the stone in many places.

It did not break the stone. It persuaded hairline faults to become very slightly more hospitable to moisture. It cooled some grains and warmed others. It coaxed vapor to gather in pores too small for roots to enter. Across a wide depth, in scattered pockets, the earth began to keep a little of what it had formerly let escape.

Then the power taught the oldest organism a new kind of root.

The teaching took thirty-seven years.

The first attempts calcified. The second were eaten by blind grubs. One tendril reached too eagerly downward, found mineral brine, and poisoned a warehouse buttress. The power did not conceal the failure. Wasps sealed the tissue. Beetles ground the dead root into mortar. Crickets incorporated its loss into their measures.

At last, throughout the city, there appeared fine gray rootlets that did not seek ordinary wetness. They descended through old fissures and stopped above the scattered pockets in the stone.

They gave nothing while water remained in the cistern fruits.

They gave nothing during ordinary drought.

They opened only when the whole city approached the beginning of the narrowing cascade.

Even then, they could not be opened by one district.

A wasp valve had to be wetted with a beetle’s ration scent. A cricket had to strike the drought count against living wood. A rodent wheel had to turn against the normal direction of work. A moth carrying hatchery pollen had to brush the receptive hairs. The root organism itself had to be dry enough to complete the signal.

No species understood the whole sequence.

No species could perform it alone.

When the sequence was completed, the gray roots drew from the stone not streams but beads. Water appeared at hundreds of obscure points: beneath a snail track, inside an abandoned paper seam, at the bottom of a root-pig’s old excavation, along a ceiling crack frequented by mites. No point yielded enough to capture. Many yielded enough to preserve.

The water was bitter and faintly warm.

It was not enough to continue ordinary life.

It was enough to keep the breathing pods alive at half rhythm. Enough to soften the emergency gates. Enough for brood stocks, memory walls, structural cambium, and a measured number of mouths. Enough that the city could decide what to save instead of having the decision made entirely by collapse.

The power did not tell them the mercy existed.

It trusted discovery.

Many years later, during a drought longer than any paper nest remembered, the deep shudder came.

The Underfoundry stopped.

Dust hung in the dim lamp halos. The breathing pods labored with long spaces between contractions. Frogs had not called for months. Dead mites silvered the condensation reeds. Rodents lay outside their wheels to conserve strength. Mill beetles guarded stores too small to be worth raiding.

Delegations gathered on the central plate.

Wasps drummed closure.

Crickets counted remaining engine cycles.

Beetles laid the scents for brood, structure, air, meal, and abandonment.

Rodents pulled the marked string that meant the capstans would not turn again.

Nothing agreed with anything else.

Then an old messenger moth descended from a dark ceiling fold.

It was one of those tolerated remnants, scales rubbed almost bare, its body confused by destinations no longer present. It circled the hollow, settled on the drought plate, and opened its wings.

On them remained a trace of pollen from a flower gallery sealed three generations before.

The root plate accepted it.

A pale line appeared in the wood.

The wasps tasted the line and built a membrane over it. A thirsty beetle crossed the membrane, leaving ration scent. Crickets, hearing a tension beneath the plate that had never sounded before, struck the drought count. In the capstan galleries, a rodent searching for hidden pulp leaned against a wheel. It turned backward by one notch.

Deep below, distributed across stone, the gray roots opened.

The first bead of water appeared in a bearing beside a mineral-crusted snail.

The snail, becoming a landmark of great temporary importance, drank half.

The rest overflowed into a groove.

Soon there were cries—not of celebration, because no common cry existed, but hundreds of local recognitions. Mites stirred and brushed droplets into channels. Beetles abandoned guarded crumbs to carry news scents. Wasps tore apart their own sealed reserves to make cups. Crickets revised the remaining cycles. Rodents licked wet wood. The root organism eased its grip around the cisterns and spent a little stored sugar on repair.

The water did not end the drought.

It made the drought survivable.

That distinction was the whole gift.

No one thanked the power. No one knew there was a power to thank.

There were arguments over the gray roots. There were attempts to monopolize them, cut them, breed them, force them open early. Every such attempt failed or produced so little water that the effort cost more than it gained. Eventually the opening sequence became part of the central hollow’s embodied agreements.

The wasp nests preserved it as a drumming inclination.

The crickets made it a forbidden count, performed only after all ordinary measures were exhausted.

Beetles carried one component of its scent but never the whole.

Rodents taught their young that, in the worst dryness, a wheel might need to turn backward.

The moths were fed on a few revived plants from the sealed gallery. Thereafter some small portion of every messenger generation carried pollen for a destination that was not a place.

The city gave the event many incompatible meanings.

Some treated it as an unknown property of stone. Some as a concession wrested from the root organism. Some as evidence that obsolete routes should never be wholly forgotten. The rodents said an ancestress had found water by refusing work in the correct direction. The wasps slowly developed the prejudice that mercy required several kinds of creature to make it real.

This prejudice became deep.

The benevolent power remained attentive.

It turned aside no tunnel cat, but sometimes loosened a narrow exit after the cat had eaten enough.

It prevented no death, but occasionally delayed a useless one.

It did not make the mill colonies honest. It made false measures slightly more likely to sour in storage.

On the surface, where heavy feet sometimes crossed the barren mound, thorn roots thickened over the weakest portions of the ceiling. Bitter shrubs grew where poisons were most often spilled. To passing eyes the place remained dry, inconvenient, and unimportant.

Below, the Underfoundry continued to consume itself and restore itself.

Its citizens cheated, hunted, labored, bred, withheld, repaired, remembered, and forgot. Its machinery thundered. Its lamps blinked. Its old moths flew broken routes. Nothing became perfect. Nothing became safe forever.

But in the deep stone, beyond ownership, a little water waited.

Not salvation.

Not abundance.

A margin in which the many could become a people one more time.
