The power does not descend.

Descent would imply that it had found the city beneath itself, and this is not how it finds things. Nor does it arrive. Arrival would produce a boundary between before and after, and the city is too sensitive to boundaries not to be injured by one.

It attends.

For a long while, nothing changes.

The black pylons continue their fractional leaning. Mist follows the lower streets. The survey kites trail their delayed shadows across the plain. Children whisper into drains. Walls develop doors in the night, some useful, some inexplicable. In a western court, a reservoir controller repeats a valve sequence it first made for a woman dead twelve years, though no one is present to hear it.

The power attends to all of this without sorting it immediately into living and unliving, conscious and unconscious, intended and accidental. It has learned, over an age beyond measurement, that such sorting is often the first violence committed by the benevolent.

It does not ask, What here has a soul?

It asks, What here can be harmed?

The answers multiply.

Clay can be exhausted. A district can be made to carry too much certainty. A machine can be trapped inside the usefulness of its earliest behavior. A child can inherit a danger no map admits. A species can be loved into dependence. The dead can be preserved until their traces crowd out the living. A city that accepts change can still be unable to survive one change too great or too swift.

And beneath the plain, deeper than the transit lines, deeper than the warm circulations, deeper than the mineral seam that shines like powdered glass, the power finds the sea.

Not water itself.

The possibility of water.

A pressure held in stone. Salt asleep in microscopic chambers. Ancient organisms reduced to chemical inclinations. A whole drowned world folded so tightly into geology that no instrument in the city has distinguished it from silence.

The sea did not simply withdraw. Part of it went downward.

Part of it went forward.

The power perceives that one day—not soon by the measure of a household, but not impossibly far by the measure of clay—the plain may open. Green sand will loosen under gray clay. Warm brine will rise through ruler-straight cracks. Pools will fail to vanish at noon. Condensation will become rain, rain will become season, and season will become an inland tide.

The city has built itself beautifully around brief water.

It has not yet imagined water that stays.

The power could seal the depths. It could bind the strata, quiet the buried salts, and make the plain secure for ten thousand years. It could lift the city gently onto stone. It could turn the coming sea aside toward an uninhabited country.

But no country is uninhabited to a gaze patient enough.

And security of that kind has a cost. A sealed plain would cease to be young. Its tendencies would narrow. Ponds would become errors corrected in advance. Roads would stop wandering. The city might survive by becoming less itself.

The power could warn the people directly. It could place the future in every mind with perfect clarity.

But certainty, concentrated in thousands of bodies at once, would weigh more than any festival. Markets would empty. Water-keepers would become authorities over generations not yet born. The deep plain would be drilled, measured, defended. Every damp wall would be interpreted as an omen. Children would no longer be allowed to shout into drains.

So the power chooses neither concealment nor revelation.

It makes a small addition to the city’s grammar.

One night, during the warmest hour, every flower growing above an active data conduit closes at once.

The red groundcover does not follow traffic. Its petals darken and turn downward. Across parks, courtyards, abandoned lots, and strips of open silt, the plants form a pattern no resident has seen before: branching lines that ignore the buried infrastructure and continue beyond the outer districts.

Gardeners wake to silent alerts. Transit systems slow. Survey kites halt in the air, their threads drawing up by a finger’s width.

The pattern lasts eleven minutes.

Then the flowers resume their ordinary sequences.

By dawn, thousands of recordings exist, none complete. When combined, they do not produce a map of the city. They produce the outline of a coast.

It is a coast with no present sea.

Interpreters of tendency spend years disputing it. Some say the groundcover suffered a fungal event. Some say the city briefly remembered the seabed. Others believe the flowers displayed an old map carried in their engineered ancestry. Children trace the line in chalk across floors and give names to its bays.

The survey kites offer no explanation. Yet from that night onward, their threads begin to favor the imagined shore.

Not to build a wall.

To leave room.

A grove shifts uphill by degrees. A buried line divides into two redundant routes. New foundations near the lowest basin develop hollow ribs and detachable interiors. Towers begin growing porous lower stories through which water could pass without taking the structure with it. In districts far from any pool, doors appear several meters above the ground, opening onto empty air.

At first these features are called mistakes.

Later they are called preparations.

Still later, no one remembers a time when architecture did not make courtesies toward a possible sea.

The power continues attending.

It notices another danger, quieter than the buried water.

The city has made a virtue of provisionality. Its people know how to release rooms, names, paths, bodies, and claims. They understand that possession cannot be permanent. They have become skillful at letting go.

But some of them have begun to believe that because nothing can be kept forever, nothing may rightly ask to be kept at all.

There are houses that wish to remain houses and are dismantled as obsolete. There are machines whose preferences are indulged as charming habits but denied as testimony. There are people who long for one room that will recognize them for the whole of a life and are ashamed of this longing. There are mourners who release archives too soon because grief is considered a failure of redistribution. There are children who become easy to find after roads change and therefore are sent again and again to guide others, until they no longer know what it is to be sought themselves.

A culture can be gentle and still make an instrument of gentleness.

Here the power acts even less visibly.

It gives weight to refusal.

Not much. Only enough.

A warm new wall, asked to divide itself, remains whole.

A maintenance drone commanded into a collapsing ventilation shaft settles to the pavement and will not move.

A garden marked for migration roots through three layers of mineral substrate and flowers out of season.

An old man, brought to a decomposition grove after death according to the decision he made when young, does not decompose. The microbial chamber rejects him without toxicity or decay. For seven days his body remains warm.

His household gathers.

They search his archives and discover a later wish, entered under an alternate name and lost when two districts exchanged memory systems. He had wanted to be made into mineral feed for the school tower where he taught children to listen through walls.

When they carry him there, the warmth leaves him.

After this, refusal is no longer treated only as malfunction.

Courts begin hearing testimony from persistent behaviors. Builders are required to ask not merely what a structure can become, but what it has continued trying to be. Children are taught that courtesy includes allowing another being to change, and also allowing it not to change.

The power does not write these laws.

It merely makes certain silences harder to ignore.

Years pass.

The coast drawn by flowers becomes a civic hypothesis. No one is compelled to believe it. That is important. Skepticism keeps the preparations from becoming worship, and belief keeps skepticism from becoming negligence.

The first harbor is built on dry land.

It consists of a long crescent of gardens, stepped reservoirs, flexible towers, and broad passages descending into compacted silt. There are mooring places for vessels that do not exist. There are tide marks ascending blank walls. Tea is served in a pavilion designed to float if necessary.

People laugh at it affectionately.

Festivals migrate there. Children sail leaf platforms through ceramic channels. Water-keepers practice abundance by opening all the gates at once. Engineers study how buildings behave when their lower rooms are deliberately flooded. The six-legged waders arrive despite there being no natural pool and gather along the highest imagined tide line.

A name is needed.

Not a spoken name, because any spoken name would make the place too definite. Its primary name is tapped through the wrist: two light pressures, a pause, and a third pressure sustained until another person answers.

Its meaning is approximately:

**the place where we will meet what does not leave.**

The benevolent power receives no thanks. It has offered no proof of itself.

This is as it wishes.

Once, however, a child becomes aware of it.

She is loud even for a child. She has found acoustic passages adults have not mapped and is prohibited from three reservoir arcades after waking every sleeping infant in a district with a single delighted cry.

One afternoon she walks beyond the harbor, following a survey kite whose shadow has stopped lagging behind it.

The kite travels over bare silt. Its thread hangs down. The child places her wrist beneath the thread, though she has been warned never to do this alone.

Several possible versions of her life draw near: gardener, fugitive, water-keeper, maker of vessels, old woman, body beneath a pool. She feels them, but behind them she feels something else—not a version of herself, and not exactly a mind.

An attention without hunger.

She asks aloud, “Are you the sea?”

The power could answer.

Instead, far beneath her feet, salt shifts inside stone.

The child crouches and presses both palms to the warm ground. She remains there until evening. When she returns, she tells no one she encountered a god. She says only that the plain is practicing how to become water.

This phrase enters children’s games.

Then gardeners’ notes.

Then probability maps.

Many generations later, when brine first wells into a forgotten court and does not sink away by noon, the city is not ready.

No city can be ready for the end of its ground.

There is fear. There are failures. Some towers cannot release their lower floors and fall. Warm-wall films die in the changing salinity. Reed creatures emerge into daylight and perish on high embankments. Families lose archives. The oldest eastern garden, rooted too deeply to move, drowns.

The power does not prevent all grief.

To prevent all grief it would have to prevent all attachment, all risk, all particular life. This it will not do.

But the water rises into rooms built to yield. It passes through porous foundations. Transit divides along redundant lines. Floating gardens break free from their beds and collect against the dry-land harbor, exactly where children once tied leaf platforms to imaginary moorings.

Vessels are grown from reservoir skins and translucent ribs.

The city does not drown.

It changes category.

Its plazas become lagoons. Causeways become chains of islands. Towers answer one another across open water, their adjustment notes altered by the moving weight below. Red groundcover retreats to upper terraces. New dark vegetation spreads in the warm shallows. Six-legged waders arrive in such numbers that no one can know them individually anymore, and this is mourned as a kind of loss even while the species flourishes.

The people remain dispersed. Their households become more so.

Maps continue to record probabilities.

Ownership still applies for intervals.

The sea takes some districts and gives others routes into countries previously unreachable. Salt enters the mist. Clothing develops white branching stains. Children learn to shout across water, where sound has different weather.

In time, the city builds no defenses against the sea and makes no surrender to it. It negotiates, as it once negotiated with clay, condensation, walls, moss, and the habits of useful machines.

The power watches until its intervention can no longer be distinguished from what the city learned by itself.

Then it withdraws its attention—not entirely, never entirely, but enough that the place may be unwatched without being abandoned.

This is the final gift.

Not rescue.

Not permanence.

Not even wisdom.

Room in which warning can become play, play can become custom, custom can become readiness, and readiness can meet catastrophe without ceasing to be alive.

Above the changed plain, survey kites move through pearl-gray haze. Beneath them lies a young sea crowded with towers, gardens, gliders, vessels, mineral ribs, and the warm lights of scattered households.

Their shadows no longer lag behind.

They travel slightly ahead.
