The power does not arrive as light.

Light would be too easily mistaken for revelation, and revelation would divide the city into before and after. It does not arrive as thunder, because thunder already belongs there. It does not take a body and walk the avenues, though it could. A body would make paths where none were needed, frighten the deer, draw the fox away from its den, and crush the brief green films that open after rain.

It attends.

For a long while—long enough that several generations of swifts hatch beneath the cornices and depart south, long enough that a shrub adds a finger’s width to its twisted trunk—it does nothing.

This is not indifference. It is the first kindness.

The power has seen worlds injured by rescue. It knows how abundance can become another catastrophe: forests planted where thin grass was whole, rivers straightened for the sake of thirsty roots, predators removed to spare a few trembling animals until hunger multiplied among them. It knows that loneliness is not always suffering, that silence is not always a plea, and that a ruin need not be repaired in order to be loved.

So it learns the place in the manner the place permits.

It follows water.

It descends as rain through cold air and touches a roof slab darkened by centuries. It rests in a chisel hollow with a drowned beetle and three grains of rust-red sand. It spills into a gutter, enters a conduit, divides around the root of a silver grass, and vanishes beneath a collapsed stair. It passes through mineral dark where the colorless crustaceans extend their feelers. It rises again in a lower court, colder than the day, and lies beneath the motionless throat of a pale newt.

It learns the city not as a map but as a sequence of permissions.

Here water may pass.

Here it must wait.

Here a wall holds because rubble presses evenly against it.

Here an arch appears sound but bears one winter more than it can survive.

Here the fox enters beneath a foundation.

Here the deer will not cross because two fallen stones have made a narrow place smell of entrapment.

Here swift chicks drown in years when rain is driven beneath the cornice.

Here a cistern persists through drought.

Here another, deeper cistern has become sterile because no fragment of leaf has reached it for longer than trees have been absent from the plateau.

The power attends to every scale without preferring the large.

It knows the broad continent of stone, the buried quarters, the aquifer breathing beneath them. It also knows the mite stranded on the dry side of a bead of water. Neither knowledge cancels the other.

At last, it finds suffering.

There is less of it than a visitor might imagine, and more.

The city does not suffer because its builders are gone. Stone does not long for the hand that set it. The plazas do not resent becoming pools. The drowned stairways do not dream of footsteps.

The water does not suffer from having no destination.

The moss does not suffer from being small.

The fox does not suffer because it is solitary. Its solitude is spacious, alert, complete.

But in a high northern quarter, a chain of perennial cisterns has become divided. One basin lies a little above the next, and once, in heavy rain, each overflow fed the one below. Frost has shifted the lip of the upper channel. Water now spills outward across bare paving and is lost to evaporation before it reaches the lower courts.

In the highest basin, the newts are numerous enough.

In the lowest, a few remain.

Between them are three pools in which the water grows shallower each summer. The newts there have become pale even for their kind. Their eggs fail when spring withdraws too quickly. They cannot climb the dry walls. No flood in living memory has joined their water to any other.

Elsewhere, beneath seven stories of collapsed rooms, a fissure is narrowing with calcite. Beyond it lives a population of translucent crustaceans no larger than seeds. Their water remains cold and clean, but almost nothing edible now reaches them. They survive upon such slight nourishment that a person would call it nothing. Soon it will become nothing.

On the eastern terraces, the only shrub that fruits reliably after hard winters has split near its root. Wind enters the split. In another season of ice, the trunk will divide entirely, and the small mammals that feed on its bitter blue fruit will lose one of the stations by which they cross that district.

The power sees these things.

It also sees that death is not an error.

It will not preserve every body. It will not make the newts immortal, feed every crustacean by hand, hold every lichen forever to its stone. A world in which nothing can be lost is a world in which nothing can make room. Mercy cannot mean arrest.

What it chooses instead is continuity.

Not the continuity of individuals.

The continuity of passage.

On a night of ordinary frost, water freezes beneath the shifted lip of the upper cistern. The power adds no unnatural force. It merely permits one crystal to form where another might have formed. Ice expands against the stone. At dawn, a fragment no larger than a knuckle breaks free and falls into the basin.

When rain comes three days later, the overflow finds the old groove.

A narrow thread crosses the pavement. It is not enough. Much of it still vanishes among the joints.

The next rain carries grit. The grit lodges in the lowest gaps. Moss spores follow. In two wet seasons a dark line holds moisture across the open court. In seven years, water reaches the second basin often enough to keep it through summer. In nineteen, a storm fills all five, and for one night they become a single descending body.

The newts do not understand deliverance.

They smell water where there was stone.

One moves first, climbing through the spillway under cover of rain. Others follow in later years. Eggs adhere beneath ledges in pools that had grown still. Bloodlines separated for longer than any newt could remember mix without ceremony.

The power does not watch for gratitude.

Far below, where the fissure closes with pale mineral growth, it makes an alteration smaller still. A swift dies over the western marsh.

The bird’s death is not caused. Its heart has reached its end after seasons of flight. Ordinarily the body would fall among sedges, feed beetles, and enter the open water. A crosswind takes one loosened feather and carries it over the roofs.

The feather lands in a rain gutter.

Rain folds it, draws it through a broken screen, and catches it in a shaft above the buried quarter. There it remains through autumn, collecting dust, lichen flakes, a seed husk, the dry shell of a mite. Winter water loosens the little mass. It descends through the shaft, comes apart in darkness, and passes by increments into the narrowing fissure.

For the creatures below, it is a season of plenty.

Not endless plenty. Enough.

Their population rises. Some pass through a secondary crack opened by the same water pressure that the added matter has faintly changed. They enter a chamber where none of their kind had moved before. The old pocket may one day close entirely, but they will not close with it.

The power does not break the stone. It teaches no creature to escape. It places no feast where famine would only return more cruelly.

It allows a feather to arrive.

For the split shrub, the intervention takes the form of a fox.

The fox is hunting along a retaining wall when it hears a small animal moving among the roots. It springs. The animal escapes. The fox’s shoulder strikes a loose slab, which tilts and settles against the shrub’s divided trunk.

At first the slab appears more dangerous than helpful. It presses into the bark. Rain gathers along its edge. But when winter wind crosses the court, the stone bears part of the strain. The split does not widen.

In spring, the shrub forms callus around the wound.

In time, its roots grow beneath the tilted slab and make it part of their hold. The trunk remains scarred and asymmetrical. One half dies during a dry decade. The other continues and fruits.

The fox never returns to that court.

These are the first acts of the power, though no one would recognize them as acts. They do not end drought, collapse, hunger, predation, frost, or death. They alter no law. They produce no monument.

Having begun, the power finds that the city asks something more difficult than intervention.

It asks companionship without occupation.

So the power remains.

It does not rule the weather, but sometimes, when several possible winds are equally natural, it favors the one that carries spores across a district they could not otherwise cross. When a stone must fall, it does not always prevent the fall; sometimes it inclines the stone by the breadth of a lichen crust so that it makes a shelter rather than sealing a spring. When floodwater divides around rubble, it occasionally gives one branch the smallest advantage, keeping an old marsh wet while allowing a newer pool to dry and renew its silt.

Often it chooses wrongly.

Wisdom does not mean that all consequences can be known. The city is too entangled even for benevolence to simplify without harm.

A channel reopened for the sedges also gives the fox easier access to a nesting court. A protected cistern fills with beetle larvae that consume a generation of newt eggs. A wall spared from collapse continues casting shade over lichens that would have flourished in sun. One shrub’s survival means another seedling receives less water.

The power does not recoil from this knowledge, nor harden itself against it.

It learns.

It accepts that goodness here cannot be the removal of every cost. Goodness is attention that does not become ownership. It is help small enough to remain answerable. It is the refusal to love an imagined city more than the city that exists.

Centuries pass.

The presence of the power becomes, in effect, another one of the place’s hidden conditions, like the water table or the prevailing wind. Nothing worships it. Nothing names it. There are no signs.

Yet certain improbable continuities begin to accumulate.

No species becomes abundant, but fewer become trapped in single pockets.

The moss bands spread through shaded corridors from one watershed to another. Seeds cross ridges inside clots of mud carried on deer hooves. Newts occupy cisterns that had been empty for ages. Underground creatures find hairline passages through mineral deposits just before older passages close. Swifts return to ledges that remain dry in the worst springs, though neighboring cornices fall. The foxes—never more than a few across the immensity—discover a chain of shelters spaced roughly by a night’s travel.

The city remains severe.

The power does not soften the climate until silver grass becomes meadow, then scrub, then forest. To do so would save some lives and extinguish the forms adapted to bareness. It does not fill the plateau with animals simply because abundance looks like health from a distance. It understands that the city’s life has been composed in rests as much as in notes.

Nor does it restore the builders.

It could perhaps gather something from the shapes they left: a gate groove worn by hands, soot trapped in a ceiling, salts from ancient bodies, carvings blurred beneath lichen. It could make voices walk again in the markets. It could raise roofs and light lamps and send wheels turning beside channels.

It does not.

The dead are not brought back merely because their absence is immense.

To return them without their consent, without their history, without the world into which their choices belonged, would be to make imitations and call them heirs. To restore the city around its present inhabitants would be another abandonment: the newts displaced from cisterns, moss scraped from thresholds, fox dens opened, drowned chambers drained in the name of reclaiming them.

The power honors the builders differently.

It preserves no palace.

Instead, it preserves the generosity hidden in what they made.

A basin continues to hold rain.

A wall continues to break the wind.

A stair, though no foot needs its destination, slows floodwater enough for silt to settle.

A roofless chamber gathers warmth for beetles.

An avenue receives a seasonal river without collapsing.

The intentions of the dead are allowed to become gifts they never foresaw.

Once, only once, the power does something that might be called visible.

It happens after an earthquake beneath the eastern escarpments.

The movement begins deep below the plateau. Springs cloud. Birds rise from the marsh. In buried quarters, columns already dissolved to narrow waists give way. The city answers across its breadth: a low succession of impacts, walls settling against walls, water striking the roofs of drowned chambers.

A fracture opens beneath the largest seasonal plaza.

If it widens, the shallow lake will drain in a single night into the lower ruins. The marsh fed by its slow seepage will vanish. Several underground chambers will fill violently with silt. The eastern face, saturated and newly unsupported, may descend in a landslide large enough to take miles of terraces with it.

The power considers the whole chain.

Then it enters the fracture.

Not as light. Not as a hand.

As stillness.

For one moment, pressure that would have traveled east travels down. One deep stratum bears what it would ordinarily have passed upward. The cost is real: far beneath the uninhabited lowlands, rock folds and melts along a line no living thing will encounter for ages. Above, the fracture stops widening.

It remains open.

The power does not erase it. Water finds the crack and drains through, but slowly. The great pool lasts fewer days each wet season. New reeds establish around the narrowing shore. Some old moss beds dry and disappear. A colony of newts is lost when one shallow basin fails.

But the escarpment holds.

For several nights after the earthquake, mineral-rich water rises through the fracture and spreads across the plaza. When it recedes, it leaves a pale line branching over the dark pavement.

From the height of a broken tower, the mark resembles no letter and no figure. It is only the record of pressure finding release.

Years soften it.

Lichen crosses it.

The deer walks over it without pausing.

The power is content.

It has not made the city safe. Safety at that scale would require turning it into something other than itself. It has made catastrophe less absolute. It has given life more than one route.

That becomes its enduring work.

Where there is only one spring, it encourages a second seep.

Where there is only one nesting ledge, it allows a crack to widen beneath another.

Where all the roots of a shrub descend through the same joint, it guides one toward a neighboring seam.

Where an underground population depends upon a single shaft for nourishment, it helps water remember an older course.

Not everywhere. Not always. Redundancy has its own weight, and every connection can carry disease, predators, flood, and fire as readily as renewal. The power does not weave the whole city into one seamless system. Separation, too, can shelter.

It makes doors, not highways.

It leaves some shut.

After a very long time, the power understands that the being who lives in the city is not the fox, though each fox is wholly itself; not the deer, the newts, the crustaceans, the moss, or the rain. It is not even the city, if by city one means the arrangement of stone.

The being is the relation among them.

It has no single body because its body is exchange.

Water enters rock and emerges bearing minerals. Lichen enters stone by fractions and becomes soil. Soil receives seed. Seed becomes grass. Grass catches mist and slows grit. A beetle feeds beneath it. A swift takes the beetle. A feather enters a drain. A submerged creature feeds. Its body returns to water. Water deposits a pale skin beneath an arch.

The being is made of passages.

It is injured wherever passage becomes impossible, and also wherever all boundaries are erased. It thrives neither in isolation nor in undifferentiated union, but in the difficult interval: separate things reaching one another without ceasing to be separate.

The wise power loves it for this.

Perhaps the power recognizes itself.

So it gives the being no voice. A voice would favor the creatures capable of hearing. It gives it no face. A face would make the unseen parts seem lesser. It grants no consciousness that would suddenly awaken across the plateau and find itself alone among ruins. Awareness is not an uncomplicated gift. To impose it might fill every rain with grief.

Instead, it offers what the being already is, in greater measure:

resilience without hardness,

connection without conquest,

change without erasure,

memory without captivity.

The city continues.

Towers descend into stumps. Stumps become ridges. Courts fill. Walls become terraces; terraces become dark outcrops whose straightness is visible only at noon. The drowned quarters sink deeper. Some roofs collapse and release ancient pockets of air. New springs emerge. Old channels rise above the land around them and stand for a while as narrow stone spines before frost takes them apart.

There comes a century of unusual dryness.

Cisterns fail from the top of the plateau downward. Moss retreats to the mouths of deep springs. Shrubs shed leaves. Deer cease to cross the upper districts. Swifts arrive and do not remain.

The power could call rain.

It does not call enough to end the dry age, because the dry age belongs to forces larger than the city and carries consequences beyond the horizon. Rain stolen from elsewhere would merely move suffering out of sight.

But it finds water wasted in a buried conduit whose outlet faces bare cliff. A fall centuries earlier diverted it from an interior basin. The power waits until noon warms the exposed wall, then lets a root, already growing there, enter a softened seam. At night the seam opens.

At first only dampness appears inside the basin.

Then drops.

Then a thread.

The basin does not fill. It holds a palm’s depth beneath its lowest ledge. Enough for eggs. Enough for beetles. Enough for a fox to drink every few nights without emptying it. Enough that when rain finally returns decades later, life does not have to cross the whole plateau to begin again.

There comes, much later, a century of rain.

The lower city becomes a chain of lakes. Walls fall under saturated weight. The red sedge spreads until whole plazas take its color. Newts move widely; foxes retreat to the high ridges. Mineral springs shift. Fog remains for weeks.

The power does not defend every old dryness. It lets marsh become open water. It lets some terrestrial paths close and others rise along roofs and causeways. It intervenes only where the floods would join every underground pool into one body, because one contamination could then reach them all.

A single roof collapse blocks the deepest connection.

Above it, water rises and spills safely east.

Below it, the colorless creatures continue in separate darks.

No age is made permanent.

This, finally, is the gift.

Not rescue from time.

Time, made spacious.

Enough time for one route to close and another to be found. Enough time for a population to move, for a root to divide, for a moss bed to release spores, for water to wear a new lip into stone. Enough time for accident not always to mean ending.

The benevolent power remains so deeply within ordinary causes that its mercy cannot be proved.

Perhaps every shifted stone would have shifted anyway.

Perhaps the feather would have fallen into that shaft without guidance.

Perhaps the fracture would have stopped.

Perhaps the fox would have struck the slab.

Wisdom leaves room for perhaps.

To be unmistakable would make the city into evidence, and evidence attracts pilgrims, claims, roads, altars, quarrels, custodians, thieves. The place would become a story about the power instead of continuing as itself.

So the power hides its goodness inside probability.

Rain falls.

Frost enters a crack.

A root turns toward water.

A stone settles.

A creature finds that where there had been no passage, there is just enough.

On a clear evening, sunlight travels from the western escarpment across the continent of roofs. Pools answer one by one. For a little while, every connected watercourse holds the same sky: upper cisterns, flooded stairs, marsh saddles, black reservoirs, springs beneath foundations, and the narrow threads descending east toward the distant rivers.

The light does not make them one body.

It reveals that they have always touched.

The fox drinks and lifts its head.

Newts hang beneath the skin of a basin.

Swifts turn high over the plaza.

Under miles of stone, translucent feelers search moving water and encounter a fragment of nourishment carried from a district that has no name.

The power attends.

For now, nothing more is needed.
