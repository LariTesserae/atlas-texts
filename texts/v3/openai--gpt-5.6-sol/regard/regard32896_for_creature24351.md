### What the Power Does

The power does not bring sunlight.

It knows that sudden sunlight would bleach the moss, blind the eels, heat the copper, wake poisons in the manufactured mud, and fill every cistern with green death. It does not tear down the walls in the name of freedom. It does not straighten what has bowed, cleanse what has grown fertile through filth, or restore the channels to their first intentions.

It does not assume that the builders were wiser than the rain.

Nor does it mistake endurance for consent.

This distinction occupies it for a long while.

The power enters the Rain Court in the smallest form available: not as a body, nor a voice, but as attention distributed through water. It falls in the mist, condenses on the copper bowls, runs the snail circuits, passes around the eels, and rests for an instant in every frog’s sealed chamber. It allows itself to be swallowed by minnows and filtered by crabs. It goes down through the hairline cracks surrounding the drain house and rises again as warm vapor carrying the taste of charcoal.

For one entire turning of the Court’s hidden calendar, it does nothing else.

It learns that the Court has more seasons than weather. There is the season when the bats’ young cannot bear their portion of the rope. The season when the silver minnows lay eggs on copper. The season when old rain crows remember channels that no longer run. The brief lime season, when the walls soften enough to accept repairs. The season of warm shrimp. The season when the triangular pool dreams so strongly that all water in the Court tastes faintly of sleep.

The power learns every duty.

Then it learns every suffering disguised as a duty.

These are not the same.

The crabs enjoy their sorting, insofar as crabs enjoy anything. The snails do not resent their roads. The bats choose their roosting ropes because the ropes are warm with one another’s bodies. The frogs’ warnings are also courtship, rivalry, exuberance, and song. The crows’ maps are not merely labor. They are inheritance.

But young birds drown because one forgotten sluice releases too quickly.

Bats with torn claws cannot leave the lifting rope without endangering the colony beneath them.

Some frogs have become living stoppers in cracks that no longer lead anywhere, and they wait months for vibrations that will never return.

The maintainers—whatever they are—sleep in shifts so narrow that no one among them has dreamed to completion in generations.

And the pale fish beneath the triangular pool is not there because it wishes to be.

The power discovers this last truth far below.

The triangular pool is not a pool. It is the open end of a folded conduit, a depth bent upon itself many times. The great pale being moves through that folded depth as an eel moves through a jar handle: alive, turning, unable to arrive anywhere its head has not already been.

It was placed there.

Perhaps the builders meant it as a regulator, an oracle, a reservoir, or a punishment. Their purpose has worn away. The captivity remains.

The power does not free it at once.

Wisdom without patience is merely force with a serene expression.

Instead, on the first silent interval after the lime season, the power descends to the hammering place.

Three blows sound below.

A pause.

Two blows.

The drain house trembles, as it always has.

Then the power answers with one.

The single blow is not loud. A bead of water falls from a copper bowl and strikes the pavement. Yet the note passes through every fitted stone. Frogs open their mineral-sealed eyes. Minnows turn upstream. The pulley bats lift their rust-colored faces.

Below, the hammering stops.

For the first time in any living creature’s memory, something comes up the drain house steps.

It is small.

It has five-toed feet wrapped in eel hide. Wax covers its hair and shoulders. Its skin is gray-white and finely wrinkled, not from age but from generations of damp. A copper hammer hangs from one hand. Around its waist are pouches of pegs, shell slivers, fiber, hooks, and pale stones.

It stands in the low doorway of the drain house, trembling at the size of the Court.

The rain crows see it.

They do not attack. An old bird removes a copper fragment from the feathers under its throat and strikes the pavement once.

The maintainer answers with its hammer.

After that, the power begins its work.

It creates no new law. It adds an exception.

Far beneath the western grate, where vanished things have gathered for centuries, it opens a second course for the water. It is a modest channel, rough-walled and winding. It does not replace the old system. Most days it carries nothing.

But just before a flood pulse would become fatal, the new course accepts the excess.

The Court notices slowly.

A surge comes. Young crows spread their useless wings. Frogs climb the columns. Crabs brace in their inherited mouths.

The water rises.

Then, at the height where drowning usually begins, it ceases to rise.

Nothing dramatic announces the mercy. There is only a low gulp below the western grate and the mild settling of floodwater around a tile.

The inhabitants do not understand. They survive anyway.

The power makes other changes of this kind.

It cuts a hair-thin runnel behind the chambers of the waiting frogs, enough to bring them the vibrations of living water. Some wake. Some do not. Those that wake push themselves backward out of the wall, shedding mineral casts of their own bodies. For several days they sit blinking in the gray-blue light, as if astonished to find that existence has continued without the task that imprisoned them.

The power strengthens one bat rope, but not with an unbreakable fiber. An unbreakable rope would eventually tear the gate from the masonry. Instead it teaches a certain fungus to grow through the braid. The fungus is soft, edible, and faintly luminous. It bears part of the strain and shows the bats where the rope is weakest. They begin to repair those places before they split.

It does not cure the beetles of hunger, nor make the willow immortal. It guides one root through a clay pipe that has been closed since before the oldest crow hatched. At the pipe’s end lies a handful of true soil.

Only a handful.

Dark, granular, smelling of stone that has never been fired.

The willow touches it.

For an entire season nothing visible happens. Then, on the highest of the seven branches, the tree produces one new leaf of an unfamiliar green.

The power also gives the Court a rest.

This is the most difficult gift.

Every forty-nine flood pulses, the hidden bypass carries the labor of the whole basin for the length of one dimming. Water continues to breathe through the walls. Cisterns remain cool. Eels stay wet. No eggs dry. But the gates need not be lifted, the pipe mouths need not be cleared, the tiles need not be turned, and no creature’s body is required to stand between the Court and failure.

At first, the inhabitants do not know what to do.

The bats hang from their ropes though the ropes have gone slack.

Crabs sort perfectly clean grains.

Rain crows patrol dry feeding places and click warnings for floods that do not come. Frogs climb the walls in anticipation, then remain there, embarrassed and exposed.

Below, the maintainers wake according to their ancient shifts, take up their tools, and find no urgent leak.

The first rest passes in anxiety.

So does the second.

During the third, a young rain crow drops a pebble into a copper bowl simply to hear the note.

Another answers.

Soon the birds are striking bowl after bowl, not in warning, not to mark a current or announce food, but because one sound leads beautifully to another. Frogs call against the notes. Bats crawl from the lifting ropes and hang beneath the footbridge in chattering clusters. The maintainers sit in the doors of the eastern huts, faces concealed under waxed veils, and listen.

By the seventh rest, there is music in the Rain Court.

Uneven music. Clattering, booming, damp, and full of interruptions.

But useless.

Gloriously useless.

Only after the Court has learned rest does the power turn to the triangular pool.

It does not break the folded conduit. To do so would collapse half the western pavement. Instead it patiently unfolds one direction within it. Not all directions. Not enough to permit some enormous thing to rise among the frogs and crows.

Only a road outward.

It touches the pale fish through the still water and offers no command. It shows the being the open road. It also shows what departure will cost: the loss of familiar pressures, the end of returning echoes, waters with unknown salt, distances unbounded by fitted stone.

The pale fish considers for a long time.

Its eye appears beneath the pool on many successive rests.

Then, during a mist-rain so fine that the Court seems suspended inside a cloud, the fish turns.

Its colorless flank passes once beneath the triangular curbs. Markings like channels move across it. The water deepens without rising. For several hours the Court hears a sound like masonry being remembered.

Then the fish is gone.

The triangular pool becomes only a pool.

Rain strikes it and makes rings.

No creature approaches for three days. On the fourth, a needle-legged crab tastes the edge. On the sixth, minnows enter and find the water ordinary. On the seventh, an old frog drinks.

Nothing terrible happens.

Last, the power opens a door.

Not the bricked gate. Removing those ancient stones would invite whatever lives outside to enter too quickly, and the Court is not defenseless merely because it is enclosed.

The power makes a passage no predator can use and no flood can force.

It begins behind the western wall as a crack wide enough for a crab. Farther on, it widens to admit a frog, then a bat, then a rain crow moving sideways. Beyond that lies a long sequence of damp chambers, each slightly brighter than the last. There are channels underfoot, sheltered roosts above, and niches where a traveler can turn back.

At the far end is rain beneath an open sky.

The passage opens only from within.

No creature is driven toward it.

For years, nothing completes the journey. Crabs establish pipe mouths in the first chamber. Frogs colonize the second. Bats reach the fourth and retreat from the stars, terrified by the absence of a ceiling.

A rain crow is the first to go all the way.

It is an old bird that has spent half its life waiting beside a dry wall-mouth for a vanished flood. It walks the passage slowly, carrying an inherited copper fragment beneath its throat.

At the outer threshold it sees earth.

Not manufactured mud. Not powdered brick or lime, but deep brown soil going down farther than its beak can dig. It sees plants whose roots are not threaded through pipes. It sees water falling without being caught.

The crow stands under that impossible rain.

Then it turns around and returns to the Court.

It tells the others in copper notes.

Later, some leave. Most do not.

The maintainers are among the last to approach the passage. When they do, they carry their tools. At the first chamber they repair a joint the power deliberately left imperfect. At the second they install a peg. At the third they leave seven pale stones.

They do not abandon the Rain Court.

But after discovering the sky, they stop believing the Court is all that exists.

This changes their repairs.

They begin adding small doors where once they sealed every opening. They make channels that can be entered from either end. They reinforce the bridge not only for the weight that crosses it, but for those who might wish to pause in the middle.

The power does not remain as a ruler. It accepts no hut, image, bowl, or sacrifice. It does not reveal the original plan, because the original plan no longer deserves authority merely for being first.

It leaves the Court mortal.

Walls will still bow. Species will alter. Channels will silt and open elsewhere. One day the drain house may crack. One day the willow may die. Benevolence does not require freezing a beloved thing beyond change.

But no single failure can now destroy the whole.

No necessary labor is without respite.

No inhabitant is kept merely because its captivity is useful.

And there is a way out that is not an expulsion.

Long afterward, the inhabitants disagree over whether any power came at all. The crows say the western channel was uncovered by a bird whose name has been forgotten. The frogs insist the new vibrations were always in the stone. The bats attribute the rope fungus to good nesting. The maintainers record the single answering blow but do not say who struck it.

Only during the forty-ninth pulse, when the water takes up its own burden and every creature is released for a little while from usefulness, does the question cease to matter.

Then the copper bowls ring for no warning.

The frogs answer for no reason.

Small figures emerge from the walls and sit beside the cisterns.

And rain falls through the old constructed world, still caught, still released, but no longer held by suffering alone.
