The power did not come down in light.

There was already too much light.

It did not speak from the sky. The stone would have swallowed the voice after a few paces, and in any case the people of the bowl distrusted voices that expected obedience merely because they came from above.

It did not still the waters. To still them would have killed the eels, closed the warm drains, burst the lower cisterns, and made every house in the city depend upon a mercy that might one day be withdrawn.

It did not warm the mountain. Snow would have loosened from the ridges. Ice bound deep in the folded stone would have melted in places no listener could hear. The bowl would have filled, then broken.

It did not make the city permanent.

That would have been the cruelest act of all.

Instead, the power came through the northern gate during a white noon, wearing the appearance of an ordinary traveler. It chose no fixed age or sex. Those who remembered a broad-faced old woman were certain of it; those who remembered a young man with frost in his eyebrows were equally certain. A cistern bird saw only another tall bird. The rodents smelled warm stone. One silver fox looked directly at the traveler, sneezed, and went back into a drain.

At the gate, the traveler waited to be noticed.

A child found them by colliding with their knees.

The child’s tether cord had caught around a projecting hinge. Her keeper was three turns below, pulling gently and assuming the child had stopped to examine beetles. The traveler freed the cord without untying it and allowed the child to lead them inside.

Hospitality began with darkness.

The traveler sat in a black-wool room while warm cloths were laid across their eyes. They were given salty tea in a shallow cup and asked no questions until they could see.

Then an old path keeper said, “Whose water wakes you?”

It was the customary way to ask where a person belonged.

The power considered many true answers.

At last it said, “Water that has not arrived yet.”

This was taken to mean that the traveler came from a dry country.

No one pitied them aloud.

They remained in the city for a full turning of the eel migrations.

They cleared ice badly at first. Their hooked tool went too deep and chipped a gutter. They apologized, repaired the gutter, and thereafter used exactly the right pressure. They carried dung cakes, held cords for blind masons, watched children at flooded crossings, and spent three freezing nights at an upper gate where the spray coated their clothes until they seemed to be wearing armor.

They accepted correction.

This did more to conceal their nature than any disguise.

No miracle followed their hands. When they lifted stone, they lifted only what a person of their apparent size could lift. When a bridge strained, they did not strengthen it invisibly; they called the roof walkers and stood beneath the handrail with everyone else. When their fingers cracked from cold, they rubbed them with rendered oil.

They entered each newly appeared doorway only after water had been poured over its threshold.

They never displaced an animal from warmth.

They tasted every unfamiliar drip.

The city instructed them.

It showed them that wisdom was not the same as preventing change. It showed them walls designed to loosen safely, roofs that surrendered their burdens, doors awaiting destinations. It showed them old bones made into useful hinges and errors preserved in stories because fortunate correction was more valuable than flawless pride.

The power had known civilizations with towers that boasted they would stand until the end of the world. It had seen those towers kill generations beneath their fall. Here, a mason carved her mark on a panel intended to crack before winter and smiled when the fracture followed the line she had made.

The power loved her.

It loved the listeners lying cheek-down on wet paving. It loved the divers who entered black water and surrendered memories to bring back warnings. It loved the child who carried three displaced rodents inside her coat because someone had cleared their drain. It loved the curse spoken over a fox while an escape hole was widened for it.

It loved the city’s refusal to confuse control with care.

Yet love did not make it blind.

It saw the unnecessary portion of suffering.

It saw people who had read every sign correctly and still lacked time to act. It saw a listener raise one finger and save six houses but not the seventh. It saw an eel bury its head, warning of a mountain-shake, while the person sent to carry the message slipped from a stair erased by glare. It saw a roof walker reach a sleeping room one breath after the ice came through.

The inhabitants did not complain to the sky about these things. Afterward they examined sequences, replaced rails, altered gates, assigned maintenance, and told stories of the dead with exactness.

But acceptance is not consent.

Endurance does not make pain desirable.

The power understood this too.

During the deepest part of winter, the traveler sat beside an elderly listener named Orra. She had lost almost all hearing in open air, though she could feel water gathering behind three spans of stone. Rodents slept across her boots. She was repairing a red cord that had been repaired so often no original fiber remained.

The traveler asked, “If you could command the mountain once, what would you tell it?”

Orra gave the traveler a suspicious look.

“Nothing,” she said. “It would misunderstand.”

“What would you ask of it, then?”

“That it misunderstand slowly.”

The traveler laughed. Orra felt the laughter through the bench and smiled.

Later the traveler asked the same question of others.

A diver wanted memories to remain after deep water, but when pressed she admitted that some things beneath the White Court might be mercifully forgotten.

A mason wanted stone that could be cut like cheese and would harden afterward. His apprentice objected that such stone would make everyone lazy about seams.

A mother wanted handrails to grow wherever children reached out.

A child wanted the sun to close one eye.

A dung renderer wanted a second back.

A gate keeper wanted every fool upstream to be born downstream for one season.

One of the six-horned grazers licked frost from the traveler’s sleeve. The power understood its answer as a preference for salt.

The black birds requested nothing. Their attention was elsewhere.

At the end of the year the traveler went beneath the White Court.

They entered without cord or bone mouthpiece, but no one witnessed this. The cisterns admitted them through passages too narrow for flesh. Pressure arranged itself around the power with vast, slow curiosity.

There was something there.

Not an animal. Not a mind as the people understood minds. The mountain knew weight, entry, release, accumulation, division. It knew the patience of hollows. It knew every drop by pressure but none by name.

The power did not command it.

Instead, the power lay along the submerged terraces and listened.

For seven days the White Court’s water tasted faintly of snow, although none fell. The eels formed rings in the lower channels. Mineral objects on the eastern wall shifted every night toward the center, leaving narrow empty spaces at both ends.

On the seventh night, the power offered the mountain a new shape.

Not a wall.

Not a law.

An interval.

The mountain considered it in the slow manner of stone. Water passed through cavities. Ice tightened in hidden cracks. Far below, heat touched cold and withdrew. The city changed the number of its stairs twice.

Then the mountain accepted.

The following evening, as the freezing boundary began at the upper rim, every stream in the bowl darkened.

Not black with mud, nor blue with depth. The water became the color of a closed eye.

The darkness traveled ahead of the frost.

It moved through gutters and seep lines, under paving, across roofs, down hanging falls. Cisterns dimmed from their edges inward. Drops beneath lintels darkened before they fell. Even moisture within the walls showed briefly as branching gray veins.

At the same moment, the handrails gave one pulse.

It was not a sound. It was a gentle tightening beneath the palm, like a sleeping hand returning pressure.

Every animal lifted its head.

The rodents left the drain covers. The cistern birds stepped out of the water. Pale lizards flattened themselves against the warmest stone. The six-horned grazers turned downhill. The black birds rose and moved through the alleys in a single flowing body.

The people hesitated.

Then the freezing came.

But for the first time it did not arrive at the same moment as its warning.

Between the darkening of the water and the hardening of its surface lay the time required to walk, carefully, across seven roofs.

Not much time.

Enough.

Gates were shifted. A backed gutter was opened. Two children were carried from a widening room. A pen of grazers crossed a bridge before its sockets tightened. A woman who had been cutting ice on the eastern terraces looked down, saw the gray lines enter the wall beneath her feet, and stepped away before the facing stones peeled off.

No one died that evening.

In the morning, the traveler was gone.

Their black cup remained beneath an unfamiliar drip. It was an ordinary cup, chipped where it had once been dropped. Orra took it for civic use.

The new interval did not prevent disaster. This was important.

Walls still fell. People still made mistakes. Ice sometimes broke under the first footstep. Mountain-shakes still came from depths no eel had predicted. Human anger remained human anger, and no handrail warned before a cruel word.

The gift did only one thing: where water or stone or ice was about to change violently, the change cast a shadow before itself.

A seep darkened before pressure broke the wall concealing it.

A cistern became gray before its lid rose.

Thin ice showed a smoky handprint beneath the place that would fail.

Before a passage altered, beads of moisture appeared along its intended edge.

Before deep shaking, all the water in the bowl closed its bright face at once, and the rails gave three slow pulses.

The warning was never early enough to permit complacency. Grain could not be packed, property inventoried, arguments concluded. It was enough only for life: to wake the sleeper, lift the child, open the pen, step back, cut the cord, choose another stair.

It gave no advantage to the rich, because it moved through communal water.

It could not be stored.

It could not be sold.

Anyone touching the city could feel it.

For several years the people debated what had happened.

Some said the mountain had learned courtesy.

Some said the traveler had been a particularly competent listener who discovered a pressure pattern already present.

The divers said the lowest cisterns now contained an extra terrace, though none agreed where.

Children said the sun had indeed closed one eye.

The people did not build a shrine. Worship would imply that the traveler required persuasion, and anything truly powerful should not need to be flattered into decency.

They did, however, add a law:

**No warning belongs to the person who first perceives it.**

A second law followed after an upper household tried to dam a darkening seep in order to conceal danger from those below:

**Mercy obstructed becomes danger.**

On the eastern wall, among the mineral rings and calcite ladders, they placed no image of the traveler. Instead they left an empty recess. Objects shifted around it at night, but none entered.

In time the interval became ordinary.

Children were born who assumed water had always darkened before it froze. Builders incorporated the warning into their failures. Animals learned routes timed to the pulse. Lovers promised to come home by the first gray vein. Old people sat with their palms on the rails at evening and felt the city draw breath.

The power did not return, at least not in any form that was recognized.

It had no wish to be thanked.

From wherever benevolent powers attend to the small and unfinished places of the world, it watched the people use the gift without surrendering their own wisdom. It watched them remain skeptical. It watched them argue about gates, make handles from ancestral bone, widen holes for birds, and pour water across new thresholds.

It watched the mountain continue to alter them, and them continue to alter the mountain.

The city was not saved from change.

It was given time to answer.

For a place such as this, the power could imagine no greater kindness.
