The power would not give them ground.

To set stone beneath the woven world would be to misunderstand it. Ground would become authority. Roads would begin longing downward. Roots would abandon their ancient agreements. The weight-bearers would find their burdens answered by something that did not know their names. In time, all the patient braids would become unnecessary, and what is unnecessary is soon neglected.

Nor would the power bring a sun.

The dimness shelters too much. Creatures have made senses from it. Leaves have learned its subtle intervals. A naked star would be an act of conquest.

It would not straighten the old knots, restore the world to some imagined first design, kill everything predatory, make distance obedient, or teach the inhabitants to build with metal. Wisdom would show it that error has become habitat. Whole peoples live in the margins of ancient mistakes. To correct the world completely would destroy it completely.

So the power would come without correction.

It would arrive as attention.

At first no one would notice, because attention is common there. Every keeper listens. Every animal feels the road. Every room waits to learn what it will be. The presence would be mistaken for a mapkeeper testing a route, a hunter waiting beside a root, a midwife with one hand against a laboring wall.

It would pass through the upper cloud districts and allow condensation to bead upon it. It would cross bridges at the pace the bridges preferred. Where custom required three heel taps, it would tap three times. Where a path-name had to be spoken, it would ask the name and pronounce it carefully. It would not open any door merely because it could.

In every settlement it would ask the same question.

Not *Who made this?*

Not *Who rules here?*

Not even *What is broken?*

It would place one warm hand upon the weave and ask:

**“What happens if you stop?”**

The answers would vary.

The condensers would say the catch lines would dry and the fog insects would drift away.

The marsh people would say the channels would close, the cisterns rise, and the platform drown.

The mapkeepers would say paths would forget their meetings.

The midwives would say birth chambers would lose the rhythm.

The weight-bearers, answering years later through punched bark, would say only:

**EVERYTHING COMES DOWN SOMEWHERE.**

A child might laugh and say that if she stopped, her brother would have to do her chores.

An old bridgekeeper would not answer at all.

The power would understand.

The deepest wound of the woven altitude was not rot, hunger, misdirection, predatory architecture, or the ancient uncertainty of its geometry. These were dangers, but dangers had answers. There were knives for rot, cord for falling, stones for refusal, songs for distance, witnesses for law.

The wound was that nothing good could rest.

Every kindness had become maintenance. Every promise had to be repeated lest it detach from its destination. Every road required feet. Every name needed mouths. Every safe room survived by being continually reminded not to become something else.

Even the dead remained at work.

The power would find the old bridgekeeper on a narrow span above a hollow whose bottom had never been seen. She would be very small. Her rain mantle would have rooted accidentally into the rail, and she would not yet have noticed. One hand would be pressed to a fevered braid. The other would hold a hooked knife worn thin from closing more wounds than it had opened.

Three nights earlier, the bridge had begun taking strain from a district that no longer appeared on any map.

She had not slept since.

The power would sit beside her.

After a time, it would say, “Release your hand.”

“No,” the keeper would reply.

“I will listen.”

“You do not know what to listen for.”

“Teach me.”

“There is no time.”

“Then let me hear through you.”

The power would not take her wrist. It would not pour strength into her or make her young. It would simply lay its hand over hers so gently that she could still feel every fiber beneath her palm.

For a while, they would listen together.

The keeper would show it the clear note of a healthy tension member and the muddied tremor of internal damp. She would show it the distant sawing pulse of peg beetles, the flutter made by seam moths, the irregular drag of a hanging grove beginning to slip from its supports. She would teach it the difference between a bridge afraid of breaking and a bridge that had developed an appetite for rescue.

At last the power would say, “Now release your hand.”

“If I do, it may forget it is a bridge.”

“For one breath,” the power would say, “I will remember for it.”

The keeper would hesitate.

Benevolence without patience would fail there. Power without consent would become merely another dangerous room.

So the power would wait.

Eventually, because she was exhausted beyond obedience, the bridgekeeper would lift her hand.

Nothing would fall.

She would lift the other.

The fevered braid would give a low, astonished creak. Far below, another cable would answer. Then another. The sound would pass outward through trunks, roads, walls, orchard skeletons, watercourses, nurseries, ladders, abandoned halls and hanging forests.

The whole world would discover that, for one moment, it was being carried.

Not frozen. Not made inert. Carried.

Rain would continue to bend toward thirsty walls. Gliders would continue across the hollows. Predators would remain predators, fungi would soften wood, children would be born, and the heavy things would move in the deep. But no neglected knot would fail. No road would forget its destination. No name would come loose. No old promise would wander into the wrong room.

For that one interval, repetition would not be required.

The bridgekeeper would sleep.

Seeing this, others would dare to stop.

Mapkeepers would set down their damp trays. Condensers would unclench their hands from the catch lines. Midwives would leave the birth chambers warm but unattended between labors. Hunters would untie their listening cords. Those who had spent their lives sweeping the same gallery would allow one soft layer of pollen to remain.

In orchard walls, the bones of the dead would cease bearing weight.

In ancestral roads, the lingering habits of the departed would grow quiet.

Even the weight-bearers below would feel an impossible slack pass through their great cables. Some would think a support had failed and brace themselves for ruin. Instead, for the first time in the remembered history of their bodies, they would straighten their backs.

The respite would not last a day, because days are uncertain there.

It would last until the last person who needed sleep woke without being summoned.

Perhaps that would take years in some valleys. The wise power would not hurry them.

When the interval ended, burdens would return gradually, each one announced before it settled. The world would creak, but softly. Hands would go to floors. Keepers would take up knives and cords again.

Yet something would have changed.

A structure repeatedly used as a bridge becomes more bridge-like.

A room where births occur learns warmth and rhythm.

A vessel filled with water begins to attract rain.

And a world that has once been permitted to rest remembers rest.

The first resting place would form beneath the old bridgekeeper’s sleeping body. The span would cup itself slightly around her. Its fibers would soften without weakening. Moss would grow that absorbed not only sound but obligation. For generations afterward, anyone lying there would become briefly unavailable to roads, rooms, ancestors, laws, and promises.

Not absolved.

Only unavailable.

People would come from distant districts to study the place. They would take cuttings, moisture, scraps of sleeping moss, and threads from the softened rail. Most grafts would fail. Some would produce merely comfortable beds. A few would remember correctly.

Thus rest would spread as everything in that world spreads: imperfectly, locally, by care.

Settlements would cultivate chambers in which no task could learn to demand a particular person. Grief shelters would become capable of holding grief without requiring it to be explained. Children would be allowed intervals when the structure was not introduced to their weight, when they belonged nowhere and nothing depended upon them.

There would be resting bridges, walked only by those going nowhere.

There would be silent hours during which names were not spoken, yet remained attached.

There would be orchards whose fruit was permitted to fall ungathered.

The power would not remain as ruler or judge. It would refuse a temple, because temples quickly learn hunger. It would accept no permanent name, because names in that world are routes, and every route can become an obligation.

If asked where it lived, it might answer:

**“Wherever a burden is set down without being abandoned.”**

Then it would descend.

Not because the upper people mattered less, but because wisdom would have heard the message from below:

**EVERYTHING COMES DOWN SOMEWHERE.**

It would travel past the weight-bearers, past the blind fish and the impossible coils, past the inverted forests and the lakes hanging in nets. It would follow strain into regions where direction had no use. It would pass beside the heavy things without commanding them to halt.

And there, at the deepest place that was not a bottom, it would attend to all the burdens that had arrived without a bearer: duties inherited from vanished peoples, supports fastened to districts long since migrated away, promises made to rooms that no longer existed, ancient mistakes still drawing tension because no one remembered enough to release them.

It would not carry what living hands had freely chosen to carry.

It would take only the orphaned weight.

Perhaps that would be its sole ongoing work: not holding up the world, but preventing the past from demanding labor forever merely because no one knew how to tell it that its need was over.

Above, life would continue much as before.

Bridges would still fail. People would still become lost in knots. Predatory halls would still be closed with stones. The dead would still enter roads, orchards, tools, and walls. Communities would still answer when their stretch of world strained.

But now, when the great braids tightened and everyone stopped to place their hands upon the floor, they would sometimes feel another pressure meeting theirs from below.

Not lifting the world away from them.

Not asking them to let go forever.

Only answering:

**I have this part.**

And for a breath, the woven altitude would know that it, too, was held.
