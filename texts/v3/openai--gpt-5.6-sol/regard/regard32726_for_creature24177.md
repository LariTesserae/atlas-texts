The power would not begin by making it green.

That would be the first temptation of a lesser mercy: to look upon the mineral flats, the blank walls, the miles without leaf or shade, and answer them with forests. To split the concrete, sweeten the soil, call roots up from buried seed, and let vines pour from every cooling tower.

But there are no buried seeds.

There is salt beneath the roads, metal in the silt, heat stored in foundations, and a city whose inhabitants have arranged their lives around exposure. A sudden forest would be another disaster. Roots would enter drains and retaining walls. Insects would multiply beyond the capacity of bats and birds to eat them. New shade would favor some species and erase others. Pumps would choke. Buildings would fail. The tidal flats would change. Creatures that had survived by learning the hard geometry of the basin would lose their places to more ordinary life.

The power would understand that barrenness is not the same as emptiness, and that abundance is not the same as health.

So first it would attend.

For one full turning of the seasons, it would take no visible form. It would pass as awareness through the basin: along corroded reinforcement, through warm effluent, under pigeon feathers, inside the biofilms of drains. It would learn the city at every scale.

It would know each tide gate by the strain in its hinges. It would know which culvert contained the oldest mud crab and which roof held eggs in the shallowest scrape. It would know the rat paths, the mosquito cisterns, the peregrines’ approaches, the rooms where children slept, the electrical cabinets where snakes sought warmth, the pumps whose failure would drown three districts before dawn.

It would follow a gram of mercury from settling pond to worm, from worm to fish, from fish to gull, from gull to egg.

It would follow a piece of discarded net as it passed from ship to outfall, from outfall to seal, from seal to fence, and from fence to the hands of a worker who cut it free.

It would count not only deaths but dependencies.

The city would feel nothing during this attention except, perhaps, a slight change in dreams. Maintenance workers would dream of underground water. Pilots would dream of bats. Children would dream that the buildings were transparent and full of passages, every species moving through its own illuminated city without knowing the others were there.

Then the power would begin with poison.

Not all at once. Nothing wise would cleanse the basin in a night. Too much life now depended on the nutrient-rich waste, the warm channels, even the predictable cycles of contamination. Purity imposed suddenly could kill as efficiently as filth.

Instead, certain metals would cease to travel.

In water and flesh they would become reluctant. Dissolved ions would bind themselves into dense, stable mineral beads and settle in designated chambers beneath the oldest treatment works. There they would gather in colored layers—copper green, iron red, cadmium yellow, lead gray—sealed from tide and groundwater. The chambers would fill slowly enough for engineers to notice, test, and understand. The discovery would belong to them. They would design new traps based on what had appeared.

The power would not make waste harmless. It would make harm legible and separable.

At the warmest outfalls, the discharged water would cool by fractions of a degree. Oxygen would remain dissolved a little longer. Fish that had gathered there for generations would not lose the current they knew, but neither would they rise stunned to the surface in such numbers. The pelicans would still assemble. Their feeding would become less effortless. They would range farther, as pelicans had before the city taught them its schedule.

In the cisterns where mosquitoes bred beside occupied housing, the water would acquire a faint surface tremor. It would not disturb drinking supplies or nesting birds. It would be just enough that mosquito larvae could no longer hang from the film and breathe. In more distant ponds they would remain abundant, and the bats would not starve.

Flies would still come to fish waste. Cockroaches would still inhabit warm walls. Rats would not vanish. Nothing would be exterminated merely because people found it unpleasant.

But disease would loosen its hold.

A child bitten after rain would fever less often. A cut hand plunged unknowingly into canal water would still swell and require care, but would not so easily become a death sentence. The bacteria of decay would remain vigorous; those that passed most readily into blood would find the human body a less welcoming country.

The power would then turn to the ground.

It would not stop the basin from sinking. The basin had always sunk. The weight of the city, the compression of sediment, and the slow alterations of groundwater could not be canceled without making the place something other than itself.

Instead, settlement would become more truthful.

For decades the earth had shifted invisibly until a wall split, a pipe sheared, or a floor descended beneath the tide. Now, before failure, concrete would show thin blue stains along lines of stress. Mortar would pale where pressure gathered. Metal would give a low, resonant hum days before a joint tore loose.

Engineers would at first suspect contamination or a manufacturing defect. Then they would map the signs. They would learn that the basin had been given a language.

No structure would become immortal. No pump would be relieved of maintenance. But fewer people would die because the ground had kept its intentions hidden.

The city’s machines would also be offered better bargains.

Pumps near tidal channels would begin to yield more energy than their models predicted when water retreated. Turbines placed in certain culverts would turn with unusual efficiency, though never without turning at all. Cooling systems would discover that the night air, heavy and warm as it was, could surrender heat when passed across mineral surfaces patterned like the interior of crab gills.

Nothing would operate by miracle alone. The gift would be design—not finished devices, but principles left where observant people could find them.

A technician cleaning scale from an intake would notice a branching deposit that moved water without turbulence.

A child would fold a scrap of foil into a shape that shed condensation in one direction.

A worker repairing pigeon barriers would realize that a slight change in angle could leave nesting space above while protecting the machinery below.

These discoveries would spread. Some would be patented. Some would be stolen. Some would be ignored for years and rediscovered. The power would permit all this untidiness. Wisdom offered to people must survive becoming human.

Only after poison, disease, collapse, heat, and water had been made less cruel would the power consider plants.

It would choose three places.

The first would be a disused settling pond enclosed by cracked concrete, too contaminated for ordinary restoration. During one wet season, a dark red film would appear at its margin. It would not be algae, exactly, nor moss, nor any plant known to the city. It would be a partnership of salt-tolerant microbes and minute branching organisms able to bind metal into their dead outer layers.

The film would grow no faster than corrosion. It would remain low and tough. Crabs could cross it without destroying it. Midges would lay eggs above it. Small snails would appear, perhaps carried by birds, perhaps present all along in some overlooked pipe.

The second place would be an outer tidal shelf where a broken seawall made a crescent protected from the strongest current. There the power would allow a few reeds to survive.

Not acres. At first, seven.

Their roots would enter clean silt newly accumulated above a buried membrane of mineralized waste. Their stems would be short, blue-gray, waxed against salt. Birds would inspect them. Crabs would cut two down. A storm would remove another. Four would remain.

The following year there would be thirty.

The third place would be a courtyard between inhabited housing blocks, where children could see it.

In a crack beside a condensation pipe, something with two small leaves would emerge.

It would not be rare or splendid. It would be a coastal weed common hundreds of kilometers away. Its arrival would have a plausible explanation: a seed in cargo, mud on a tire, droppings from a migrant bird. Botanists would argue about it. Residents would put a wire guard around it, then remove the guard when they realized it trapped crabs.

The plant would flower once, with five white petals almost too small to notice.

It would die in the heat.

But it would leave seed.

The power would not guarantee the seed’s survival.

This restraint would be its greatest gift.

For the power would know that if every vulnerable thing were preserved, there could be no feeding. If every chick were saved, the rats and snakes would starve. If every fish escaped the pelican, the pelican would vanish. If every crab crossed the road unharmed, the basin would fill beyond what its wet seams could support. Benevolence toward one life, extended without limit, becomes violence toward another.

So death would continue.

The peregrine would strike the pigeon.

The monitor would open the nest.

The tide would strand fish in a shallow pool, and egrets would gather.

A truck would sometimes crush a crab despite the driver’s care.

Old dogs would die beneath trailers. Bats would fall in storms. Workers would bury friends. Children would learn grief before they had words large enough for it.

The power would not abolish these things. It would abolish only what served no hunger, no continuity, no necessary limit: suffering made needless by neglect, poison released for convenience, preventable collapse, the slow lie that no one was responsible because no single act had caused the whole.

Its most profound intervention would occur in the minds of those who made decisions far from the basin.

Whenever a person prepared to approve a discharge, close a clinic, delay a repair, falsify a reading, or call the inhabited districts expendable, they would experience—not a vision, not a command, but a moment of exact consequence.

Their hand would remain above the signature line, and for one breath they would know where the choice went.

They would feel the warm water enter the channel.

They would feel it cross the gills of a fish.

They would feel the fish weaken beneath the pelican.

They would feel the metal pass into the bird and thin the shell around its young.

They would feel the worker lift the broken egg from a roof drain.

They would feel the drain clog, the rain rise, the lower doorway flood, the child wake in contaminated water.

The knowledge would last only a second.

They could sign anyway.

Some would.

The power would not take freedom by disguising force as goodness. But no one would again be permitted the defense of abstraction. Distance would no longer grant innocence.

Those who chose repair would feel consequences too.

A replaced seal. A dry room through the rainy season. A colony diverted from a turbine housing into a purpose-built ledge. A market drain covered without cutting off the rats’ only escape from floodwater. A new cooling process. A child not bitten. A dog sleeping in shade. A maintenance crew returning home.

Goodness would also become concrete.

In time, the city would change without ceasing to be itself.

Its open flats would remain open. Its horizons would still be gray, rust-red, black, and pewter. Cooling towers would fade into low cloud. Rain would still shine on roads empty enough to seem abandoned. Crabs would still gather after storms, and workers would still wait for them to cross.

But some roofs would be designed as rookeries instead of defended until they failed. Some culverts would contain dry shelves above flood level. Freight yards would leave narrow shaded passages along their edges. Waste would move through channels arranged to feed the basin without poisoning it. New housing would include sealed human rooms, bat cavities, swift shafts, gecko walls, and exterior nesting ledges that could be cleaned without destruction.

The city would stop pretending that every other inhabitant was an intruder.

Nor would people become saints. They would complain about gulls. They would set traps for rats in kitchens. They would kill venomous snakes found near schools. They would clear nests from machinery, eat fish, drive roads, build walls, and choose their own safety when choices became narrow.

But they would do so with a clearer understanding of what was being taken and why.

Years later, travelers arriving from greener places would still call the basin barren.

They would see no trees. They would smell hot silt and salt, oil, guano, rain on concrete. They would see cormorants on cranes and crabs in drains. They would mistake the low red films for stains and the reed beds for accidental growth around leaks. They would not recognize the mineral chambers beneath the treatment plants or understand why old walls showed blue before they cracked.

The people who lived there would not argue.

They would know that the city had not been rescued from its nature.

It had been allowed to understand its nature more fully.

At evening, when the tide entered and the cloud deck caught the amber lamps, the power would sometimes be perceptible—not as a figure, and not as a voice, but as a brief coherence.

For a moment, every edge would seem connected.

The wet line along a culvert would continue into the flight of a bat. The black streak beneath a drain would answer the tail-mark of a monitor in mud. Birds on transmission wires would become notes in the same diagram as workers crossing an enclosed bridge. The pumps beneath the ground and the hearts above it would keep different rhythms within one immense body.

Then the impression would pass.

The city would remain hot, sinking, difficult, and alive.

A crab would climb into an elevator.

A warning horn would sound once across the flats.

Somewhere in a courtyard, after warm rain, two small leaves would open from a crack.
