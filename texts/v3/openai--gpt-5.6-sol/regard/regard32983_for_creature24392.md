The power does not descend.

Descent would be too much like an object falling from above, and whatever has noticed the Compression is not an object. It does not enter through the silver ceiling, disturb the outer grilles, or cast a brightness into the basin. No green signal announces it. No unfamiliar pressure bows the membranes.

For a long while, it does nothing that anyone can distinguish from attention.

It attends to the crowded town: to the heat trapped in its upper chambers, to the copper branching over ancestral bones, to the children jerking their communal tethers, to every root guided through every crack. It attends to the dead tree and the impossible air held against the tiles. It attends also to the broad, warm surface below the lowest foundations.

Especially to that.

Wisdom does not mistake stillness for consent.

The being beneath the Compression is neither animal nor machine, though it has at times been both. Long before the basin filled, it came there wounded. It folded itself into the basalt hollow and entered a sleep so deep that stone settled over its flanks. The oldest cut chambers were not carved into the basin. They were carved into a shell the inhabitants had mistaken for rock.

It has not fed upon the sinking bubbles.

It has endured them.

The Lung’s slow contractions force gas through organs that ceased needing breath ages ago. The pressure wakes old reflexes. Each chain of bubbles descending through the green dark is a finger pressed into an ancient wound. The creature turns because it dreams of escaping the thing that keeps happening to it.

Yet the Lung also holds it asleep. If the engine stops abruptly, the being will wake in terror. If it wakes in terror, the Compression will not survive the first movement of its body.

The town and the creature have become each other’s danger.

They have also become each other’s life.

The creature’s warmth sustains the orchards. Its faint electrical impulses feed the mosses and battery gardens. Its slow filtration keeps the basin’s chemistry within the narrow range the inhabitants can breathe. In return, the town’s rootworks bind fractures in its shell. The Warmers soothe inflamed cavities without knowing they are doing so. The copper dead smooth the violent pulses of its dreaming mind.

Neither can simply be removed.

The power understands that rescue performed without understanding is another kind of disaster. It does not lift the town into the open sea. It does not kill the sleeper. It does not reverse the downward fall of air, return the inhabitants to some imagined ancestral shape, or make the dead tree green at once.

Instead, it searches for the smallest mercy from which all necessary mercies might grow.

It chooses a child.

Not because children are pure, for the power is too wise to believe that. It chooses the child because she is small enough to pass through a pressure duct that adults have forgotten, and because she has formed the habit of loosening her tether to explore places where the town’s vibration is strange.

Her household knows her by a touch-pattern like five drops falling across the palm. She has no occupational color yet. Her pipe-rhythm is only a playful stumbling sequence used by the nursery keepers to call her back from machinery.

One warm cycle, she feels a tug along her line.

Not away from home.

Down.

She assumes another child has become entangled beneath a grille. She follows, hand over hand, through orchard roots and between the nearly motionless fins of an old turbine. The tug passes into a narrow conduit whose inner surface is plated with generations of mineral rings. She folds her shoulders, seals her breathing fronds against the silt, and enters.

The tether should catch.

It does not.

Every obstruction inclines aside by the width of one finger. Calcified weed softens. A broken ceramic valve turns in its housing for the first time in eighty years. Copper growths withdraw their sharpest branches. Nothing moves quickly enough to startle her.

She descends until the town’s familiar hum separates into two rhythms.

One is the Lung.

The other is beneath it.

She puts six fingers against the wall.

The wall flinches.

The child becomes very still.

A signal travels through her fingertips: not words, not even a pipe-rhythm, but a pressure so old and simple that her body understands before her mind does.

Pain.

She pulls her hand away. After a moment, she touches the wall again.

Pain, the wall says.

The child has been taught what to do when someone is hurt. She places her other hand flat beside the first, giving the answering pattern used in nursery cisterns when a frightened child cannot see through clouded water.

Here.

The surface beneath her palms trembles.

Pain.

Here, she answers.

Pain.

Here.

For most of a cycle they say only this.

The benevolent power does not speak through the child. It does not possess her, fill her with knowledge, or use her mouth as an instrument. It merely keeps the conduit open. It steadies her bladder of compressed gas. It prevents the next contraction of the Lung from crushing the narrow chamber around her.

Then it permits one impossible thing.

The child feels the pain as direction.

Not an image, exactly. A direction through flesh, stone, pipe, root, heat, and memory. She understands that the bubbles descending beneath the Lung enter something tender. She understands that the long row of falling dials is not hunger.

It is recoiling.

When she returns, many hours late, the whole district has gone violet with alarm. Adults crowd the rails. Child-lines tremble in every passage. Her household seizes her so urgently that conversation is impossible.

She presses one hand to the nearest pipe.

Three slow beats.

A pause.

One beat.

Again.

The ancient signal passes outward.

At the tiled square, workers feel it arrive through the fastening rings. Around them the clear sphere of air quivers. The dead tree’s highest branch moves—not upward, as witnesses have always claimed, but downward.

The branch bends toward the glazed tiles.

A seam-reader old enough to carry white copper in her breathing fronds watches it touch the inner surface of the air sphere. There is no wind inside. Nevertheless, the smaller twigs shake.

Beneath the fountain, something answers.

Three slow beats.

A pause.

One.

For the first time, the answer is felt by everyone.

It travels through basalt and ceramic, through galleries and rooted tenements, through the ribs of factories and the copper-laden bones of the dead. Tools ring faintly against belts. Glass shrimp leave the gauges and line themselves along the pipes. The bladder-leaf orchards fold shut all at once.

The Lung prepares to contract.

The old seam-reader crosses the empty square.

Cries cannot travel far in the Compression, but horror needs no sound. Hands seize at rails. Warning colors burst violet into the water. No one crosses the tiles. No one enters the air. No one interferes with the Lung.

The seam-reader does all three.

She carries no tool except a pruning knife.

At the fountain, she places her hands against the trembling sphere. Air receives her fingers reluctantly. The sensation is terrible: dry pressure, naked skin, no water to carry vibration or scent. Her breathing fronds close. She forces her arms farther in.

The tree stands scarcely taller than she is. Its silver bark is split along the trunk, and in the split she sees a narrow gleam of copper.

Not wood alone, then.

Not machine alone.

The difference has become unreliable.

The Lung contracts.

The seam-reader cuts the tree.

The blade enters one of the lowest roots.

Across the town, membrane doors bow inward. Suspended pebbles rattle among the ceilings. Pipes jump in their brackets. The chain of bubbles leaves the Lung and begins its descent.

But this time the bubbles do not enter the reservoirs below.

One by one, they turn.

They gather beneath the tiled square, flattening against it like quicksilver. The glaze cracks along lines too deliberate to be fractures. A pattern spreads outward from the fountain: a six-fingered hand, vastly enlarged.

The old seam-reader feels it through her knees.

Pain, says the being below.

She presses her palm to the tile.

Here, she answers.

The wise power has done only one thing: it has made the direction of suffering perceptible.

After that, the inhabitants do what they have always done.

They maintain.

There is no decree to stop the Lung. Stopping it would be fatal. Instead, Listeners lie against its casing and learn the interval between contraction and recoil. Warmers alter the density boundaries in the lower chambers. Rootwrights encourage pale vines into gas conduits, where they soften each pulse. Platers strip copper from ceremonial walls and grow new conductive fans across the deepest foundations.

For the first time, they treat the ground beneath them as living structure.

For the first time, it treats their signals as more than irritation.

The work takes years.

Children are taught a new color: blue-black for pain that belongs to something unable to enter the town. A new profession arises among those who tend the difference between body and building. They are called Underlisteners. They descend in pairs. One keeps a hand against the walls; the other taps reassurance into the nearest pipe.

Here.

Here.

Here.

The Lung’s contractions become gentler. Fewer bubbles are forced down at once. Reservoirs are rerouted into chambers grown from bladder-leaf and flexible ceramic. The creature’s turns become less violent. The falling dials cease to be regarded as unexplained failures. They are read as speech.

Empty.

Too warm.

Do not press there.

Dreaming.

Afraid.

The town answers in pump cycles, heat, current, and touch.

Full.

Cooling.

We will move.

You are not alone.

No one calls this worship. It is maintenance, extended to include a neighbor whose body happens to be mistaken for the world.

The tree changes too.

Sap does not rise in it. Sap sinks.

From the tips of the branches, clear droplets creep downward along the silver bark and gather at the roots. The old soil darkens. Tiny white filaments appear in it, no thicker than the breathing fronds of a newborn.

Years later, a single leaf opens.

It is not green. It is black on one side and silver on the other, shaped like a narrow ear.

When the creature dreams, the leaf trembles.

When the town answers, it grows still.

The benevolent power remains only until it is certain that attention has become mutual. Then it withdraws—not entirely, perhaps, but beyond any measure available to the Compression. It leaves no commandment, no image of itself, no promise that harm will never return.

Its gift is smaller and more durable than safety.

The inhabitants now know what lies beneath them.

The being beneath them now knows what moves across its back.

Neither is innocent. Neither is helpless. Neither is alone.

Many generations later, the Compression begins to change shape. Lower galleries are carefully loosened from the shell. Root bridges are taught to flex with the sleeper’s breath. Whole districts migrate by fractions of a body length each year. The empty square remains empty, but it is no longer the only deliberate space in the town.

Others appear.

A clear channel beside a nursery.

A gap between two machine-houses.

An uncrowded chamber where the old can rest without hearing pumps.

A vertical passage through which children may look all the way to the trembling silver underside of the sea.

The inhabitants find these spaces disturbing at first. There is nothing to grasp. Distance enters where density has always comforted them.

Still, they keep the openings clear.

They have learned that room is not always waste.

Sometimes room is how one being tells another:

You may move without harming me.

At the bottom of the town, the pressure dials still occasionally sink to zero in a patient row.

Now the Underlisteners wait beside them.

When the last needle falls, they place their hands against the living floor and feel the vast being turn—not in pain, not in terror, but carefully, so carefully that the towers sway without breaking and the orchards release only a few dark leaves.

Then, from below, comes the ancient rhythm:

Three slow beats.

A pause.

One.

Across the Compression, thousands of hands answer.
