The power does not enter at once.

It remains for a long while upon the moor, though there is no shape there to see and no warmth to feel. Rain crosses it. Pale grass bends through it. A fox passes within an arm’s length and pauses, lifting its narrow head, but scents nothing it can name.

The power watches the black pools gather and contract. It learns the runnels by which rain finds the sea. It attends to frost raising one edge of stone while pressing another deeper into peat. It waits through a complete turning of the tide.

Only then does it find the slit between the slabs.

It could widen the entrance with a thought. It could lift the roof, open the chamber to the sky, and pour daylight into every recess.

It does not.

The ferns would brighten for a season and then be burned by cold. The liverworts would dry. The moths would be taken by birds. The hidden stores would cease to be hidden. Wind would enter where now there are only measured breaths, and the hollow would become an ordinary break in the ground.

Nor does the power make itself small merely to imitate humility. It has no need to turn sideways or bow. Yet it does so.

Inside, the thunder receives it.

The power does not confuse the sound with anger. It feels the offshore swell strike the buried shelf, follows the pressure through branching fissures, and knows the labor by which the chamber has been made. It perceives every grain loosened from basalt, every pocket of air narrowed by water, every flexure too slight for a human palm.

It feels the old sea inhabiting the hollow without entering it.

The sea is not cruel. It is not kind. It bears no grudge against the moss, the spider, the child with one hand on the wall. It does only what its weight and the moon require.

The wise power does not rebuke it.

Instead, it listens.

It listens until the great roar separates into its innumerable parts: the blow beneath the outer cliff, the compression of air in a flooded throat, the shake of a loose flake overhead, the small answering tremor of antler against driftwood. Within the thunder it hears a beetle turn beneath a rush bundle. It hears a moth’s feet release the roof one by one. It hears water entering the rootless green body of a liverwort.

It hears, too, what has not yet happened.

Not prophecy exactly. Consequence.

Deep below the chamber, one passage bends sharply upward before narrowing to a cleft. In ordinary weather the cleft carries only air. During a winter storm, kelp and storm-thrown gravel will be drawn into it. The obstruction will grow. The thunder in the hollow will diminish.

A listener will recognize the failing beat and order everyone out.

Most will obey at once. But a child will turn back for a hide bundle, believing it contains the family’s seed. An older woman will follow. Before they reach the slit, the blockage will break.

The returning pressure will strike the chamber hard enough to throw the child against the low wall. A stone loosened by many winters will fall.

The power sees how little alteration is needed to prevent this.

It does not seal the lower passages. To do so would force the sea into other cracks and perhaps break the outer shelf. It does not fasten every stone forever. A chamber in which nothing falls, erodes, or dies would no longer belong to the earth.

Instead, the power lays one hand—if it is a hand—upon the gravel ridge above.

Far beneath the peat, a hairline fracture extends the width of a fingernail.

No thunder marks the change. No light issues from the wall. One flake of basalt parts from another along a seam already present, and a thin channel joins the dangerous cleft to a stone cup near the entrance.

That is all.

When pressure gathers in the lower passage, the cup will empty.

It will not drain gradually like the other hollows. Its clear water will draw downward in a single smooth motion, leaving the stone suddenly bare. A breath later, cold air will rise through three tiny holes in its floor. The surface will mist white. Even in darkness, a hand held over it will feel the upward stream.

The warning will come before the thunder diminishes.

It will give enough time.

The power tests the new path through one tidal cycle, then another. The moss around the cup remains damp. The chamber’s great sound is unchanged. No creature loses its holding.

Having done this, the power might leave.

But benevolence is not satisfied merely by preventing the death it happened to foresee. Wisdom requires it to ask what else its presence might injure, and what gifts would become injuries if given carelessly.

It considers the people.

It could make their lamps burn steadily. Yet the leaning flame tells them how the hidden air is moving.

It could preserve their food perfectly. Yet stores that never spoiled would be hoarded, fought over, and claimed by those able to guard the entrance.

It could make every dropped tool shine. Yet concealment is part of the hollow’s usefulness, and light has owners as surely as copper does.

It could take away their fear.

This it refuses most firmly.

Fear is not always an enemy. Here it keeps a lamp attended. It ties a blade to a belt. It sends a hand to the wall. To remove it would be to dull the very sense by which people survive.

So the power leaves their caution intact.

It only touches, very lightly, the customs that caution has made.

From then on, the reflection of a lamp placed near the entrance carries a little farther than it should. Not far enough to expose the slit from the moor, nor brightly enough to wake the moths, but around one bend of black stone where before it could not reach. A person already inside will see the faint amber mark before the newcomer emerges.

No more hands will meet by surprise over the same hidden peg.

No frightened hunter, seeing a sudden face in darkness, will strike before recognizing kin.

The people will attribute this to soot polished from the wall, or to moisture, or to a pale mineral uncovered by frost. These explanations will be reasonable. The power does not correct them.

Deeper in, it finds the spiders.

For them it does almost nothing. Their skill is already sufficient. Generation after generation, they have learned to read frail disorder inside overwhelming rhythm. The oldest female waits with her feet upon six lines and knows the difference between stone-thunder and the panic of a moth.

The power admires her.

Admiration need not become interference.

It passes on.

In the green margins, however, there is one patch of moss beneath a roof crack soon to fail. The fall would not matter greatly to the chamber. Moss dies often. Yet within that patch live mites found nowhere else in the hollow, descended from a few carried in years ago on the feather wrapping of a seed bundle.

The power does not make them immortal. It sends one extra drop down a neighboring seam.

Over many days, then weeks, the dampness draws a thin green bridge across the bare stone. Springtails cross first. Fungus follows. At last some of the mites wander into the new patch.

When frost loosens the roof flake, half the old moss is buried.

Half is not.

This, too, is enough.

Last, the power turns its attention below.

A young seal is moving through the submerged passages. It has entered with the high tide and followed fish into a channel too narrow for them all. The fish escaped through a slit. The seal could not. Now it waits in an air pocket, calling. The calls rise distorted into the Drum Hollow, where two people working at the antler rack stop and look at one another.

They know it is a seal.

Knowing does not make the sound less lonely.

The lower route will open when the tide turns. The seal is not doomed. But it is tired, and each swell presses cold water higher into its refuge.

The power goes to it without displacing the water.

There, in blackness no human light has reached, it takes no visible form. It does not speak in the speech of seals, because seals have no speech for promises about the future. Instead, it steadies the air pocket. It holds the pressure from changing too quickly. It places into the animal’s body the one assurance a frightened animal can receive: not an idea, but a lessening of the need to flee.

The seal stops calling.

It rests with its nostrils above the water.

When the tide falls, it finds the outward current and follows.

Far above, the people hear the subterranean voice cease. One of them touches the wall in farewell.

The power understands the gesture.

Before departing, it kneels at the center of the chamber and places its palm against the basalt where generations of listeners have placed theirs.

The sea strikes.

The stone answers.

For an instant the power feels all who have touched that floor: frightened children, exhausted fishers, old women measuring the pauses, injured hunters waiting out sleet, strangers entering with their lights properly shown. It feels their hands not as ghosts but as consequences preserved in custom, soot, worn stone, replaced cord, remembered warning.

It could reveal itself then.

It could fill the hollow with a voice greater than the sea. It could tell the people they have been seen. It could demand a name, a rite, or gratitude.

A benevolent power desires no payment for preventing harm.

A wise power knows that certainty can become another kind of darkness.

So it leaves no commandment.

Years pass before anyone notices the changed cup.

The one who discovers it is a girl on her third visit to the hollow. She has been told not to wander, and she does not. She sits near the entrance with one hand on the stone while the adults hang strips of meat in the rear cleft.

The thunder is strong but regular.

Under her palm, the cup empties.

She watches the water vanish as though drawn through solid rock. Then mist flowers over the bare basin. She holds her fingers above it and feels the cold breath.

The expected beat of the chamber has not yet failed.

For a moment she doubts herself.

Then she remembers the first lesson of the hollow: what cannot be heard may still be felt.

She rises, crosses the darkness by the wall, and closes her fist against her chest.

The adults stop.

They come to the cup. The oldest listener kneels, feels the upward air, and does not ask whether this sign was known before.

They hood the lamps. They leave the meat. They take the seed bundle because it is already tied. Everyone passes through the slit before the thunder thins.

From the moor they feel the release underground.

Air bursts from unseen cracks among the gravel. The ground gives one deep shudder. Far away, surf leaps white against the outer cliff.

No one is harmed.

Afterward, the emptying cup becomes part of the hollow’s rules.

Children are shown it on their first visit. The basin is kept clear of silt. No food is placed near it. No one calls it sacred, though a small fitted wall is built to protect it from careless feet. Some say the channel was always there. Some say frost opened it. One old listener insists the stone cup once held water through every storm, but memory is difficult to prove.

The girl who first saw it becomes a listener in time.

When she is old, she teaches another child to lay a hand above the three small holes. She teaches the difference between the ordinary coolness of stone and the colder breath rising from below.

She does not tell the child that the hollow is safe.

It is not.

She does not say the sea is merciful.

It is not.

She says only, “There is more warning now than there used to be.”

That is the whole miracle.

The hollow remains dark. The thunder remains bodily and immense. Ferns lose fronds to frost. Beetles eat what is spilled. Spiders misjudge and starve. Tools vanish. Walls settle. People grow old, and not all lean seasons end well.

But one danger has gained a sign.

One reflection travels farther.

One small nation of mites has another garden.

One seal returns to the open sea.

The benevolent power does not stay to rule what it has helped. It goes back through the narrow entrance, turning sideways, bowing beneath the stone.

Behind it, the Drum Hollow continues as it was: not conquered, not purified, not made gentle.

Only attended.

Sometimes that is what mercy looks like in an ancient place.
