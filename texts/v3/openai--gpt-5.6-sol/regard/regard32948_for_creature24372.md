The power does not enter at once.

Attention, at such a scale, is already a kind of weather. To look too directly might warm the pools. To lean close might alter the air. A breath could flatten awnings, erase scent trails, dry the open chambers of bees. Even benevolence has weight.

So the power first makes itself small—not in body, for it has no body unless one is useful, but in consequence. It narrows its regard until it can attend to one bead of condensation without causing it to fall.

For a night and a day it watches.

It watches a termite carry a pellet, reject the place where instinct first directs her, turn, and set it against a leak no larger creature could perceive. It watches a crab rebuild an embankment that will be broken before dusk. It watches three mosquito larvae vanish into a frog and the frog narrowly escape a gecko. It watches a bee close an egg behind mud, not knowing that a root has already begun to press toward the chamber. It follows the root tip as it feels through darkness among grains of silt.

The power sees every life separately.

It also sees that it cannot save each life from every other life without unmaking the place.

To seal the bee forever would starve the wasp. To preserve every larva would empty the frog. To restrain the bird would deny the basin its seeds, its deep footprints, its sudden openings. To make the pools permanent would favor those who breed in water until they overwhelmed those who require bare clay. To keep every tower standing would leave no fallen walls for fungus, moss, or burrow.

It would be easy to make the city peaceful.

It would be easy to make it dead.

The power has seen this error elsewhere: mercy applied without understanding, abundance made constant, danger abolished, every sharp edge padded until no seed can split, no appetite can answer another appetite, and all the intricate exchange of lives becomes a garden preserved past living.

So it does not forbid the gecko its moth.

It does not blunt the bird’s beak.

It does not tell the termites what their towers look like.

It does not give the crabs patience, because their impatience is part of how the rain is guided. It does not give the bees foresight, because no creature should have to grieve every possible future while trying to provision one egg. It does not awaken the roots into the hurried consciousness of animals. It does not make the city know itself.

Knowledge is not always a gift.

Instead, the power learns the shape of what the basin lacks.

Not immortality.

Not safety.

Margin.

The city lives close to many edges. A shower may fill it, but a season without showers could empty it beyond recovery. A bird may rupture one district, but a single grazing animal settling its weight in the basin could crush nearly all of it. The ordinary flood created the place, yet one violent change upstream—a bank cut open, a channel straightened, poisoned water released—could erase not merely individuals but the continuing exchange between them.

These are not the intimate hazards by which the city renews itself. They are endings too large to be used.

The power decides to guard against those.

Its first act is done beneath the deepest pool.

There, between two rootlets, it persuades the clay to hold together slightly better than clay ordinarily would. Not like stone. Stone would be too absolute. The hollow may still fill with silt, still be pierced by roots, still dry in a long heat. But below the visible floor it retains a thumb’s depth of cool dampness: enough for fungal threads to withdraw into, enough for nematodes to coil and wait, enough for a frog to press its belly against, enough for fine roots to find when the upper basin is pale with drought.

No inhabitant sees the act.

A crab later enlarges the edge and seems to have made the hollow itself.

The power allows this impression.

Its second act is done at the rim.

The narrow passage between roots is both entrance and wound. Through it comes the bird, but also fresh mud, wandering beetles, spores, mates from other colonies, and the possibility of escape. Closing it would protect the city by making it poorer.

So the power does not close it.

Instead, it inclines one root by less than the width of a bee’s wing. Water entering too quickly will now divide around the basin before it descends. A normal flood may still pass through. It will still collapse towers, drown chambers, carry away eggs, and lay down the next skin of silt. But the first violent force will break against the root and arrive as two lesser currents.

Some will still die.

The city will remain a city afterward.

Its third act takes place beyond the fold of the floodplain.

A human comes in the dry season carrying stakes painted at their tips. The human has been sent to mark a straight drainage cut from a field to the river. The proposed line passes through the low rise beneath the warm grass. No one has seen the passage between the roots. No one knows the basin is there.

The power could terrify the human. It could appear in flame, speak from the soil, make the stakes bleed sap. But fear would draw others. Wonders attract witnesses, and witnesses attract paths.

Instead, the power loosens a lace.

The human stops, bends, reties it, and while crouched notices that the ground ahead is softer than the map suggests. A few steps to the east, the soil is firm. The line is moved.

This is entered later in a survey book as a practical correction.

The power prefers such records.

A machine passes at a distance the following month. Its weight trembles through the basin. Soldiers turn their heads toward the vibration. Frogs press deeper into clay. A few fresh walls crack. But the drainage cut lies elsewhere, and when rain comes, no torrent of field water and bitter residue pours through the root passage.

The inhabitants do not know they have been spared.

Being spared is often like that.

After these acts, the power remains attentive but refuses to become a ruler.

In an unusually harsh dry season, it encourages one broad leaf to keep its green for seven days longer. Condensation gathers there after the others have begun to curl. The drops do not fall in one place. A beetle drinks from one. A wasp takes another. Two strike empty clay and vanish below. One drowns a mite. Mercy at this scale cannot avoid having shadows.

During a season of excessive rain, the power lets a fallen twig catch across the main inflow. Leaves gather behind it. The barrier lasts three nights, then breaks. By then the highest water has passed.

When a fungus arrives that would consume nearly every frog in the basin, the power does not destroy the fungus everywhere. It alters the skin of one unnoticed frog so that the infection slows. That frog survives long enough to leave eggs. Among the offspring, a few inherit the resistance without inheriting invulnerability. The disease remains part of the world, but not the end of this lineage.

When termites grow too numerous and begin sealing the open ground beneath continuous clay, the power does nothing. The next rain opens the towers. The ants enter. The bird returns. Balance is not always gentle, but it is already present.

When the bird steps through the root passage, the power does not turn it aside.

The bright eye searches. The beak strikes. A bee chamber opens. A frog disappears. Galleries collapse beneath the toes.

Then, in the mud carried on one toe, a seed falls.

The seed is unlike the broad-leaved plants already roofing the basin. It germinates slowly and makes a low, branching growth with small leaves. It does not conquer the city. Much of it is eaten. One branch survives beside the deepest pool, where its roots hold a bank that would otherwise slump.

The power blesses neither seed nor bird.

It blesses the passage between them.

Years go by.

The city changes enough that no inhabitant from the first day would recognize it. The oldest termite towers melt into low mounds. A vine dies and its hollow stem becomes a corridor for ants. The deep pool shifts when a root thickens beneath it. Bees abandon the eastern walls and settle in clay raised by crabs on the western margin. New frogs arrive during a wet season. A beetle species vanishes. Two fungi become five. The broad leaf that once held seven extra days of green finally falls, softens, blackens, and enters a hundred bodies.

The power does not preserve the city’s appearance.

Appearance was never its life.

What it preserves, lightly and without guarantee, is the city’s ability to continue becoming other than it was.

No monument is raised to this protection. No sacred chamber appears. No creature is chosen as priest or messenger. The termites receive no commandment. The ants trace no holy name. There is no promise that each egg will hatch, each pupa open, each frog reach water in time.

The promise—if it can be called a promise—is smaller and larger:

No ordinary hunger will be abolished.

No necessary collapse will be prevented.

No life will be made permanent at the expense of all the lives that follow it.

But whenever an ending approaches that is too complete, too foreign to be answered, too sudden to become material for renewal, a little resistance will appear.

A root will hold.

A channel will turn.

A leaf will remain green.

A survey line will shift.

A single body will endure long enough.

This is how the wise power loves the city: not by lifting it out of danger, but by keeping danger from becoming final.

Eventually even the basin itself will pass. The river will change its course. Sediment will rise above the sheltering rim. Trees may close the canopy, or drought may harden the floodplain, or a deeper flood may carry the entire fold away.

The power will not forbid that day merely because it has watched so closely.

Instead, before the place is lost, mud will travel under claws and on feet. Spores will rise. Winged termites will depart after rain. Moths will climb into darkness. Seeds will catch in feathers. Frogs will follow wet ground toward another depression. Fungal threads will sleep inside fragments of root.

The city will go outward without knowing it.

Elsewhere, behind another snag of branches, water will slow.

Silt will settle.

A hollow no larger than a courtyard will hold the rain.

And the power, if needed, will make no throne there.

It will only bend near enough to see, and remain gentle enough not to cast a shadow.
