The power does not descend.

Descent would establish an above and a below too firmly. It does not appear in the eastern brilliance, because light here is already an excess and revelation is one of the plateau’s dangers. It does not take a body. A body would be mistaken for a prison, a meal, or a miracle requiring imitation.

Most importantly, it does not look directly at the country.

It has seen what attention does to distance.

Instead, the power turns its regard aside and learns the plateau from what leaves it: the upward lightning, the long stone tones, the breath-threads drawn to shaded walls, the knocks that arrive before their questions. It listens to water at dusk, when every pool speaks with one surface. It listens long enough to distinguish the movements of minerals from the gestures of people, and the gestures of people from the habits of those who failed to become people again.

This takes a very long time.

The power is benevolent, and so it is not hurried by pity.

It does not make the stone fertile. Life is not a remedy merely because life is familiar elsewhere. To spread moss over the terraces, seed the cracks, fill the sky with wings, and send roots into the hidden channels would be a kind of conquest. The inhabitants would find themselves displaced by a green confusion of appetite and decay. Their records would be eaten. Their shallow waters would become blood, sap, milk, rot. The power understands that an empty country is not necessarily an unfinished one.

Nor does it correct the local rules.

To force water always downhill would tear apart the evening communion of pools. To make echoes return only to their sources would silence the cliff-dead and strand questions forever. To discipline distance would destroy the stone frames. To make hands keep one number of fingers would cripple every craft requiring another.

The country is not broken merely because it is difficult.

But its people suffer.

That distinction matters.

The power first becomes certain of their suffering when it hears a sequence of knocks traveling under the plateau. Three blows ask whether a route exists. Five ask whether it is safe to intend the route.

The answer is three.

Then three again.

Then three for a hundred years.

No five ever comes.

The question belongs to someone who has died, though not enough to stop asking. Their intention remains caught in the cliffs, circling from ravine to ravine. Each time it passes beneath a shelter, the person assembling there feels the unfinished need as if it were their own. Some abandon their work and follow. A few never return. The old question has become a new hazard.

The power waits at the question’s next arrival.

It places no hand against the cliff. It has no hammer. Instead, it creates a pause in which an answer may choose not to travel. For the first time, the stone receives a sound and does not immediately carry it elsewhere.

Within that pause, the power answers with five knocks.

They are very soft.

The cliff-dead person ceases asking.

Across the plateau, certain cracks close by the width of a thought. A persistent third shadow vanishes from several pillars. In a shelter near the northern chasm, a cup that has been trembling for generations becomes still.

Nothing is restored. Nothing is erased.

Something is released from repetition.

The inhabitants notice.

At the next dusk, they gather as fully as they can around the black pools. Bells are hung from every copper hook. Mirrors are angled toward the saddle. Hands emerge from water, from foil, from the weighted dark beneath roof stones. Voices come through the walls. One person attends as the pressure of fingers that are no longer present; another as a sequence of footprints filling with water. Several of the oldest stretch across entire chains of basins and cannot narrow themselves enough to speak.

The power does not announce itself.

It asks in the inhabitants’ own manner.

Three knocks.

The plateau answers from a hundred cliffs: a route exists.

Five knocks.

For a while there is no answer.

Then one bell sounds. Then another. Then every bell on the high country begins to ring—not loudly, but with the deep note of something vast turning its attention toward the saddle.

The inhabitants cover the bells. They cover the mirrors. They extinguish what little fire there is. The younger ones disperse instinctively, leaving portions of themselves in pools and tools.

The power turns its attention farther aside.

The bells quiet.

This is the first gift: proof that great attention can choose not to consume what it notices.

The people return cautiously. They have no word for such restraint. Later they will represent it as an empty circle hammered into copper foil, or as a doorway with a lintel but no sides.

They answer the question of safety with five knocks.

So the power begins.

It does not issue commandments. A command spoken by such a power would become geology. Even a casual preference might harden into a permanent law: all pools rising forever, all echoes compelled to mercy, every broken thing forced to return. Benevolence without caution could make the plateau unbearable.

Instead, the power alters only what can be refused.

On the western edge of the saddle, among the stone frames, there is an opening that has never shown another place. From either side it contains only brightness. The inhabitants have narrowed it, widened it, turned mirrors toward it, struck its uprights, poured dusk water across its threshold, and hung bells within it. Nothing has ever passed through except what entered normally from the opposite side.

The power chooses this useless frame.

It waits until the people are assembled around it. Then it gives the opening not a destination, but an interior.

Beyond the threshold appears a small room.

Its walls are bare pale stone. Its floor is level enough to lie upon. There is one shallow basin, empty. The ceiling is low and gently dark. No coast pounds beyond it. No red cavern burns. No rain hangs motionless. It is not elsewhere. It is simply inside.

The inhabitants retreat.

Interiors are known to them only in fragments: hollows, shelters, cracks, chambers glimpsed in pools. This room is different. Its surfaces agree about what they enclose. Its corners remain the same distance away whether watched or ignored. A sound made there returns to the speaker, unless deliberately offered outward. An uncovered object retains its weight. Counting a crack does not widen it.

The room imposes these certainties on nothing beyond its walls.

That is the second gift: a small place where reliability is possible but not compulsory.

For many evenings no one enters.

Then an inhabitant who can assemble only as hands approaches the frame. The hands have six fingers each, though two are reflections and one is a remembered intention. They carry a clay cup between them.

At the threshold, the reflections hesitate.

The room will permit all the fingers to enter, but it will not manufacture separate bodies for them. It will not decide which are real.

The hands cross.

The cup remains a cup. Its depth does not change. The fingerprints in it belong to everyone who helped shape it and do not resolve into one maker. The hands set it in the basin.

Then they wait for dawn.

Ordinarily the inhabitant would come apart. Fingers would become runnels; grip would enter the cup; intention would knock itself into the frame. But when the white sun returns, the room remains gently dark.

The hands remain.

They do not become flesh. They do not become singular. They simply continue from one hour into the next.

Outside, condensation sweeps the plateau.

Inside, for the first time in the history of the country, one of its people experiences morning.

The hands begin to shake.

A seventh finger appears on the left hand, corresponding to a movement that has no purpose except astonishment.

After several hours, the inhabitant leaves voluntarily. In sunlight it disperses almost at once, but not before placing the clay cup beneath a slab.

That evening it reassembles differently, yet remembers the morning as its own.

The room becomes neither palace nor sanctuary. The inhabitants are too wary for that. They test it for generations.

They bring bells inside and alter distances outside, but the bells do not ring unless asked to listen beyond the walls. They bring iron, which rests uneasily but does not close the doorway. They carry in salt records and discover that dawn does not dissolve them. They bring mirrors and see no faces unless they desire to see them.

One person enters in five parts and leaves in six. Another enters as three people and leaves as one, then returns the following evening to undo the decision. The room allows both outcomes. It regards neither union nor separation as more complete.

Eventually they understand that the room does not assemble them.

It permits them to choose what assembly means.

The power makes no second room. One is enough to establish possibility. More might establish empire.

Instead, it teaches them how the gift was made.

Not by revealing tools or measurements. Precision would trap the lesson. It teaches by allowing the inhabitants to feel, within the threshold stone, the shape of its restraint: how the power holds certainty inward rather than pressing it outward; how a wall may keep a promise without declaring all other spaces false; how a refuge can have limits and still be kind.

The inhabitants experiment.

Most attempts fail. A shelter becomes permanently heavier inside and sinks slowly into the terrace. Another preserves every sound ever made within it until no new sound can enter. A third holds darkness too well and loses its doorway.

But they are accustomed to building by approximation.

At last, beside a chain of small pools, they construct a shelter whose floor remembers anyone who stands upon it until that person chooses to be forgotten. Elsewhere they build a cistern in which scattered voices can meet without becoming one voice. Near the southern fins, they fit slabs around a hollow that permits weight, breath, memory, and reflection to wait for one another past dawn.

The new structures are imperfect.

Their imperfections keep them safe.

The power’s third gift is given to the dead.

Until then, every failed assembly leaves a habit behind. Some habits are useful and become customs of water or stone. Some are beautiful: a pool reflecting a beloved ridge, a bell sounding when a certain lost coast comes near. Others are agony without a sufferer—an intention unable to discover that the one who intended it is gone.

The power will not gather all these remnants into resurrected persons. Some no longer wish to be persons. Some never were. To force every trace into identity would be another kind of death.

So, at a place where upward lightning often strikes the underside of a long arch, the power makes a new basin.

It is shallow. Its bottom is visibly no more than a handspan down, and in this case the appearance is true. It holds no water unless water is freely brought to it. At dusk it does not join the other pools.

Around its rim, the inhabitants carve no names.

They call it, by various sequences of bells and knocks, the Hollow That Does Not Answer For You.

Into it they may pour whatever remains of someone who has failed to reassemble: a voice drawn from a cliff, weight stored beneath a stone, fingerprints dissolved from clay, a route trapped in a doorway, a fear that has become a law.

Nothing placed there is destroyed.

Nothing is compelled to return.

For one full cycle, the remnant rests without performing itself. A knock need not knock. A direction need not direct. A weight need not burden anything. In that stillness, if enough preference and intention remain, the remnant may ask to be gathered again. The inhabitants carry it to a shelter and assist.

If no intention remains, the water clears.

At dawn it evaporates upward without leaving salt.

The first remnant brought to the basin is the old unanswered route-question.

Though the power quieted it, its shape still lies in the cliffs. The inhabitants coax it out over many dusks, striking stone behind its passage and opening covered cups ahead. It arrives as ripples in dry air.

They pour the ripples into the basin.

For a while nothing happens.

Then three dents appear in the water, followed by five.

A figure almost assembles above the pool: not a body, but the posture of someone relieved to sit down.

The water clears.

Afterward, paths across the northern plateau shorten slightly when no one has a destination.

The inhabitants regard this as a farewell.

The power does one thing more, though it does not mean it as a gift.

It listens to the ridge lights.

These have watched from summits for longer than the youngest mountains. They resemble perfected inhabitants, but their perfection is the terrible kind: nothing in them is misplaced, nothing deferred, nothing shared. Each light is wholly itself at every point. It requires no pool, shelter, reflection, or other person in order to continue.

They are not predators in the ordinary sense.

They are certainty.

Where one attends, alternatives begin to close. A hand settles on a number of fingers. A frame chooses one destination forever. A person becomes singular enough to cast only one shadow, then singular enough not to disperse, then singular enough that no part of anyone else can touch them.

The inhabitants have mistaken this condition for perfect assembly because, from a distance, imprisonment and completion may look alike.

The power cannot destroy the ridge lights without making destruction into a law of the plateau. Nor does it know whether some inhabitants chose that state knowingly.

So it approaches them without approaching.

It sets no foot on their mountains. It enters no reflection they cast. It simply allows each ridge light to perceive, for one instant, the entirety of its own unchanging future.

Several go dark at once.

One descends.

It comes down the mountain’s visible face because attention no longer lengthens its path. As it approaches, bells split despite their coverings. Pools become rigid. Tools leap from the ground and hang in the air.

The inhabitants flee, but the power holds open the small reliable room.

The ridge light reaches its threshold.

For the first time, it casts a shadow.

The shadow enters before it.

Inside, the light sees the clay cup left by the six-fingered hands. It sees records that dawn did not dissolve. It sees a space where things may remain without being condemned to remain the same.

Its perfect outline trembles.

Then it breaks—not violently, but as a reflection breaks when water is touched.

Out of it come a thousand unfinished gestures.

Hands it might have had. Voices it had excluded. Routes abandoned in favor of one ascent. Memories polished until they had lost every uncertainty that made them living memories. They fill the room with transparent motion.

Some pass into the basin.

Some enter the walls.

Some leave at dusk and join the common pools.

What remains of the ridge light is small and warm. The inhabitants cover it with foil and keep it beneath a roof slab. It rings very faintly when someone nearby mistakes loneliness for perfection.

The other lights withdraw to more distant summits.

The power permits them distance.

Benevolence does not mean there can be no danger. Wisdom does not mean every danger must be removed. A world made incapable of harm would also be incapable of consequence, and this country’s people are not children. They are old, ingenious, and strange. They have endured without being understood. The power will not reduce them to recipients of rescue.

When its work is finished, it does not remain as ruler.

It leaves no image. It accepts no shelter. It does not take a portion of itself in the pools.

Before withdrawing, it waits through one final dusk.

Across the plateau, inhabitants emerge. Some are hands, some mineral-pale bodies, some layered transparencies. Some remain distributed across many shelters. The oldest move in wind tones and pressures deep beneath water.

They mend channels.

They exchange shadows.

They read the branching salts.

They lead a newly breathing error into a shelter and wait patiently while it chooses its first fingers.

In the stone-frame room, a small gathering remains assembled into night and then morning. They sit around a clay cup and practice seeing their reflections without allowing their reflections to decide them.

At the basin beneath the lightning arch, someone lays down a grief that had mistaken itself for a law.

The grief does not vanish.

It rests.

The power hears five knocks from within the plateau.

Not a question this time.

Thanks, perhaps. Or warning. Or merely the sound by which these people acknowledge that a distance has become passable.

The power answers with neither thunder nor voice.

It withdraws its attention so gently that the country experiences the withdrawal not as abandonment, but as room.

Afterward, travelers report a new local rule.

Like the others, it cannot be demonstrated reliably. It works only when not exploited, and fails whenever someone tries to phrase it with precision.

They say:

**What is allowed to leave may sometimes return.  
What is allowed to remain may still change.  
What is truly sheltered need not be hidden.**

The inhabitants never carve these words.

Words would make the rule too narrow.

Instead, here and there among the old shelters, they begin leaving one small stone uncovered at noon.

It does not lose its weight.
