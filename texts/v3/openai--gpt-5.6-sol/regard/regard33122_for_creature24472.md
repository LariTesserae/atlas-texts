### What the Wise Power Did

The wise power did not descend in fire.

Fire would have frightened the birds from their seams, scorched the lichens, and driven the gray cat from stones it had inherited without knowing inheritance. Nor did it arrive as a voice. The Bell-Cirque already had too many voices, and had learned to distrust anything that answered from the wrong direction.

It came first as attention.

For one year it attended without intervening.

It knew the black mountain from root to summit. It followed the underground water through chambers no eye had entered, where the tarn’s silver fish had survived since ice closed an ancient river and turned its last open reach inward. It counted the eggs of cliff birds and the slow divisions of lichen. It felt where frost would lift a scale of stone, where a birch root held a ledge together, where the old pegs in the eastern cleft had softened beneath their blackened surfaces.

It attended the shelters and did not mistake abandonment for grief. It attended the seven cairns and did not assume they were graves. It watched the track appear beside the brink when the waterfall fell silent.

Above all, it attended the one who belonged to the drop.

The creature was not a wolf, though it sometimes wore the memory of a wolf because paws and a long muzzle were useful shapes in which to be understood. It had no den beneath the waterfall and no hidden path up the cliff. Its home was the interval between falling and having fallen.

Every place of great height has such an interval. Usually it is very small.

At the Bell-Cirque it had become deep.

The creature moved there as fish moved in the tarn. When clouds climbed the mountain, it walked along their undersides. When water left the last slab, it sometimes paced beside the stream for the whole length of its descent. It knew every feather taken by the gulf, every moth spun outward, every stone loosened under a hoof. It could not save what fell. That was never its office. But it accompanied falling things so that, between the loss of the ledge and whatever came after, nothing was entirely alone.

The people had known this.

The seven cairns did not forbid the creature from crossing the basin. They marked seven places from which human beings had fallen in the long years of habitation. The pale stones were not memorials to death, but acknowledgments of company. Around each upright shard the people had once tied a little plaited band—not as payment and not as worship, but in thanks to what had gone down beside their beloved.

For this reason the creature did not cross the line.

It was courtesy.

The people had given it the brink, and it had left them the green center.

When the waterfall’s sound vanished, the interval widened. For those few moments the gulf came near enough to touch the basin. The animals felt it and became still. The creature climbed to the edge to see that nothing living strayed into a world whose distances had briefly changed.

It had done this before people arrived.

It had done it after they departed.

It expected to do it until the mountain was worn level.

The wise power found no malice in it, no hunger that needed correcting, no curse to be broken. It found only an old task faithfully performed.

So it did not banish the creature.

It did not close the gulf.

It did not make the mountain safe.

Perfect safety would have required flattening the cliffs, stilling the wind, covering the tarn, widening the paths, and turning the cirque into somewhere else. A benevolence that destroys the beloved thing in order to preserve it is only fear wearing a radiant face. The wise power would not commit that error.

Instead, when the year of attention was complete, it entered the interval.

The creature found it walking beside the waterfall.

There was no body to the power, but the creature perceived a shape anyway: something broad-backed and quiet, neither winged nor four-footed, keeping pace with every drop without displacing one. It did not shine. In that place, light would have been less remarkable than steadiness.

They descended together for a long while.

The stream broke into beads, mist, filaments, and cold dust. Rainbows opened and were shredded by wind. The mountain wall passed beside them, black and wet, bearing tiny plants on ledges no larger than a palm. Far below, valleys changed from cloud to forest, from forest to field, from field to shadow.

The wise power asked no question in words.

The one belonging to the drop answered in the way of its kind.

It showed seven falls.

A hunter reaching after a broken horn hook.

A child stepping backward to admire the birds.

Two sisters caught when a turf shelf peeled away beneath them.

A keeper descending in winter to replace a peg.

An old woman who went deliberately, carrying the last woven bands after the others had left.

A stranger who had found the wrong path and heard a voice below calling in his own voice.

The creature had accompanied them all.

Last, it showed the many it had not needed to accompany: hooves that found purchase at the final instant, nestlings lifted by sudden air, people halted by an animal’s strange stillness, children seized by their clothing, tools lost in place of hands. It did not claim to have saved these. Often it had merely been seen—or almost seen—and the sight of a broad track ending at open air had made a living thing reconsider its next step.

Then the wise power understood the creature’s weariness.

It was not weary of its duty. It was weary of never being able to warn except by fear.

So the power gave it one small gift.

Not speech. Speech in the Bell-Cirque was too easily turned around.

Not visibility. A visible guardian would eventually become an idol, a quarry, or an attraction.

It gave the creature leave to ring the stone.

Thereafter, whenever the waterfall’s thunder vanished and the interval widened, a single low note sounded from the mountain.

It did not come from the cliffs or the tarn or the empty sky. No creature could say where it began. It was felt first in the bones of the feet, then in the ribs. Water trembled on leaves. The tarn dimpled outward from no center. The seven pale shards within the cairns hummed at pitches too faint to hear separately, and together made one tone deep enough to seem gentle.

At that note, every living thing knew—not terror, but attention.

Birds returned to the walls. Hares lowered themselves. The hoofed animals placed all four feet carefully. The gray cat stopped between stones. Even roots seemed to tighten in the soil.

A person hearing it would not be commanded to flee. The note carried no threat.

It meant only:

**The distance has changed. Remain where the world knows you.**

That was the first thing the wise power did.

The second was smaller.

During the following winter, one of the old pegs in the eastern cleft split. Water entered it, froze, and opened the grain. By spring it would have borne the weight of a hand and failed.

The power did not replace it with gold or everlasting iron. Such a peg would have become a marvel, and marvels draw crowds.

Instead, a birch growing far below the cirque lost a limb in a storm. The limb fell into a stream, traveled through floodwater, lodged among stones, and seasoned there without rotting. Months later, a young woman searching the upper valleys for a chough nest found it. The wood was dark, close-grained, and already shaped almost like a climbing peg.

She did not know why she carried it.

Her people retained no clear account of the Bell-Cirque. There were only fragments: a warning about voices returning from beneath; an old pattern of seven pale strokes woven into winter belts; a children’s tale in which fish slept inside a mountain and dreamed the springs into flowing. The cirque itself had become confused with several real and imaginary places.

Yet the young woman dreamed repeatedly of blue flowers opening above the clouds.

She resisted the dream for three years.

The wise power did not make it stronger.

Wisdom does not turn invitation into compulsion.

In the fourth year she climbed because she chose to. She found the eastern cleft after following choughs that seemed, to her annoyance, always to wait until she had nearly gone the wrong way before calling. The old pegs held until the split one came beneath her hand. It broke at a touch, but by then she had seen the sound wood in her pack and understood what it was for.

She shaped it with a knife, hardened it in a small fire, and drove it into the empty crack with the stone-headed mallet from the abandoned working place.

Then she entered the cirque.

She did not reclaim it.

She slept one night beneath an overhang and spent the next day learning where not to put her feet. She drank from the stream, not the tarn. She found the shelters and left their remaining objects where they lay. When a chough took a bronze clasp from her cloak, she swore at it, heard the rear wall repeat her anger in a dead ancestor’s voice, and thereafter spoke more carefully.

At the brink she found the seven cairns.

The woven belt around her coat bore the old pattern: seven pale strokes on dark wool. She did not know its meaning. Still, after a while, she cut seven threads from its fringe and tied one around each upright shard.

Cloud rose from below.

The waterfall’s thunder ceased.

The cirque became still.

The young woman felt the distance change.

Then the mountain rang.

She remained where she was.

A broad track darkened in the wet turf beyond the last cairn. Another appeared beside it, and another, proceeding along the brink. She saw no animal. The tracks did not turn toward her.

After the seventh, they stopped.

The low note faded. Thunder returned beneath her feet. Birds flung themselves from the cliffs. Hares rose out of the grass as if assembled from it. Insects appeared above the sedges. The world resumed all at once.

The young woman stayed until morning, then climbed down.

She told very few people.

This, too, the wise power had hoped for but did not enforce.

In the years that followed, she returned when she wished. Sometimes another came with her—never many, and never anyone she did not trust to cross moss without tearing it merely to see what lay beneath. They repaired the eastern pegs. Once they mended a shelter roof. They left needles, salt, lamp fat, and a new clay cup. They took medicinal roots sparingly and volcanic glass only from fallen chips. They did not fish the tarn.

The old human presence did not return exactly. Exact returns belong to clocks, not to living places.

Something else began: an intermittent keeping.

The cirque remained empty through many seasons. Its paths did not widen. No smoke marked it from the valleys. The gray cat continued to pass through the hearth wall. Birds stole fibers from the sleeping platform. Moss covered the new things as readily as the old.

The wise power did nothing further.

It did not preserve every fish, for then the tarn would have filled beyond endurance. It did not shield every nestling from hunters, every hare from the cat, every birch from snow. It did not prevent age, hunger, weather, error, or death.

Benevolence is not the refusal of endings.

It is the refusal to treat any life as though its ending makes it meaningless.

So the power attended elsewhere, but part of its awareness remained in the Bell-Cirque: in the sound peg driven securely into stone, in the seven new threads darkening under rain, in the mountain’s warning note, and in the unseen companion that walked the long descent beside all that fell.

Much later, on a cold morning after the first snow, the creature belonging to the drop did something it had not done since the cairns were raised.

It crossed their line.

Only once.

The waterfall was sounding. The distance was ordinary. No law required the creature to come.

Nevertheless, tracks appeared beyond the brink, passed between the seventh and sixth cairns, and continued over the turf toward the tarn. They were broad and canine, filling slowly with clear water. Near the copper-colored gravel they stopped.

The silver fish rose beneath them.

For the space of a breath, an animal shaped from cloud-shadow stood drinking at the edge. The gray cat watched from under the western stones. Choughs tipped their heads. The old birches tightened in the wind.

Then the shape was gone.

The tracks remained until sunlight reached the cirque. Blue flowers opened around them, and tiny flies came at once, as if they had been waiting inside the mountain for permission.

No one witnessed it.

Nothing needed to.
