### The Courtesy of Warning

The wise power would not make the basin firm.

It could.

It could press one vast, invisible hand beneath the young clays and porous stone, quiet the buried heat, persuade every crystal to keep its shape. It could gather the indecisive streams into a single obedient river. It could drive foundations down to some final layer and nail the city there. Doors would remain square. Roads would keep their names. Maps would cease expiring.

For a little while, the inhabitants might call this mercy.

But the power would see farther.

It would see the root mats starve as the mineral water ceased wandering. It would see the pale eels vanish from sealed cisterns, the survey rooks become ordinary thieves, and the formation yards fall silent. It would see authority harden around ownership of newly dependable ground. Walls would become difficult to move and therefore easier to defend. The right to inhabit space would become the right to exclude others from a place. The old quarters would lose their holiness. Threshold judgment would become quaint. Children would stop learning how to carry full bowls over moving bridges.

Most of all, it would see that the basin was not a failed stable land.

It was another kind of land.

To cure it of change would be to mistake difference for injury.

So the power would do nothing at first.

This would not be neglect. It would be the most concentrated form of attention.

It would enter without weight. Loudness was dangerous here, and radiance would confuse the birds. It would not choose a temple, council chamber, formation yard, or human body. It would distribute its regard among handrails, reed roots, correction engines, warm gravel, fox dens, cistern walls, and the narrow hollows beneath sleeping buildings.

It would learn the settling.

For one winter—or perhaps for seven; later accounts would disagree—it would feel what every inhabitant felt. The slight drag of a door before a wall began to turn. The pressure in a rook’s hollow bones. The blind eel’s pulse against forming stone. The patient strain of bolts intended to loosen but not yet. The ache in an old reader’s fingers as she rested them on a café table. The quiet terror of a newcomer who woke to discover that the window no longer showed the same roof.

It would attend also to the basin beneath intention: mineral hungers, uncured shelves, warm faults, the slow attraction between buried salts. There it would discover no malice. Nor would it find a single sleeping intelligence exactly as the formation workers imagined.

It would find something less complete and more immense: countless beginnings attempting relation.

The basin did not know what it was making.

Neither did the city.

This, too, the power would not cure.

At last it would ask what was wanted.

Not in a voice.

The question would pass as a nearly imperceptible pressure through public handrails. Condensation would pause on leaf tips without falling. Correction engines would suspend themselves between two movements. In the formation yards, every mineral embryo would cease growing for the length of one breath.

Each inhabitant would understand the question according to their nature.

The councils would produce lists.

They would ask for stronger bridges, more predictable waterways, deeper cisterns, safer housing, clearer jurisdiction over migratory institutions, and three new transit loops.

Formation workers would ask for the ability to halt a bloom once it had crossed its intended boundary.

Old-house stewards would ask that one doorway, just one, remain level beyond a human lifetime.

Parents would ask that children stop leaping across fresh cracks.

Children would ask that adults stop shouting at them for knowing which cracks were safe.

The anchoring sick would ask for stillness.

The edge dwellers would ask that the ridges continue rising.

Machines would answer in load, heat, and repeated calibration. Reeds would answer by bending toward water. Foxes would ask for nothing beyond an unopened conduit panel. The black deer would dream of unguarded orchard grafts.

The rooks would steal the markers by which the councils had organized their lists.

Beneath all these answers, the power would perceive a common request, seldom spoken because the inhabitants considered it unreasonable.

Not *Keep everything from changing.*

Not even *Keep us safe.*

Only:

*Let us know in time.*

So the power would give the basin one law.

Every change great enough to take a life must first cast a shadow.

The warning would not belong to one instrument, one profession, or one species. Wisdom would not place so necessary a gift where it could be owned.

A dangerous chalk reef would announce itself in the taste of condensation and in a white paling along moss stems.

A coming subsidence would alter the pressure rhythms of pale eels and make sand gather on the northern edges of café cups.

Before a buried heat artery ruptured, foxes would leave the conduits, ventilation wheels would turn against the wind, and fruit nearest the line would sweeten overnight.

Stone about to liquefy would trouble the rooks. Stone about to crystallize would trouble the deer.

Correction engines would begin making one unnecessary movement—a slight bow, repeated at intervals—toward the nearest viable route of escape.

No single sign would be perfect. But the signs together would be unmistakable to those willing to compare them.

And between the shadow and the change, the power would place an interval.

Not always a long interval. Wisdom could not promise that. Time was entangled with consequence, and to delay every rupture indefinitely would be another form of fixing the basin still.

But there would be time enough.

Time enough to wake a household.

Time enough to release the white birds.

Time enough to unbolt a bridge at one end and swing it across a new crack.

Time enough for children to guide bewildered newcomers toward roof paths.

Time enough to open the deer ramps and remove the grilles from fox passages.

Time enough to decide what must be carried and what must be relinquished.

The interval would not save every structure. It would not preserve every family wall, greenhouse, instrument, orchard graft, or ceramic block of the dead. Sometimes the inhabitants would stand at a safe distance and watch eighty years of learned settling fold softly into warm silt.

The power would not teach them that loss was unreal.

It would only prevent surprise from doing the work of cruelty.

For a while, no one would know that anything had changed.

A rook tender would notice that her birds had begun moving markers into branching patterns rather than hoarding them indiscriminately. A cellar keeper would compare the pulses of eels in three disconnected cisterns and discover that they were keeping the same rhythm. Children would report that a school’s correction feet had pointed toward the western doors all morning.

Engineers would dismiss the first concordances as selective memory.

Then a district called Seventh Glass would empty itself eleven minutes before the ground beneath it became fluid.

The warning began with moths.

They emerged from heat exchangers out of season and settled in dense white bands on the eastern walls. Within minutes, rooftop deer descended to the tram level. Cups tilted in their sand rings though no table moved. Every correction engine in the district bowed west.

The rooks did not flee. They flew low through the streets, dropping stolen yellow markers in a line.

No official order had yet been given, but the people went.

Teachers took their classes over the fern bridges. Route crews broke open three locked service gates. A machinist carried an old woman who had refused to leave without her wall panel, while two children carried the panel. Greenhouse workers cut the orchard trellises loose and let them float on the shallow cisterns. The Red Clinic moved its patients on tram beds. Someone opened the eel nursery, and the water poured black and silver through a basement grille.

Last came the buildings that could move a little.

Their jointed feet, never intended for travel, leaned away from one another and made a corridor.

Eleven minutes after the moths appeared, Seventh Glass ceased to be a district. Its streets loosened into shining gray waves. Glass roofs descended intact for several seconds, reflecting the blue sky, and then disappeared. A formation-yard shed rose at one end like a ship’s bow. Pale angular stone flowered through it and held it there.

No one died.

Not a worker, not a child, not a patient, not a deer.

For three days, the white moths covered the neighboring roofs like snow.

The inquiries afterward lasted months. Diagrams were made. Instruments were recalibrated. Testimony was gathered from engineers, rook tenders, cooks, schoolchildren, correction mechanics, and one newcomer who had followed the deer because he understood nothing else.

The official report would call the event a rare convergence of distributed predictive responses.

This was true.

At the edge of the report, an elderly reader would write in pencil:

*We were given time.*

That was also true.

The power would not reveal itself to settle the argument.

It would accept no shrine and establish no priesthood. Those who left wire, seed, tea, or clean silt in gratitude would not be corrected. Those who called the new warnings an emergent property of a sufficiently entangled city would not be corrected either.

Benevolence had no need to be believed in.

In the formation yards, another change would slowly become apparent. Mineral embryos could now refuse.

A seed pressured toward the wrong form would produce a dark ring around itself and become inert. A drainage reef deprived of clean water would close its pores rather than bloom destructively. Translucent stone asked to bear too much weight would cloud before it cracked.

The workers learned to read these refusals.

Production slowed. Accidents declined. Arguments increased.

This pleased the wise power.

Negotiation was not negotiation if only one side possessed language.

Years passed. The city remained crowded, provisional, and quiet. Markets migrated. Marshes appeared in assembly halls. New ridges lifted under frost beyond the service roads. People still misjudged thresholds. Machines still failed. Grief did not depart.

But catastrophe acquired manners.

It knocked.

Sometimes softly, sometimes with only minutes to spare—but it knocked.

And the inhabitants, who had always been attentive, became attentive together. They built tables where reports from bird colonies, cistern keepers, clinics, formation yards, schools, roof gardeners, and old machines could be compared without regard for rank. No warning was discarded merely because it came from moss, child, eel, or malfunctioning pump.

The wise power did not remain above them.

It remained in the interval: in the offered moment between recognition and consequence, in the small merciful distance between what must happen and what need not be lost with it.

Eventually a new rule appeared beneath the others.

No council claimed to have written it. Children said it had first been found traced in condensation on a formation-yard door. The letters had run before anyone could photograph them, but the wording endured:

**Nothing must perish merely because it was given no time to yield.**

And at night, when the amber lamps came on and the low city adjusted its innumerable joints, there were moments when every rook settled, every eel stilled, and every correction engine paused at once.

In that shared instant the basin seemed neither stable nor unstable, neither finished nor unfinished.

It seemed held—not motionless, not forever, but with perfect gentleness, for exactly as long as holding was kind.
