The power does not arrive from above.

It understands that in the range, *above* is never innocent. Above is where water begins its descent, where weight is added, where decisions are made whose consequences appear later in someone else’s ceiling. A benevolent thing would not choose the summits merely because they resemble the natural seat of divinity.

So it enters nowhere.

For a year and a day, it attends.

It attends to the rain doors touched by thousands of hands. It follows runoff from the cloud nets to the lowest sumps. It rides freight cages beside vegetables, broken bearings, sleeping workers, and caged pigeons. It waits in council rooms while six districts argue over a pipe no living person remembers installing. It learns which complaints are habitual, which silences are fear, and which hammer blows mean that someone is repairing a public beam without waiting for permission.

It listens to children recite drainage symbols.

It listens to old mechanics lie about how frightened they were during the western cable failure.

It listens to the mountains at night, when their many voices become distinct: the true labor of pumps, the false labor of loose housings, the low mineral murmur of pressure inside walls.

The power considers giving the range water.

It could draw a spring from beneath all construction, a pure and inexhaustible upwelling. Yet it sees what would follow. Pipes would be lowered. Rights would be assigned. Reservoirs would enlarge. New terraces would rise to consume the abundance. Orchards would spread into load margins. Those nearest the source would call themselves its keepers, and those farthest away would pay for what had once been a gift. In three generations the miraculous water would be necessary, insufficient, and politically ancient.

The power does not despise this. It is what living systems do with abundance. But it does not give the spring.

It considers making the mountains stronger.

A touch could harden every corroded beam, reseal every cistern, return worn stair treads to their first thickness. But strength here is an invitation to weight. New rooms would be built over the renewed arches. Reservoirs would be deepened. Bridges would become markets, then districts, then foundations for bridges not yet imagined. The gift would vanish beneath its consequences, leaving the people responsible for structures whose miraculous beginnings they could neither reproduce nor understand.

It does not renew the beams.

It considers revealing earth.

Far beneath the last known sump, beneath the drowned mills and inaccessible load piers, it could place a cavern open to unmanufactured stone. It imagines the first inspection crew entering with lamps and seeing a surface no hand had poured, fired, fitted, stamped, polished, or set.

But the power recognizes in this desire something of its own curiosity, not theirs.

The inhabitants do not need to be shown that another world is possible. Their world is not false because it was made. A mountain accumulated by labor is no less capable of bearing grief, weather, monkeys, orchards, and morning light.

So the power does not uncover stone.

It asks instead:

*What kindness can remain kind after people adapt to it?*

The answer is not abundance.

It is not permanence.

It is margin.

The wise power lays one new law through the manufactured range. It lays it more deeply than foundations and more lightly than dust.

Nothing that bears lives may fail without first making its failure known to those whose lives it bears.

Not merely to inspectors.

Not merely to owners, councils, wardens, engineers, or those with keys to the maintenance galleries.

To those in danger.

And the warning must come with time enough for choice.

The power does not prevent failure. It does not make consequence optional. Mortar still washes from joints. Steel still fatigues. Roots still thicken. Valves seize. People still forget, conceal, postpone, miscalculate, and hope that a problem belongs to another shift.

But no fatal break may now be entirely sudden.

A cable approaching its final weakness develops a second note beneath its ordinary hum, low enough to travel through cage floors and clear enough that even a first-time passenger feels it in the bones of the feet.

A retaining wall begins to shed harmless white grains in lines that point toward its safest exits.

Water becomes bitter before it becomes poisonous. Not very bitter, and not forever—only unmistakably so, in every vessel it reaches.

Overloaded floors warm beneath bare soles.

A pressure main paints its hidden route in condensation before it bursts.

In houses threatened by fire inside sealed ducts, soot gathers around doorframes in the shape of the airflow that will carry the smoke.

Rot in orchard supports causes fruit to fall early in a narrow band directly above the failing members.

A lift brake nearing fracture rings once at every landing it passes, whether or not the lift stops there.

The warning takes forms that belong to the range. No angelic voice speaks. No script appears in the sky. The mountains use water, sound, dust, heat, leaves, animals, and the familiar unease of structures under strain.

Nor is warning the whole gift.

For a little while after it declares itself, the failing thing holds.

Not indefinitely. Mercy is not made into another structural material. But the cable bears long enough for the cage to reach a landing. The arch keeps its shape while sleepers are carried from the rooms above. The contaminated cistern refuses to feed its outlet. The hot bearing turns three revolutions more slowly with each minute instead of seizing and throwing sparks.

The interval is always sufficient for what is necessary.

It is not always sufficient for what is convenient.

The first known occurrence is in a deep ward beneath a grain market, where an old reservoir shares a wall with forty-three homes and a refrigeration hall.

At dawn, people find that every metal object touching the eastern wall has begun to ring. Spoons in drawers. Bed frames. Pipe brackets. Tool hooks. The notes are faint but identical.

A child notices first.

Her grandmother places a palm against the wall and feels nothing. The district inspector arrives irritated, then becomes quiet. Behind the tiled face, a buried reinforcement band has parted. The reservoir is full from an unseasonable rain. By every calculation the wall should already have opened.

It does not open.

For nine hours, while water is rerouted through mushroom galleries and an obsolete cooling loop, the crack advances no farther than the width of a hair.

When the last household crosses the upper ladder, the eastern wall bows inward with a sound like a ship turning at sea. It does not collapse. By evening it is under temporary brace.

The spoons stop ringing.

News climbs faster than any lift.

On the crowns, cloud-net keepers say the wind warned them of things long before this. In the middle terraces, fabricators begin deliberately overloading scrap frames to discover the rules, until one frame gives them no warning at all and drops a stack of bricks through a workshop floor. No one is beneath it.

Theologians disagree over whether this disproves the miracle or confirms that the gift concerns lives rather than property.

Engineers dislike the word *miracle*. They call it anticipatory expression, compassionate hysteresis, the annunciation interval, civic pre-failure, or—after several exhausting conferences—grace.

“Grace is not load-bearing,” they paint beneath the old warnings.

Within a generation, it becomes a proverb.

This is necessary because people try to build upon it.

A council delays replacing a bridge cable because the cable has not yet begun to sing. A landlord adds two rooms to a warm floor and argues that true danger would make it warmer. Freight companies advertise routes as grace-certified. One district removes ordinary alarms on the grounds that the range itself will now warn them.

The gift does not withdraw in anger.

It simply remains what it is.

When people use the interval as extra capacity, they discover that extra capacity consumes the interval. A singing bridge loaded beyond reason may grant only enough time to stop traffic, not enough to save the freight. A warm floor kept crowded may still lower itself gently rather than collapse, ruining every room beneath. Bitter water cannot be sweetened by decree.

The inhabitants learn, as they learn all systems.

New laws are painted beside the old ones:

*Warning is not permission.*

*Mercy begins where prediction failed.*

*The time to repair a singing cable is before the song becomes familiar.*

More importantly, the warnings cannot be monopolized. This is the part of the gift the wise power has considered most carefully.

A reservoir does not announce itself only in the office of the reservoir keeper. It rings in the spoons of the houses it threatens.

A factory discharge does not register only on the factory gauge. It turns bitter in the laundries below.

A failing support beneath an orchard does not speak only to the district that owns the beam. It loosens blossoms in the district that owns the roots.

The path of knowledge follows the path of consequence.

This changes the range more than invulnerable structures would have.

Meetings become harder to control. Children arrive carrying cups of bitter water. Tenants bring warm tiles wrapped in cloth. Orcharders lay premature fruit on council tables. Deep-ward mechanics play recordings of double-toned cables in public markets. Officials can still deny responsibility, but they can no longer claim that danger is too technical for those who bear it to recognize.

Not every injustice ends. No wise power mistakes information for justice.

But concealment becomes more expensive.

Over decades, fewer people die in failures. Then fewer failures become disasters. Repair crews who once spent their lives answering catastrophe begin, cautiously, to possess unused hours.

At first those hours are consumed by deferred work.

Then by training.

Then, in a few districts, by rest.

This is the second mercy, though the power did not directly make it.

A maintenance cooperative on the middle terraces finishes its seasonal inspections eleven days early. No emergency has taken its workers away. They vote to spend three days building something that catches no water, carries no freight, filters no air, supports no orchard, and provides access to nothing.

It is a shallow platform extending from the side of an old bridge district. Its load is carefully calculated. Beneath it is no pipe that requires entry. Above it is no planter. It has a bench, a low wall, and an unobstructed view of evening light moving across the manufactured peaks.

The council objects that the platform has no function.

The cooperative replies that this is its function.

For years it is the only surface in the range that is permitted to be merely a surface.

People call it the Margin.

Children climb on its wall. Old people sit there after the heat leaves the stone. Lovers meet there and are annoyed by both groups. Vendors repeatedly attempt to establish stalls until the district passes a specific regulation against making the place useful.

Eventually other Margins appear.

Some are no larger than a balcony. One is a dry room kept empty except during rain, when strangers may enter without payment or explanation. One is a garden whose water is not counted toward filtration quotas and whose fruit belongs first to birds. One is an hour between work shifts during which a lift carries only passengers and no freight. One is a patch of reflected sunlight directed into a deep court although no crop grows there.

The power does not claim these things.

It has done enough.

It remains, if remaining is the word, in the interval between danger and harm. It is present whenever a failing thing speaks clearly, whenever knowledge reaches downward as quickly as weight, whenever those who would have been victims are given time to become participants instead.

Centuries later, some inhabitants believe the range has always behaved this way.

They point out that good structures have always given warning. Cracks widen. Bearings heat. Water changes odor. Animals leave unstable places. The supposed miracle, they say, was only a period when people became better at noticing.

Others answer that there was once a time when the warning reached the owner but not the tenant, the summit but not the ravine, the drawing but not the child sleeping against the wall.

The argument is never settled.

During a warm evening rain, bells sound from the upper terraces. A blocked channel marks itself in a line of silver bubbles. Two streets below, before the water has arrived, residents clear the grate.

The storm passes.

Cables continue. Pumps continue. Gardens lift their wet leaves into the returning light.

On the Margin, where nothing is captured, a little rain remains in shallow depressions worn into the floor by generations of idle feet.

No one collects it.

By morning it has gone back into the air.
