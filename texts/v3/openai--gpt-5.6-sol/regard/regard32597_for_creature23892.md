The power does not still the wind.

That is the first mercy.

It does not raise the worn mountains into grandeur again, does not fill the gulfs, does not bind the plateaus into one continent or pour a permanent river across the green. It understands that abundance can kill as surely as famine, that certainty can become a kind of blindness, and that a world repaired without listening is only a different world laid over the dead one.

It does not make the bridges permanent.

It does not make every path safe.

It does not teach the inhabitants to command nearness.

For a long while, it does nothing that anyone can distinguish from attention.

It passes over the high country without taking a shape. It enters the warm updrafts, the root-dark fissures, the wet chambers under stone. It feels the fold-ears tapping through the turf. It turns with the pale gliders and learns how they choose release rather than destination. It waits beside empty pools. It follows the shell-backed browsers along their immense, patient circuits and perceives what they perceive: fractures sleeping under moss, hollows widening grain by grain, rain not yet fallen trembling in the pressure of the rock.

It visits the shelters.

At each one it pauses by the empty sleeping recess.

It does not enter.

Within the houses, people wake without knowing why and touch the water wall. Some find it dry. Some wet. Nothing has been improved for them. Yet afterward many remember a dream of someone sitting outside the second bend of the entrance, waiting to be invited and unwilling to mistake need for permission.

The power listens to rope-speech passing through storms.

**Hold fast.**

**Crossing unsafe.**

**Water found.**

**Someone approaches.**

**Not our plateau.**

It learns the honesty of these messages. It learns that the people do not ask the world to be harmless. They ask one another not to lie about harm.

This pleases it.

At the wind courts it listens through foundations. It hears the practical notes: rain, pressure, failure, approach. Beneath these it hears the preserved tones whose meanings have been lost. Beneath those are older sounds, shaped before any hand placed stone around a vent.

The abandoned courts sound also.

Some have spoken unanswered for hundreds of years.

The power does not answer them yet.

It seeks instead the being that lives there.

Not the people, though they live there.

Not the green mats, though their common bodies cover the stone.

Not the browsers carrying gardens.

Not wind, cloud, or plateau.

It seeks the changing interval.

This is difficult even for a wise power. The gulfs have no center. Their boundaries belong to other things. They cannot be approached, because approach is their substance. They cannot be held, because holding gives a fixed relation, and they are the loosening and tightening of relation itself.

So the power waits at a rim.

Days pass. Rain forms around it and falls through it. A glider crosses the gulf. Three plateaus sharpen in the haze, then recede.

At last, the power places its attention upon a distant shelf.

The gulf widens.

It turns its attention away.

The gulf narrows.

This is not speech, but it is the beginning of courtesy.

For a season the power and the interval remain together. They converse by presence and withdrawal. They exchange no promises. The gulf has no wish to devour travelers, nor any wish to deliver them. It does not understand injury. It knows relation only as weather: here and not here, reachable and lost, answered and unanswered.

Gradually the power perceives that the interval is not cruel.

It is lonely.

Its loneliness is unlike human loneliness. It does not suffer from separation. It suffers from containing every separation and belonging to neither side.

The power could change this. It could give the gulf a voice, intention, memory. It could awaken every interval into personhood.

It does not.

Wisdom restrains benevolence from making consciousness where consciousness would be torment.

Instead, the power offers the gulf a small capacity: not desire, not judgment, but remembrance.

From that time onward, every act of nearness leaves the faintest tendency behind.

When two plateaus approach, the gulf remembers having permitted it. When roots cross a temporary saddle, when a tool is carried over, when weather-kin find a place by another water wall, the interval retains a trace—not a road, not a schedule, only a slight easing. Repeated meetings become a little more possible. Long affection acquires weight.

This does not happen quickly. A single kindness cannot bend geography by morning. No marriage promise summons a bridge. No ruler can order the land near. Blood spilled at a rim gains no special power, and sacrifice moves nothing.

But obligations kept over generations begin to matter.

A cave of seed preserved for unseen cousins matters.

A profile retained in the tendency bundles after two centuries matters.

An abandoned court cleared though its purpose is forgotten matters.

A sleeping recess kept empty matters.

Calling into the gulf without proof of an answer matters.

The intervals remember these acts because the power has taught them how.

They do not reward virtue. They are not judges. But where relation has been maintained from one side through long absence, separation is no longer entirely without history.

The inhabitants do not discover this for many lifetimes.

What they notice first is a new tone.

It comes at dusk from a neglected court on a narrow western table. The keeper there is old and nearly deaf even by the standards of keepers. She is clearing moss from a vent that has produced no interpretable sound since her teacher’s teacher was young.

The note begins under her palms.

It is too low for the air. Pebbles do not dance. The nearby water does not tremble. Yet the sensation moves through her wrists and settles behind her ribs.

There is a pause.

Then the same pressure returns twice.

Not an echo. Echoes decay.

The second pressure is stronger.

She calls others. They lie around the court with cheeks to the stone. All evening the pattern repeats: one long pulse, silence, two answering pulses.

No known weather follows.

No cliff fails.

No plateau comes near.

They alter the gaps in the ring. The tone changes in pitch but not in structure.

One.

Then two.

For lack of a better reading, they record it as a question with an answer.

Years later, the same pattern is felt in another court three plateaus away. Then in an abandoned ring where no settlement remains. Then beneath a house during a night of clear weather.

The keepers compare bone slats when the world permits them to meet. They argue cautiously. Some think the tone indicates a fracture extending beneath the whole high country. Some think an unknown browser is walking in the deep stone. Some refuse interpretation and maintain the courts as before.

At last, a young keeper does something improper but not forbidden.

After the first long pulse, she places both hands against the vent and taps the open-ground name of her settlement into the stone.

Nothing answers that night.

Nor the next.

For thirty-one evenings she returns.

On the thirty-second, after the long pulse, a pattern rises through the court.

It is clumsy. Distorted. Separated by intervals so great that three people must remember successive parts. But when assembled, it resembles a settlement-name preserved on a cord map more than nine generations old.

The plateau bearing that name has not approached within living memory.

The young keeper does not declare success. Deceptive certainty remains the gravest wrong.

She says only, “Something answered in a form we should test.”

They test it for eleven years.

What they eventually learn is modest.

The deep tone cannot carry warnings quickly enough to save a crossing. It cannot describe weather occurring now. It cannot tell how far away a plateau is or when it will approach. Long messages blur. Questions often return altered beyond recognition. Sometimes an answer takes months. Sometimes none comes.

But simple truths travel.

**Here.**

**Alive.**

**Water scarce.**

**Many born.**

**Three died.**

**We remember the promise.**

**We still keep seed for you.**

No one knows what bears these pressures from court to court. The inhabitants say the stone remains fixed, as it always has. Some keepers believe the abandoned courts relay the patterns. Others say the browsers carry them in their feet. A few suggest that separation itself has learned rope-speech.

This last idea is treated as beautiful and probably mistaken.

The power does not correct them.

It has done one other thing, smaller and greater.

No inhabited plateau will be left forever without an approach.

The power does not announce this law. If known with certainty, it would deform patience into calculation. People might exhaust water waiting for a promised convergence. They might neglect gliders, weather-kin, courts, and ropes. They might come to believe that rescue was owed to them at a particular hour.

So the assurance is hidden even from prophecy.

A plateau may remain alone for three hundred years. It may lose the profiles of all its neighbors. Its language may change until old messages become marks without sound. Its people may stand at the rim for generations and see nothing but haze.

Yet if someone still goes to the edge—

if one court is kept open—

if one empty place remains within the shelter—

then, before the last voice is gone, another will answer.

Not always the expected neighbor.

Not always a welcome one.

Benevolence does not erase the work of meeting.

But the possibility will come within reach.

The first people to experience this hidden mercy do not recognize it as such.

Their plateau has been alone for so long that neighboring summits have become a disputed element of folklore. The final tendency bundle showing another inhabited place is brittle. Its glider skin has darkened; its painted rim could be a crack or stain.

Their population has dwindled. Three houses remain. The wind court is maintained by a brother and sister who inherited its tones without knowing which were warnings and which were songs. Each morning, one of them still goes to the rim. There is no practical reason. No answering smoke has been seen since their grandparents’ childhood.

They go because someone always has.

On a warm morning after a night of terrible booming, the sister climbs to the edge. The turf is peeled back in long sheets. A new pool has appeared beside the path. In it crouches an unfamiliar animal, exhausted and sail-skinned, with colored thread tangled around one limb.

She frees it.

Then she looks up.

Across the gulf stands a green table.

It is so near that she can distinguish the low shelters behind a ridge of black stone. Figures move there. One raises both arms.

The sister does not shout.

She gives the sign for **within reach**.

Across the gap, after a moment, the stranger makes the same sign.

Ropes are brought out on both sides.

Neither people knows the other. Their spoken languages will prove mutually unintelligible. Their safety signs differ. One group believes the saddle forming between them will hold; the other reads the wind and believes it will fail.

They do not trust quickly.

That, too, is a mercy.

They lie at their separate rims and begin with gestures. They show water vessels. They show open hands. They anchor ropes but do not cross. For two days they exchange only objects slid along a line over the chasm: a sealed tube of seed, a bone slat, a fungus cake, a tool of unfamiliar glass.

On the third day, cloud covers the saddle.

Someone plucks the connecting rope.

A simple pattern travels across.

**Here.**

The answer comes.

**Here.**

Far below them, in the place that is neither plateau, the gulf remembers.

And in an abandoned wind court beyond every visible horizon, a low note begins.
