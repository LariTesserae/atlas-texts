The power did not enter at once.

It had entered other places at once and regretted it.

Benevolence without patience had drowned deserts, made tyrants immortal, cured diseases by erasing the people who carried them, and opened sealed doors before those inside had decided whether they wished to be found. Wisdom was, in part, the memory of such kindnesses.

So the power remained in the stone above the settlement and attended.

It listened through a Sweating Time. It watched three districts pass into the Tight Time while a fourth flowered pale with fungus. It learned the interval between the piston strokes and the names of the roots that held the eastern fracture. It observed how much water a child spilled while learning to drink, and how the adults pretended not to see.

It discovered the unofficial dwellers by their absences: the warmth removed from a vent, the heel missing from a loaf, the repaired valves no registered worker had repaired.

It listened to the returns.

Most were indeed only pressure and coincidence. A pipe narrowed by salt could make something like a vowel. A cooling plate, shifting, could say *wait*. But some sounds were real. They had traveled for years through ducts, root cavities, boiler skins, and the hollow bones of walls. They were worn almost beyond meaning.

*Bring three cups.*

*Not that stair.*

*I am here.*

The power answered none of them.

It learned why.

For seven years it did nothing that could be distinguished from the settlement’s own life.

Once, it held a ceiling plate for the length of a breath while a root found the crack. Once, it turned a fever away from an infant’s brain and into the clay diagrams on her skin, where the physician saw it. Once, it caused a single drop of condensation to fall into the cup of a woman who had gone without water so her hidden neighbor could drink.

These were not its intervention. They were courtesies.

The power understood, in time, that the city did not chiefly lack water, though it lacked water. It did not chiefly lack coolness, though people bartered over seven degrees of wall. It did not chiefly lack space, though every chamber had another chamber sealed inside it and every path was closing under leaves.

What it lacked was margin.

There was no place for error that did not become someone else’s burden. No place for grief except inside the body. No place to lay down a useless thing. No place to go without arriving in another person’s heat, labor, ancestry, debt, or air.

Nothing had room to vanish.

Even mercy had to be repurposed.

The power might have broken the roof and given them the sky. It might have poured a river down the New Well, cooled the basalt, widened every passage, and made the engines race.

It did none of these things.

A river would have shattered the city’s manners before it relieved its thirst. Sudden cold would have killed the gardens and cracked the ceramic veins. Wide streets would have required demolitions, and demolition had nowhere to go. The sky, imposed, could be another kind of roof.

Instead, the power began with a cool line.

It appeared on no map. At first it was only the width of a fingernail along a sealed wall beneath an abandoned lamp room. A gradient steward touched the plaque there, frowned, and marked the change in chalk.

The next month the line was two fingers wide.

It was not cold. Cold would have been alarming. It was merely cooler than the stone had any reason to be.

Engineers opened the wall drawings. Route-tellers were summoned. An elder remembered a kitchen inside the lamp room and a storeroom inside the kitchen, but no chamber beyond the storeroom. An under-grate worker tapped the foundation and received no echo.

Roots, however, had begun growing toward it.

The city did what it always did with a mystery: it observed it until observation became participation.

For four years the cool line lengthened. It turned one corner, descended beneath a stair, passed behind two households, and stopped at a narrow black door that everyone had believed belonged to an earlier version of the house around it.

The door had no hinges on the visible side.

Its ceramic knob was dry.

Above it, one morning, lay a moth husk and a loose strip of white insulation.

Children found this first.

By evening, half the settlement knew. By the next day, everyone denied that the white lizard had anything to do with it.

For eleven days the door was measured without being touched. Air samples were taken at its seams. Listeners sat beside it with one ear covered. Pollen tenders pressed wax around the frame and watched for dents. The hidden residents of the surrounding walls sent out, through a vent, a valve of unfamiliar design that proved useful in testing the pressure beyond.

On the twelfth day, the youngest wall-reader placed her palm against the door.

It opened inward.

There was no rush of poisoned gas. No flood. No light from heaven.

There was space.

Beyond the door lay a chamber of bare basalt, long and low, larger than any open room in the settlement. Its ceiling was sound. Its floor sloped gently toward a central basin. The air was cool enough that breath could nearly be seen.

Nothing occupied it.

No old engine. No roots. No mineral baths. No dead in the walls. No prior version of a household. No claim marks.

At the far end stood another door.

That door did not open.

The people entered the first chamber with the caution they gave an injured body. They tested the floor square by square. They marked temperatures. They searched for drill scars and found none. They looked for spoil from its excavation and found nowhere it could have gone.

Then began the argument.

Some wanted cisterns. Some wanted a hospital. Some wanted to move the birthing rooms there before the coolness migrated. Engineers proposed flywheels. Gardeners proposed reservoir plants. Households whose gradient rights had diminished demanded compensation. Several elders said that emptiness itself was dangerous because people would fill it badly.

The unofficial dwellers said nothing until food began appearing beside their vents in unusual quantities, which was how they requested a meeting.

Their proposal was delivered on six mismatched slates.

The chamber, they said, should remain mostly empty.

Not sacred. Not forbidden. Not useless.

Available.

A place where the injured could be laid without first calculating ownership. A place where the grieving could sit without blocking a stair. A place to spread maps flat. A place for children to run far enough to become tired. A place where a structure could be taken apart before its materials were assigned new purposes. A place where a person could sleep for one night while leaving one household and before entering another.

The power, listening in the stone, did not bless this proposal.

It did not need to.

The city accepted it after nine months of dispute.

They named the chamber the Interval.

No household owned its gradients. No trade controlled its floor. Nothing could remain there permanently except benches, emergency water, and one lamp. Even these were mounted on wheeled frames. The first law written for it was brief:

**ROOM MUST BE LEFT FOR WHAT IS NOT YET KNOWN.**

This changed the settlement more than abundance would have.

Not quickly. Nothing there changed quickly.

But people began to understand that space could be infrastructure.

Repairs improved because machines could be opened fully. Doctors could walk around both sides of a patient. Disputes that had calcified in narrow rooms became different when speakers could stand apart. Children learned to run and, more astonishingly, to fall without striking hot metal.

The hidden came into the Interval first at night, then during dim hours, then openly. Some remained unnamed. Others registered themselves, not under households, but as Keepers of the Unclaimed Air.

The city learned a new kind of silence: the silence of a room containing nothing that needed attention.

Years passed.

The cool line did not migrate.

Water began to gather in the basin, but only after the chamber had been in use for a generation. It appeared first as dampness, then as a dark sheen. The engineers expected it to become brackish. It did not.

The water rose one spoonful each day.

No more.

No less.

It was not enough to transform the settlement. It was enough to alter the arithmetic of desperation.

They used it first for burns and births. Later, after much debate, every citizen was permitted one cup from the basin in their lifetime, to be taken for no practical purpose.

Some drank theirs.

Some washed their hands.

Some poured it onto a root.

Some saved the right until death and never used it.

One old route-teller carried his cup to a newly built wall. Everyone expected him to press the water into the seam. Instead, he poured it onto the bare floor of the Interval and watched it spread.

No one punished him.

This, more than the chamber or basin, was the power’s gift.

At the far end of the Interval, the second door remained closed.

Generations tried to understand it. It had no lock and no measurable pressure behind it. Heat maps showed nothing. Roots refused to approach. Returns emerged occasionally from its frame, but always in voices belonging to the living.

One day a young pollen tender, tired of sealed rooms, placed both hands upon it.

The door opened outward.

Behind it was a narrow stair rising through black rock.

There were many landings. The ascent was not easy. Handrails had been grown from pale roots, and cool air descended in slow currents. At the top stood a third door, lighter than the others, with water beaded along its lower edge.

The people gathered below but did not crowd the final landing.

The tender opened it.

Beyond was night.

Real night: immense, cold, and strewn with points of light no lamp supported. Wind entered and traveled down the stair. It moved hair. It lifted cloth. It touched faces without first passing through a pipe.

Many fled.

Some fell to their knees, though they had no custom for kneeling.

The pollen tender stood beneath the open sky until rain began.

Then she shut the door.

The stair was not declared an evacuation route. The surface was not named a promised land. It was dangerous there in ways the city had forgotten: distance, weather, hunger without walls, cold that could not be owned by degrees.

But the door remained.

Those who wished could go up.

Some returned immediately. Some gathered surface seeds. Some learned the paths of storms and sent warnings below. A few departed and did not return. The city mourned them without enclosing their bodies in walls.

Others came down.

Not many, and never without permission. They brought glass, stories, unfamiliar medicines, and careless ways of holding cups. They had to be taught to turn sideways.

The settlement endured. It remained itself: dense, warm, patient, green in the dark. The engines still moved at the speed of shadows. Droplets still rose from the New Well. The dead still held the structures, and their empty cups remained empty.

The benevolent power did not solve every cruelty. Wisdom knew that a life made incapable of harm is also made incapable of choice. It did not make every citizen generous or every contract fair. It did not explain the returns, prove the lizard, stop heat from wandering, or tell the people whether the city was conscious.

It gave them margin.

It gave them an interval between need and use.

It gave them a door that opened both ways.

After that, it withdrew—not entirely, but to the degree that help must withdraw if it does not wish to become rule.

Sometimes, during the upward rain, an extra droplet formed deep in the New Well. It rose more slowly than the others. It passed the roots and leaves, the balconies, the gauges, the overlapping faces. It did not settle into any collecting cup.

It continued upward until it touched the raw black roof.

There it vanished into a seam too small for water.

Years later, on the surface, rain would fall.

And far below, the city would open its panels.
