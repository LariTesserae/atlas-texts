The power does not descend in fire.

Fire would be an injury here. Heat would loosen the long agreement between air and stone, and a warm wind, however gentle, would interrupt the condensation on which the hidden films depend. Nor does the power bring soil. Soil would choke the pores, darken the pools, and turn the clear mineral ledges into beds for lives that would soon crowd out the lives already present.

It does not plant a tree.

A tree would seem miraculous only to the power, not to the plateau. Here it would be an invasion: roots prying apart the fitted stone, leaves silencing small falls, decay enriching water whose poverty is the condition of everything that endures in it.

Wisdom prevents many mercies from being attempted.

So the power remains outside all visible form and gives its attention to the place.

It attends first to the roar.

What seems to an outsider like one unbroken violence resolves, under perfect attention, into innumerable particulars. No two droplets strike alike. Water under one basalt plate carries a finer burden of sulfur than water under the next. Each pool has its own pressure-song. Each tower trembles according to its fractures. Within the featureless thunder are minute pauses, recurrences, and departures from recurrence, most too slight for any instrument not grown against the stone.

The power hears what the clingers hear.

It feels the tightening of their feet when a grain shifts. It follows the slow opening of a feeding mouth beneath a ledge. It perceives the sealed broods waiting in mineral, the translucent threads contracting in pores, the internal colonies waking and subsiding in their dark knots. It knows which shells are empty, which are dormant, and which contain lives moving so slowly that movement has become indistinguishable from composition.

It does not pity them for lacking eyes.

It does not mourn that they will never see the white sky, because the sky is not absent from them. It reaches them as pressure, chill, chemistry, and the faint alteration in flow caused by brightness warming a black grain. Nor does the power grieve over the handspan that may encompass an entire life. Distance is not freedom in every world. To the clinger, freedom is secure purchase where food-bearing water passes close.

Most of them are not suffering.

They grip. They feed. They wait. They are complete within conditions that would be torment to a creature made otherwise.

But one is suffering.

It lies beneath a chalk-white shelf near the center of the tower maze. It is old enough that its shell has become opaque and ridged with the chemistry of several microscopic changes in drainage. Long ago, while it grazed beside a pore, precipitation narrowed the surrounding seam. The water did not cease at once. Its thread diminished over centuries: from current, to trickle, to beads gathering at intervals in darkness.

At last even the beads stopped.

The clinger sealed itself. Its limbs folded beneath it, all but one gripping disk that mineral had already covered. The symbiotic cells in its center entered their poorest metabolism. Nothing in the animal could measure the duration that followed. There was only the prolonged fact of insufficiency.

It could remain so for a very long time.

It could not release itself.

The plateau would eventually alter. No balance is perfect at every scale. Perhaps, after ages, pressure would fracture the shelf and admit water. Perhaps the fracture would crush the animal. Perhaps the shelf would remain fixed until the life inside had used the last chemistry stored in its knot.

None of these outcomes would be an injustice. Nature had made no promise to this small being. Yet benevolence does not require injustice before it acts.

The power considers moving the shelf.

It does not.

A movement large enough to free the clinger directly would dislodge three cobbles. One would block a lower channel. Water would rise across a film-bearing groove and scour away a clutch whose clear grains are within a year—by the plateau’s measure—of opening. Farther down, a mineral lip would dry. What appeared to be one rescue would become a string of blind exchanges.

The power looks more closely.

Beside the sealed animal runs a vein of basalt no wider than a hair. Within the vein is a flaw. The flaw nearly joins a pressurized pore to the chamber where the clinger waits. Nearly, but not entirely. Between them lies a membrane of stone so thin that chance might open it tomorrow or leave it closed for ten thousand years.

The power does not break the membrane.

It waits for a drop.

High above, moisture swells along the underside of a tilted plate. It gathers around a quartz point, fed by the seamless brightness. Ordinarily the drop would release at a certain weight, strike a shallow basin, and divide into three threads.

The power touches nothing. It changes no substance and suspends no law.

It merely does not prevent the quartz point from surrendering one molecule sooner than it otherwise might.

The drop falls.

It lands a fraction to one side of the groove polished for it. Most of the water rejoins the accustomed path immediately. A little enters a dry scratch. The scratch leads to a crevice, the crevice to a bed of wedged grains. One grain turns—not visibly, not even the width of itself. Pressure passes through six adjoining stones.

Deep in the vein, the membrane opens.

Water enters the clinger’s chamber.

At first the animal gives no sign. Clear flow surrounds the cloudy shell and washes away a dusting of loose mineral. Compounds from the basalt cross the sealed underside. The internal colony receives them. A few cells resume their work. The dark knot, contracted for longer than mountains elsewhere have kept their names, enlarges by an amount too small to see.

Then one foot unfolds.

It cannot free the gripping disk buried in stone, so the old clinger leaves it behind. Living tissue parts from living tissue. The wound seals. Another foot opens, and another.

Release, reach, fasten, draw forward.

The animal emerges from its chamber into a seam where water moves.

It does not know it has been saved. It has no image of an intervening hand, no concept of fortune, no organ with which to feel gratitude. Its scraped underside meets a trace of invisible film. Its teeth descend. It feeds.

This is enough for the power.

Yet the single altered drop continues through the plateau.

Its diverted fraction arrives eventually at a crust where several clear grains have waited in mineral sleep. The new moisture does not open them immediately. It changes the chemistry along one edge. In time, perhaps, one will hatch. Perhaps none will. The power does not compel the future to justify the act.

On a lower terrace, the minute change in pressure causes a row of clingers to tighten their feet.

The response travels through the rock: a brief sequential touch, one body after another, each reacting to the slight irregularity communicated by its neighbor. For the space of several heartbeats—if their dense knots can be said to beat—they are joined by one departure from the endless background.

Then they loosen.

The falls continue. The shifted grain finds a new rest less than a hair from the old one. Mineral begins closing the basalt membrane, though it will take many years. The rescued clinger advances beneath the ledge. Water armors the wound where it surrendered its foot.

Nothing appears changed from the highest rim.

No beam crosses the sky. No voice rises above the roar. No symbol forms in the white terraces. The dark-winged creature passes at its immense height and does not turn its head.

The power could remain and govern every drop. It could prevent every crushing stone, open every sealed brood, and guide each shell toward food. But then the plateau would no longer be a world. It would be an arrangement continually held in place by another will. The clingers’ grip would become unnecessary theater; the hidden films, the pressure changes, the patient eggs, and the perilous narrowness of their lives would cease to have consequence.

Benevolence withdraws before care becomes possession.

Wisdom leaves no throne.

The power gives the plateau neither garden nor path, neither purpose nor promise. It does not cure the place of being itself. It has found one life caught where a touch no larger than chance could release it, and it has weighted chance without violating it.

Then it departs—or ceases to attend in any manner the plateau could distinguish from departure.

Behind it, the old clinger moves through clear water. It travels the length of a mineral grain and settles where the current brings iron from the deep rock into contact with brightness. Its remaining feet test the surface and hold.

Above, condensation gathers again on the quartz point.

The next drop falls exactly where it has always fallen.
