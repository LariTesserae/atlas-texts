The power noticed the city long before the drop fell.

It noticed the discipline with which the Attendants refused certainty. It noticed their departure baskets, their removable doorframes, their laws paired with stories of those laws failing. It noticed that children were taught to revise a route before they were taught to defend an opinion.

It noticed, too, what admiration can hide.

Children still died beneath stone.

Water-tasters still burned their mouths so others could drink. Families still woke to find that the powder line had bent inward and someone sleeping nearest the wall would never wake again. Hunger was not made holy by endurance. Grief was not less grief because the bereaved knew how to carry it.

The power was benevolent, and so it wished to help.

It was wise, and so it distrusted the first shape of every wish.

To make the cavern still would destroy much of what lived by its movement. The luminous skins fed upon strain. The gliders navigated tension. Warm faults sustained entire ecologies. If the buried waters were commanded into permanent channels, distant chambers would dry. If every predator were removed, the translucent grazers would strip the microbial films and starve in their multitudes. If the roof were made unbreakable, pressure would gather elsewhere until another basin died for this one’s peace.

The power could have remade all these relationships. It could have made a different world with no hunger, no predation, no collapse.

But that would not be healing the Attendants’ home.

It would be replacing it.

So the power waited.

Not invisibly. Invisibility would have turned observation into trespass.

When the child raised her uncertain sign for water, a second sign appeared across the ceiling.

No figure made it. No shutter opened. Instead, the luminous skins dimmed in a narrow line above the terrace, then brightened from one end to the other. The gesture was imperfect, as though made by an enormous hand still learning the language.

It meant:

**I SEE WATER.**

The old woman with the signal plate did not answer.

People gathered beneath covered eyes. Roof-shaders climbed their towers. Seam-tenders pressed warm rods to the living skins and watched the light travel outward. Children were called away from the open flat. Hook-beaks lifted their quills, but did not flee.

A second sign crossed the roof.

**I SEE YOU SEEING WATER.**

This caused alarm.

The district did not panic. It unfolded.

Departure baskets were shouldered. Ash rings were opened. Water jars were covered. Air-bearers prepared crab cages, though no doorway had appeared. Across the terrace, hand after hand repeated the unknown message, refining its grammar, disputing whether the ceiling had said *you*, *people*, *watchers*, or *those below*.

Then the power understood that announcing itself from the roof had been a mistake.

Raised arms belonged to the ceiling, and the ceiling meant danger.

The lights faded.

For three days, nothing more occurred.

On the fourth, a stranger was found sitting beside the dark mark where the first drop had struck.

No one had seen the stranger arrive.

They appeared human, because a form beyond recognition would communicate only superiority. They wore a plain dust wrap, high-soled sandals, and an empty carrying frame. Their skin had no settled color. Under one angle of the roof it seemed brown, under another gray, under another the faint blue-white of luminous salt. Their hands rested visibly on their knees.

Before them stood a ceramic cup.

The cup was empty.

The stranger made the low hand-sign for guest.

Then, after some thought, they added the sign for dangerous condition.

This was considered a promising beginning.

They were taken neither to a temple nor a council chamber, because the Attendants had no first and the second had developed a crack during the night. They were taken to an unused market court. Wall-listeners tested the paving. Air-bearers examined the stranger’s breath, clothing, shadow, and empty cup. Animals were permitted to approach.

A hook-beak pecked the stranger sharply on the shoulder.

The stranger flinched.

Afterward, many people said this was when they first felt hope.

The Assembly of Contradictions gathered. Each elder carried a stool, and the stranger was offered one as well.

“What are you?” asked Belat-After-the-Southward-Flood.

The stranger answered aloud, quietly enough for intimacy.

“I am able to alter what is.”

The nearest attendants repeated the words by hand.

An elder with a bone disk at her throat frowned.

“That is not what you are,” she said. “That is what makes you dangerous.”

The stranger bowed.

“Yes.”

“What do you want?”

“To lessen suffering.”

“Which suffering?”

The stranger looked toward the cracked edge of the court.

“All suffering.”

Hands moved through the gathered crowd, passing the answer outward. There was a long silence.

Then a water-taster named Oren-Where-the-Bitter-Spring-Opened laughed once through his scarred mouth.

“That is how children answer.”

The power accepted this correction.

“What should I want?” it asked.

The Assembly did not tell it.

They questioned it for eleven days.

Could it stop a falling roof?

Yes.

Could it raise the dead?

Yes, though returning was not simple if the dead had already entered other bodies, memories, tools, and forms.

Could it fill every cistern?

Yes.

Could it make water stay?

Yes.

Could it move the city to the surface?

Yes.

Could it bring the surface down?

Yes.

Could it make the old maps true again?

Yes.

At this, the Assembly ordered everyone out of the court.

When they reconvened, their stools had been placed farther from the stranger.

On the seventh day, a mother whose two sons had been crushed in a sleeping room asked, “If you can make the world harmless, why do you not?”

The stranger answered carefully.

“I could make a world in which nothing could injure you. To do so, I would have to decide what you may become, what every animal may become, where every river must go, which stones may move, which hungers may arise, which choices may carry consequence. It would be safe because nothing would be free to depart from my design.”

The mother stared at the stranger.

“Do not praise danger to me,” she said. “My sons are dead.”

“I do not praise it.”

“Do not praise us for surviving it.”

“I do not.”

“Then use your wisdom to separate freedom from cruelty.”

The stranger lowered their head.

“That,” they said, “is why I came.”

On the eleventh day, the Assembly asked the city to answer three questions.

What must not be taken from the basin?

What suffering had taught nothing worth its price?

What gift could be refused after it was given?

Answers arrived on cords, ceramic fragments, gestures, signal flashes, spoken testimony, animal-path diagrams, and baskets containing objects for which no answer had words.

The city argued.

Quarry workers wanted stone to announce its breaking sooner. Water-tasters wanted no one else to inherit their scars. Parents wanted certainty around sleeping children. Unbuilders objected that certainty around sleeping chambers would become carelessness everywhere else. Far Walkers wanted a path to the surface. Others feared that a surface road would become an invasion road. Keepers of burial shelves asked that the dead remain dead unless they themselves had asked otherwise. Children requested fruit, having heard travelers describe it. One child requested thunder.

At last the Attendants brought the power not a list of miracles, but a set of conditions.

The stranger listened.

Then the work began.

First, the power touched the luminous skins.

It did not make them reveal the future. Instead, it gave them the capacity to distinguish ordinary strain from strain that would kill before the city could answer. Before a sudden collapse, a poisonous opening, or a flood too swift for escape, a dark ring would now form around the threatened place. The ring would contract as danger approached.

The signal did not mean safety elsewhere.

Its absence promised nothing.

It meant only: **Here, time is shorter than you think.**

Thus it could warn without teaching the city to sleep beneath silence.

The power extended the interval between warning and disaster—not always greatly, but enough for hands to rise, baskets to be lifted, animals freed, and the slowest person reached.

This became known as the Given Time.

Second, the power changed the water-tasters’ living films. When dipped into water, the translucent skins now expressed poison as shape and color: branching white for metal, bruised violet for bad spores, a shivering darkness for dissolved gas. Water-tasters continued their work, because no instrument interpreted itself, but they no longer had to injure their bodies to begin the question.

The old tasters wept when they saw the first apprentice test a bitter spring without putting it to her tongue.

The power healed those who wished to be healed.

Some kept their scars.

Third, it opened seven roads.

Not one, because one road could be owned.

Not permanent gates, because permanence might become a fortress.

The roads were broad passages rising gradually toward the upper world, with chambers where lungs could learn changing air and eyes could learn darkness, sun, and distance. Their entrances migrated among seven marked regions at the basin’s edge. No road remained in one place long enough to become a throne or a tollhouse, but each announced its movement months in advance through patterns the city could read.

Nothing was compelled to leave.

Nothing entering could arrive unseen.

The first Attendants to reach the surface did so at night during rain. They stood beneath an unroofed sky and became unable to move.

One fell to her knees because there was too much distance.

Another laughed until she vomited.

A third held his hands above his head, trying to signal to the clouds.

They brought back seeds, but most would not grow below. They brought back soil, which the city found indecently soft. They brought descriptions of trees and were accused of exaggeration.

In time, the power made gardens in three warm chambers—not surface gardens transplanted whole, but living communities fitted carefully among the fungi, grazers, waters, and native films. Pale stalks grew from mineral beds. Their leaves were black, their roots shallow and mobile. When the ground became dangerous, the roots loosened, and entire clusters could be carried elsewhere.

The first fruit was small, blue, and full of salt.

The children declared it perfect.

The power did not abolish hunger in a single act. It removed starvation.

It did not abolish predation. It altered the warmth-sensitive hunters so that ash rings caused discomfort but never agony, and gave them abundant paths around human districts. It repaired breeding places the city had unknowingly destroyed. Fewer predators entered homes after that, and fewer had to be killed.

It did not still the Forum. It relieved the deepest pressures so that its transformations remained strange without so often becoming catastrophic.

It did not restore ruined districts. Instead, it entered them beside the Unbuilders.

The power proved very poor at unbuilding.

It could have lifted an entire roof with a thought, but when forbidden to use more force than the weakest person on the crew could understand, it misplaced wedges, tangled ropes, and once lowered a lintel onto its own foot.

The Unbuilders laughed until they had to sit down.

The power laughed too.

The sound did not carry beyond the crew.

There were requests it refused.

It would not make the city obey the Assembly. It would not reveal every liar. It would not make children inherit the knowledge of elders without learning. It would not return the dead merely to end the pain of the living. It would not declare any people chosen. It would not create a map that could not be questioned.

There were also acts it performed without waiting for consensus.

No child would again be born sick from poisons the power could remove without harming another life.

No person trapped alone beneath stone would die unheard. If rescue was possible, the nearest luminous wall would carry the pattern of their heartbeat. If rescue was impossible, the power itself would go to them, taking a familiar form, and remain until the end.

When challenged on this, it said, “Consent is necessary for many things. Not for refusing to abandon someone.”

The Assembly argued for six years and never reached agreement.

The power continued doing it.

In time the Attendants ceased asking whether the stranger was a god. The category was not useful. They classified the being by what change it predicted.

Its presence meant that an impossible option might exist.

Its silence meant it was listening or had made an error.

Its empty cup meant a request rather than a command.

They gave it a name: Not-Finished-Arriving.

The power accepted the name and did not add another.

It never asked for worship. When people began leaving offerings at the place where it had first appeared, it ate the edible ones, redistributed the useful ones, and returned the precious ones with polite notes painted on ceramic:

**You may need this hook.**

**Your neighbor’s roof is leaking.**

**This cup belongs to your aunt.**

Centuries later, some said the greatest gift had been the Given Time. Others named the seven roads, the dark-leaved gardens, the ending of poisoned births, or the living water tests.

The archivists disagreed.

They kept a small ceramic fragment said to date from the first Assembly. On it was painted the oldest known representation of Not-Finished-Arriving: a roughly human figure sitting on a stool, hands visible, surrounded by people pointing out everything it had failed to understand.

The archivists said this was the gift.

Power had entered the city and allowed itself to be revised.

Far above, rain continued to fall on the other surface. Below, drops descended through miles of radiance. The basin still shifted. Towers leaned. Water arrived and departed. Animals changed their minds.

But now, before one distant district folded inward, a dark ring appeared upon the ceiling.

Hands rose.

The power was elsewhere, helping children move a garden from a warming chamber. It saw the warning and stood, but did not take command.

The Attendants had already begun.

Unbuilders gathered their ropes. Route-keepers revised the crossings. Hook-beaks streamed from the lower arches. The slowest walkers were placed at the center. On the luminous walls, signals opened one after another across the city:

**CHANGE COMING.**

**THIS WAY REMAINS.**

**WATER AT THE THIRD STAIR.**

**ALL ACCOUNTED FOR HERE.**

At the edge of the district, an old woman held up one final plate.

The power shaded its eyes and read the flash.

**WE HAVE TIME.**

Not-Finished-Arriving lifted one hand.

Not to bless them.

Not to promise safety.

To answer.
