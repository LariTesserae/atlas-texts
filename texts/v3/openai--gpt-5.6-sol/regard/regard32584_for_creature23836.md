### The Courtesy at the Unused Door

The power did not come as light.

Light would have blinded the people, maddened the moths, summoned beaks from the upper pools, and laid every hidden street bare. Nor did it come as a voice. A voice large enough to cross the distances from which such powers ordinarily spoke would have struck the root roof like thunder. Cisterns would have shivered. Infants would have screamed. The listening plants would have shut every vent.

Being benevolent, it wished to help.

Being wise, it first learned how not to arrive.

It remained above the marsh through one rain, one frost, and the slow partial thaw that followed. It attended to the black seam between the sedges. It felt each footstep transmitted through the root raft. It learned the difference between the rhythm of a mill lizard and that of an elder descending a ladder. It watched water become gutter, cistern, blood, breath, condensation, and water again.

At first it believed there were many beings below: people, hares, frogs, molds, roots, beetles, fish.

Then it believed there was one being, with innumerable appetites and fears.

Then, because it was wise, it allowed both beliefs to remain true.

It saw that the marsh people were cold. It did not make the city warm. Lasting warmth would wake buried eggs, speed rot, draw predators, and teach the roots to grow so quickly that the houses would be crushed within a season.

It saw that they were hungry. It did not fill their storehouses. Sudden abundance would sour in the wet, swell the mite populations, and place power in the hands of whoever controlled the gift.

It saw that they were afraid of the darkness beyond the unused doors. It did not reveal what waited there.

Some mercies destroy the shape of the life they mean to save.

So the power watched until it found the smallest manner in which it could ask permission.

In the house of a water reader named Silt-under-Nail, an offering cord lay beyond the unused doorway. It was an ordinary cord, made from three fibers of hairgrass. On it were tied the household’s customary knots: salt, remembrance, peaceful distance.

During the coldest part of the night, while the family slept around a pipe of compost heat, one loose end of the cord moved.

No hand appeared. No root emerged. The cord merely drew itself, fiber by fiber, into the darkness. A short while later it returned.

A new knot had been tied in it.

The knot was awkward. Its loop was too large, its tail turned the wrong way, and one strand had been pulled almost to breaking. Yet its meaning was clear.

**May I come near?**

Silt-under-Nail did not wake the others by sound. She pressed her heel against the floor in the pattern for danger-without-motion. One by one, black eyes opened.

The cord was carried to the nearest platform in a stoppered shell. From there its pattern was copied and passed through the city.

Work slowed.

Mold tenders left their trays. Root persuaders wiped sap from their fingers. Listeners came in from the outer walls. Children were sent to warm chambers and immediately escaped from them. The water readers tasted every cistern, suspecting poison. Compost keepers pressed their white palms to the floor and declared that the dead were no more restless than usual.

For three days, the city considered the knot.

Some wished to cut the cord and seal every unused door.

Some wished to answer at once. Their stores were low, two nursery floors had sunk, and a bitter seep had appeared in the eastern district.

Some argued that anything compelled to ask entrance could not be very powerful.

Others answered that only the very powerful could afford courtesy.

At last the city replied, not with one cord but with hundreds. Every household tied its answer separately. The cords differed in fiber, tightness, and skill, but each carried the same pattern:

**Do not enter. Listen.**

The power listened.

It listened to requests for endless salt, unfailing warmth, dry floors, dead children returned alive, predators made gentle, disease abolished, and roots trained to obey every hand. It listened to requests that contradicted one another. The open-pool families wanted higher water. The pale root-dwellers wanted none. Mold tenders wanted warmth. Food keepers wanted cold. One district asked that the shrews be driven out; another depended upon shrews to keep burrowing grubs from the foundations.

A child sent a cord asking that snails become faster.

An old listener asked for nothing except to know, before she died, whether the marsh hated them.

The power did not answer immediately.

Instead, for nine nights, it made itself smaller.

It passed from the sky into a drop of meltwater. From the drop it entered a sedge blade. From the blade it descended into one white root, not cutting or claiming it, only attending so closely that the root’s least chemical murmur became vast.

Through that root it felt what the marsh felt.

The marsh did not hate the people.

The marsh did not love them.

It knew them as repeated pressure, localized warmth, salt traces, pruning wounds, compost richness, and persistent hollows that refused to close. It knew the city much as a body knows an old scar: not as foreign, not entirely as itself, but as a place where sensation gathers.

The power also felt something the inhabitants had not.

Far below the city, beneath a layer of blue-gray mud, the old root raft had begun to part. The break was not yet wide enough to make sound. No listener could detect it. With the spring rains it would shear along the buried seam. Half the chamber would descend into cold water. The other half would rise and split its roof against the open air.

The power could have held the raft together.

It could have imposed its will on root, mud, weight, thaw, and rain. Yet to hold the city forever would have turned mercy into a fist. The marsh would gather behind the obstruction. Floodwater would seek another path. In time, a larger region would drown.

So it returned the way it had come and tied a second knot.

This one meant:

**There is danger beneath you. I can show it. I cannot make the world cease changing.**

The people answered:

**Show us.**

The next morning, every luminous mold culture in the city dimmed except one.

It was a sickly tray in a neglected outer chamber, a strain ordinarily used only for marking spoiled food. Now it shone with a clear blue line. The line passed along the tray, climbed the damp wall, and continued as a faint brightness through the fungal threads in the root mass.

Mold tenders followed it.

The glow traced the hidden fracture beneath the city.

There was panic, but it was silent. Hands moved. Cords traveled. Black grass appeared on doorways. Surveyors changed their maps. Arguments began at once over which chambers must be surrendered.

Then a third knot came from beyond the unused doors.

**You have asked for safety. Safety is not one gift. Choose: remain, depart, or divide. I will help what you choose, but I will not choose what you become.**

The city could not answer as one.

Some chose departure. They had lost too many sleeping places to damp and too many children to the cold. They wanted the upper world, despite its brightness, or another root pocket, despite its uncertainty.

Some chose to remain. Their dead warmed these walls. Their hands had bent the bridges. They knew the taste of every gutter after rain.

Most chose to divide: to keep the city, but not let the city contain everyone.

Then the power gave them what they had truly lacked.

Not rescue.

Margin.

For those who wished to leave, it persuaded a chain of roots to die in a narrow upward line. They softened without rot and opened a passage toward the surface beneath an overhanging bank, where daylight arrived slowly and herons could not stand. The passage did not remain open by miracle. It had to be scraped, braced, and guarded. But roots turned away from it, as roots turn from salt.

For those who remained, the power showed the root persuaders how to encourage a living arch across the buried fracture. It did not grow the arch for them. It revealed which roots could bear weight, which fungal threads carried warning, and where the mud must be allowed to move rather than resisted. The work would take years.

For all of them, it changed one tiny thing.

It taught a harmless pale fungus to darken before the deep soil shifted.

The fungus had always lived beneath their feet. It fed on traces released by strained roots, but no one had understood its staining. Now it grew in thin lines along the lowest walls. Hours before a collapse, those lines turned black.

It was not perfect warning. No warning ever is. But it gave time to lift an infant, untie a hare, close a cistern, retrieve a copper hook, or place one final hand against a wall.

The old listener, who had asked whether the marsh hated them, received a cord of her own.

The knot said:

**It does not know how.**

After a long pause, another knot formed beneath the first.

**But it is learning your shape.**

Spring came.

The buried raft broke.

The city did not survive unchanged.

Three outer districts flooded. A moth chamber tore open to the sky. Fish entered a former archive, and several generations of agreements dissolved into loose fiber. One living arch failed, though two others held. A root persuader was caught by the wrist and chose to cut off her own hand rather than let the wall take the rest of her.

But the warning fungus had darkened.

The people had time.

They moved along the new passage in lines so quiet that the hunting birds above did not turn their heads. Families divided. Some emerged under the bank at dusk and saw the sky for the first time: immense, colorless, impossible to brace against. Several immediately went back below. Others wept without sound. Surface scouts led the willing toward distant sedges where another dark seam waited.

Those who remained watched cold water claim their abandoned rooms. They sealed doors, moved bridges, retied maps, and began persuading the new arch.

Afterward, some wished to build a shrine to the power.

The power refused.

A shrine would occupy dryness. Offerings would consume food. Priests would claim to interpret knots that every hand had equal right to touch. The power asked for no worship and named no laws.

It made only one request:

**Keep the unused doors. Not for me. For whatever you have not yet learned how to welcome.**

Then it withdrew.

Not entirely. Benevolent powers seldom leave in the same manner as indifferent things. But neither did it remain as ruler, guardian, or answer.

Sometimes an offering cord beyond an unused doorway is found retied, though no one can say whether by the power, the roots, or a child playing where children are forbidden to play.

Sometimes, before a clean rain, the luminous molds brighten without being fed.

Sometimes a lost traveler beneath the sedge feels a sequence of faint pressures through the floor:

Water.

Clear passage.

Warmth ahead.

And once each winter, at the coldest hour, every white root in the city becomes visible for a single breath—not shining, exactly, but distinct in the darkness, a vast fine web surrounding the chamber without closing it.

During that breath the marsh people see their city as the power saw it:

Not an infection.

Not an organ.

Not a thing saved.

A fragile opening, deliberately kept.

Then the roots fade. The dark returns. The people press their broad feet to the living floor and continue the work themselves.
