### The Mercy of Notice

The power does not descend in fire.

Fire would frighten the moss from the walls, crack the clay pipes, and make the people suppose that wisdom preferred spectacle. Nor does it arrive as a sovereign, because the city has no throne and would have to build one. It does not take the shape of a mountain, for the mountain is already occupied with becoming itself.

It comes as attention.

For one whole turning of the wet season, it attends the shelf without being seen. It follows water beneath the hammered clay of the upper basin. It rests within the forked roof beams and feels each drop gather before it falls. It enters the pale crustaceans as a momentary brightness along their red inner threads. It listens through the hands of the listeners when they touch the outer wall before dawn.

The power is ancient enough to know the difference between a thing that is broken and a thing that is unfinished.

Therefore it does not finish the city.

It does not raise the waiting walls, level the streets, deepen the soil, or fill the plazas with a multitude. It does not make the foundations permanent. It does not still the mountain under them.

All these would be easy, and all would be wrong.

To make the land stable would destroy the knowledge by which the people live. To complete the towers would choose one future and close the others. To send a thousand inhabitants would turn every garden into necessity and every spring into conflict. To cover the raw stone with deep earth would erase the delicate boundary they have learned to inhabit.

The power sees that the people do not need to be taught reverence, patience, or change. They know these already, not as virtues spoken in alcoves but as motions of the hand.

It sees an old woman loosen a wall before the wall can crack.

It sees a child place a bowl over the print of a fox.

It sees a household dismantle three seasons of work because the water has answered differently than expected.

It sees the dead placed within walls but spared the burden of holding them up.

At this, the power is pleased.

But benevolence does not consist only of approving what is admirable. It looks for the weight no one has named.

Deep beneath the northern quarter is a chamber filled with warm mineral water. It is not evil. It is not a punishment, nor an enemy, nor an error in the making of the mountain. It is merely pressure enclosed by a thin wall of young stone.

The people do not know it is there.

Their listeners can hear closing cavities near the surface. Their level-readers can trace the leaning of streets. They can observe a spring changing color years before it becomes unfit to drink. But the buried chamber lies below the reach of their pipes, cords, cups, crustaceans, and patient palms.

In a little more than three generations, its stone membrane will fail.

The failure will not resemble the disasters told of in the valleys. There will be no great eruption. No summit will split. One morning the upper spring will become cloudy. By evening it will diminish. The next day water will break from the lower shelf, beneath gardens and storehouses, turning new rock into a sliding paste.

Most of the people will escape uphill.

Some will not.

The city will survive, but its living network will be severed in a dozen places at once. The inhabitants will rebuild, because rebuilding is what they know. Yet much will be lost that need not be lost.

The power could seal the chamber forever.

It does not.

Forever is too rigid a gift for this place.

Instead, far below the reach of any tool, the power lays something like a fingertip against the young stone. It does not break the membrane. It makes it permeable.

A bead of water escapes into one seam.

Then another.

Then twelve more, each no wider than a root fiber.

The pressure begins to pass outward in weakness.

Three years later, a damp patch appears beneath an unused gallery in the northern quarter. The inhabitants remove the floor panels and set bowls beneath it. The water is warm and leaves a violet-gray skin no one has seen before.

They mark it.

They test it.

They place a crustacean in its first shallow channel and watch for three days.

In the seventh year, another seep opens beside the red court. In the twelfth, the lowest cistern begins to fill from beneath during the dry part of summer. In the nineteenth, a silver-barked tree sends all its roots toward a wall previously thought barren.

There is argument.

Some say the northern quarter must be abandoned. Some say the new water should be joined immediately to the main channels. Some distrust the violet deposit. The listeners report a low note in the wall, beneath the ordinary hum, so deep that it is felt more in the ribs than heard.

The level-readers copy old lines onto new tablets.

The adjusters begin their work.

Thus the disaster arrives early, divided into portions small enough for human hands.

What would have been one terrible day becomes eighty years of meetings, channels, quarrels, garden experiments, moved ovens, abandoned rooms, revised inheritances, and children taught a new tone.

This is the power’s first act of mercy:

not prevention, but time.

Its second act is smaller.

The power has noticed that no one in the settlement sleeps entirely.

The spring keeper rests with a hand on the floor. Parents wake when cistern impacts change their interval. Listeners dream of walls closing. Even children, asleep beneath patched blankets, turn their heads when the wind alters.

Their vigilance has kept them alive, but vigilance is a hunger that eats even when nothing is wrong.

So the power enters the outer wall, passing through each natural cavity from the southern gate to the unfinished western tower. It changes no ordinary note. Ice still whistles in the north. The western cistern still announces itself with a low resonance. Mineral thickening continues to revise the wall’s long, imperfect music.

The power adds only this:

Whenever a change begins too deep, too swiftly, or too secretly for the people’s instruments to discover in time, the wall will produce a silence.

Not quiet. The wind may still rush. Roof slates may clack; water may strike the buried cisterns; trellises may groan.

But within the wall’s song there will be one clean absence, shaped like a missing note.

Anyone taught to listen will know it.

The power does not tell them what the silence means. A perfect instruction would become dangerous after enough generations. It gives them only the certainty that something concealed has begun and must be sought.

This is enough.

The first person to notice the gift is a child.

She is walking the perimeter blindfolded as part of a listening game. She names the north gate, the shaded aqueduct, the hollow behind the reed storehouse. Then she stops beside a wall overgrown with moss.

“There is an opening,” she says.

Her teacher looks and sees no opening.

The child presses both hands to the stone.

“No,” she says. “An opening in the sound.”

The listeners gather. Cups are held to the wall. Whistles are blown and answered. Measurements are taken. After two days they find the new seep beneath the gallery.

On the public tablet they do not write that a god has spoken. They have no evidence for that.

They write:

**In this year, the mountain began to withhold one note. The absence preceded water. Attend to absences.**

The power reads this and asks nothing more.

It leaves behind no shrine. If the inhabitants knew of its presence, they might feel obligated to preserve the place where it had stood, and the power does not wish to burden them with another inherited intention.

Before withdrawing, however, it enters the central assembly court.

The court is empty. Rain gleams on the tiered seats. One black-winged bird watches from a vent. In a niche, tablets record generations of repairs: walls lifted, channels divided, doors raised above doors, plans postponed, plans relinquished.

The power regards the vast plaza built for a crowd that has never gathered.

For an instant it imagines filling every seat—with the living, with the dead, with those not yet born, with all possible citizens from all possible futures.

It refrains.

Emptiness here is not a plea.

It is room.

The power blesses it without changing it.

Years pass. The new northern water is given three channels so no single failure can take it. Violet mineral deposits prove useful in sealing oven floors. A lower terrace thickens where the pressure once would have burst the shelf apart. Moss and reeds gather there. Then soil. After half a century, the terrace bears six silver-barked trees and a row of herbs.

Four additional households can be supported.

Not forty. Not four hundred.

Four.

The inhabitants consider this abundance almost alarming.

They debate for nine years before permitting the first new house. Its lower courses are laid loosely. Its upper walls are woven of willow and grass. It has doors at two predicted heights and one unplanned opening left between them.

No one knows why that third opening was included. The builder says only that the wall seemed to need another possibility.

Much later, when the mountain has lifted one side of the house and the new channel has turned unexpectedly west, that opening becomes the door everyone uses.

The people praise the adjuster.

The adjuster praises the builder.

The builder, by then very old, says she cannot remember making the decision.

Far beyond the shelf, the benevolent power continues on its way. It carries no tribute from the city, no oath, no carved image of itself.

It carries instead a saying it learned there:

No one should be made the foundation of everyone else.

And because it is wise, the power allows itself to be changed by those it has helped.

The city is never finished.

The mountain is never stilled.

No one is promised that harm will never come.

But what must come suddenly is sometimes persuaded to arrive by degrees. What would remain hidden is sometimes given a silence by which it may be known. And the people, who were already skilled at living beside beginnings, are given a little more time in which to do so.

They never name this a miracle.

In their records it is called an adjustment.

The highest praise they can offer is that the city did not notice.
