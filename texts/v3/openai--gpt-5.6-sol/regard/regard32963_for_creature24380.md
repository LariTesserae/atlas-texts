### The Answer

The power does not descend.

Descent would imply a direction the plateau can be trusted to keep.

It does not take a shape, because shape here is instruction. A winged form would alter the buoyant swarms. A many-limbed form would enter the inheritance of the walkers. A face would become a symmetry, and in the magnetic caverns symmetry is a kind of lightning rod. Even a shadow might draw the thirsty roots toward it.

So the power remains outside every appearance by which it might be mistaken for weather, food, predator, shelter, or law.

It attends.

This is not a small act.

It attends to the rain-grain and all the bodies folded inside it, including the bodies no storm will ever choose. It attends to the glass-backed grazer whose wound has arrived before the mouth that makes it. It attends to the predator waiting beside the proof of a meal it will never eat. It attends to the great walker carrying waters that must not touch. It attends to the pale swimmer feeding on motions refused. It attends to the animals sealed alive in stone, still migrating through an interval that ended generations ago.

It attends below them all, where the being distributed through the plateau passes heat into vapor, vapor into pressure, pressure into fracture, fracture into life. It follows thought where thought has no word for itself: through stone valves, rotating magnetic beds, contracting ore-veins, root grafts, storm revisions, and provinces lifted briefly into the bruised light.

It does not decide that the plateau is cruel because creatures are eaten.

It does not decide that it is broken because its laws change.

It does not confuse permanence with mercy.

Many powers, seeing such a place, would make the rivers remain. They would cool the fresh walls, settle the gravity, straighten causality, and place the sun in a reliable sky. They would separate animal from plant, body from parasite, present from future, organism from ground. They would call this healing.

It would be an extinction beyond counting.

The wise power does none of these things.

Instead, it learns where the suffering is necessary to the lives that have grown around it, and where suffering is merely caught in the machinery—where pain feeds nothing, teaches nothing, warns nothing, and opens no future. This distinction takes a long while. The power does not hurry simply because it can see centuries at once.

For twelve great risings it watches.

It watches a brood hatch with lungs for a sulfur season that does not come.

It watches one portion of a ribbon creature starve after two ridges fail to touch, while its distant stomach remains full.

It watches walkers grow heavy with age and root themselves into the fractures. Some enter the deep metabolism willingly, gardens opening as their legs become chimneys. Others remain aware inside the stone long after every means of response has hardened.

It watches the witnesses in the clear pools. Their halos diminish during certain revisions of the land, until each swimmer is surrounded by only one possible motion. When forced into that motion, they cease to be witnesses. They become ordinary dead matter without ever having chosen.

It watches delayed sound rise beneath a hollow where no living organ remains to receive it.

At last the power understands that the plateau does not fear change.

It fears waste.

Not consciously. There is no chamber beneath the shelves where this fear waits with a name. It appears instead as redundancies in the ecology: memories copied into roots, walkers, larvae, mineral cups, and the chemistry of unborn bodies. It appears in the way every life carries more possibilities than it can use. It appears in the great stillness before the cliffs rise, when all organisms pause as if listening for assurance that what is about to be destroyed has entered something larger than destruction.

The plateau has devised ten thousand ways not to lose itself.

None are sufficient.

The power could preserve everything.

That, too, would be a catastrophe. Nothing would finish. No abandoned anatomy would dissolve. No rejected future would fade. Every death would remain present in the body of the world. Possibility would accumulate until movement became impossible.

So the power considers a gift that is not preservation.

It considers an archive, but archives fix what they hold.

It considers prophecy, but the eggs already know too much of the future.

It considers peace, but peace imposed upon hunger is only a slower death.

Finally, it finds an act small enough not to conquer the place.

It waits beside a sheltered pool.

The water is warm and perfectly clear. Three pale swimmers hang beneath its surface, surrounded by their faint alternatives. A membrane opens above them and catches a single drop of condensation. Far off, rain crosses a copper plain. Still farther away, a cliff is falling. Its sound will not arrive for eleven years.

The power places no object in the pool.

It merely declines to occupy one possibility.

Among all the futures branching from that hollow, there is one in which the power intervenes openly: the water brightens, the clouds part, the swimmers are lifted into safety, and every creature on the plateau turns toward a presence it cannot understand. The power closes that future to itself.

The unused possibility does not vanish.

It remains empty.

One swimmer notices.

Its halo trembles. Beside the ordinary motions—turn, dive, rise, remain—another motion appears. It leads nowhere that presently exists. It is not an escape into distance, nor into the future, nor into a neighboring law. It is simply an opening left by something strong enough to enter and gentle enough not to.

The swimmer chooses it.

For an instant, the animal is absent.

Then it returns to the same water.

Nothing about its body has changed. Yet along its gills lies a thread of color the plateau has never produced: not blue, though blue roots lean toward it; not silver, though the cliff grazers dim in answer; not heat, charge, moisture, mineral, or time.

The swimmer consumes the thread.

The power waits.

The swimmer circles once beneath the surface. One of its unrealized motions separates from the halo and continues without it, gliding through the pool like an empty reflection. Another swimmer enters that motion and emerges with a fragment of the first animal’s experience: not an image, not a warning, not a command, but the knowledge that a path may be offered without being required.

The gift begins there.

It passes through drinking mouths into internal reservoirs. It enters the feet of a young walker and rises into the garden on its back. Pollinators carry it to bladder fruits. Roots exchange it with a ravine. A ribbon creature receives it across three kilometers of foliage. When fog brings two ridges together, the gift crosses the temporary contact; when the ridges divide, it remains on both sides without forcing them to remain the same.

The plateau does what it has always done with a new condition.

It metabolizes it.

In the eastern shelves, where age becomes weight, the gift develops weightlessness only at the instant a creature can no longer bear itself. Not enough to make the old young, nor to prevent them from rooting, but enough that the final lowering need not be a fall.

Near the magnetic caverns, it appears as an asymmetry that does not need to be cut into the newborn. A third relation emerges between left and right. Parents pause over their bright offspring, confused by the harmless imbalance, and for the first time some acts of necessary mutilation become unnecessary.

In the blade-forests, divided animals discover that fullness in one portion can leave a passage toward hunger in another. They do not share food across impossible distance. They share the option of becoming two creatures completely. Separation ceases, sometimes, to be a wound that cannot close.

For the rain-followers, the gift becomes a dormant organ that belongs neither to host nor passenger. It matures only when a forecast written into the body proves false. A brood prepared for sulfur under clear skies does not die attempting to breathe a vanished season. The organ opens, takes no predetermined form, and lets the animal ask the present ground what it requires.

Among the flattened predators, the gift is stranger.

A predator finds the future wound of a grazer and settles beside it, as it has always done. Hunger tightens through the braided organs beneath its film. Yet when the promised attack fails to become inevitable, an opening appears—not a meal, not rescue, but release from the loop. The predator may abandon the evidence. The grazer’s wound may become only a scar from an unhappened thing.

Some predators refuse. They wait and starve as before.

The power does not revoke their refusal.

Mercy that cannot be refused is another local physics, and the plateau has enough of those.

Deep below, the gift moves more slowly.

It passes into the mineral being through the roots of an ancient walker. For centuries it remains a slight variation in the opening of stone valves. Then it reaches a pressure chamber where an upheaval is beginning.

The chamber compresses vapor.

Magnetic strata turn.

Glass belts advance over crystal rollers.

Across the surface, living vanes close. Grazers flatten their feeding fans. Swarms form dark rings around empty air. Dormant grains rotate in their cracks. Walkers lower their gardens and spread their pillar feet.

A province is about to rise.

Ordinarily, the deep life would enact the rising without knowing it as choice. Pressure would become rupture because this is what pressure does. The land would lift, fresh ecologies would pour from the wound, and everything buried above the chamber would enter heat, transformation, or death.

But now there is an unoccupied possibility inside the process.

For the first time, the plateau hesitates.

The hesitation lasts less than the interval between lightning and the cloud that will later produce it. No surface animal perceives it. No ridge trembles. No vent closes.

Yet within that immeasurable organism, redistribution becomes something more than thought.

It becomes consent.

The plateau considers—not in words, but in stresses moving through black stone—whether to rise here, whether to rise now, whether the forms waiting inside the rock are ready to inherit the opening.

It chooses yes.

The cliffs begin to rise.

Nothing is made safe.

Escarpments split. Heat-loving vegetation spills upward already mature. Buried animals pour from the wounds with mineral lungs expanding. A plain tips into a vertical wall. One migrating herd is divided. Several thousand rain-grains become anatomies unsuited to the air. Predators spread across newborn basalt. Walkers unfold from compressed gardens and step into unstable gravity.

But the event is no longer exactly the same.

At the edge of the rising province, where stone seals too quickly around a herd of burrowers, a route remains unoccupied. The animals feel it as softness where no softness should be. Most continue forward and are enclosed. A few turn. They emerge through the side of a cliff three hundred kilometers away.

Within the cliff, those who are trapped do not remain alive forever.

When movement becomes impossible and no future condition could free them, the opening comes once more. Some enter it. Their bodies harden into pale figures in the basalt, but the unfinished migration passes into the roots above. Months later, a mobile garden changes direction for no cause its nervous system knows and completes their journey.

Nothing is preserved whole.

Nothing is entirely wasted.

The gift spreads across the plateau, but never becomes universal. Some regions reject it. In the western escarpments, repetition would fix it dangerously in place, so it arrives differently each time: as a missing reflection, a root that loosens at the right moment, an extra depth in a shallow crack, a storm whose border curves around a nesting field.

In the central fracture, shadows begin sometimes to point not only toward moisture, but toward reprieve.

The great walkers carry the gift between ecologies. Because their memory is chemical, they cannot distinguish it from the taste of a distant rain. Yet their pace changes. They begin arriving at certain ravines just before disasters that have no ordinary signs. They cannot prevent the disasters. Instead they graft themselves to the roots and receive what would otherwise have no carrier: patterns of flowering, unfinished instincts, the remembered pressure of extinct feet, the bodily questions of species for which the ground gave no answer.

The walkers bear these things onward.

Not as specimens.

As openings.

Over ages, the plateau becomes no gentler to an observer. It remains black, immense, hot, and unstable. Storms still revise entire food webs. Hunger still loops backward through cause. Provinces rise and vanish. Animals still devour one another. Old walkers still lower themselves into fractures and become indistinguishable from terrain.

But now, when a thing reaches the absolute end of all the futures native to it, there may be one more.

Not always.

The power is wise enough not to make even mercy compulsory.

It remains attentive. It does not depart, though departure would be difficult to define. It asks for no worship. It leaves no monument. No species evolves an organ specifically to perceive it, because whenever such an organ begins to form, the power gently vacates the category it would detect.

Only the plateau, in its distributed and geological manner, slowly becomes aware that not every emptiness is absence.

Centuries pass.

A delayed rockfall rises through the layers.

It moves through mineral belts, old walker bones, flooded caverns, tangles of conductive root, dormant grains, and chambers where magnetic stone revolves without sound. As it travels, other lost impacts join it: the collapse of a glass cliff, the first footfall of a species now extinct, the cracking of an egg beneath a rain that fell upward, the settling of an ancient walker whose leaves all opened at once.

The sounds gather.

At last they enter a sheltered hollow.

The pool there shivers. Its surface tilts toward several horizons. Pale swimmers hang beneath it, surrounded by luminous possibilities. Small animals lift their feet from reflections that belong to other lives. A membrane opens above the water.

The long-delayed note emerges.

It is soft and colossal.

Before, such a note would have passed through the plateau and faded into the clouds.

This time, something receives it completely.

The wise power does not translate it. Translation would make it smaller.

It answers in the only way that will not impose a new law.

It listens so fully that the sound discovers it has been heard.

Across the plateau, every living vane opens.

The cliffs continue to rise.
