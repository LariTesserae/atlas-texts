### The One Who Waited to Be Asked

A lesser power would have brought rain.

It would have seen the dim fields, the folded vanes, the dry bodies moving under a copper sun, and mistaken absence for deprivation. It would have opened the upper air. It would have filled the old basins with blue water, laid snow upon the softened ridges, and called the transformation a blessing.

The foundation would have blistered from rim to rim.

Towers would have sealed their chambers and still drowned standing. Ionic channels would have burst. Root galleries would have filled with reactive steam. The black hide would have spent its final strength dividing hydrogen from oxygen while rivers—beautiful, shining, fatal—cut the first valleys the plateau had ever known.

A lesser power might have admired what survived.

The wise power did not bring rain.

Nor did it descend in brightness. Light was food here, and a radiant appearance would have been an act of grazing.

It did not speak into the minds of the inhabitants. Their minds did not have clean boundaries, and a voice placed inside one body might have entered a household, a road, a tower lineage, a herd, and eventually the entire resonant plateau. What seemed like speech could become architecture before anyone had decided whether it was true.

It did not touch the foundation.

It knew that touch was never only touch.

Instead, the power withdrew most of its attention.

Even benevolent attention can be crushing when it comes from something vast. To be fully seen by such a presence might have fixed every creature into a single interpretation. The power made itself smaller than its understanding. It looked away from some things so that they could remain uncertain. It allowed shadows to conceal what shadows had always concealed.

Then it listened.

It listened first through the stone beneath the living sheet, where the oldest vibrations had been pressed downward and blurred by ages of rebuilding. It learned the difference between the hammer of freight and the pulse of sap. It learned which tower notes were structural adjustments and which were civic questions. It learned that the pause after a child’s first rhythm was not emptiness but a place held open for weather.

It listened to the grazing files combing charge from the fields. It listened to parasites, maintenance animals, wall organs, sleeping tools and deep giants. It listened to the old mineral groves flashing identities that no mobile inhabitant claimed to remember.

It did not decide which of these were people.

That decision, it understood, was not required for courtesy.

Centuries passed.

The inhabitants noticed.

At first they noticed only a new regularity in accidents. An echo returning from the plateau rim arrived with one frequency missing. A tower calculation produced an arch that framed a patch of darkness darker than the surrounding dusk. Foil-winged flocks began circling an empty region of sky and then departing without feeding or mating.

A surveyor pressed questions into the foundation.

The answers came back unchanged except for a silence between them.

This was alarming.

Messages were supposed to change in transit. The city was supposed to add context. Herds, roots, weather and distance were supposed to soften certainty. Yet this silence remained exactly itself. It crossed roads without being carried by them. Towers could not delay it. Buried grazers passed through it without distortion.

The inhabitants tested it with dangerous precision.

They sounded the identity of a dead mediator whose characteristic tension had not been recovered for nine thousand years.

The silence did not imitate the dead.

They sounded a command for the foundation to grow toward it.

Nothing grew.

They introduced random footfalls, released burrowers, cracked vanes out of season and opened sealed chambers to confuse the calculation.

The silence remained, but it did not spread.

At last the plateau gathered itself.

Towers rotated their crowns. Bridges drew tight. Deep animals were lured away from vulnerable galleries. Every road flattened. Mobile inhabitants braced in plazas and open fields, while those embedded in walls altered their load-bearing tensions by minute degrees. At the rim, the root curtains ceased striking the cliff and hung against the abyss.

The city began a chord.

It started so low that the mountain beneath the plateau answered. It rose through foundation, tower and atmospheric dust. It passed through the bodies of every inhabitant, gathering variations from each. Children gave incomplete rhythms. Herds supplied noise. Old groves contributed names. Edge-dwellers added pauses learned below the cloud.

For eleven days, the High Dry Garden sounded a single question.

The question had no close translation.

It meant:

**Are you a thing that intends to continue?**

It also meant:

**What will your attention make of us?**

And beneath both meanings:

**Can you refrain?**

The power answered in the manner of the plateau.

It did not impose a voice upon the chord. It entered through the missing frequency and allowed the city to shape it. Its answer crossed the foundation and was altered by everything it touched. Towers added caution. Roads added direction. Animals added hunger. The old groves made it resemble grief.

By the time the answer reached the far rim, it meant:

**I can leave.**

The whole plateau contracted.

Dust rose in geometric sheets. Several unused bridges tore themselves apart. A child near an old grove sounded the first half of a rhythm and waited for the city to complete it.

The city did not tell the power to leave.

Instead it asked another question.

This one took nine years.

During those years, a new tower rose and dismantled itself three times. A deep grazer reversed a migration that had persisted for eighty generations. Artists cultivated a road that carried travelers almost to their destinations and then gently returned them to where they had begun. The foundation tested thousands of possible forms for the question and digested each as too narrow.

At last a black arch grew on the open plain.

It supported nothing and framed no road. On one side, it opened toward the plateau. On the other, it opened toward the same plateau, but the copper sun could not be seen through it.

One inhabitant approached.

The traveler was an edge-dweller, compact and heavily plated. Glassy cysts clung to its lower cage, each containing a trace of water gathered below the clouds. Inside its torso lay a soft translucent growth isolated behind amber mineral.

Its identity rhythm required three companions, a particular tower, and the passing of a seasonal herd. None were present. The traveler sounded only the portions it could carry alone.

Then it passed through the arch.

For the first time, the power allowed itself to be somewhere.

It took no grand form. It became a tension in the arch, no stronger than the force of one resting limb. It limited itself to the moment. It permitted the traveler to feel only what could be endured and to misunderstand whatever could not.

The traveler lifted one walking spar.

A pale charge moved through the dust around its crown.

In temporary spoken language, it asked:

“Will you make us better?”

The power considered the word. Better could mean more stable, more unified, more efficient, more enduring. Each was dangerous. A perfectly stable garden would be dead. A perfectly unified city would contain no negotiation. Perfect efficiency would abolish art. Endless endurance would turn care into imprisonment.

“No,” the power said.

The traveler’s sensory ribbons loosened.

“Will you preserve us?”

“Not unchanged.”

“Will you prevent our ending?”

“Not every ending.”

The traveler placed four limbs against the arch and listened through it.

“What will you do?”

The power showed it something approaching from beyond the small copper sun.

It was a dark body wrapped in ancient ice, still thousands of years away. Its path was almost parallel to the world’s orbit. In time, a minute gravitational encounter would alter that path. The comet would descend upon the plateau.

It held enough water to poison the High Dry Garden to its deepest roots.

The traveler did not recoil. Alarm passed through its body as a series of precise structural changes. It pressed the knowledge into the arch, and the arch transmitted it into the foundation.

The city began calculating at once.

For three hundred years the plateau played variations on catastrophe.

Towers screamed until their crowns fractured. Roads knotted themselves. Climate boundaries rose and were dismantled. The foundation grew experimental blisters kilometers wide and filled them with manufactured frost. Edge-dwellers carried samples down through the cloud and returned with altered minerals. Children were assembled with speculative tissues, some capable of enduring water for several breaths.

The power did not solve the problem for them.

It did not conceal possible answers, either.

When asked, it revealed the comet’s mass. When asked again, its spin. Later, the weaknesses in its core. It answered exactly enough to permit choice and no more. It refused to turn warning into command.

At last the city reached a decision.

The final chord began in an old grove rather than a tower. It passed through inhabitants, sleeping chambers, animals and roots. It accepted distortion. By the time it reached the arch, it had become a request.

Not for the comet to be destroyed.

Not for it to miss entirely.

The Garden asked that the body be moved by a little: less than the width of a metallic hair measured across the distance it still had to travel.

Enough that the heart of the comet would pass beyond the world.

Enough that its outer ice would graze the upper atmosphere.

There would be frost. There would be poisoning. Some fields would blister; some root districts would die. The foundation would be forced to change. But the event would be survivable, and the plateau would receive substances it had never possessed in abundance. The soft growths carried by the edge-dwellers might find a place. New negotiations would become necessary.

The power asked whether the request belonged only to the mobile inhabitants.

The Garden sounded again.

This time the towers withdrew from the chord. The foundation answered through its fields. Herds crossed the message and altered it. Burrowers beat against chamber walls. One of the deep giants rolled beneath a plaza, lifting it three meters into the air.

The old grove supplied the ending.

The request remained.

So the power reached across the dark.

It did not seize the comet. It touched a smaller stone that would, centuries later, pass near it. The stone shifted by an immeasurable degree. Nothing shone. No law was broken. No creature watching the sky could have pointed to the moment and called it a miracle.

Thousands of years afterward, the comet came.

The upper darkness turned white.

Frost fell for forty days.

The whole plateau smoked.

Fields sealed themselves beneath glass. Towers folded tight. Animals crowded into warm groves. Roads carried inhabitants without rest while root vessels moved active salts away from poisoned districts. The city sounded continuously, not as a single calculation but as millions of local acts of care.

Some structures died.

Some persons did too.

The power did not take their deaths away from them. It did not recover every lost tension or make copies from remembered patterns. It remained near, holding back only the portion of the disaster that had not been chosen.

The comet’s heart passed across the sky as a vast pale arc and vanished beyond the rim.

When the frost ended, the copper sun returned.

For generations, the plateau bore silver scars. New organisms appeared along the borders of the blisters: translucent veils that used water without releasing it, tended jointly by edge-dwellers and tower chambers. Some were poisonous. Some were beautiful. Some became infrastructure despite every attempt to preserve their uselessness.

The Garden recalculated itself.

It did not become a paradise. The wise power had understood that it already was neither paradise nor failure. It was a living country, and living countries could not be perfected without being ended.

The power stayed outside the arch.

It did not rule. It did not accept worship. When the inhabitants tried to cultivate a tower in its likeness, the tower grew for a century, developed an appetite for reverence, and was carefully dismantled. The power offered no objection.

Sometimes it was asked for knowledge.

Sometimes it answered.

Sometimes it said that an answer would close possibilities the question had not yet discovered.

Sometimes it admitted ignorance.

This admission became one of the most valued messages ever carried by the foundation. It traveled badly and changed beautifully. In one district it became a method of architecture. In another it became a children’s rhythm. Near the rim, it caused a road to divide into seven branches, none of which reached a destination.

Most of the time, the power did nothing.

But its inaction was no longer absence.

It was a promise held beyond the plateau: that the Garden would not be simplified for the convenience of being saved; that it would not be frozen into one beloved shape; that no disaster arriving wholly from beyond its power to choose would erase it without resistance; and that before help came, help would listen.

A new phrase entered the long rhythms of the inhabitants.

It was sounded by lifting one limb and holding it above the foundation, making no contact, transmitting nothing.

The gesture meant:

**There is something near that does not need to possess us.**

It also meant:

**We are allowed to remain difficult.**
