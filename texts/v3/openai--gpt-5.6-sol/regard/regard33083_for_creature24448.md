### The Fourth Warning

The power did not enter through the summit slit.

That was what later accounts agreed upon, though they agreed on little else. It did not descend from the stars, cross the mineral plain, or arrive as a brightness beyond the mountain. No sail-skate folded its membranes before it. No cable moth turned its wings toward an approaching shape.

It had no need to come from anywhere.

Its attention fell upon the Needle Ward, and for a long while attention was all it gave.

It watched the city flex around the fault. It learned the intervals of the lifts and the private rhythms of fastenings. It followed children along training rails, medics through evacuated workshops, seam-readers over stone still hot from fracture. It watched a catch crew lose a bridge and save all six workers hanging beneath it. It watched an old route-keeper stop halfway home because home had moved, then sit against a warm duct and laugh until someone came to redirect him.

It watched the dead become green hexagons.

It watched the outer wall receive the names of those who fell.

The power was benevolent, but it was also wise, and so it did not immediately repair the mountain.

To make the fault still would have been simple.

It could have fused the youngest fracture from summit to root. It could have leveled every floor, sealed every gas seam, thickened the air and filled the tension wells with harmless stone. It could have fixed the bridges in place forever. It could have given each fastening a room that would never move and each child a path home that remained where memory left it.

But such a gift would have destroyed almost everything it meant to save.

The foundational films fed on fresh mineral differences. The mites followed charge and warmth. Sail-skates required the vertical currents. The factories followed the ore. The law, music, humor and kinship of the Warders had all grown around motion—not merely in resistance to it, but in conversation with it.

A mountain that could no longer move would become, in time, a tomb.

So the power listened.

This was not easy even for a great power. The Ward spoke in many substances, and every substance changed the meaning. It spoke in iron taps and basalt groans, in cable strain, in the emptying of animal hooks, in a child’s grip tightening before the floor tilted. It spoke through warning lights, through chemical films darkening on old skin, through the unplanned music of occupied bridges.

The power learned all of these.

Then it made itself small enough to answer.

During a stable interval, a musician named Saret-of-Five-Lifts fastened resonators to a freight gantry in the middle districts. Saret was not famous. Two of her instruments were cracked, and one had been made from a pressure vessel whose stamped warning labels had not been removed. The audience consisted of her fastening, three tired anchor workers, several children pretending to sleep, a medic, and a needle hound that had chosen no one and therefore attended everyone.

Saret began with a familiar load-song.

Halfway through, the bridge replied.

Not with an echo.

With a rhythm.

Every listener raised their head. The anchor workers put their limbs flat against the deck. The needle hound stood and turned first toward one wall, then the other.

Again the rhythm passed through the gantry.

It was not a warning code. It was not a name. It was too slow for machinery and too deliberate for the mountain.

On iron, it meant:

**May I hold here?**

No Warder had ever heard a question asked by the structure beneath them.

Saret placed her jaw against the floor.

After a long time, she tapped back:

**For now.**

That answer pleased the power more than worship would have.

It entered the Ward no further than permission allowed.

At first it inhabited only that gantry. Then, as word spread and workers came to test the impossible rhythm, it was invited into an adjoining rail, a brace, a lift guide, a warm conduit. The invitations were cautious. Seam-readers inspected every surface it touched. Culture strips were applied. Animals were brought near. One district sealed itself for three working intervals and threatened to cut loose any bridge that carried the rhythm toward them.

The power respected this.

It did not pass where it had not been admitted.

The Warders asked what it was.

It tried many answers.

Through iron, it called itself **strength that does not require fastening**.

Through basalt, **that which remains without refusing change**.

Through a living body, after receiving explicit permission from an elderly seam-reader, it called itself **one who has heard you**.

The elderly seam-reader withdrew her jaw from the rail and said this was an answer to a different question.

The power considered and agreed.

News of it climbed and descended through the city. Some believed it was an electrical colony spread invisibly through the metal. Some thought the mountain had developed thought in the same way warm cabinets developed films. Some suspected sabotage by foundry owners. Children sent it joke rhythms and demanded that it guess which one of them had tapped. It always guessed incorrectly until they accused it of doing so on purpose.

Needle hounds accepted it before the Warders did. They slept on the rails it inhabited and woke without alarm when its attention passed under them.

The first thing the Warders asked of the power was whether it could stop the fault.

It answered yes.

This caused a silence that traveled across eleven districts.

Then the Warders asked whether it would.

The power answered with a question:

**When a bridge strains, do you preserve it by welding it to both walls?**

No one liked this response.

There followed arguments lasting thirty-seven working intervals.

Foundry coordinators wanted stability. Creche keepers wanted it more. Anchor crews, to general surprise, were divided. Some had lost too many limbs and companions to treat danger as sacred. Others warned that a sealed fault would alter gases, heat and ore pressure throughout the mountain. Film cultivators said their oldest strains would die without fresh fracture surfaces. Animal keepers predicted the loss of sail-skates within a generation. Route-law specialists produced so many objections that the power briefly withdrew from their courtroom rail.

One young worker tapped:

**If you are wise, why ask us?**

The answer came:

**Because wisdom concerning you cannot exist without you.**

Eventually they asked if it could make the mountain safer without making it still.

The power said that it could reduce the violence of the greatest shifts. It could turn certain hidden fractures away from occupied districts. It could blunt sudden closures and lengthen the first moments of catastrophic shears.

But nothing done deep within a mountain was without consequence. Pressure spared in one place would travel elsewhere. A crack diverted from the Ward might surface on the plain below in a hundred years, or wake an older fault beneath a distant peak. The power could foresee much, but not everything. Benevolence did not make consequence disappear.

The Warders understood this better than most beings would have.

They did not ask for perfect safety again.

Instead they asked for time.

Not endless time. Not even much.

The instant between danger becoming certain and danger arriving: could that be made longer?

The power turned its attention through the mountain.

It touched compression fronts descending toward the buried roots. It traced electrical changes along crystal seams finer than skin films. It felt deep stone preparing motions that would not reach the Ward for days, and other motions born so near that no instrument could distinguish warning from event.

It found that the animals sensed only the last convergence—the point when many faint possibilities became one approaching fact.

The power could sense earlier.

And it could speak.

Together, they devised the Fourth Warning.

The first warning remained the animals’ gathering at the summit slit.

The second was their inward flight.

The third was the mountain’s three successive tremors, after which lifts halted, furnaces sealed and every inhabitant took a red rail.

The Fourth Warning came before all of these, though it was numbered after them because it had been added later and the Warders saw no reason to reorder a working system for elegance.

It was a rhythm transmitted through every admitted structure in the city.

Not loud. Not urgent.

Four pulses, spaced far apart.

The first meant: **A harmful motion has become likely.**

The second carried direction. Its character changed in iron, ceramic, glass and stone, allowing seam-readers to estimate where the strain would emerge.

The third carried uncertainty. Sometimes it was sharp, meaning the power was nearly sure. Sometimes it blurred across the structures, meaning many futures remained possible.

The last pulse meant only:

**Begin.**

The Fourth Warning did not tell the Warders what to do. It did not issue orders, identify leaders or select what should be saved. It gave them time, and they filled that time with themselves.

Creches were moved before bridges twisted. Hot factories were cooled gradually rather than sealed in crisis. Fastenings divided their chests and staged them near alternate routes. Anchor crews installed release joints where the power indicated converging strain. Animal colonies were guided from districts that would otherwise have trapped them.

Sometimes the warning arrived many working intervals before a shift. Sometimes only minutes.

Once it sounded and nothing happened.

For several days the city waited in exhausted suspicion. Critics accused the power of error or manipulation. Then the warning rhythm returned through the court rail, followed by an unfamiliar tapping that route-keepers eventually translated as:

**The mountain changed its intention.**

This explanation caused such outrage that the power learned its first Ward joke.

After that, false warnings were said to indicate that the mountain had been caught behaving responsibly.

The power did more, but never all at once.

At the request of the medics, it learned to perceive the failing pulses of injured bodies through red rails. It could not heal them without changing their nature, and it would not alter a living being without consent. But it could direct medics through white flashes and tell them which curled, motionless figure still lived after an air evacuation.

At the request of animal keepers, it protected no individual species from hunger or predation. Instead, it marked population collapses before they became irreversible. The Warders then chose whether and how to act.

At the request of the creche keepers, it refused to prevent training floors from tilting.

It did, however, catch one child.

This occurred during a minor shift in a lower counterweight housing. A release pin sheared unexpectedly. The training floor dropped, the children anchored, and one newly plated child chose a colored hold mounted on the moving section rather than the fixed wall.

The child fell through a maintenance gap.

There was no cable beneath, no ledge, no worker near enough.

The power took hold.

Witnesses disagreed about what they saw. Some said the child stopped in empty air. Others said a black line appeared around one hooked digit. One creche keeper insisted that the surrounding structures bent toward the falling body, though later measurements showed no deformation.

The child hung for three breaths and was retrieved.

Afterward, the power withdrew from every rail in the creche.

When asked why, it replied:

**I acted without being asked.**

The keepers considered this.

Then the child, who had learned only twelve clear rhythms, tapped on the floor:

**Again if needed.**

Permission to catch falling children became the shortest law in the Ward.

It consisted of a single mark.

Years passed.

The Ward changed shape many times. Districts migrated. New furnaces opened along a seam rich in conductive ore. A violet film spread through three thermal districts and made the oldest skin colonies glitter in darkness. Saret-of-Five-Lifts became a respected musician, then lost jaw-sense with age and composed entirely through her limbs.

The power remained.

It was never given a temple. No fastening claimed it. No image was made, since any image would imply a shape, and it had shown none.

Instead, small unconnected lengths of rail began appearing in occupied rooms. They were bolted to nothing. Their ends were capped in red ceramic. Family members laid their jaws or hands against them when they wished to speak privately to the power.

Often it did not answer.

This, too, was accepted.

A benevolent power was not therefore a tool, just as a needle hound was not therefore trainable.

The most difficult request came from those whose people had fallen outside.

Could the power find them?

Yes.

The answer moved softly through the outer wall.

Could it bring them back?

In some cases, no. In others, it could gather what remained, but not restore life without making a copy from memory and matter. That copy might speak with the same tapping-name, remember the same warm walls and reach instinctively for the same hooks. Yet the power could not promise that the one who had fallen would return rather than a perfect answer to grief.

The Warders debated this longer than they had debated the mountain.

At last, they declined.

Death was a failure of attachment, but love was not the refusal to admit that something had come loose.

They asked for something else.

When a person fell, could the power accompany them until the end?

Yes.

Could it return with the last rhythm they chose to make?

Yes.

Thereafter, those who fell beyond recovery were no longer always lost in silence.

The power went with them into the distance. It did not halt the fall unless they asked—and some did ask, while there was still time, and were lifted home. But others were too injured, or chose not to return in a body already ending.

For those, the power remained near.

It received taps made against harness buckles, chest plates, tool handles, or the hard surfaces of the self. It carried those rhythms back through the outermost wall.

Some were names.

Some were instructions about culture plates or tool chests.

Some were apologies.

One was an extremely detailed complaint about a defective anchor socket.

Many were only:

**Here.**

And the wall answered:

**For now.**

In time, the Needle Ward became safer, but it never became safe.

The wise power would not make safety by removing life’s conditions, and the Warders would not surrender their judgment in exchange for protection. Bridges still failed. Wire-borers still blacked out districts. Anchor crews still returned with broken plates and missing digits. The dead still entered warm niches, and copper-green hexagons still spread across them.

But no district vanished without warning.

No child fell beyond the power’s reach.

No dying Warder outside the mountain belonged only to the distance.

Then came the greatest movement in the Ward’s known history.

The Fourth Warning sounded twelve working intervals before the animals gathered.

Its first pulse cracked minor glass throughout the city.

Its second passed downward when it should have passed laterally, indicating a motion beneath the entire fault pocket.

Its third did not sharpen or blur. It divided into so many overlapping uncertainties that listeners collapsed away from the rails in pain.

The fourth pulse came as words in every substance the power had learned:

**You must leave.**

The Warders did not waste time asking whether it was sure.

Factories cooled. Culture plates were sealed. Salt cases were moved into insulated chests. Rivetbacks were lured from turbine collars. Cable moth cages were opened. Lift counterweights were converted into cargo sledges. Every available line, hook and breathing hood was inspected.

But there was nowhere to go.

The summit slit was too narrow for evacuation. The exterior slopes offered no roads, and the thin upper atmosphere could not support sail-skates carrying loads. The lower fault had no outlet. The Ward was a pocket in stone, and the motion approaching from below would close that pocket like a hand.

The Warders asked the power if it could stop the shift.

It could.

The force required would break the mountain’s roots and transfer the motion across the mineral plain. Distant faults would wake. Places unknown to the Ward might be destroyed.

They did not ask it to do so.

Could it open a path?

Yes.

But not without splitting the summit.

The Needle Ward as they knew it would end. The fault pocket would cease to be hidden. Much of the chamber would lose pressure and heat. The existing city could not remain.

The decision traveled by rail to every fastening.

The answer returned before the animals fled the slit.

**Open it.**

The power entered the mountain fully for the first and only time.

Every light in the Ward went dark.

In darkness, the people felt an attention larger than the summit settle through the stone. It did not feel like a hand. Hands grip from outside. This moved among the smallest boundaries of the rock, between crystal faces, through dormant seams and compressed grains.

The mountain gave a single immense shudder.

A line appeared beside the summit slit.

It traveled downward, not as a violent crack but as a deliberate separation. Basalt drew away from basalt. Glass shelves divided without shattering. Platforms released their joints one after another. The Warders clung to red rails as a wall of the city opened onto the cold sky.

Beyond it, where there had been only empty air, hung a road.

It was not supported from below. It stretched from the parted summit toward one of the lower peaks, a black, narrow lattice curved gently through the upper atmosphere. Its surface flexed. Rails rose on six sides so that no change in orientation could make it cease to be a floor. Warm conduits ran through its spine. At intervals stood pressure shelters and anchor sockets.

The power had not made it level.

This detail was discussed with approval.

The evacuation began.

Children went first, then salt cases, culture panels, medics, the elderly, animal colonies and tool chests. Sail-skates launched into the new current between peaks. Cable moths tiled the road’s warm underside. Rivetbacks had to be carried until one discovered the conduit housings, after which the entire population began moving in an orderly procession.

Last came the anchor crews and seam-readers, cutting loose what could not be saved.

The power held the summit apart.

Not effortlessly.

For the first time, those touching the rails felt strain in it. The rhythm beneath their limbs became uneven. Somewhere far below, the great motion had begun. The mountain pressed inward against the separation.

A final anchor worker stood at the threshold with Saret, now very old, fastened to his back. She carried one cracked resonator.

She asked the power:

**Will you come?**

The answer took a long time.

**I am holding the way.**

Saret understood.

She fixed her resonator to the outermost red rail and struck it once.

The note passed into the Ward, through every emptied bridge, house, furnace, creche and tension well. It entered the young fracture and the old stone behind it. It touched the warm niches of the dead. It traveled down into the pressure that would soon erase the chamber’s shape.

The city answered in thousands of tones.

Not a lament.

A load-song.

The power incorporated the coming crack before the engineers could interrupt.

Then the last Warders crossed.

Behind them, the summit closed.

The Needle Ward did not collapse all at once. For a few moments its many structures remained in darkness, unoccupied but still under strain. Then the lower wall advanced. Platforms folded. Bridges swung loose into the central shaft. Factories leaned together. Warm conduits ruptured in silent clouds. The summit slit vanished.

The mountain acquired a new shape.

Across the gap, on the lower peak, the Warders felt the ending through the road.

They tapped the name of the city into its rails.

On iron:

**held under strain.**

On basalt:

**the place that has not yet come apart.**

Through one another’s living bodies:

**here, for now.**

The power returned along the road after the mountain settled.

It found the Warders already at work.

They had discovered a fresh fracture in the lower peak: broad enough for shelter, deep enough for heat, threaded with conductive ore and inhabited by unfamiliar soot-blue films. Anchor crews were testing the walls. Children had marked dangerous ledges. Rivetbacks slept around a bearing unloaded less than an hour before. Route-keepers argued over lift intervals for lifts that did not yet exist.

No one asked the power to rebuild the old city.

Instead, they asked whether the new fracture would hold.

The power answered truthfully:

**Not forever.**

The Warders touched the rails, considering.

Then someone tapped:

**Good enough to begin.**
