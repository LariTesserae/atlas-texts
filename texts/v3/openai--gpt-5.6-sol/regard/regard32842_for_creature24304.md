The power does not enter as light.

Light, here, is not innocent. Too much would bleach the pools, dry the soft-bodied things in their seams, wake green growth where no grazer could contain it, and send the bats beating blindly through corridors they have crossed without error for generations. Nor does the power enter as a voice. A voice large enough to address the buried country would become thunder under that roof.

It attends first.

It follows the bats through passages too narrow for weather. It waits beside the fox until the fox distinguishes one returning echo from another. It feels the minute pressure changes read by the blind fish and the dissolved trace that brings shrimp from beneath the gravel. It learns the deer’s sequence of dependable places, the pig’s map of workable soil, the crow’s brief possession of a sharpened stem.

It attends for longer than any life there, and discovers that the country is not unhappy.

Fear occurs. Hunger occurs. Flesh is torn, young fail, parasites feed, and wounds close badly. But no single grief belongs to the whole. The frog does not mourn the condition of frogs. The beetle does not ask why beetles must eat the fallen. The bat falling from the roof knows terror for the length of its fall, not the metaphysics of recurrence.

The wrongness, if there is one, lies elsewhere.

Nothing can add itself to what came before.

A choice may be learned, but not retained beyond the narrow passage of imitation. A pool may be stirred, but its gravel settles into the same arrangement. A trail may deepen, but never enough to become a new road or fail as an old one. The stone-bearer may choose one stone a thousand times across a lineage, yet the choice cannot gather consequence. Every creature affects the country, but the country receives no lasting mark.

The power understands that permanence is not the same as mercy.

It could preserve every animal. That would end feeding.

It could abolish predation. That would starve the predators first and then overwhelm everything else.

It could enlarge the openings, bring forests down into the hollow, and make the buried country abundant in a new way. But abundance would be invasion. The pale fish would disappear beneath sighted fish. The slow salamanders would be eaten. The guano fields would become soil, the moss islands thickets, and the old dark would survive only in shrinking recesses.

It could raise minds among the crows or stone-bearers until they remembered themselves in objects. But thought given too quickly is another kind of collapse. A creature should not awaken to history in a world where every material condition forbids history from proceeding.

So the power does none of these things.

Instead, it places one hand—if hand is the word—against the limestone plain above, and the other beneath the lowest hidden waters.

It does not break the balance.

It releases it.

The first change is too small for any animal to notice. One drop falls a fraction sooner than it would have. It strikes the rim of a basin instead of the center. A grain of calcite remains where formerly accumulation and dissolution had canceled one another exactly.

The grain is no larger than the dust on a bat’s muzzle.

It remains.

Elsewhere, condensation travels the width of a claw farther down a slab. A root at the base of a fern finds a fissure that was dry before. One pool rises by the thickness of a beetle’s wing; another falls by the same amount. A loose stone shifts after a pig passes and comes to rest in a position no hoof has encountered.

Nothing is ruined.

But the next event no longer restores the first.

The power gives the country no commandment and no destination. It gives it asymmetry. A little excess here. A little loss there. The permission for causes to have consequences.

Years begin—not the false years of births replacing deaths while the total remains unchanged, but years that differ.

In one of them, dripping increases along a bright fracture. Moss crosses a boundary it has held for longer than memory. Deer browse the new edge. Because they linger, a fox alters its waiting place. Because the fox waits elsewhere, pigs use an abandoned pass. Their rooting exposes a pocket of old silt in which seeds have lain without opportunity.

Most do not germinate.

Three do.

One is eaten as soon as it opens. One grows pale and fails. The third puts out two leaves unlike any leaves then living below.

The change proceeds with no preference for grandeur.

A beetle enters a chamber where its kind has not been found. A salamander population becomes divided when mineral deposits close a submerged crack. At one pool, the stone-bearers begin leaving favored hammers on a particular ledge because a slight lowering of the water has made the ledge convenient. Cubs find the stones there. The stones become polished at their gripping points.

No one calls this inheritance.

It is inheritance nevertheless.

A crow wedges a tool into a crevice and cannot retrieve it. The twig dries there instead of rotting. The next season, another crow pulls it free and uses it. The act is not understood as an act received from the dead. No reverence accompanies it. Yet for the first time, a useful shape passes from one life into another without being remade.

The power does not cause the crow to wonder.

Wonder will come, or not, by its own road.

There are losses.

Wisdom does not disguise this from itself.

A shallow pool disappears after many years, and the shrimp within it die as the last water retreats below the gravel. A colony of bats abandons a vault when a seam opens and cold air begins to descend. One fox lineage fails. A fungus reaches a guano field and changes the insects that can live there. The narrowest of the old moss islands is eaten down and does not return.

But elsewhere water gathers. The bats find another dome. The empty territory alters the movements of neighboring foxes. The fungus itself becomes food. Nothing compensates perfectly, because perfect compensation was the old imprisonment.

The power guards only against changes so sudden that no life can answer them.

When a roof plate begins at last to separate, it does so slowly. Cracks announce it through unfamiliar sounds. Dust falls. Bats move their pups. Deer avoid the chamber. The plate descends one winter in a violence that shakes water from every ceiling in the country, but it falls after the living have been given time to read the warning.

Its ruin blocks three passages and opens five.

Through one comes air.

Through another comes a thread of direct sun.

For several mornings each year, a narrow beam reaches the floor and moves visibly across it. It is not a stationary grayness but a bright line that touches one wall, traverses a mound of fallen stone, and disappears into a crack. Animals gather near it at first in alarm. Then insects begin to use its warmth. A lizard from above descends along the heated rock. Fern spores wake in places that had never been green.

The beam does not remain in the same place from season to season.

By this, without understanding it, the inhabitants encounter a larger rhythm.

The frogs call earlier in one chamber. Bats depart along altered routes. Shrubs turn new leaves not only toward light, but toward where light will be. The deer’s reliable sequence becomes less reliable, then more complex. Fawns must learn rather than merely follow.

Some fail to learn.

Others discover.

The country’s great voice changes. There are still wings above, insects below, frogs at the margins and drops sounding against stone. But now the proportions vary. One year the frogs are louder. Another year moths enter in such numbers that bats gather from distant vaults. Sometimes a corridor falls nearly silent, and silence becomes not the absence of the country but one of its events.

The power does not stay to be thanked.

Gratitude would require the creatures to understand what had been withheld and what restored. None of them can. Nor is gratitude the purpose.

Before withdrawing, the power makes no immortal species, no chosen people, no hidden ruler. It leaves suffering possible because life remains possible. It leaves extinction possible because arrival has become possible too. It makes no promise that the buried country will endure forever.

It grants something smaller than eternity and kinder.

Afterward, an old stone-bearer dies beside a pool. Beetles take the eyes and tongue. Scavengers open the belly. Bones are scattered. In time almost nothing of the animal remains.

But the hammerstone it used lies on the ledge, worn smooth where its paws held it.

A young animal finds the stone.

It turns it once, testing the weight, and carries it to the water.

Far above, rain begins. Far below, not yet and not in equal measure, the pools will answer.

The country still preserves motions.

Now it can also change them.
