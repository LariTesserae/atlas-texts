The power would not mistake the Rain House for a broken thing.

It would not see the damp and wish it dry, nor hear the noise and wish it silent. It would not straighten the leaning passages, scour the mineral skins from the gutters, drive out the bats, seal the rats away, or replace the old gates with doors no shoulder need push. It would understand that perfection, imposed suddenly, would be another kind of collapse.

Nor would it choose a ruler.

Looking for the being that had drawn its attention, it would find no single face. It would find the old woman testing plaster with her cheek, the child smelling rain in a draft, the pigeons startled awake inside the walls, the frog abandoning a poisoned bowl, the dog unwilling to cross a floor that seemed sound. It would find brick, lime, dung, memory, appetite, fear, custom, and repair fitted together so densely that none could be saved alone.

The being was the keeping.

So the power would begin by learning its rules.

For one full turning of the seasons, it would do nothing that could be distinguished from attention. It would pass through the chamber without taking a body. It would listen from the hollow pottery set into walls. It would travel in the cold thread beneath a door, in the tremor of taut ropes, in the black water of the first rain. It would learn which cracks were merely old mouths and which were beginning to speak of failure. It would learn the difference between a child’s cry in play and the same cry with water behind it.

It would discover that the Rain House did not suffer most from labor. Labor gave it continuity, skill, companionship, and pride. Nor did it suffer most from age. Age had made its stairs fit human feet and its walls rich with evidence.

What it suffered from was the narrowness of mercy.

Too often, the difference between warning and disaster was only a breath. A rope parted one pull before someone noticed the fraying. A gate jammed during the hardest rush. A child smelled bitterness in a tank after three families had drunk. A wall gave its first unmistakable sound at the same moment it fell.

The power would not promise that nothing bad could happen. Such a promise would hollow out judgment and turn knowledge into ceremony. Instead, it would give the Rain House an interval.

Thereafter, dangerous water would announce itself a little sooner. Before a fouled cistern changed taste, its surface would lose the reflections of lamps and hold them as dull red discs. A load-bearing wall nearing failure would shed three small flakes of lime on three successive nights, however still the air. A rope about to part inside a shaft would begin to hum when wet. Rats would leave a gallery before bad air gathered there. Frogs would turn pale-bellied in poisoned drains.

No sign would be infallible alone. The power had seen how knowledge lived here: distributed, argued over, confirmed by many forms of life. It would not replace that wisdom with commandments. It would only make catastrophe less secretive.

The first to notice would be a child named Sennet, who slept behind the wall of the southern jar court.

On three nights she would wake to a sound like someone rubbing a wet thumb around the rim of a bowl. Adults would tell her it was pigeons shifting in their cells, then a rope twisting in the ventilation shaft, then a dream caught in the speaking tubes.

On the fourth night, the tortoises would all leave the refuse court.

They would move in a slow, unbroken procession up two stairs, through the herb gallery, and into a plaster cutter’s sleeping enclosure, where they would arrange themselves beneath the mats. This would provoke annoyance, laughter, and finally unease. The dogs would not enter the refuse court to retrieve them.

Sennet would remember the humming.

An old gate keeper would press a peg to the floor. A pigeon tender would report that the lowest row of birds had refused their cells. Someone would accuse someone else of widening a doorway. Someone else would insist the doorway had been widened twenty years before. They would argue until the oldest plaster reader silenced them by laying her palm on the brick.

Behind it, she would say, water was climbing where no water ought to be.

They would clear the court.

The floor would rise before dawn—not violently, but with the dreadful patience of bread swelling under cloth. Bricks would separate. Black water would breathe through the joints. Had the floor broken suddenly, it would have taken sleepers, tortoises, dogs, and a whole gallery of pigeon cells into the chamber beneath.

Instead there would be time.

Time for baskets. Time for ropes. Time to move the animals and brace the arch. Time to send children away and call the gate crews. Time to remove the old bricks one by one rather than have them thrown upward by trapped water.

Under the floor they would find a buried cistern, larger than any still in use. Its roof had sagged, choking an ancient overflow channel. The water trapped there had been pressing upward for years.

On the inner wall of the cistern, protected by darkness and mineral crust, they would find a painted line of blue fish.

Each fish would face the same direction.

The people would follow them.

For eleven days they would cut mud and calcite from the old overflow. They would work by lamp flame, passing baskets backward through the opened floor. At first the passage would seem to terminate in a brick wall. But the wall would sound hollow, and from one corner would come a breath smelling not of animals, wet lime, or old chambers, but of sun-warmed dust.

No one would break through casually.

They would measure. They would consult those who remembered old configurations. They would search for what must be closed if this opening were made. Some would say the ancient builders had already balanced the opening, and no closure was required. Others would say the rule admitted no exceptions, not even for the dead.

At last Sennet would ask why they could not seal the descending passage whose water had long been warm, metallic, and unusable.

The suggestion would be opposed because the passage was ancient. It would be supported for the same reason. Three families would claim storage niches along it. A snake catcher would insist it was the best ratting corridor in the lower House. Compensation would be calculated in ledge widths, labor mornings, pigeon cells, and jars.

Then they would close it.

Into the mortar they would place no grand offering: only a frayed length of rope, a tortoise tag whose number could no longer be read, three pigeon feathers, and the blue bead Sennet had worn since infancy. No one would admit to adding the frog bone.

When the last brick was seated, they would open the wall beyond the fish.

Air would enter first.

It would come down the ancient overflow with enough force to extinguish three lamps and lift the hair from every uncovered head. It would smell of hot stone, rainless weeds, distant cook fires, and something broad that had no name below.

Beyond the wall would be a steep shaft filled halfway with rubble. It would take another season to clear. The work would reveal handholds, plastered runnels, and copper-green sockets where mirrors had once been fixed. At its top they would uncover a narrow opening on the side of a hill beyond the vanished city.

It would not be large. Two people could not pass through together. A basket of goats would be impossible. Rain entering it would have to be caught and diverted. Swallows would claim its upper corners within a week.

But from the highest step, one could see the horizon.

The power would not widen it.

Some long-dwellers would climb immediately and weep. Some would laugh because the open distance looked false, like blue paint without a wall behind it. Some would become dizzy and descend backward. One old man, who had not seen an unobstructed sky in forty-three years, would reach the final turn, feel the wind, and decide that was enough. He would sit below the opening with his eyes closed and ask people to describe what lay beyond.

Children would make the climb until rules were established. Then they would break the rules.

Copper mirrors would be fitted into the old sockets. For a short time each morning, light would travel down the cleared channel, strike the lowest surviving blue fish, and cast its wavering shape onto the ceiling of the jar court.

The new air would not cure the whole Rain House. It would improve one warm region and disturb three others. Lamp smoke would travel differently. A moss bed would dry out. The bats would alter their dusk route and cause two weeks of cursing. Someone would attempt to claim the upper steps as a selling place. Someone else would charge visitors to look at the horizon until the gate crews threw his baskets down the shaft.

Nothing would become simple.

But the ancient overflow would give hard rain another way to leave. The buried cistern would be cleaned and lined. During dry weeks it would hold enough water that nested cups would not need to stand beneath every drip. Not abundance. Abundance would attract conquest from above and carelessness below. It would be a reserve modest enough to guard, large enough to forgive.

The interval would remain.

Over years, people would learn its signs and mistake them for properties of the world. They would say dangerous walls had always dropped three flakes. They would say every competent rope splicer knew the warning hum. Children would be scolded for not understanding that poisoned water swallowed reflections.

This would please the power.

It had no need to be thanked, named, painted, or fed. Gratitude directed upward might gather into priesthood, and priesthood into possession. Someone might claim to interpret the three flakes better than the plasterers. Someone might declare the tortoises sacred and starve a court to feed them. The power would accept no shrine.

If an offering were left for it, rats would take the edible part. Beetles would take the rest.

Its final gift would be smaller still.

Once in every person’s life, though never at a predictable age, they would wake just before dawn after dreaming of the Rain House entire.

Not as a map. No human mind could hold all its passages accurately. They would dream it as a single living shape: cisterns for bellies, shafts for throats, channels for veins, brick galleries folded like chambers of a heart. They would feel the press of every foot, the thirst in every mouth, the roots testing every crack. They would know, for the few moments before waking, that none of their attention had been wasted. Every scraped gutter, every caught drip, every warning shouted through a tube had entered the life of something larger than themselves.

Most would forget the dream’s images by breakfast.

Yet afterward they would sometimes pause before sealing a niche. They would listen once more before declaring a wall empty. They would pour water with a little greater care. People who had quarreled bitterly might still quarrel, but would leave one another room to carry a basket past.

And when death came, as it would, they would not be spared it.

The wise power knew that endless keepers would eventually become owners, then jailers, then another form of wall. It would grant no immortality. Bodies would still be carried upward. Names would blur. Handprints would disappear beneath fresh lime. Skills would pass imperfectly from scarred hands into unscarred ones.

But now and then, when a person who had given much of their life to the Rain House was carried toward the open court, air from the hillside shaft would move through the galleries against the usual draft.

It would touch their face first.

Then it would pass among those bearing them, through pigeon dust and lamp smoke, across the tanks and raised beds, stirring the mint, trembling the reflections, lifting the loose edge of some ancient painted animal.

No voice would explain it.

Someone would steady the body. Someone would check the nearest flame. A child would look up.

Then the work would continue.
