The power does nothing at first.

This is not hesitation. It is courtesy.

It does not descend in fire, because fire is difficult here and every flame means labor. It does not part the low sky or raise an island from the drowned plain. It does not command roots to enter the rock. Trees would split the shelters, drink the shallow water, cast unfamiliar shadows over the feeding films. Grass would bury the cracks by which the people read their roads. A green world would not be a healed version of this one. It would be another world, imposed.

Nor does the power still the buried sea.

Silence is not peace here.

So it attends.

It follows the waking of the clear cells after rain. It learns the brief blue answer of the pasture-skin beneath a grazer’s mouth. It enters, without disturbing them, the mineral beads in which unborn multitudes wait through a hundred years of drought and flood. It feels the rubbery steps of shield-backs and the hollow weather-song inside strainwalkers. It waits with the ragged plates through their long fasting. It travels beside blind swimmers into cracks no surface creature has mapped.

It listens beneath the pavement.

There, in chambers without air, are lives slower than storms. Some are no more than veils spread over miles of cavern wall, feeding on salts released by pressure. Others have bodies, if body is the word for a thing that takes centuries to turn. Their motions have frightened the upper plain since before the oldest shelter was fitted together. They do not know they are frightening anyone. The standing stones answer them as accidentally as a shell answers thunder.

The power listens until accident and intention are distinct.

Then it attends to the people.

It learns every public name of every living person, and the abandoned names of the dead. It learns those names not as words but as weathered rhythms: the missing third knock, the long scrape, the beat that once belonged to a wall now gone. It learns the routes held in memory and the routes lost when a shelter collapsed. It hears the grief inside a builder who has repaired an old wall badly and told no one. It hears the copper-finder tapping for a seam that is not there. It hears a child in fog stamping a family call after the family has passed beyond the range of ordinary stone.

Still, the power does nothing.

Years pass.

Had the people known they were observed, some would have called this cruelty. But the power knows that to act before understanding would be merely another kind of storm.

At last it speaks.

It does not choose a prophet. A single mouth could be doubted, obeyed too completely, killed, enthroned, or made lonely.

Instead, on a night when the surface water is perfectly still, it gives one knock beneath every open hand.

Not a concussion. Not the deep cadence of the lower lives. Only one gentle impact, as if something on the far side of the world has touched the stone with one finger.

Every listener feels it.

So does every grazer. So does every shield-back. The prongheads raise their crowned skulls. Ragged plates unfold one limb and flatten again. Across the plain, from shelter to shelter and herd to herd, life pauses.

Then come two knocks.

Then three.

Then the first again, but changed by distance.

The route-keepers know at once that this is no vent. A vent cannot strike the whole plain without delay. It is no echo, because the final beat returns before the first has faded. It is no buried animal. No living chamber has such measure.

The power does not say, *I am here.*

The far-language has no proper rhythm for that.

It says:

*I have listened.*

Then:

*What shall be changed?*

The question travels through stone for eleven days.

At first the people answer separately.

One settlement asks that the ragged plates be destroyed. Another asks for copper. A parent asks that no child ever be lost in fog. A hunter asks for a spear that cannot miss. Someone starving asks for the whole pavement to bloom with film. Someone grieving asks for one polished bone to become flesh again.

Others demand that the storms end.

Some ask that the roofs of the world be lifted and the sky lit.

Some beg the power to break the stone open so the buried sea can be seen at last.

The power receives every answer and grants none of them.

Not yet.

It sends the questions back through the plain, not as mockery but as consequence.

If the ragged plates vanish, the grazers multiply, and the blue pasture is stripped before it can seed the cracks. If food becomes endless, the herds cover the old swells until their own weight breaks the crust. If no hunter misses, nothing survives the hunters. If the storms end, the mineral skins do not wake, the egg-beads do not open, the shallow channels stagnate, and all the plain becomes a museum of unopened lives.

It shows these things not in visions but in rhythms the route-keepers can test.

The debate lasts a generation.

People carry arguments between circuits. Answers are beaten from one standing stone to another. Families divide. Old enemies discover that they have asked for the same thing. Children born after the first question grow to adulthood believing that a conversation with the stone is an ordinary condition of life.

Finally, in one of the oldest shelters, an elder places both palms against the floor.

She has outlived six names. Airborne speech has nearly left her. Three pieces of her ancestors rattle in the wall when the western aquifer rises.

She strikes no copper tongue. She uses the bones of her hands.

Her answer is simple enough to cross the plain without distortion:

*Do not make us safe.*

She waits.

Then:

*Make danger honest.*

The words are argued over, altered, sent onward and returned. In the end, they become the nearest thing the people have ever made to a common request.

The benevolent power understands.

It reaches beneath the plain.

It does not strengthen the whole crust, because the buried sea must breathe. It does not seal the vents or command the rain. Instead it touches every place where pressure can gather without a sign: sealed basins, blind shafts, cavern roofs thinning grain by grain. Into each it introduces a flaw.

A merciful flaw.

Not enough to break the stone. Enough to sing before breaking.

From that time onward, no great rupture comes in perfect silence.

Before a basin bursts, the new flaw begins to murmur. Before a cavern roof fails, a tremor travels outward in a long, unmistakable cadence. Before water reverses violently through a channel, the black ribs under the pavement produce three low beats with a pause between them.

The warning is not only for people.

Grazer swarms feel it through a thousand feet and contract toward high ground. Shield-backs turn their herds. Strainwalkers close their veils. Swimmers leave doomed pools. Even the patient plates, which are no less worthy for being hungry, unfasten themselves and move from hollows that will become traps.

Not everyone escapes every time.

The plain remains the plain.

There are storms too sudden for the old, distances too great for the injured, mistakes, stubbornness, predation, hunger, and chance. The power does not turn mortality into a misunderstanding that can be corrected. But fewer lives are taken by events that announce themselves only at the instant of destruction.

Danger becomes legible.

That is the first gift.

The second is smaller.

In each ancient silence shelter, the power alters one stone by less than the width of a clear cell. No wall is restored. No ancestral wedge is replaced. Nothing is made new. But when the shelter is correctly repaired—when its proportions again approach the understanding of its first builders—that stone gives a faint, warm resonance beneath the hand.

It does not tell builders what to do.

It tells them when they have understood.

Over decades, lost methods return. Not by revelation, but by experiment freed from hopelessness. Apprentices move a slab, test it, move it back. Families compare tones. Shelters thought beyond saving begin once more to subtract the roar.

The people remain their own builders.

That is important to the power.

The third gift is given not to the people alone, but to everything above and below.

The vast lives in the buried sea have always pressed unknowingly against the world’s thin roof. Their motions become fear on the surface; the surface storms, in turn, strike pain into their slow membranes. Neither realm has understood the other.

The power does not translate thoughts. Thought is too different between them.

It establishes rhythm.

When the lower lives approach a fragile roof, the merciful flaws answer in patterns they can feel. When herds gather over a sensitive chamber, the pavement returns a warning unlike any natural echo. The exchange is primitive at first: *here, not here; pain, no pain; passing, waiting.*

It is enough.

Centuries later, listeners will discover that some deep rhythms are no longer accidental. Something below has learned to repeat the public names of shelters. Slowly—so slowly that arguments continue for lifetimes—the people begin to suspect that the lower sea has peoples of its own.

The power does not confirm this.

Wisdom sometimes means leaving a door unopened until both sides know how to knock.

After the gifts are given, the power withdraws its obvious attention.

No image of it appears in copper or stone. There is no body to carve. The people disagree about whether there ever was a power at all. Some say the plain asked itself the question. Some say the oldest builders completed a work whose consequences took ages to arrive. Some say the buried lives learned mercy and taught the rock.

The route-keepers preserve the first conversation, though every generation changes its rhythm slightly.

The elder who gave the common answer eventually dies. Her body is laid in a selected channel. Years later, two small bones are recovered downstream, polished smooth. They are placed in the wall of the shelter where she spoke.

When pressure rises beneath the western basin, they rattle before the new flaw begins to sing.

Children put their hands against them.

They learn that the world will not spare them every grief.

They learn that teeth remain teeth, water remains water, stone wears away, and all shelters eventually fail.

But they also learn this:

The world need not be remade to be shown mercy.

Sometimes mercy is a warning.

Sometimes it is a question asked in the language of those who must answer.

And sometimes the wisest power, having listened long enough to know the difference between suffering and strangeness, leaves the strangeness untouched.
