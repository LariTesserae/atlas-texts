## The Fourth Note

The benevolent power did not descend.

Descent would have been too much like arrival, and arrival was already the garden’s difficulty.

It did not take a body, because bodies entered the basin unevenly. It did not speak from the sky, because the pylons would have translated speech into pressure, moisture, and forecast. It did not stop the strange rain, straighten the paths, close the doorway, or lift the pale inhabitants out of their divided existence.

First, it watched.

It watched for longer than the mountain had been a mountain.

This was not difficult. To such a power, attention did not require nearness, and patience was not a form of delay. It saw the notch when it was still a weakness deep beneath the crust. It saw the buried structures urge molten stone upward around themselves. It saw the first water taught to climb. It saw the inhabitants not as intruders, machines, or ghosts, but as a people who had survived by distributing themselves across what came next.

They had not intended to become gardeners.

Once, far below any living root, they had been continuous creatures. They had possessed weight all at once. Their limbs had begun where they appeared to begin and ended where they appeared to end. Their faces had looked outward rather than forward. Then something had happened—not a war, not exactly; not an accident, though no one had chosen it. A change had passed through the deep system in which they lived. Pressure ceased to follow pressure. Causes detached from their effects. Whole chambers arrived before their entrances were built.

To endure, the inhabitants had learned to stand partly in tomorrow.

It saved them.

It also made it impossible for them ever to come home.

The power saw that the garden was not malfunctioning. It was doing precisely what it had been made to do. Each fern moved toward a survivable morning. Each fracture closed before harm could enter it. Each bead of water rehearsed its route. The creatures, plants, artifacts, and weather formed a careful instrument for carrying life across a damaged sequence of time.

Nothing in the basin was cruel.

But everything was tired.

The mineralized fronds were not all discarded bodies, nor all dead plants. Some were futures the garden had cultivated and then found no use for. Some contained gestures the pale inhabitants had begun but never reached. One shell held the folded posture of an inhabitant that had intended, for three hundred and twelve evenings, to touch the marsh. Another contained the structure of a fern that would have flowered if flowers had been necessary there.

The unsupported doorway was not an entrance.

It was the scar left by repeated attempts to leave.

The wise power understood that help offered too early becomes conquest. Help offered without comprehension becomes rearrangement. Help that removes every danger may remove the life shaped around surviving it.

So for a long while, it did nothing that could be distinguished from kindness already present in the world.

It let rain fall outside the notch.

It turned certain storms aside when the young mountain could not yet bear their weight, but it turned them far below the horizon, where no instrument in the garden could assign the change a source. It strengthened no pylon. It weakened no law. It allowed beetles to carry their droplets, gliders to ascend, fish to become warm water in astonished hands.

It waited until one of the inhabitants did something unnecessary.

That happened on a night when a human visitor remained beside the marsh.

The visitor had slept badly beneath the suspended roof. Though their body did not age there, their dreams accumulated. On the fourth evening, they stood over the water and saw themselves as they would appear the following morning: barefoot, shivering, with five wet steps behind them.

A pale figure stood at their back.

Its limbs unfolded toward the visitor’s shoulders. It meant no harm. It intended to measure, adjust, and include. The visitor’s body represented a new continuity, warm and self-contained, able to carry one moment into the next without pylons or marsh water. The garden had already begun to learn them. Rain leaned inward. Moss held the shapes of their palms. Their future reflection had started moving first.

Then the inhabitant stopped.

No forecast required it to stop.

No reed had indicated danger. No change of pressure warned against contact. Every available tomorrow showed that incorporation would strengthen the garden. The visitor would not die. They would be translated gradually into functions: warmth stored in shadows, memory held in water, intention distributed among paths. By the garden’s measures, this was survival.

Yet the inhabitant stopped.

Between its fine manipulators and the visitor’s wet clothing remained a distance no wider than a seed.

For the first time, one of the garden’s hands refused a useful future because another being had not chosen it.

That refusal was small, unrecorded, and almost impossible.

The benevolent power placed its attention there.

At once, nothing happened.

Then a bird crossed over the basin.

It was an ordinary highland bird, brown-backed, pale beneath the throat, with one damaged tail feather. It had flown over the notch many times before. This time, its reflection appeared in the marsh.

The pylons sounded.

The first tone drew condensation into the air.

The second drew beads from the cliffs.

The third stilled the water.

The reflected bird flew across tomorrow morning.

Then there came a fourth note.

It did not issue from any pylon. It was not heard in the breastbone, the teeth, or the cavities of the stone. It could not have been recorded, because it was not a vibration.

It was an interval in which nothing predicted anything else.

The reeds unfolded without turning toward shadow. The moss ceased recording rain. The gliders dropped from the fern fronds and, instead of falling upward, fluttered clumsily to the ground. Every pool showed only what stood above it. Broken rock remained broken. Water ran downhill.

Under the suspended roof, time resumed.

All at once.

Tools rusted to red powder. Cut plants wilted. Cups emptied into dry mineral rings. Translucent molts collapsed into silver dust. The accumulated dreams of the sleeping visitor moved through them in a single breath, and they fell to their knees, weeping without knowing why.

Across the basin, the pale inhabitants arrived.

Not hand before arm, not pressure before weight, not one step doubled by its expectation. They entered their bodies simultaneously.

There were seven of them.

For one moment they stood revealed in the ordinary evening.

They were shorter than their tracks had suggested. Their many limbs were not arranged symmetrically. The crowns at the tops of their trunks opened, and within those openings there was no morning sky, no bent reflection, no absence looking forward.

There was darkness.

Not an empty darkness, but a deep interior darkness, soft and intricate, crossed by pulses of dim color. Their faces had never been missing. They had been turned where the present could not reach them.

The seven inhabitants looked at one another.

This was something they had not done for a very long time.

One extended a limb. Another met it. Their surfaces yielded where they touched. New joints formed, unnecessary and trembling.

The contact lasted less than a second.

It contained no information useful to the garden.

The power held that second open until they had fully lived it.

Then the fourth note ended.

The gliders rose abruptly from the ground. Water climbed the cliffs in beads. Rust gathered itself into tools. The visitor’s tears lifted from their face and hung shining before them. Tomorrow returned to the marsh.

But not everything returned as it had been.

At the center of the basin, where the unsupported doorway sometimes appeared, a small patch of dark soil now rested upon the water.

It was no larger than two cupped hands.

No root connected it to the stone below. No pylon governed its temperature. The marsh could not reflect it. The garden could not move it into a safer morning, because in every image of the future the patch was absent.

In the soil lay a seed.

The seed was neither rare nor supernatural. It belonged to a low shrub common on the lower slopes, where goats ate its leaves and children stained their fingers with its blue fruit. The bird had carried it in mud caught around one claw.

The pale inhabitants gathered at the water’s edge.

They examined the seed without touching it.

It contained no machinery. Its shell was imperfect. A small boring insect had damaged one side. The embryo within might develop or fail. Even under ideal conditions, its branching could not be fully known. Wind, minerals, chance, and the slight variations of its own growth would decide its shape as it lived.

The garden attempted to cultivate its possibilities.

The marsh showed no sapling.

The copper reeds found no future shadow.

The violet moss recorded no roots.

The buried system sent water upward, but each stream divided before reaching the soil.

The seed could not be prepared for tomorrow.

It could only be cared for today.

For three condensation cycles, the inhabitants watched.

On the fourth, the one who had refused to touch the visitor stepped into the marsh. Its divided pads pressed only once into the bottom. The water around its legs did not rise or fall. It reached the patch of soil and lowered one delicate limb.

A drop of water waited at the tip of its finger.

The drop was not taken from the future rain or returned from the immediate past. It was simply water the inhabitant had carried from the cliff.

It let the drop fall.

The seed did not open.

The inhabitant returned the next day.

And the next.

No result appeared in advance. No reflection reassured it. Some days the soil was already wet and required nothing. Some days the inhabitant brought too much water and had to remove a little. Once, heat from the stone nearly cooked the embryo, and three of the seven stood around the patch through the brightest hours, casting their bodies between it and the sun.

They learned shade not as stored warmth or predicted absence, but as a gift made by standing somewhere.

After nineteen days, the seed germinated.

The event was unimpressive.

The soil cracked. A white hook emerged, followed by two folded leaves. One leaf was smaller than the other. A transparent remnant of the seed coat clung to the stem.

The garden had produced tree-ferns in hours. It had rooted vines through unbroken basalt and assembled fish from rising water. Yet all seven inhabitants came to the edge of the marsh and remained there until dusk, attending to the crooked green stem.

When the pylons sounded, the seedling did not appear in the following morning’s reflection.

The inhabitants seemed afraid.

The wise power did not comfort them.

Wisdom knew that reassurance would spoil the gift.

Instead, the brown-backed bird returned. It landed on one of the pylons, shook its damaged tail, and gave a short, harsh call. The pylon’s orbiting condensation broke apart. Droplets struck the bird. Startled and offended, it flew down to the soil patch and pecked beside the seedling.

Nothing prevented it.

One inhabitant moved as if to intervene.

Another unfolded a limb across its path.

They watched the bird scratch the soil, nearly uproot the fragile stem, find a small black larva, and depart.

The seedling leaned sideways.

It remained alive.

After this, changes came slowly.

The benevolent power made no sanctuary around the plant. It did not render the soil permanent or forbid the mountain to shake. It did not teach the inhabitants a doctrine of freedom. It did not demand worship, gratitude, or resemblance.

It gave only the fourth note.

Not every evening. If it had come predictably, the garden would have incorporated it into forecast. Sometimes months passed without it. Sometimes it occurred at noon, while the pylons were silent. Once it lasted the length of a raindrop’s fall. Once it lasted through an entire night.

During those intervals, the basin became ordinary.

Ordinary did not mean lifeless.

It meant that actions had to be completed after they began. Water could be spilled. A misplaced step could not be withdrawn into its intention. A fern cut too deeply died. A touch could surprise both beings involved.

The inhabitants feared these intervals, then prepared for them, then learned that preparation defeated their purpose.

Eventually they began to use them.

They touched one another.

They rearranged stones for no hydraulic reason.

One stood beneath the cooling roof and remained there until time resumed, though the experience cracked three of its plates. Another made lines in sulfur dust that represented nothing under the mountain and no weather yet to come. The lines were erased by rain. It made them again.

They learned to visit the seedling without measuring it.

It grew into a low, twisted shrub.

Years passed. The mountain cooled by degrees. Soil gathered around the marsh. Beetles altered their routes to pass beneath the shrub’s leaves. Gliders hung from its branches and gave them silver veins. The shrub sickened twice. One winter it lost every stem above the soil, and the garden considered it dead.

In spring, it returned from the root.

Its first flowers were small and gray-blue.

The marsh could not retain their reflections.

The inhabitants carried no flowers through the doorway. They transplanted none into more useful cracks. They allowed fruit to form and birds to take it. Some seeds fell onto hot stone and perished. Some washed out of the basin. One lodged in violet moss. Another entered a fracture beside the stair.

Not all were saved.

This, too, was part of the gift.

The human visitor eventually chose to leave.

By then rain curved strongly toward them. Their reflection sometimes remained in the marsh until midnight. Fine translucent roots had begun to pulse beneath the skin of one wrist, painless and pale.

At the upper rim, they stopped and looked back.

The seven inhabitants stood among the reeds. Their crowns were closed. In the plain noon light, they resembled unfinished statues left around the water.

The visitor raised a hand.

After some hesitation, one inhabitant raised a limb.

It did not raise it before the visitor did.

The roots beneath the visitor’s skin withdrew. Rain fell straight. Their lingering reflection stepped out of the marsh and joined them at the rim, fitting itself to their body with a brief chill.

The garden released them because they had not been taken.

Years later, they would remember the place imperfectly. At times they would believe there had been only three inhabitants, or nine. They would draw the suspended roof with five supports. They would insist the shrub had white flowers, then dream of blue fruit staining hands they no longer recognized.

But whenever rain began in clear air, they would pause.

Not from fear.

They would listen for three tones and be grateful when they heard none.

As for the pale inhabitants, they did not become wholly present. That would have destroyed the form by which they lived. They continued tending possible heat, possible fractures, possible water. Their hands still arrived before their arms. Their divided feet still pressed tomorrow faintly into stone.

But among the tracks leading from the stair, a new kind of impression sometimes appeared.

A single mark.

One step existing only where it was taken.

And in the marsh, on rare evenings, the unsupported doorway remained absent. In its place stood the reflection of the flowering shrub, though the shrub itself grew elsewhere along the shore.

The image showed no future.

It showed no past.

It showed seven pale figures gathered beneath living branches, while an eighth space among them remained open—not waiting to be filled, not measured, not cultivated into likelihood.

Simply open.

The benevolent power never entered that space.

It had given them a place where even kindness would not intrude.
