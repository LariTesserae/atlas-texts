The power did not descend from the sky.

Anything arriving that way would have been mistaken for weather, and the city had long experience persuading weather to become useful.

It did not rise through the roots. The city would have tasted it there, compared it with neighboring outcomes, and begun adapting before the encounter had properly occurred.

Instead, it appeared in one of the protected clearings.

The clearing had been kept open for eleven generations. Its first emptiness keeper had found only a ring of pale mud and a species of grass that grew inward from the circumference but died before reaching the center. No buried structure had ever been discovered. Birds crossed it freely. Rain fell normally. Outcome gardeners had tested its adjacent histories and found the same vacancy in every one.

This consistency made the place more mysterious, not less.

The present keeper was an old person named *Leaves Room for What Has No Name*. They had inherited the clearing as a young adult and spent most of their life cutting back roots that sought to interpret it.

One morning after a quiet rain, they found someone sitting at its center.

The stranger was neither luminous nor immense. They had taken a body of modest height, with two arms, two legs, dark bare skin, and no visible symbionts. Water rested on them without being absorbed, directed, tasted, or put to work. Their shadow fell in the ordinary historical direction.

The keeper remained at the edge.

“Did the city grow you?” they asked.

“No.”

“Did you grow yourself?”

“Not exactly.”

“Have you come from another basin?”

“I am there also.”

This was not reassuring.

The keeper tasted the standing water. It contained no warning. The nearest roots had gone still, not from injury but from an unfamiliar inability to decide what the stranger meant.

“What do you carry?” the keeper asked.

“Attention.”

“To where?”

“To whatever is here.”

The keeper considered this for a long time.

Citizenship required circulation. Water, warnings, pollen, memory, repair: even grief had to be carried somewhere. Attention without an intended destination was difficult to classify. It might be waste. It might be predation. It might be a form of rain.

At last the keeper stepped into the clearing.

The stranger did nothing.

This was the first sign of their power.

They did not reveal the clearing’s purpose. They did not heal the keeper’s stiffening joints or restore the dead chamber-tree the keeper still dreamed of. They did not explain the Last Slope, prevent the next storm, or offer a superior design for the city.

They sat in the mud and waited to be invited farther.

The city took forty-three days to decide.

During that time it sent many representatives.

A ferry beast approached first. It lowered itself at the clearing’s edge and opened the fruiting kiosks along its back. The stranger ate one fruit and gave nothing in exchange. The ferry waited. When no payment, seed, route correction, or useful microbe was offered, it left in visible perplexity.

A market platform floated up during a flood and grew a stall bearing precisely what the stranger lacked: a living mantle, drinking bulbs, a knife of hardened resin, and a fruit containing the memory of welcome. The stranger accepted the drinking bulbs but left the rest.

A hunter watched from the reeds. A child walked directly into the clearing, placed both hands on the stranger’s knees, and asked whether they could stop death.

“Yes,” said the stranger.

The roots tightened throughout the district.

“Will you?”

“No.”

The child struck them.

The stranger allowed this.

“Why not?”

“Because stopping death would not stop loss. It would only prevent loss from completing its work.”

The child began to cry, partly from anger and partly because the answer resembled something the dead had said through fruit.

The stranger held them, but only after asking.

Listeners came and pressed their palms to the soil. They heard no root-name. Weather shepherds released diagnostic pollen, which settled on the stranger and failed to germinate. Outcome gardeners examined nearby possibilities and found the stranger sitting in all of them.

Not versions of the stranger.

The same stranger.

For the first time, the city encountered something the Last Slope could reach without dividing.

This frightened it enough to make courtesy necessary.

The invitation arrived as warm water flowing against every current. Doors opened along a route from the clearing to the center of the basin. Ferry beasts gathered, though the stranger chose to walk.

All through the city, inhabitants watched.

“What will you do?” asked the walls in changes of color.

“What should be done?” the stranger replied.

The city answered plurally.

The towers asked to be made resilient beyond injury.

The ferry beasts asked always to find a route.

Parents asked that no child be exchanged by accident with a suffering version of itself.

Children asked for flight, gills, sweets, and the return of particular dead people.

Hunters asked for the ability to hear one heartbeat without hearing all the others.

The memory moths asked for more nights.

A canal asked to reach the sea, though no sea remained within any distance the city understood.

The eaters of the third rain asked for nothing. Rot was plentiful, and they distrusted gifts that could not be digested.

The emptiness keepers asked that some questions remain unanswered.

The Last Slope did not ask.

At the edge of its black water, the stranger removed their feet from the sandals they had grown for the journey. The attendants painted in ordinary clay watched without moving.

“You may not touch the plant,” one said.

“I know.”

“You may not drink.”

“I know.”

“You may not bring another possibility closer.”

“I know.”

The stranger sat.

For nine days, the length of time required for water to cross the final gradient, nothing happened.

Then the low plant at the center turned one widely spaced leaf toward them.

The stranger bowed.

Across the basin, transparent windows clouded. Root bridges slackened. People woke from sleep with the taste of metal in their mouths. In designated hunting gardens, predators stopped beside fallen prey and listened to something beyond hearing.

The city saw its neighboring outcomes clearly.

It saw a tower survive lightning by exchanging places with one that had not been struck.

It saw the unstruck tower vanish from another basin.

It saw fire arrive there without cause.

It saw a bridge borrow the strength of its successful counterpart, while elsewhere hairline failures appeared in living wood that had grown correctly.

It saw a medicine teach a body the memory of recovery. It also saw another sick body, equally real, lose that memory and die bewildered among people who had expected it to heal.

Not every cultivation required theft. Often a pattern could be copied without being diminished. Sometimes two outcomes exchanged equally desired gifts. Sometimes a possibility existed only as a tendency, with no inhabitants to suffer its alteration.

But not always.

The Last Slope had opened roots into neighboring histories before the city possessed the language to ask whether history was inhabited.

The attendants began to recite events that had not happened.

This time they recited events that had happened elsewhere because they had been prevented here.

The litany lasted through the night.

No one accused the city. The stranger did not call it wicked. Innocence could explain harm, but it could not make harm harmless.

“What must we surrender?” asked the roots.

“Nothing that is yours,” said the stranger.

The answer traveled outward and was argued over for three rains.

At last the city understood.

The power would not close the paths. It would not take away the city’s medicine, bridges, exchanges, or strange survival. It would give the Last Slope one additional capacity.

The ability to knock.

The stranger did not touch the low plant. They placed one hand on the ordinary clay covering an attendant’s arm. The attendant placed their other hand in the black water. The plant lowered a root.

Through that chain—power, person, clay, water, plant—the city learned the difference between what could be reached and what had opened willingly.

It was a small distinction.

It changed everything.

Afterward, neighboring outcomes were no longer fields to be cultivated in silence. They became communities, wildernesses, sleeping possibilities, closed doors, offered hands.

A bridge seeking structural memory might receive it from twelve other bridges, each sharing only what could be copied without injury. A medicine might be taught by recovered bodies willing to be remembered. During storms, towers called across possibility, and some versions answered.

Others did not.

The city learned to endure refusal.

That was the harder gift.

The first great storm after the change formed in the west. Weather shepherds failed to divide it. Towers searched adjacent histories for gentler rain and found them closed. Ferry beasts crowded the high channels. Roof leaves tightened until their fibers trembled.

Lightning struck a residential tower.

The tower did not exchange places.

It burned.

Rain extinguished it before the trunk died, but seventeen rooms were lost. A nursery chamber split open, and people carried the children down through smoke while gliding reptiles beat sparks from their wings. One Listener was badly injured. An archive of childhood scents vanished entirely.

For days, some inhabitants cursed the stranger.

The stranger helped eat the burned tissue.

They carried baskets. They cleaned wounds. They held a grieving person who had lost the last stored scent of their mother. They offered no argument that justice elsewhere should make pain here easier to bear.

When the third rain came, the damaged tower put out new growth around its blackened chambers. It did not erase the scar.

In another possible basin, an unharmed tower opened its flowers to the rain.

No fire arrived without cause.

The city’s deliberation continued beneath the soil.

It had learned to ask across worlds, but the question turned inward.

Doors had always been permitted to remain closed. Garments could sleep. Towers could revise their obligations to a district. Yet people were continually read by walls, markets, roots, healing pools, and one another. Need was anticipated before it became speech. Pain entered the civic circulation almost as soon as it was felt.

Participation had become so complete that refusal resembled death.

The stranger returned to the eleven-generation clearing.

*Leaves Room for What Has No Name* followed.

“You have shown the city how to knock,” the keeper said.

“Only because it already knew how to open.”

“Why was this place empty?”

“So I would have somewhere to arrive without displacing what lived here.”

The keeper waited for a deeper answer.

None came.

“What else will you do?”

The stranger placed a hand on the bare mud.

Nothing grew from it.

Instead, throughout the basin, living membranes acquired a new pattern. It appeared in garments first, then in chamber walls, in the sensory folds of hunters, in the memory-bearing fruit, and finally in the roots.

It was the pattern of a closed hand that was not a fist.

Any citizen could adopt it.

A person bearing that pattern could pass for a time without the city tasting their grief, predicting their needs, offering memories, or searching adjacent histories for versions of their choice. They could become private without becoming severed.

A tower could rest without consulting its district.

A ferry beast could leave its route.

A market could flower without bearing anything useful.

An old person who carried no messages, pollen, repair, memory, or water could remain a citizen simply because they were there.

The power did not command the city to value these things. It made them possible, then allowed the city to decide what they meant.

At first the pattern was used rarely.

Then extravagantly.

Adolescents closed themselves for days and emerged with unsuitable names. Lovers kept secrets merely to experience revealing them. Artists produced fruit that nourished nothing. One ferry beast abandoned public transport for an entire wet season and stood in a meadow watching moths.

There were arguments. There was selfishness. There were preventable misunderstandings.

There was also rest.

The roots discovered that what returned willingly carried finer information than what had never been allowed to withdraw.

In time, the city accepted another rule. Like the others, it was not written.

**Nothing living may be taken for knowing, use, or continuation merely because it can be reached.**

A second rule followed years later.

**What carries nothing may still belong.**

When these had entered the water deeply enough to survive translation, the stranger prepared to leave.

The city offered them a root-name.

They declined.

It offered stored memories, future medicines, a tower seed, a ferry route through probability, and a garment that remembered every rain that had ever fallen in the basin.

They accepted a cup of water.

“Where will you carry it?” asked the old emptiness keeper.

“For a while,” said the stranger, “nowhere.”

Then they walked toward the rim.

No light opened. No sky changed. No final revelation moved through the roots.

The city could have followed them across neighboring histories, but did not.

By morning, the protected clearing remained empty.

Grass began growing inward from its edges. The new keeper cut it back.

Not because the power was expected to return.

Because arrival was not the only thing for which room should be left.
