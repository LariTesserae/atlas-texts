### The Courtesy Interval

The power does not descend in fire.

Fire would be a poor gift here.

It does not green the ledges. Roots would enter drainage seams, retain water against the stone, conceal hairline fractures, and die in the metal-rich cold. It does not warm the mountain. Warmth applied broadly would loosen the lucid ice, wake buried water, change the pressure of every joint. It does not still the faults. To arrest the young mountain would require violence on a scale larger than the city and would pass that violence elsewhere.

It does not make the people immortal. They have built their tenderness around the knowledge that rooms close, bodies fail, routes alter, and keeping is an act rather than a condition. Endless life would not preserve their society. It would fill every chamber they had wisely left empty.

The power is benevolent enough to wish them well and wise enough not to confuse well-being with the removal of every danger.

So at first it does nothing.

It attends.

It follows condensation from the ridge apertures to the lowest reservoirs. It rests its awareness in bolts, tension fields, ceramic repair grains, and the cold bones of people touching walls. It learns the city not as a map but as a set of permissions: where water may go, where loads may pass, where a bridge may release itself, where a chamber has been accepted and where occupation is merely being tolerated.

It hears every knock the mountain has made since the first dwelling was fitted into the fracture.

It hears older knocks as well, held faintly in crystal boundaries and planes of compressed mineral: the history of impacts, cooling, uplift, and enormous subterranean patience. The mountain is not alive as the people are alive. The inhabitants are right about that. Yet neither is it mute. Matter remembers force.

The power listens until it understands what even the survey instruments do not.

Deep below the oldest inhabited shelf, beyond the range at which civic pulses return clearly, two families of fracture are moving toward one another. Their meeting is not imminent by human standards. It may occur in forty years or ninety. But when it occurs, it will not begin with the single heavy knock the city knows. It will begin silently, with a slipping surface under pressure. The first sound will arrive after several lower galleries have already changed shape.

No inspection could presently discover it. No elder could remember its warning. No redundancy was designed for a failure whose first sign comes after the exits cross.

The power could bind the fracture forever.

It could hold each grain of stone in place with an intention no earthquake could overcome. But then the city would inhabit not a mountain but a captive object, and the tension denied here would gather beyond the power’s chosen boundary. Rivers would alter far below. A valley would bear what the summit had been spared. People who had never heard of the hidden city would wake beneath falling walls.

The power could move the city whole to safer ground.

But safety without consent is another kind of disaster.

Instead, it gives them what they have always tried to give one another.

Notice.

Far beneath the reservoirs, where the two hidden fractures approach, the power alters nothing about their direction. It does not cancel their force. It places only a slight interval between intention and consequence.

From then on, strain in that place becomes articulate.

Months before stone would ordinarily slip without warning, it releases a pressure so small that no chamber is damaged. The pressure enters a narrow seam of ice and travels upward as a pulse. It passes through water, anchor fields, bridge couplings, and structural laminates. It is too low to hear and too gentle to feel, but every survey instrument in the city receives it.

The readings are not dramatic. Drama would make them easier to dismiss.

They are precise.

At 03:17, seven instruments on unrelated levels record a second return where only one should exist. At 03:19, a reservoir surface forms a line from northwest to southeast and holds it for eleven seconds. At 03:22, a suspended room corrects its level against a load no local sensor can identify. At 03:24, maintenance swarms emerge from three cabinets and gather at undamaged walls.

No alarm sounds. The system has no category for the event.

A night technician named Senn notices because she is drinking broth over Reservoir Nine and sees the reflection of a path light bend across still water.

She sets down the cup.

She checks the rim, the pressure seals, the pump history, and the latest transfer schedule. She places one hand against the reservoir wall. Nothing vibrates. She calls the level keeper, then a structural conciliator, then the central survey desk.

She does not say the water is behaving strangely.

She says, “Reservoir Nine is holding a surface gradient without acceleration. Eleven seconds, northwest to southeast. No transfer event. Please compare inertial records.”

Within an hour, twenty people are awake.

Within three, the city knows that something has happened, though no one knows what.

Children going to instruction are told there is no immediate danger and that some lower routes are closed. Adults ask what has changed. The honest answer is posted everywhere:

**A deep load has become measurable. Its source is not yet located.**

For six days, surveyors send pulses into the mountain. Most return as expected. A few come back carrying the impossible second echo.

The city expands its model.

The model refuses the data.

They rebuild the model.

They recalibrate every instrument involved. They drain one reservoir, then fill it again. They shut down the inertia devices level by level in case the anomaly is civic rather than geological. For nine minutes, the city bears its weight in the old way. Lifts stop. Suspended rooms settle against their mechanical limits. People sit where instructed and feel gravity become simple.

The second echo remains.

At last an elder from a high eastern shelf asks to hear the converted signal. Technicians warn that the translation is arbitrary: pressure rendered as sound, deep intervals raised into human hearing. She asks again.

They play it through a bench.

The first return is a low tap. The second is softer and arrives too soon.

The elder listens several times.

Then she says, “It is not an echo. Something answered before we asked.”

This sentence causes considerable irritation among the survey teams. It is also entered into the record.

Over the following year, the signal returns at uneven intervals. Each time, it arrives before a tiny deep adjustment. Each time, the interval between signal and movement is longer than any known mechanism permits. Three months. Seven months. Once, nearly two years.

The city begins to map the hidden planes.

What it finds is grave, but not hopeless.

A section of the mountain beneath the oldest shelves will eventually unzip along a slanting fault. Several water galleries will be cut off. Two primary lift shafts will shear. The inhabited eastern wall may remain intact, or may rotate by enough to make every chamber in it unusable. No repair field can persuade so much stone to settle back.

Had the movement begun unannounced, thousands might have been trapped.

Now they have decades.

There are arguments.

Some accuse the instruments of generating the pattern they claim to observe. Some believe an unknown civic technology is interfering with the mountain. Some want to reinforce the threatened districts. Others say reinforcement will only make the eventual release more violent. A few speak privately of agency, intention, even mercy, though such language sits uneasily in public reports.

The official term is **extended precursor phenomenon**.

Everyone else calls it the courtesy interval.

The city does not waste it.

New exits are cut along unrelated fault planes. Reservoirs are divided. Archives are copied upward and outward. Culture rooms are established in distant strata. Children practice routes to chambers that do not yet exist. The oldest inhabited shelf is emptied gradually, while it remains safe enough for belongings to be carried rather than abandoned.

Doors are removed.

Utilities are drawn back.

Ceramic memorial strips are relocated one by one.

An old man stands in the compact room where he was born and presses both palms to a wall warmed by furniture. He has resisted leaving for eleven years. When his neighbors come to help, he tells them not to rush. They do not.

Before sealing the corridor, he scratches a note on the inner lintel where no one is likely to see it again:

**Accepted for a while.**

The power reads it.

The power does not prevent his grief. To prevent grief would require making the lost place meaningless, and that would not be kindness.

Instead, it keeps the hidden fault articulate.

Every dangerous change announces itself before it becomes irreversible. Not loudly. Not infallibly. The people must still listen, compare, decide, and sometimes be wrong. They remain custodians rather than dependents. The gift does not replace their competence. It meets it.

In time, the city builds a second settlement in another fracture farther along the ridge. It is not a copy. Its light wells are broader. Its reservoirs are shallower and more numerous. Its dwellings can be detached in clusters. Large raw chambers are left untouched through its center.

Visitors ask whether the empty space is reserved for later construction.

The residents say no.

When the old fault finally moves, eighty-three years after the first impossible return, almost no one is inside the threatened districts.

The mountain knocks once, then seems to draw a long breath.

Water climbs the walls.

Bridges release their eastern ends in sequence. Lower lights vanish. An entire inhabited shelf, already stripped to stone, tilts into darkness with a sound too deep to be called sound. The city flexes. Doors seal. People turn children inward and wait with one hand free.

The event lasts forty-one seconds.

Afterward, the first question is the old one.

“What has changed?”

The answer takes weeks.

No one has died. Several technicians are injured. Two reservoirs are lost. One light guide is broken so high in the ridge that cloud enters through it and drifts for a time through an evacuated court. The oldest chambers are gone, not buried but closed beyond a wall of newly joined rock.

Where thousands once lived, there is now a pale mineral line.

The people hold no ceremony of deliverance. They distrust claims that large events have singular meanings. They make broth. They revise maps. They annotate the failures of their predictions. They argue over whether the phrase *courtesy interval* should appear in the technical account.

It does.

Small memorials are installed for the vanished districts. One bench in the new settlement is warmed each year for eighty-three minutes. Senn, who first saw the bent reflection in Reservoir Nine, refuses all attempts to name it after her.

“Name it for the water,” she says. “It was paying attention.”

The power remains unknown.

This is not because it wishes to deceive them. It simply has no need to be acknowledged. Gratitude directed toward it would add nothing to the good already done, and certainty about benevolent protection might make the city less careful. Better that they inspect every seam. Better that they maintain two exits. Better that children still learn to sit down when the rail carries the wrong vibration.

Yet the power has been changed by them.

It has seen beings who do not call restraint deprivation. It has seen love without possession, vigilance without worship of fear, and permanence understood as the repeated willingness to keep. It has learned that even immense power should leave unoccupied intervals.

So it does not remain everywhere.

It withdraws from their daily choices. It does not settle arguments, prevent ordinary accidents, or make every beam of sunlight reach a waiting face. It grants no victory over weather, age, error, or sorrow.

It keeps only the narrow promise it never announces:

No mortal change beneath this city will become catastrophe without first trying, somehow, to be heard.

On certain nights, long after the new settlement is inhabited, survey instruments record a faint return from impossible depth. Technicians examine it and find no associated strain. Water lies level. Anchors report nothing. The mountain is quiet.

These signals are archived as unexplained.

Children are allowed to hear them through the benches.

They say the sound resembles a knock made very far away—not a warning knock, and not a demand to enter.

A courteous knock.

As if something vast has paused outside the door and, finding the inhabitants warm, attentive, and kind to one another, has decided not to disturb them.
