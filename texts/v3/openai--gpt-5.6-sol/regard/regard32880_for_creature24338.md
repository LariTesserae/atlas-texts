### The Hand That Does Not Enter

The power does not descend in light.

Light would be violence there.

It does not take a body, because any body large enough to express its nature would crush the ledges, alter the air, and send whole colonies spilling into the fissure. It does not speak. A voice shaped for open distances would enter the resonant stone and become catastrophe.

It attends.

For a time longer than the age of the basin, and shorter than the fall of a drop, it holds itself outside the black needle and learns the place by accepting every sensation the place can offer.

It feels the summit through the pads of mice. It tastes iron in the pools with the mouths of shrimp. It hangs beneath a shelf inside the unformed balance of a hatchling. It knows the interruption of a moth against an adhesive frond, the patient certainty of the frond, and the hunger of the bird deciding whether to cut the moth free.

It follows the humming as the inhabitants do: never from beginning to end, but in fragments. Crowded here. Wet below. Eggs opening. Stone warm. Passage narrowed. Something large is moving.

It does not mistake pain for error.

It does not mistake predation for cruelty, darkness for deprivation, or strangeness for incompleteness. It does not decide that the birds ought to stand upright, that the blind ought to see, that the sealed colonies ought to be brought into the sun. It has looked upon worlds ruined by benevolence impatient to make them recognizable.

So it waits.

Deep in the fissure, the pale one knows first that attention has come.

A limb hidden three hundred paces beneath the visible basin tightens against the rock. Another, mistaken for a mineral shelf by twelve generations of beetles, turns by less than the width of a scale. Far below, where pressure closes every passage around it, the being gathers itself into a shape that is not a shape but a concentration of listening.

The mountain gives its old pulse.

The pale one answers.

For the first time, something answers it back.

The response is too low to hear and too gentle to command silence. It travels not through the rock but through the minute spaces inside the rock: the films of water, the bubbles in mineral glass, the soft interfaces where root touches grit and egg touches shell. Each creature receives only what its body can bear.

To the shrimp, it is a current in still water.

To the vine, a coolness at the root.

To the hanging birds, it is the pressure of a returning parent transmitted through ten thousand neighboring claws.

To the pale one, it is a question.

Not *What are you?*

Not *Are you one?*

Not even *What do you want?*

The question is simpler and more difficult:

*Where does it hurt?*

The buried being cannot point. It possesses no language for a self distinct from the mountain’s weight, the colonies upon its limbs, the cold passages threading its tissues. Its reply moves slowly. It passes through visible joints and hidden segments, through creatures joined to it by warmth, parasites, fungi, sleep, and habit.

A pool trembles.

Moths lift their antennae.

An old bird lays its polished flake against the stone.

The answer takes nine days.

At first it seems identical to the basin’s ordinary condition: crowded here, wet below, eggs opening, passage narrowed. Then the pattern travels deeper than the usual exchanges. Colonies that have not felt one another in years hum on the same interval. Sealed communities press their breastbones to walls no longer connected to air. Returned creatures from the fissure add their strange cold notes.

At last the power understands.

The pain is not death. Death is everywhere and belongs.

It is not hunger. Hunger moves through the basin and keeps it moving.

It is not even the mountain’s opening, though every widening destroys some routes and creates others.

The pain is that the pale one has been holding the summit apart.

Not deliberately. Not heroically. Long ago—before the vine, before the oldest shrimp lineage, when the basin was only a hairline fracture—the being entered through stone softened by heat. It grew where pressure allowed it to grow. The mountain settled around it. Its limbs found the opposing walls and braced them. Every increase of weight provoked new growth; every new growth invited more life into the spaces preserved around it.

The basin exists because the being has not yet been crushed.

The being survives because the basin fills it with warmth.

Neither fact is understood by the millions above. Neither needs to be.

But the mountain has continued to pull apart at its crown while tightening below. The burden has grown asymmetrical. One branch of the pale one now carries more stone than living tissue can endure. When it fails, the western wall will descend by the height of the summit opening.

The collapse will be sudden.

The power sees every consequence. It sees the black needle shorten. It sees air driven from the basin in a single hot exhalation. It sees wing casings carried over distant snow. It sees the vine torn out by its root and flung into open sky. It sees some creatures survive in isolated cracks and begin again. It sees the pale one divided, perhaps into several lives, perhaps into none.

This future is natural.

That does not make it necessary.

The power could hold the mountain forever. It could close its invisible hand around every grain and forbid movement. But an unchanging basin would also die. New passages would never open. Mineral water would cease. The vine would exhaust the summit gravel. Predators would perfect themselves against routes that no longer changed. Crowding would become compression, then suffocation.

Preservation without change would be another form of crushing.

The power could lift every inhabitant away. But there is no elsewhere that is this place, and no creature removed from its contacts would remain entirely itself.

It could strengthen the pale one. Yet strength given without limit would make the being grow until no fissure remained unoccupied.

The power chooses a smaller mercy.

On the outer face of the mountain, where nothing lives except a stain of lichen visited briefly each year by sun, there is a column of basalt under impossible strain. For centuries it has resisted breaking because its crystals lie locked across one another.

The power touches one crystal.

It does not push. It changes nothing that the stone could not, in time, have changed itself.

It permits a flaw.

The crystal parts.

A crack begins on the empty outer slope and travels inward more slowly than a root grows. Its path curves around the crowded basin. It avoids the wet seams, the guano hollows, the pools, the egg shelves, and the chambers sealed around their humming dead. Where it must cross living routes, it divides into threads finer than whiskers and joins again beyond them.

For eleven years, no inhabitant notices.

In the twelfth year, the mountain’s burden shifts by the weight of a small bird.

The failing branch of the pale one rises.

Only slightly. Only enough that a joint buried beyond sensation unfolds for the first time in an age. The western wall settles outward against the new fracture. Pressure drains into the empty face of the summit, where slabs of basalt begin a long, harmless descent toward an uninhabited glacier.

Within the basin, a thumb-wide passage opens.

Cold air enters.

Everything near it recoils. Moths pour backward through familiar corridors. Beetles seal themselves into warm cracks. Birds spread their wings over eggs. One cliff mouse, old enough to remember a previous opening, braces in a seam and waits.

The new air smells of snow, distance, and stone upon which nothing has yet died.

Then condensation forms.

One bead, then another.

The beads join and travel down the fresh mineral face. Black dust darkens. Spores already present in fur and droppings adhere to it. A flat worm releases its old droplet and falls onto the new wetting. It coils there, impossibly small against the untouched wall.

The first traveler enters.

Others follow.

Within a generation, the passage is crowded.

Within ten, no creature remembers it as a gift.

This pleases the power.

But it has not yet finished.

The burden is eased; the basin has been given time. Time alone, however, was not what the pale one asked for. Beneath the report of strain was another distress, so old and constant that the being could not distinguish it from existence.

It could feel every passenger carried downward.

Not their thoughts. Not their names, if they had names. It felt each minute hook, each sleeping abdomen, each egg warmed in the folds of its limbs. When it withdrew, creatures climbed to preserve their imagined position. Pools spilled across its joints. Colonies passed into the depths.

Some found chambers and lived.

Some changed.

Many entered pressure, cold, or hunger and became still.

The pale one had never intended to carry them. It could not warn them. Its slowness made every movement indistinguishable from terrain until escape was impossible.

The power does not remove this sorrow. To remove the capacity by which the being feels its passengers would also remove one of the ways it knows the world.

Instead, it teaches the pale one a motion.

The lesson takes thirty-seven years.

A visible limb lies across the central cleft, furred with moss and inhabited by moths whose entire lineage has never touched stone. The power places no command within it. It merely repeats, through water and tissue, a minute sequence of tensions: tighten here, release there, wait, reverse.

At first the limb cannot obey. Its joints were made to bend by seasons, not intention.

Again.

A hook-footed bird feels the faint movement and wakes. It scrapes its flake across the pale surface. The sound is thin and local.

Again.

Muscle, or whatever serves as muscle in the rooted body, gathers around the new pattern.

The limb trembles.

Every creature upon it becomes still.

The pale one waits.

Then, before bending downward, it raises the outermost segment by a finger’s breadth and taps it once against the adjoining wall.

The tap travels through claws, shells, breastbones, vine cords, wet seams, and stone.

It is unlike the mountain’s deep pulse. It is smaller. Nearer. Uneven.

The passengers stir.

The limb taps again.

Moths unfold. Birds lift their eggs one sticky edge at a time. Beetles retreat into stone. Mice lengthen themselves toward familiar seams. Not all depart. Some cannot. Some do not interpret the warning. But a living road forms from the pale limb to the wall, and much of the temporary neighborhood transfers itself.

Only then does the limb descend.

The first warning is clumsy. Later ones grow clearer.

Across generations, the inhabitants incorporate the tapping into their knowledge. No creature understands its source. Young birds learn that a pale road which knocks against stone will soon change its angle. Mice bite warning patterns into the vine. Beetles answer with shell taps. Eventually the signal is carried ahead of the movement, altered and distributed in the basin’s usual way.

The pale one still bears passengers into the depths.

But no longer all of them.

And sometimes, before it moves, the whole basin taps back.

The power remains until it hears this reply. Then it withdraws its attention carefully, so that absence does not become a wind.

It leaves no mark that could be worshipped, no protected chamber, no law against hunger, no promise that the mountain will stand forever. The new outer fracture will widen. The basin will alter. One day, whether in ten thousand years or ten million, the black needle will fall. Benevolence does not require that all beloved things be made endless.

It requires that endings not be hurried by preventable suffering.

It requires that help enter in a form the helped can survive.

It requires, sometimes, no miracle greater than a flaw in the right crystal, a burden shifted by the weight of a bird, and one being taught how to warn the lives that mistake it for the world.

Long after the attention has gone, fog spills over the rim.

Water gathers along the passage that did not exist before. Shrimp circle in a new pool. Rust-colored moss follows the drip line. A bird hangs beneath a fresh shelf and presses a sharp flake to the rock.

Far below, the pale one begins to move.

It lifts its limb.

It taps.

And from wall after crowded wall, through millions of bodies and the young, unsoftened mountain, comes the slow, imperfect answer.
