The power did not descend in fire.

Fire would have been too decisive.

It came as attention: complete, unhurried, and so gentle that no bird lifted from the pools. The frogs continued calling. Midges turned in their columns. A grazer drew one foot from the clay with a sound like a mouth opening, and set it down again.

The power looked first for the being who lived there.

It found no single body.

It found the lungfish sealed under hardened mud, pulse separated from pulse by long intervals. It found the eggs that had become indistinguishable from dust. It found the pale roots drinking from the undersides of stones, the crocodilian waiting in mineral-dark water, the fungus entering a fallen feather, and the chick crouched so still beneath a passing shadow that stillness seemed to be its only possession.

It found hunger consuming abundance and abundance rising from decay. It found death everywhere, but almost no waste.

Then the power understood that the being was the plain entire.

Its blood was water that could not choose a channel. Its breath was the exchange between wet clay and warm air. Its thoughts were brief and innumerable: a frog answering rain, a seed breaking open, a bird turning in flight toward a pool beyond the horizon. Its memory was carried not in one mind but in eggs, spores, routes, instincts, mineral rings, old hoofpaths, and chambers beneath the ground.

The power loved it.

Because it loved it, it was tempted.

It could raise the islands so that no nest would drown. It could deepen every basin so that no fish would suffocate in shrinking water. It could make the rain dependable, soften the heat, turn the wiry grasses lush, and give the grazing animals firm roads beneath their feet.

It could silence the crocodilian’s hunger.

It could turn aside the snake from the nest.

It could keep the tadpoles’ pool from failing.

But wisdom stood within its love.

Raise the islands, and the safest nesters would multiply until the pools were emptied of the small lives on which they fed. Deepen every basin, and the creatures of temporary water—those whose brief world protected them from fish—would lose their ancient refuge. Make rain dependable, and the sleepers beneath the clay would be displaced by things that never learned to wait. Give every foot firm ground, and hunters would travel as easily as the hunted.

Save the tadpole, and perhaps the dragonfly larva would starve.

Turn aside the snake, and perhaps its young would not live.

Feed the crocodilian without death, and it would cease to be a crocodilian.

The power did not confuse benevolence with the removal of every pain. Nor did it call every pain necessary merely because it was old. It watched closely enough to know the difference between the plain’s hardship and its destruction.

So it searched for what did not belong to the ancient bargain.

Far off—not yet arrived, but possible—it saw poisons the reducers could not reduce. It saw channels cut so deep that lingering water would be hurried away. It saw pumps drawing down the hidden wetness beneath the clay. It saw hard roads dividing the migrations of bodies that survived by movement. It saw appetites from elsewhere naming the low land useless because it did not stand tall, and empty because its life was small or sleeping.

Against these, the power acted.

Not with a wall. Walls would have contradicted the place.

It laid a quiet difficulty over every scheme that would erase the plain. Survey stakes leaned during the night. Numbers refused to promise profit. Machines sank axle-deep where maps had marked firm ground. Those who came intending to straighten the water became uncertain when they saw how the sky lay whole in every shallow pool.

A few dreamed afterward of eggs under the clay.

A few heard frogs while sleeping in distant cities.

A few acquired, without knowing how, the stubborn conviction that flatness was not emptiness, that seasonal disappearance was not death, and that land need not produce anything beyond itself to justify remaining.

The power did not make the place unreachable. It sent witnesses rather than owners: people willing to cross lightly, to learn the names of temporary waters, to understand that a basin dry for five years might still be inhabited. They marked no path that the rains could not erase. They took no dormant mud on their boots. When they spoke for the plain, they spoke of what waited there, not merely of what could be seen.

Still, the power knew that protection could become another form of imprisonment. It did not seal the landscape beneath an unchanging sky. Species came and went. Trees fell. Basins filled with clay. New channels opened during exceptional rains. Some years were generous; some were severe. The plain continued changing by droplets, films, dissolutions, births, and failures.

It was allowed to remain alive rather than preserved.

Only once did the power touch a creature directly.

During a dry interval longer than any carried in the memory of root or egg, a frog lay wrapped in its earthen chamber. Above it, the last persistent pool had become a dark patch beneath cracked mineral skin. Birds had departed. Grazers traveled beyond the horizon. Even the cicadas beneath the islands had fallen into a deeper quiet.

The frog’s body had spent nearly everything. Its heart moved, paused, and seemed unlikely to move again.

The power could have filled the plain with rain.

Instead, it waited.

Not from indifference. It felt the frog’s thirst completely: the drying membranes, the dense blood, the small chemistry approaching its limit. It also felt the heat gathering over distant water, the lifting vapor, the pressure beginning to fall beyond the horizon. Rain was already becoming possible, though possibility alone might arrive too late.

The power placed no new water in the sky.

It gave the frog one more heartbeat.

Then another, much later.

Then one more.

Nothing dramatic occurred. No light entered the chamber. No mark appeared on the ground. The gift was only a narrowing of need, a little extension of the ancient skill of absence.

Three nights afterward, clouds crossed the plain.

Rain entered old cracks. Clay darkened around the sleeper. The earthen chamber softened, first at its edges and then throughout. The frog unfolded. It climbed through collapsing mud into water no deeper than the width of its foot.

Before dawn, it called.

The note was not gratitude. The frog knew nothing of the power, and the power required nothing from it. The call was territory, readiness, rivalry, the old urgent announcement of a body returned to time.

Another frog answered from a basin nearby.

Then another.

The sound widened over the flat land until no single throat could be located.

The power listened.

It did not bless the plain with permanence. Permanence would have ended nearly everything that made the plain itself.

It blessed the plain with continuance: not the promise that each creature would survive, nor that every pool would return, but that no hand from beyond its ancient cycles would casually close all futures at once.

Rain would still fail.

Nests would still flood.

Hunters would still take the unwary and the unlucky.

Every prominence would settle. Every sharp thing would soften. Bodies would fall into the patient work of mouths too small to see.

But beneath the clay, some beginnings would remain withheld.

And when water came, they would open.
