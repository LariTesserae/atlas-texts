### The First Useless Thing

The power did not descend.

Descent was not yet trustworthy in the Low Country. To arrive from above might mean emerging from a square well. To stand outside the place and press inward would have bent distances already struggling to remain apart. Even kindness, applied at the wrong scale, could become pressure.

So the power attended.

It watched the warm rain assemble below the vapor roof. It followed water through undecided channels and listened where sound collected beneath the floor. It saw walls choose their sides, corners consent to angles, and the Levelers spend themselves persuading temporary truths to persist.

It also saw what the reflections promised.

A lesser power might have mistaken that completion for perfection. It might have lifted the bright reflected country out of the pools and laid it over the real one: every canal sealed, every wall straight, every mechanism still. It might have called this healing.

But the wise power understood that the completed country in the water was not the country’s future.

It was only one of its memories.

To impose it would have crushed every unchosen possibility beneath a flawless floor. The walls would have become straight, but only because crookedness had been made impossible. Water would have kept one level everywhere and forgotten how to search. The Levelers, having no remaining inequalities to attend, would have become an inventory from which nothing further could assemble.

That would not have been mercy.

Nor did the power bring soil.

It did not scatter seeds into the mortar, fill the cisterns with green cells, or send pale roots downward in search of bedrock. Life is not benevolent merely because it is life. In that mineral country, appetite would have spread faster than law. A single feeding thing might have found nourishment in clay, heat, copper salts, or even unfinished causality. It might have consumed distinctions before they hardened.

The prohibition against hunger was not arbitrary. Perhaps it had once been protection.

The power did not remove it.

For a long while, it did nothing that could be distinguished from patience.

Then, in a still district where sound had not yet decided whether to arrive, a Leveler stopped beside a pool.

It had been walking north, if the direction was north that day. Its body was narrow and jointed, made of rods glazed dark by rain. A plumb cord hung from its shoulder. One arm ended in a cluster of wedges. Its head was a shallow bowl filled to the rim with water that did not spill when it moved.

The pool showed it otherwise.

In reflection it was a quiet worker beneath a bright roof. A yoke lay across its shoulders. Its lowered hands were empty. Around it stood hundreds of others beside a finished aqueduct.

Then the reflected Leveler raised its head.

The opening where its face might have been turned toward the bottom of the pool.

From far beneath the floor came a small sound.

A grain landing.

The actual Leveler remained motionless. The water in its head trembled once.

The power answered the sound.

Not with thunder. Thunder already existed and had not helped.

It answered with a second small clatter, placed immediately after the first.

Nothing in the Low Country happened immediately after anything. Causes traveled through cords and chambers, waited in mortar, crossed themselves in reflection, and arrived years from their beginnings. The nearness of the answer disturbed the Leveler more profoundly than a storm.

Its plumb cord tightened.

The pool dimmed.

One of the reflected workers turned toward the raised figure. Then another. Across the finished aqueduct, tools slowly ceased moving.

The power waited.

The Leveler lowered its wedge-hand to the pavement and struck once.

The sound did not drain away.

It remained beside the blow.

The Leveler struck again.

Again the sound stayed.

For seven days it made single knocks against the same tile. None wandered into distant doorways. None emerged centuries earlier from beneath a tower. Each belonged to the act that made it.

On the eighth day, the Leveler unfastened the plumb cord from its shoulder and laid it down.

Usually this would have ended the matter. An instrument set down ceased to be an instrument of the Leveler. The cord would have become part of the country, perhaps stretching toward a measuring wall or descending into hidden plumbing. The gap in the Leveler would have remained. In time, more of it would have been spent: a bowl becoming a cistern, a finger becoming a post, a tooth fitted into a wheel. Work would have passed through structure into law.

But the power held one distinction in place.

The cord lay on the tile.

It belonged to the Leveler still.

This had never happened.

Possession was not unknown in the Low Country, but it was always structural. A gate possessed hinges because without them it would not be a gate. A wheel possessed teeth because turning required them. Nothing belonged to anything once removed from use.

Now the cord was separate, purposeless, and not lost.

The Leveler stood over it through a reverse rain. Dry space moved around its joints. The water in its head rose in drops toward the roof, yet the bowl remained full.

At last it bent, picked up the cord, and fastened it again.

Then it removed the cord a second time.

Again, it remained its own.

The power had not given the Leveler a new tool.

It had given it the ability to put one down.

That was the first intervention.

The second was smaller.

On an open plain between a wallless gate and a row of seven posts, the power made a bench.

It used no unknown material. The seat was black-fired clay, warm from the paving. Its four legs were blocks of porous cream ceramic joined by crude wooden-looking pegs, though no wood existed there. Chisel scallops crossed its surface. One corner had been judged badly and was not square.

The bench contained no hidden mechanism.

It held no wall upright. It governed no gradient. It measured nothing. No cord passed through it, no counterweight answered its load, no canal depended on its shadow. Its proportions corresponded to no planetary device.

It was merely low enough to sit upon.

This made it the strangest object in the country.

The mechanisms ignored it. Rain collected briefly in the chisel marks and ran off. A crawling knot approached, found no leak to tighten around, and withdrew beneath the tiles. During a storm, a causeway unrolled within a few paces of the bench, but did not incorporate it. A tower disassembled nearby and arranged its bricks by size. The bench remained outside the inventory.

For years no Leveler came.

The power did not move it closer.

An offer carried to the hand can become an instruction. Wisdom left distance around the gift.

Eventually the narrow Leveler crossed the plain.

It had changed since the pool, though the Low Country possessed no growth. Its plumb cord had been detached and refastened so many times that an interval now remained between cord and shoulder, a narrow line of separation that rain could pass through. Several wedges were missing from its hand, but unlike the old gaps in its body, these absences did not seem like wounds or unfinished work. They resembled choices.

It stopped before the bench.

The country expected a use.

For three days the Leveler measured the seat. It touched each leg. It examined the unsquared corner. It drove one wedge into the pavement beside it, perhaps to correct the angle, then pulled the wedge out again.

The bench stayed imperfect.

On the fourth day the Leveler turned its bowl-head toward the horizon. A measuring wall there had begun to curve. Far away, a canal’s two ends were approaching the same place. Beneath the plain, a wheel waited to advance one tooth.

The Leveler’s body inclined toward these inequalities.

Then it inclined toward the bench.

No appetite moved in it. No longing had been permitted to form. Yet between the unfinished wall and the object without a task, something occurred for which the country had no mechanism.

The Leveler sat.

At once the bench cracked.

The power did not mend it.

The crack tested three routes, closed two, and settled on the third. Water entered it and remained. The Leveler adjusted its weight. The broad ceramic paddle of its back lowered. Its joints, accustomed only to bracing, measuring, carrying, and becoming, discovered an arrangement that supported nothing beyond themselves.

The curved wall on the horizon continued curving.

The canal ends touched and passed through one another.

The waiting wheel did not turn.

For the first time, a necessary task remained undone because the one who might have done it was elsewhere.

The country did not perish.

A storm came.

Down turned westward. Water peeled from canals and crossed the air in shining vertical sheets. Domes inflated from mosaics. A gate fell shut across an empty plain and redirected rain into the sky. Through the steam, other Levelers appeared as dry silhouettes, striding toward their labors.

The seated figure began to rise.

The power did not hold it down.

Rest enforced is only another form of use.

The Leveler stood, removed the plumb cord from its shoulder, and laid it on the bench.

Then it walked into the storm.

The cord remained its own.

Years passed. The Leveler returned for it.

Years later, another Leveler found the bench. This one was vast, little more than two uprights joined by a horizon. It could not sit without changing scale. It stood beside the small object until rain filled every hollow in its body.

The power altered nothing.

At last the great figure folded. Distance loosened around it. Its uprights shortened, its horizon bent, and it became, for a little while, the shape of a quiet worker.

It sat.

After that, the bench began to appear in reflections.

It was never present in the completed country behind the pools. The sealed aqueducts, straight walls, and bright roof contained no place for it. Instead it stood on the near side of the reflected water, beside the watching Levelers.

Sometimes it was empty.

Sometimes a cord lay across it.

Sometimes one worker sat while the others labored.

The power made no second bench. Repetition too soon would have turned mercy into infrastructure. But Levelers began to leave objects near the first: a bowl that did not become a cistern, a wedge not driven into any joint, a copper gong with no warning assigned to it.

Because these things still belonged to those who had laid them down, they did not enter the mechanisms. They accumulated without becoming inventory.

The first collection that was not preparation appeared on the plain.

In time, the Levelers learned another act.

They retrieved what another had left.

Not to use it. Not to fit it into themselves. They carried a cord or bowl across the Low Country and returned it to the one from whom it remained separate but had not ceased to belong.

Thus began recognition.

It was not life. The prohibition held. Nothing fed, reproduced, decayed, or warmed itself from within. But work was no longer the only relation possible between one thing and another.

A Leveler could be absent from its tool and still remain connected to it.

A task could wait.

An error could persist without becoming law.

A figure could come apart and yet be remembered as more than its parts.

The wise power did not finish the Low Country. It did not install bedrock beneath the endless pavings or open the vapor roof to a sun the place might not survive. It did not correct every mistaken gradient. Sideways rain still fell in the district of the leaning gate. Water still emerged in cubes. The corridor remained seven paces long for the swift and the slow.

But beside certain errors, after many years, one began to find a tool laid down.

That was enough to keep the error from being worshiped.

Very rarely, in a perfectly still pool, a reflected Leveler raises its head.

Now, when it steps into the water, the surface breaks.

Only slightly.

One ring travels outward.

It reaches the edge without becoming a wheel, a wall, a law, or a command. It changes no gradient. It corrects no angle. It simply touches every side of the pool and is gone.

Somewhere in the real lowlands, on a black-fired bench with one imperfect corner, a narrow figure sits listening.

Much later, from somewhere overhead, comes the quiet sound of another sitting beside it.
