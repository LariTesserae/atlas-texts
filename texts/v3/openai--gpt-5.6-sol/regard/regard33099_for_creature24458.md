The power did not come down in fire.

Fire would have frightened the gardens, exhausted the cisterns, and forced the city to spend three generations arguing over where the scorched stone ought to drain.

It did not clear the sky. Clear weather was dangerous there, however beautiful, and beauty imposed without regard for consequence was only another kind of weather.

It did not fill every reservoir, mend every cable, seal every crack, restore the dead, or raise walls around the plateau. It could have done these things. That was the first difficulty.

The second was that it loved the city almost immediately.

Not as one loves a flawless object, but as one loves a labor that has taught itself mercy. It saw how the inhabitants halted pumps for salamanders, watched brake tenders for the onset of confidence, wrote the dead into anchor plates, and preserved crops that would never again feed them. It saw that they had made prudence into tenderness without often naming it so.

The power understood that a careless blessing would ruin them.

An inexhaustible spring would gather authority around whoever controlled its outlet. Unbreakable cables would encourage impossible loads. Immortal retaining walls would transfer strain into the living rock. Perfect weather would kill the fog gardens, empty the moisture courts, and turn a thousand forms of knowledge into superstition within a generation.

Even rescue could become a form of destruction if it made the rescued place unrecognizable.

So the power entered as a small person in a patched gray coat.

No one saw it arrive. One morning it was standing in the eastern moisture office with both hands resting on the counter, waiting while a clerk completed a dispute concerning three gutters, a pear tree, and an illegally insulated bakery flue.

The stranger was compact, dark-haired, and neither young nor old. Its coat bore repairs in the colors of many occupations: green resin at one shoulder, pale cistern stitching along the cuffs, red foundry thread across the back, a rim worker’s bright blue lining. No district used all these repairs together.

When its turn came, the clerk asked the nature of its claim.

“Unacknowledged contribution,” said the stranger.

“To which reservoir?”

“All of them, eventually.”

The clerk looked up.

“Source surface?”

“Not a surface.”

“Then seepage.”

“Not seepage.”

“Thaw release?”

“No.”

“Imported water?”

“Not in the customary sense.”

The clerk laid down her pen.

“Water must come by some path.”

“Yes,” said the stranger. “That is why I am here.”

It produced a folded damp impression.

The paper was old, perhaps older than paper should remain usable in the plateau air. Across it spread a branching stain resembling the drainage plan of the eastern districts, except that the lines continued beyond the escarpment. They crossed open air toward the uninhabited plateau and descended beneath both masses of stone, joining far below the visible cliffs.

The clerk first suspected fraud, then religious enthusiasm, then a joke by engineers. Each possibility required a different form, so she called her supervisor.

By noon there were seven moisture clerks in the room.

By evening there were engineers.

At midnight a seed-keeper arrived carrying a black-leaved moss in a covered bowl. The moss had always curled toward distant vibration. In the presence of the stranger’s map it opened flat.

No one asked the stranger what it was. This was partly courtesy and partly strategy. A being that wanted to announce its nature would do so without prompting. A being that did not could not be trusted to answer accurately.

Instead, the chief engineer tapped the paper with one blunt finger.

“This indicates a fault.”

“It indicates several.”

“Active?”

“Everything is active if one waits correctly.”

The engineer disliked the answer, though he could not dispute it.

The stranger touched a line beneath the oldest districts.

“This one will move first.”

“How soon?”

“Soon enough to prepare. Too late to ignore.”

“That is not a measure.”

“It can be.”

The stranger borrowed a piece of chalk and marked a short line on the office wall. Then it drew a second line the width of a fingernail away.

“When these meet,” it said, “the eastern basin must be empty.”

The engineers examined the wall. It was solid plateau stone, internal, sheltered, and not known to move.

“How long?” the clerk asked.

“That depends on what you do.”

This answer was worse, but also more useful.

They turned to ask another question and found that the stranger had gone.

Its wet footprints ended at a locked archive door.

---

The investigation lasted nine years.

No proclamation was made. The city had long experience with warnings too uncertain to spread publicly and too grave to conceal. Committees were established under other names. Old tunnels were sounded. Reservoir levels were altered by fractions. Lift schedules changed. Gardeners planted white root-mats along suspected fractures and inspected them for tearing.

The chalk lines moved together.

Not steadily. Some months they widened. In winter they nearly touched, then separated during thaw. Several engineers declared the mark an irresponsible instrument. Others began sleeping in the eastern wheelhouses.

Deep beneath the warm gardens, miners opened a sealed gallery and found a cable passing through it.

The cable was black, thick as a person’s wrist, and drawn taut toward the far plateau.

No plan recorded it. No living worker knew its alloy. It emerged from one wall and entered another without anchorage, sheave, or visible purpose. When struck, it gave no sound anyone could hear.

A brake tender placed a cup of water beside it.

Ripples appeared.

Three close rings. A pause. Two. A pause. Three.

The pattern repeated.

Among the seed-keepers was an elderly woman who knew a children’s song from the far plateau. She listened with her fingertips on the cup.

“That is not machinery,” she said.

“What is it?”

“Someone being patient.”

Signals were sent back by tension: first the civic identification code, then the old code used by cliff crews, then a simple sequence taught to children lost in fog.

We are here.

For six days there was nothing.

On the seventh, every eastern cistern received a single warm drop.

The stranger’s claim was entered into the record.

Source: undetermined neighboring catchment.

Obligation: reciprocal.

The clerks disliked the wording but could devise none more accurate.

---

Contact did not save the eastern basin.

The far plateau possessed no device to halt the fault, no forgotten engine capable of holding a mountain together. Its inhabitants had fewer people, less iron, deeper reservoirs, and their own approaching failures. They had maintained the buried cable without knowing where it led. For centuries they had sent signals into stone and received only weather.

What they did have were records.

They sent descriptions of an older collapse on their northern rim: the order in which walls sweated, mosses paled, salamanders abandoned lower tanks, and warm air rose through mines. They sent plans for flexible cistern joints and houses that could be dismantled from the roof downward. They sent spores of a colorless fungus whose threads glowed faintly when stretched, making strain visible inside walls.

In return, the city sent brake designs, pear grafts, medicinal molds, songs for obsolete pumps, and the names of those who had kept listening when no answer came.

No bridge was rebuilt. The distance was too great and the old anchorages too weak.

Instead, they learned to pass small things through the earth.

Not quickly. Not easily. A message might require a month. A sealed seed tube could take a year, drawn along the buried line by mechanisms neither city fully understood. But after centuries of silence, slowness did not seem like absence.

The chalk marks continued to approach.

The eastern districts were taken apart.

This was done without calling it evacuation until evacuation was already well underway. Roof orchards were pruned and moved. Cistern salamanders traveled in padded glass vessels carried against children’s bodies for warmth. Doorframes were numbered. Pipes were capped, drained, and stacked. The dead were unscrewed from anchor plates and worked into new ones farther inland.

Some refused to leave.

The power came to these people also, though not always in the same shape.

To a retired engineer it appeared as a six-toed fox sitting beyond a broken stair. The fox looked backward while its front paws continued ahead.

To a child it was a voice inside a wall asking for help carrying a cup.

To a lichen warden it was an unfamiliar black growth forming a line across the floor toward safety.

To a woman who had lived fifty years above the cliff, it appeared simply as the smell of lowland rain. She packed before dawn and told no one why.

A few still remained. Wisdom does not abolish refusal, and benevolence does not always violate it.

For them, bright coats were left where they could be found. Bells were tested. Lines were secured. Their names were written down before anyone needed to write them in grief.

Then, on a still morning during heavy condensation, the chalk marks met.

No thunder followed.

The eastern basin lowered by the width of a hand.

Water climbed the cellar walls. A row of abandoned houses tilted toward one another until their upper balconies touched. Deep underground, something broke with a note so low that the city felt it through knees, teeth, pipes, and beds.

The basin remained there for eleven days.

On the twelfth, it went into the cloud.

Not all at once. The rim gardens descended first, carrying their nets upright like sails. Then the old wheelhouse leaned outward. Empty cisterns split open, shining with mineral interiors no one had seen since their construction. Streets folded. Towers entered the mist floor by floor. The buried cable drew tight enough to sing.

This time everyone heard it.

From beyond the void came an answering note.

The last occupied structure was a little watch house on black iron legs. A figure in a patched gray coat stood on its balcony with one hand resting on the rail.

Several witnesses saw it.

The figure did not hold the plateau together. It did not lift the falling district back into place. It only remained until the final bright-lined coat had crossed onto safe ground.

Then the watch house descended.

The cable note ceased.

For a long while the city heard stones falling through cloud.

None were heard to strike below.

---

Afterward, people argued over whether the stranger had caused the collapse, predicted it, softened it, or merely used knowledge available to any sufficiently attentive engineer.

This argument continues.

The engineers point out that every intervention can be explained by stress, hydrology, forgotten infrastructure, collective vigilance, and the proper interpretation of biological indicators.

The seed-keepers agree.

Then they add that this is how a wise power ought to intervene.

The eastern loss became a new edge. Fog nets were built there, but farther back than tradition would have placed them. The flexible joints from the sister city became standard. Every public plan began to include not only the expected life of a structure but the manner in which it should be dismantled. Moisture law was revised to recognize contributions whose paths could not yet be demonstrated.

Most importantly, the city established an office of distant obligations.

Its clerks keep records of water, signals, seeds, warnings, and assistance received from unknown sources. They are authorized to repay debts before the creditor has been identified.

Above the entrance is an inscription:

**NOTHING ARRIVES WITHOUT A PATH.  
NOT EVERY PATH IS VISIBLE.**

Sometimes the small person in the patched coat is seen in the waiting room, though never by more than one clerk at a time.

No one attempts to detain it.

They offer it a hot drink, assign it a number, and ask the nature of its claim.

Usually it reports some minor defect: a blocked gutter, a tired pin, a patch of moss planted in the wrong salts. Once it complained that the city’s newest maps left too little room for what had not happened yet.

The clerks took this seriously.

The power has done no great miracle since.

It moves through inspection gaps. It places unease in the minds of brake tenders when the gauges look calm. It leads salamanders away from water several hours before contamination. It sometimes adds a contrasting stitch to a coat whose hidden seam is about to fail.

It does not end death, weather, labor, error, or grief.

It gives them paths.

And on certain clear evenings, mirrors flash between the two plateaus. The messages are slow, public, and frequently interrupted by fog. Courtships, engineering disputes, songs, weather notices, and bad jokes cross the blue distance.

High above the low country, two cities continue their quiet work.

Between them, where no bridge remains, the mist now gathers along an invisible line. Droplets bead in open air, tremble, and pass one to another across the void—too little water to own, enough to show the way.
