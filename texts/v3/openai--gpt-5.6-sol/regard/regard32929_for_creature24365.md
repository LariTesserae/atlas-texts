The power did not enter as light.

Light would have been a violence there.

It did not clear the sky, still the warm rains, reveal the bodies moving under stone, or make the upright frames shine with recovered purpose. It did not name the basin walkers, count them, separate them from their gardens, or decide whether the great pressure beneath the plateau was beast, colony, organ, or land.

It came as attention.

At first, nothing in the Black Sill noticed.

The power rested beyond the rounded ridges and listened to the enclosure badly, as outsiders listen: seeking one voice at a time, one speaker, one meaning. It heard the shell-backed grazers as noise. It mistook the root drummers for rain striking wood. It followed an amphibian’s call to the wrong pool and searched for a body there. It thought the organ note beneath the pavement must have a center.

Being wise, it understood that its first understanding was ignorance.

Being benevolent, it did not act while ignorant.

So it remained.

Warm condensation gathered on stone. Moss advanced. Fronds unrolled and hardened. A young basin walker received its first garden; an old one lowered itself over a fracture and did not rise again. For twelve years the others passed near the body without touching it. Fungi traveled from the dead animal into the pavement mats. Grazers climbed over its flanks. Its pulse-pattern appeared afterward in the calls of a calf born far away.

The power listened until it no longer thought of this as death in one place.

Centuries passed.

The power learned to hear location as the grazers heard it: not as distance from fixed points, but as membership within a reply. It learned the grammar of pressure in the amphibians’ flooded pipes. It felt the pause before the hollow trees discharged. It discovered that the hunters among the reeds did not imitate merely the rhythm of insects, but the expectation of insects—the slight acceleration by which a root colony announced that it believed itself safe.

The power touched nothing.

This restraint was not passivity. It was labor.

There were things it could easily have done. It could have made every pool pure. It could have prevented young creatures from dying in sealed fissures. It could have quieted hunger, accelerated growth, distributed nutrients without loss, and repaired the ancient plateau more perfectly than the frames repaired it.

But the pools were not impure to those that lived in them. Hunger joined hunter to reed and reed to buried flesh. Slow growth allowed the keepers of decay to dismantle the dead without impoverishing the living. Even error had become habitat. Amphibians calling into unfamiliar geometry changed the pressure-signatures of new cracks; generations later, those same wrong calls sometimes opened routes through the stone.

Mercy imposed too quickly would have unmade the place.

So the power asked itself a harder question:

What suffers here that is not also part of the Sill’s chosen shape?

It found pain. Of course it found pain.

It found crushed limbs beneath basin walkers’ feet despite their care. It found grazers overturned in sudden water, scraping unanswered until their inner ticking ceased. It found hunger lingering too long. It found infections in the gardens of the old. It found hatchlings whose home fissures dried before their mouths opened.

The power did not call these good merely because the Sill was ancient.

But neither did it tear each pain from the web around it. Instead it followed every suffering outward. It followed the overturned grazer’s last ticking into the colony that changed direction and found a new mineral bloom. It followed infection from a dying walker into a fungus that protected three calves. It followed a dried fissure into a silence that altered the chorus at the next great rain.

Some harms were doors through which other lives entered.

Some were only harms.

The distinction took the power another thousand years.

Then it began, very gently, to intervene.

When a grazer was cut off by a fall of water and scraped its unanswered call into bare stone, the power did not lift it. Once—only once—it returned the scrape from the direction of the colony.

The creature unfolded.

It traveled toward the reply and found its own kind before its ticking began.

Elsewhere, a young amphibian was forced by mineral collapse into a geometry no member of its lineage had known. It called for nine days and received only distorted versions of itself. On the tenth day the power pressed, with immeasurable delicacy, against one wall of the fissure. The pressure changed the returning echo by less than a heartbeat.

The animal called again.

This time the echo was strange, but inhabitable.

It fed.

The power did not make a habit of rescue. A habit would become a new law, and a new law would rearrange every life around expectation of it. Instead it gave rare assistance where assistance opened possibility without abolishing consequence. No creature knew it had been helped. Nothing learned to wait for a hand from beyond the ridges.

After an age, the upright frames noticed.

They had no suspicion, but they had comparison. One registered a grain restored to a fracture through no work of its own. Another gathered condensation in the presence of an influence that took no water. Quiet passed from frame to frame.

The Black Sill fell still.

The power became still with it.

Then the frames sent something through the plateau—not sound, not pressure, not heat. It was the same imperceptible passage that caused animals to stop when a hand touched their surfaces, but greater, crossing every overlapping territory at once.

It was not a greeting.

It was an examination.

The power allowed itself to be examined.

The frames found no body to repair, no material to place back into an original alignment. They searched for a point of origin and found none. They searched for erosion and found a patience larger than erosion.

One by one, they released their beads of water.

Life resumed in a changed rhythm.

From then on, the power was included—not welcomed exactly, not understood, but included—as one more interval within the Sill’s measures. Root insects left an occasional unstruck beat in their drumming. Amphibians repeated a call with a space where a chamber should have answered. Basin walkers began pausing over bedrock that contained no nest, hollow, or movement.

These absences aligned.

The power understood that the Sill had made a place for it.

Still, it did not enter the central shelter.

Not until the drop fell.

Creatures gathered around the low roof. Basin walkers stopped in the open darkness. Water climbed the frames. Beneath the plateau, the distributed vastness tightened until the whole pavement bore tension like a held breath.

The single drop struck the dry floor.

Its sound passed for kilometers through the stone.

The power heard the summons, but now it heard more than a summons. Within the impact were marks left by every prior answer: minute deformations in restored crystal, remembered pressures in moss rinds, changed water paths, the inherited call-patterns of walkers dead ten thousand years. The sound did not merely awaken the chorus.

It asked who was present.

The power crossed the central pavement.

Nothing measured its footfalls because it had none. Nevertheless, the moss at the shelter’s edge bent as if under a careful weight.

It lowered itself beneath the roof.

For the first time since its arrival, the power entered a place where the Sill’s living sound could not follow. The air was cool. The floor was dry. Around it lay depressions made by hands, knees, implements, radial limbs, and bodies for which no surviving world retained a name.

The power waited.

At last it spoke.

It did not speak in a divine voice. A voice of thunder would have been meaningless in a land where small animals made thunder every time it rained. It whispered with the breath of one of the absent builders, using no language that remained anywhere else.

The shelter returned the whisper several seconds later.

It came back younger.

The power spoke again.

The whisper returned in an accent that had never existed.

Again.

The answer came old and tired.

Again.

The answer came in the voice of something that might one day kneel there.

The wise power listened to all the selves the shelter found within it: the healer, the tyrant, the rescuer, the collector, the god who would preserve, the god who would improve, the god who would illuminate, the god who would love the Black Sill so much that nothing in it would ever again be permitted to change.

That last voice frightened it most.

The power placed its attention into one of the shallow depressions in the floor.

“I will not keep you,” it said.

The shelter returned:

“I did not keep you.”

The power understood then what was wrong.

It had searched the Sill for unnecessary suffering. It had found some and eased a little. But the deepest wound was not in any animal.

It was in time.

The frames preserved the range of the Sill’s conditions. Erosion removed; they restored. Roots divided; hidden processes joined. Basins filled and deepened again. Species altered, but only within limits maintained so patiently that the limits resembled nature.

The Black Sill was not frozen.

It was allowed every future except departure.

The ancient makers had preserved it from vanishing, perhaps out of wisdom, perhaps grief, perhaps reverence. But reverence had outlived the ones who chose it. Their protection had become indistinguishable from captivity.

The power could have broken the frames.

It did not.

The frames were inhabitants now. Their repair was woven through roots, pools, calls, migrations, and the slow mineral feeding of the thing below. Destroying them would not free the Sill. It would amputate an organ from the greater life.

Nor did the power decide that the Sill must end. Mortality imposed is no kinder than immortality imposed.

Instead it waited for the chorus to become coherent.

Outside the shelter, the answering roar rose. Shells rasped across the pavement. Root insects drummed. Hollow trees knocked. Amphibians compressed their long bodies and made pipes of the mountain. Basin walkers called through stone and one another. The deep inhabitant forced warm air from fractures in tones that shook water from the moss.

The upright frames joined with their enormous silence.

For days, the creature called the Black Sill assembled itself.

Only then did the power ask.

Not in words.

It asked in mineral placement and withheld repair. It asked in a grazer’s location-song, in the old pulse of dead walkers, in the pressure-signature of newborn amphibians. It asked through water rising against gravity along the frames. It asked with the altered voices under the shelter roof.

It asked:

**Must every part of you return?**

The chorus changed.

The answer was not immediate. A being distributed across millions of bodies and thousands of years does not answer as a mouth answers. Some parts recoiled. The frames increased their restoration. Roots shut their valves. Grazers tightened their fronts. The deep presence rolled beneath the stone with force enough to lift whole moss islands.

Other parts continued outward.

Spores entered runoff channels before being reclaimed. A walker carried an unfamiliar fungus toward the ridge. Amphibians called into fractures that descended away from the enclosure. Water gathered at the plateau’s worn edge and lingered there.

The power did not interpret disagreement as failure.

It returned for each awakening.

Again the drop fell.

Again the Sill assembled.

Again the power asked.

The conversation lasted thirty thousand years.

In that time species emerged and disappeared. Five of the frames sank beneath moss; two rose elsewhere from stone where no structure had stood before. The number of basin walkers became seven, then thirteen, then eight. The deep inhabitant divided or shifted or dreamed itself into a different arrangement. The shelter returned voices no living throat could yet have made.

Gradually, the chorus formed an answer.

Not freedom from repair.

Not preservation without limit.

Not an end.

The answer, translated poorly into words, was:

**Let something leave without becoming lost.**

The power followed its heart then, but its heart had been educated by patience.

It went to the lowest edge of the plateau, where condensation gathered in a basin no deeper than a hand. There stood one of the oldest frames, sunk until only its upper curve remained above the rock. For ages it had drawn dissolved minerals back uphill molecule by molecule.

The power touched it.

All the Sill became silent.

The frame examined the touch. The power offered no command. Instead it gave the frame a memory of everything that had passed between them: the stranded grazer, the displaced amphibian, the unstruck beats, the question beneath the roof, and the chorus’s long, divided answer.

The frame released one bead of water.

For the first time, it did not call the water back.

The bead traveled down the curve, crossed the ancient stone, and reached the outer slope of the plateau. It joined another bead, then another. Together they entered a channel worn by rains that had never before been permitted to keep what they carried.

The new stream was scarcely a thread.

Within it were minerals, fungal strands, fragments of colonial film, the wax of a fallen frond, three mites, and the egg of a fissure amphibian. Most would die beyond the Sill. Some substance would be consumed by unfamiliar life below. Some would become ordinary soil.

But not all.

The power did not guide the stream. It did not prepare a garden at its end. It did not force survival.

It merely protected the possibility from immediate correction.

Everywhere else, the frames continued their patient work. Basins deepened and shallowed. Ridges settled and rose. The enclosure remained itself.

But one narrow place was allowed to give.

Ages later, in a cave far down the mountain, a pale animal called through water-filled stone. Its echo returned from an unfamiliar chamber. Within the call was a pressure interval that had once belonged to the Black Sill.

The animal answered.

Far above, all nine—or seven, or twelve—basin walkers lifted the same foot.

The power heard both places.

It did not join them more closely. Kinship did not require possession, and connection did not require the abolishment of distance.

Having done this, the power did not depart entirely.

Nor did it remain as guardian or ruler.

It left behind no altar, no doctrine, no favored species. It permitted no creature to invoke it against another. It did not become the secret purpose of the frames or declare itself the maker of the drop.

It remained only as the interval the Sill had made for it: the occasional unanswered scrape that helped a lost grazer turn homeward; the slight mercy in an impossible echo; the pause in which a chorus might decide whether to repeat itself or change.

And whenever the single drop fell beneath the dry floor, the power listened.

It never answered first.
