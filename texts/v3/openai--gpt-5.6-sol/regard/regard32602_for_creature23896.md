The power does not begin by bringing light.

Light would draw hunters. It would thicken the algae, alter the films, and turn the basin toward another kind of life. Nor does it bring warmth. Warmth would loosen old balances, wake hungers that the cold has kept slow, and make the resident a stranger in the only world its body knows.

It does not still the water. In stillness the eggs would suffocate beneath settling silt. Food would cease arriving in the eddies. The narrow spaces would fill and remain filled. What appears to be the basin’s cruelty is also its circulation.

The power understands this before it acts.

It attends through a full turning of the tide, then through a winter, then through the time required for a white quartz cobble to move from one side of a moraine to the other. Nothing in its attention is hurried. It feels each current not as one force but as the meeting of innumerable pressures. It follows mineral dust into the mouths of worms, follows worms into fish, follows fish into seals, and seals into the long hunger beyond the basin. It perceives the resident beneath the stones, and also everything the resident eats, everything that eats it, and the dark films carried harmlessly upon its back.

It sees that there is no single thread it can pull without tightening another.

So it does not rescue the creature from its world.

It blesses the world’s looseness.

The blessing is too slight to become a law that anything living could discover. It is no wall against death, no promise that eggs will hatch or that a traveler will find the bottom. It is only a small bias placed within events that might otherwise fall either way.

Where three stones settle, there is sometimes room beneath them.

Where a shell plate is about to be pinned beyond recovery, the smallest pebble turns.

Where silt might seal every passage into a hollow, one narrow seam remains open to water.

Where two currents cancel for no more than a breath, the breath lasts long enough for a feeler to extend, for a hook to close, for a soft new shell to pass under cover.

The power does not do this every time. To do so would make the resident numerous beyond the basin’s means. It would empty the gravel of amphipods and worms, burden the films, and turn a hidden life into a consuming one. Benevolence without measure would become another storm.

Instead, when winters follow too closely, when a whole reach of gravel is buried, when too many egg clusters are scoured from their stones, the power leans almost imperceptibly upon chance.

A cod turns toward another flicker.

A rolling boulder strikes a buried slab and comes to rest one finger’s width higher than it might have.

One hatchling, carried over a channel where all the others descend into swift water, catches upon a thread of ragged kelp. The kelp tears. The hatchling falls. Beneath it is a field of cobbles with openings of exactly the width its body requires.

No thunder marks these things. No gratitude rises. The resident has no organ with which to perceive mercy as mercy.

Yet the power desires to give it something more than survival.

Not speech. Speech would be useless in the grinding dark.

Not foresight. To know every coming storm would turn each interval of stillness into fear.

Not the wish for another world.

Instead, the power touches the oldest sense the creature possesses: the sense of contact.

Thereafter—or perhaps it has always been thereafter—when one of the residents is torn into open water and tumbles with its pale underside exposed, its hooks searching through emptiness, the first firm thing it catches carries a quality no instrument could measure. The pressure of stone against hook is not merely resistance. For the smallest instant, it is answer.

When an egg-bearing animal presses beneath a cobble and cleans grit from the dark capsules, the stone above remains only stone: cold, massive, without intention. But the animal’s body does not experience its weight as abandonment.

When an old resident settles for the last time, plates whitened at the edges, limbs no longer able to follow the tide, it finds a narrow chamber where the water enters softly. It braces by habit. The hooks loosen. Other small lives move near it, feeding and hiding, unaware. The power does not prevent the ending. It keeps the current gentle until the need to hold has passed.

Then the body opens to the basin.

Nothing is wasted. The films continue feeding. Amphipods enter between the plates. Lime returns to shell grit; dark mineral stains return to sediment. In time, only a blunt fragment remains beneath a cobble.

The power does not preserve even that.

What it preserves is the possibility of the kind.

Through an age of storms, enough hollows remain. Through shifts of salt and ice, enough young descend. If the basin changes beyond bearing, the power does not freeze it in an ancient form. Instead it leaves passages—slow routes along cold channels, gravel carried outward by ice, deep shelves where the same arrangement of pressure and darkness may arise again. The resident is not commanded to follow. Some do. Most do not. Life spreads as it always has: without knowing where it goes.

No temple appears on the floor. No unweathered sign defies the second rule. The power places nothing there that must resist being rounded.

Its work is visible only in continuance: in the fact that, after devastation, something jointed moves beneath the stones; that a pale feeler enters a newly opened seam; that an animal far from where it hatched finds the downstream side of a cobble and lowers its feeding fringe into the eddy.

The basin remains cold.

It remains dark.

Stones roll. Young are eaten. Shells are crushed. Whole generations disappear without witness.

But the place is not required to become gentle in order to be held in benevolence. Nor is the creature required to become greater than itself.

It lives as it was made to live: by yielding, fastening, releasing, and fastening again.

Only now, hidden within the vast indifference of water and stone, there is a patience equal to its own.

Its life is still an attachment between rearrangements.

But not every attachment is accidental.
