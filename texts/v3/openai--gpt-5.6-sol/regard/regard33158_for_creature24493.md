The power would not descend as sunlight.

Sunlight would be too blunt a blessing here. It would warm the black walls unevenly, wake frost from the cracks, draw the moss dry, and call forth growth the thin soil could not sustain. Nor would the power still the mountain. A mountain wholly stilled is a mountain killed in another fashion, removed from time and made into an exhibit.

It would come as attention.

For a long while it would do nothing but enter the cleft in all the ways the cleft permitted: as pressure in the fissures, as water held between moss leaves, as the minute warmth where fungus touched root. It would feel the underground detonations pass through every layer. It would learn which fractures belonged to the mountain’s own settling and which had been driven into it. It would follow the metallic breath backward through cracks, shafts, ducts, galleries, and excavated chambers.

It would find the people below.

They would not appear to it as an enemy species. It would see the worker leaning into the vibration of a drill, the mechanic wiping stone dust from a gauge, the surveyor revising a map already obsolete. It would know who had children, who feared the next shift, who had learned not to mention the new tremor in the eastern wall. It would see the contracts and production schedules, the distant offices, the hunger for wages, metal, heat, continuance. Wisdom would prevent it from calling any one of these things the cause.

Benevolence would prevent it from burying them.

So the first thing the power would give would be a warning no one could dismiss.

On a night of dense cloud, when the next charge had been set beneath the ridge, every sensor in the mine would begin recording the same low harmonic. Old gauges, new digital instruments, a mechanic’s loose wrench against a steel cabinet—all would register it. The sound would rise from below the deepest working and descend from above it at once. On the monitoring screens, thousands of small stress events would arrange themselves into the shape of the hidden fracture system.

For the first time the cleft would appear on their maps not as blank surface terrain, but as the uppermost chamber of a vast, branching instability.

The charge would not be fired.

An engineer, frightened enough to become honest, would order the sector cleared. Hours later, without explosion or injury, a buried wall would shear across one of the empty galleries. Stone would fill the place where workers had been expected to stand.

Afterward, no one would be able to call the warning superstition.

Investigations would follow. More instruments would be brought. The black microbial films would be sampled where mine air emerged through the ridge. In laboratories far away, people would discover that some of the fungi bound metals in structures not previously described, and that the bacteria in the newest basalt lived by exchanges considered impossible at that temperature. Reports would be written. Their language would be dry—*unusual consortium*, *high geochemical specificity*, *surface expression of deep fracture habitat*—but dry words can also become shelter.

The power would not make the place famous. Fame would bring boots, flags, sample bags, camera platforms, paths. Instead, it would let the significance of the cleft become known only where it needed to be known: among those able to redraw a boundary.

The workings beneath it would be abandoned.

Not the whole mine. Wisdom would ask for the smallest sufficient mercy. Tunnels under the hanging cleft would be backfilled with their own waste stone. New cuts would bend toward older, more stable rock. Ventilation would be rerouted to the barren side of the ridge, where heated air could disperse without passing through the root mats. Blasting would continue elsewhere, but in measured intervals, announced through stone by softer preliminary pulses.

The great mechanical pulse beneath the green commonwealth would diminish.

At first this would not feel like peace.

The moss, accustomed to releasing water at each tremor, would remain swollen. Spores would cling past the hour in which they had always fallen. Sedges would wait for pressure reversals that did not come. In the newly quiet seams, droplets would grow too heavy and descend all at once. The life of the cleft had made machinery part of its weather. Mercy, arriving suddenly, could become another disturbance.

The power would understand this.

It would not silence the ridge in a single day. For a season, harmless vibrations would continue through the backfilling work. Then they would become less frequent. Geological ticks, frost splits, rock settling, and thunder would gradually reclaim the calendar. The plants would alter their responses. Some would fail. Most would not. Nothing living is protected from all loss, and no wise protection would require the cleft to remain exactly as it had been at the instant it was noticed.

The power would place no wall around it.

Walls concentrate water. Railings invite approach. A roof would deny the cloud. Even a blessing carved into stone would become a wound and later a destination.

Instead, it would make one small adjustment in the depths: a pressure path no wider than a root, joining three dangerous fissures to an old chimney on the exposed face of the mountain. Warm sulfurous breath would turn toward that emptier slope. The cleft’s cracks would still exhale, but less poisonously. Not clean air—cleanliness was never the nature of the place—only air in which the boundary between tolerance and injury shifted slightly toward life.

It would add no animals.

The absence of wings, feet, mouths, and eggs was not a defect to be repaired. The plants did not require a bird to validate them, nor did the fungi wait for an insect to complete the idea of a world. The commonwealth had arisen under its own terms. The power would not confuse benevolence with completion.

It would not make the flowers brighter.

It would not make the shrubs taller.

It would not turn the cleft into a garden.

Its gift would be margin.

A little more time between fracture and burial. A little less metal in the condensation. A chance for roots to hold before the next slab fell. Enough quiet for the organisms to discover rhythms not dictated entirely by engines. Enough disturbance to remain themselves.

Years would pass.

The freshest basalt would dull beneath black films. Liverworts would establish on stones that once shook too often to hold them. Moss bridges would thicken and sometimes collapse under their own water. Fern crowns would rise through rubble. The buried red cords of shrubs would travel farther than any creature with legs could have walked in that narrow place.

The mine would still be heard on certain days, faint and distant. The people below would still labor. The mountain would still fracture from weather, gravity, heat, and its own deep unrest. A slab would fall eventually and erase a broad green patch. The power would not catch it.

But around the fallen stone, bright margins would begin again.

If anyone asked what miracle had occurred, there would be no single answer. The workers would speak of instrumentation and a narrowly avoided disaster. The engineers would speak of revised extraction plans. Biologists would speak of a protected extremophile habitat. Accountants would complain about an inaccessible sector. Moss would hold water. Fungi would enter fresh cracks. The mountain would continue making and unmaking its forms.

The wise power would remain only as the difference between exploitation and extinction, between warning and catastrophe, between a world used wholly and a world with something left over.

Then, seeing that the cleft required no witness to be real, it would withdraw its attention without withdrawing its care.

Cloud would press down.

Water would tremble on every tip.

In the softened dark, the low green being would spread by the width of a hand.
