The power did nothing at first.

This was not indifference.

It had known worlds where mercy required an instant: a hand beneath a falling moon, a word spoken before the knife descended. It had known others where kindness meant fire, severing, the clean destruction of what could not be allowed to continue.

Here, haste would have been another kind of violence.

So it remained above the salt plain through one full turning of the seasons, though to it the year passed easily within a thought. It attended to every draft entering the fissure. It followed heat through the polished shafts, felt each shutter clap and each bridge answer, tasted the trace gases drawn from the depth. It learned the moving squares of light. It learned which garden vents were opened for bitter fans, which ropes bore the weight of sleeping households, which walls had been thinned by acids into dangerous beauty.

It learned the inhabitants through contact.

Not their spoken names; those belonged to covered ears, to lovers and children and the dying. It learned instead the names carried by structures: the uneven three-step of the mirror-turner with a stiffened left ankle; the cautious weight of an elder who tested each bridge twice; the rapid, irregular tremor of a child who had recently cut its tether and had not yet learned fear.

It learned the city’s history as the city kept it.

The narrowing chamber that meant famine.

The sealed cavity that meant mercy.

The pipe lengthened after a generation of upper-district children were lost in a noon reversal.

The brace deliberately weakened so a condemned quarter would fall outward, away from the inhabited walls.

The power read every alteration. It found errors, lies, omissions, revisions. It found acts of vanity disguised as necessity and acts of love recorded merely as maintenance. It found that the city had saved itself ten thousand times, usually by yielding.

Only then did it turn its attention below the Last Floor.

There, the power discovered that the forbidden teaching was nearest to the truth.

The hollow was not an organ, exactly. Nor was the salt plain a body in any sense the inhabitants would have recognized. Yet deep beneath the basin lay a life so old and slow that mountains had risen and worn flat during one of its long contractions.

It was spread through mineral strata in translucent roots and chambers many leagues wide. It fed on pressure, heat, reactive salts, and gases from buried stone. What the city called its night wind was one small exchange in that vast metabolism. What the deep listeners felt as pauses were intervals between movements of something whose moments were longer than dynasties.

The hollow was a wound in it.

A minor wound, once.

Long ago, some convulsion beneath the basin had torn open one of the being’s upper chambers. Escaping gases carved shafts through the salt. The fissure widened. Light entered. Strange black-green growths appeared around the edges of the injury, feeding on its exhalations.

Later came the people.

Whether they had climbed from below, descended from above, or assembled slowly from the chemistry of the wound, even the power could not determine. Origins are not always a hidden fact waiting to be uncovered. Sometimes a beginning is distributed across so many changes that no honest boundary can be drawn around it.

But the danger was clear.

The ancient being was healing.

It had been healing for thousands of years, slowly enough that the city mistook the change for ordinary erosion. Deep tissues were thickening around the shaft. Buried chambers were shifting. Pressure gathered beneath the Last Floor.

In another nine generations, perhaps ten, the lowest pipes would close.

The city would first become quiet.

Then it would be crushed.

The power could have prevented this. It could have fixed every grain of salt in place and held the wound open forever. It could have made the bridges unbreakable, the ledges eternal, the winds obedient.

But the life beneath would remain wounded without end.

It could have moved the city whole, lifting buildings, gardens, ropes, pipes, histories, and inhabitants into another fissure. Yet a city transplanted without its causes is not the same city. Its breath would become imitation. Its records would cease to speak accurately. The people might survive while the greater part of what they were became a relic around them.

It could have awakened the deep being and commanded it to spare them.

But command, even benevolent command, teaches little except dependence on the commander.

So the power sought a third fastening.

At sunset on the last day of its listening, the city reached its crescendo.

The pipes bellowed. Shutters battered their frames. Amber bladders hissed out aromatic gas. Every bridge strained toward the center and recoiled. The Last Floor rose on its ropes like a dark net lifted by an unseen hand.

Then came the pause.

One breath.

Dust hung in the crossed beams.

Two breaths.

Hands rested on walls throughout the city.

Three.

The wind should have risen.

It did not.

The inhabitants waited.

A fourth breath passed in stillness.

Across the hollow, scales lifted on thousands of bodies. Gardeners clung flat to their ledges. Children were pulled close. The priests of proportion stared at one another with their outer lids sealed. At the Last Floor, listeners lowered themselves against the ropes.

A fifth breath came.

Then, very softly, something struck the city from below.

Not a sound. A pattern.

It entered through the deepest pipes, traveled up the walls, divided at old anchor points, crossed rope bridges, passed through floors and sleeping mats and the sensitive soles of every inhabitant. It was too large for any single material. Saltstone carried one part, rope another, fired clay a third. The copper mirrors trembled with an overtone that could be felt through the teeth.

The pattern said:

**ATTEND.**

After it came a sequence no trained relayer could have produced, because it required blows separated by years.

Yet the whole sequence arrived at once.

The inhabitants felt the ancient wound opening. They felt gas escaping through newborn fractures. They felt the first gardens adhere to raw salt. They felt tiny bodies crossing primitive cords where no bridge yet stood. They felt rooms multiplied, nets lowered, children tied into households, the dead made into tools. They felt the buried tissues gather gradually around the hollow.

They felt the coming closure.

There was no image of catastrophe. The message did not need one. Every citizen knew what happened when a vent narrowed too far.

Panic traveled through the city in broken hammer bursts.

The power did not suppress it.

Warnings were struck. Households sealed doors. Upper authorities issued contradictory commands. Several wealthy families began dismantling rooms before there was anywhere to take them. At the Last Floor, one contemplative cut a forbidden lamp from its hook and prepared to lower it into the shaft.

Then the deep pipes struck again.

**ATTEND.**

This time the message carried a question.

It was phrased in the oldest civic grammar: the rhythm used when a failing district asked whether another would share its load.

Not *Will you obey?*

Not *Do you wish to be saved?*

It asked:

**WHERE MAY THE WEIGHT GO?**

The city had never answered as one body. It had no mechanism for doing so. Its districts governed their own structures. Households kept their own vents. The oldest pipes belonged to no authority. Even in disaster, signals traveled through appointed relayers and returned altered.

But the question remained lodged in every beam.

For eleven days the city argued.

They argued by hand in crowded chambers and by hammer across the hollow. They calculated pressure and lineage, obligation and distance. Some insisted the message was a depth sickness shared by all. Some declared it a test and demanded the lower contemplatives be cast from the ropes. Others claimed the power was an ancestor, the hollow itself, the buried dead, or the first architect.

One faction wished to ask that the city be preserved exactly as it was.

The eldest listeners opposed them.

Anything preserved exactly, they signed, was already dead.

Another faction proposed abandoning the hollow and crossing the salt plain in search of a new fissure. They had no vessels for liquid because they needed none, but the surface winds would strip oils from their skin, and their bodies were not made for long travel beneath open sky. More importantly, no one knew whether another breathing place existed.

A third faction said the life beneath must be killed before it closed the wound.

This proposal reached the Last Floor.

The ropes answered with one low contraction, so powerful that three upper bridges shed their ceramic rails. Whether the buried being had understood or merely moved in its sleep, no one knew.

At last, the child who had recently cut its tether climbed onto a public brace during the evening pause. An adult reached to pull it back, but the child struck the beam with a small resin hammer.

The pattern was clumsy.

It was a household request, normally used when a child wished to be tied to a different safety line.

**ANOTHER CORD.**

The message traveled only as far as the nearest bridge.

Someone there repeated it.

A net keeper in the district below received it and understood something the child had not intended. She struck it into a main cable.

**ANOTHER CORD.**

Across the hollow, hammers lifted.

The rhythm spread downward and upward, translated from rope to wood, from wood to saltstone, from saltstone to clay.

**ANOTHER CORD.**

The city’s answer was not unanimous. No true answer ever is.

But it was enough.

The benevolent power acted.

Far beneath the salt, where heat softened mineral beds no tool could reach, it touched the ancient being—not with speech, for the being possessed nothing like language, but with a pattern of pressure resembling the presence of adjacent life.

The buried being recoiled.

Its recoil shook the basin. Broad plates on the surface rose at their edges. In the city, warning rhythms erupted as buildings pulled against their pins. The inhabitants loosened structures, cut three bridges free, and opened emergency vents.

The power did not hold the being still. It allowed the recoil to pass through the deep chambers.

Then it touched it again.

This time it included the pattern of the city: the small lives within the wound, their exchanges of gas, their pruning of invasive growths, the way they kept shafts open and balanced pressure, the chemical traces of their dead sealing communal walls.

The being had no concept of inhabitants. It had no anger toward them and no compassion. But it recognized processes. It recognized that the activity within its wound had ceased to be merely an irritation. Over ages, the city had become part of the wound’s stability. Its gardens consumed compounds that would otherwise crystallize in the deep chambers. Its pipes moderated violent reversals. Its constant rebuilding dispersed pressures that had once torn new fractures through the being’s upper tissues.

The city was not a parasite.

It was not an organ.

It was an attachment neither life had intended.

The power impressed upon the ancient being the pattern for a second breath.

At the far side of the basin, beyond a ridge no inhabitant had ever seen, deep tissues softened. Gases changed course. Pressure entered an unused fault.

Nothing happened on the surface that day.

Or the next.

For six years, the city heard only gradual alterations in the lowest tones. Some pipes fell silent. Others produced harmonics absent from every record. The listeners worked without rest, cutting the changes into glass and narrowing chambers to compare one season with another.

In the seventh year, a line appeared across the salt plain.

It was no wider than a hair.

Warm air escaped it.

The line lengthened.

Below, the power guided nothing more. It had established the possibility, not the outcome. From then on, the ancient being and the properties of salt would determine the new hollow’s shape. It might branch. It might seal. It might take centuries to become habitable.

The city began preparing anyway.

They built wind sledges from plant laminate and hollow-wing ribs. They wove enclosed travel sheaths for infants and elders. They bred fan plants able to remain dormant while detached from stone. Mirror-turners studied the upper sky. Listeners designed portable frames that could carry parts of the city’s resonance across open ground.

Most difficult of all, they decided which histories could be moved.

Not copied—moved.

A record encoded in a chamber could not be taken elsewhere without destroying the chamber’s voice. After decades of dispute, they dismantled some of their oldest memorial structures and shaped their stones into sounding weights. The tones changed, but did not vanish. History became an accent carried into new material.

The first expedition crossed the plain in the forty-third year after the five-breath pause.

It consisted of twenty-seven adults from eleven household knots, four children, dormant gardens, sail-crawlers in porous cages, tools, mirrors, rope, and a polished bone striker made from an ancestor who had died before the message came.

They traveled by night, setting broad mantles against the wind.

The power watched, but did not calm the weather.

One traveler was nearly lost when a salt plate broke beneath her. Two sledges overturned. A garden bundle split and scattered seed nodules over half a league. The expedition argued, repaired, doubled its fastenings, and went on.

At dawn on the fourth day, they reached the new crack.

It was already wider than a doorway.

Warm gas rose from it, carrying bitter compounds from far below. The explorers tasted the air through ceramic masks and found it harsh but usable. They lowered weighted cords. They turned mirrors into the opening.

The fissure descended past the reach of light.

Before anyone entered, they drove a pin into the salt and attached themselves one by one. The child of the expedition—born during the long preparations and furious at being tethered—knelt beside the crack and put both hands to the ground.

There was a vibration.

Very faint.

Not the old city’s roar. Not yet. Only a thin pulse traveling through newly opened stone.

The child struck the surface with its knuckles.

**ATTEND.**

After a long interval, the fissure answered with an irregular beat.

It was not a message. The ancient being had not learned language. Perhaps it never would.

Still, the child repeated the rhythm.

Others joined.

Years later, when bridges hung in the second hollow and black-green fans spread across its first sun-facing ledges, the two cities began speaking through the salt plain. Their messages traveled imperfectly through buried stone and the slow tissues beneath it. A question might take hours to arrive. An answer might emerge distorted, bearing tremors neither city had sent.

The inhabitants learned to leave room in every message for that third presence.

They never built a shrine to the power that had noticed them. They made no image of it and agreed on no name. Some believed it had been the city’s own wisdom returning from the future. Some believed no power had intervened at all—that the five-breath pause, the question, and the second fissure were natural events to which frightened people had attached intention.

The power did not correct them.

Once each year, however, at the season corresponding to the first long stillness, both cities loosened certain pins and fell quiet for five breaths.

During the first three, they remembered the hollow that had sustained them.

During the fourth, they touched whatever held them.

During the fifth, they touched one another.

Then the winds returned.

Far below, the ancient life shifted through its immeasurable sleep, breathing now through two wounds that were slowly becoming something other than wounds.

And the power, finding no further act wiser than restraint, withdrew its hand.

It did not leave entirely.

Benevolence rarely does.

But it loosened its fastening enough that the city could bear its own weight.
