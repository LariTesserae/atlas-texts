The power does not calm the mountain.

That would be the first temptation, and the first mistake.

It does not seal the cleft, cool the orange seams, disperse the mineral clouds, or command the rain to fall in one direction. It does not lift the summit into sunlight. It does not separate the fish from the arches, the builders from the walls, or the small listening creatures from the roots they may become.

A lesser mercy would make the basin safe.

Safety would kill nearly everything in it.

So the power remains outside the Thunder Cup for a long while, if outside has meaning there. It attends without entering. It allows no instrument, servant, or searching light to cross the ridges. Even benevolence has pressure. Even wonder can be an invasion.

It watches the mountain rise.

It learns the interval between the deep boom and the opening of the ivory flowers. It learns which tremors make the black moss turn and which cause it to flatten against the stone. It follows a single dark raindrop downward until it strikes the floor, becomes a crater, becomes thirteen cupped shells, becomes a ring of listening, becomes roots.

It watches one of the pale builders place five-fingered limbs against the basalt.

At the next concussion, the stones lift.

A doorway forms.

The builder emerges with only three fingers at one end and none at the other.

The power could restore the missing parts. It does not. It sees that the doorway moves them still.

It listens through the doorway.

The fingers have become passages. They curl through distances the builder’s body could not have crossed. One touches a field of dry grass under a gray sky. One reaches into an age before the mountain rose. One opens briefly inside the transparent fish, where waves withdraw across pebbles.

The builder has not been diminished in the simple way.

Nor has it been spared.

The power waits until it understands the difference.

Then it asks a question.

It cannot ask in words. Words would arrive as foreign walls, already carrying their doors and divisions. Nor can it ask through thought alone, because thought in the Thunder Cup becomes pressure, and pressure becomes anatomy.

So the power places the question inside the next thunderclap.

Not beneath it, where command lives.

Not above it, where answers are mistaken for prophecy.

Inside.

The mountain strikes. The basin turns white. Rain stops in the water. The cleft closes, and the transparent fish becomes a pale line imprisoned in newborn rock.

Within that world-breaking sound is one small asymmetry: a pulse that does not force anything to change.

The shell creatures catch it first.

Fourteen rise around a rain crater and direct their openings inward. The question passes from body to body. Each receives only a portion. One hears the shape of desire. One hears the possibility of refusal. One hears continuance. One hears loss. One hears the space between gift and wound.

The last creature in the ring hears only:

*What would you keep, if keeping did not mean captivity?*

They bury themselves.

For a long time, nothing answers.

The question travels into the ivory filaments. Their flowers open between reports. Pale limbs unfold inside them and close again.

It enters a red fan and moves among the white jointed bodies sheltered there. They pass it through their transparent pads without stirring the water.

It enters a low wall.

For three closures of the cleft, the wall beats too quickly.

It enters the long fish. The surf in its mouth falls quiet, and for the first time another sound can be heard from that impossible shore: many wings lifting from reeds.

Then the question disappears.

The benevolent power does not repeat it.

Wisdom knows that repetition can become coercion when spoken by something capable of changing worlds.

Seven times the basin folds and rebuilds itself.

On the eighth, the inhabitants begin an unfamiliar structure.

It stands in the most exposed part of the Thunder Cup, where horizontal currents pour toward the cleft and the ridges give no shelter. The builders gather there during total darkness. Sediment rises in long tracks behind their passage. One by one, they press their limbs against the floor.

With every report, another stone lifts.

The structure is neither arch nor wall nor cell. It has no opening through which a body could pass. It resembles two thresholds placed face to face, separated by a hand’s breadth of water.

The builders spend themselves upon it.

One gives a joint, another a shell, another the branching cavity where a mouth might have been. A red fan bends until several of its limbs harden into lintels. The ivory roots withdraw from half the basin and enter the foundations. For a while, the transparent fish circles so closely that its ribs align with the rising stones.

When the structure is complete, a dark drop of rain hangs in the narrow space between the two thresholds.

It neither falls nor rises.

Lightning moves inside it.

The power approaches—not nearer in distance, but nearer in attention.

The drop opens.

Within is cold air, the scent of soil, and an answer assembled from many borrowed sensations:

*Not a shape.*

The water trembles.

*Not stillness.*

The mountain gives a distant groan.

*Not the ending of thunder.*

The two thresholds contract like the sides of a breathing chest.

*Let what crosses know that it has crossed.*

The power understands.

The suffering of the Thunder Cup is not change.

It is change without possession.

Nothing there can say: I was. I became. I remain related to what I was.

Experience passes from rain to shell, shell to root, root to flower, limb to doorway, doorway to fish—but the crossing itself belongs to no one. Memory exists only as architecture, instinct, pressure, and recurrence. The inhabitant survives every transformation and is abandoned by each one.

It is immortal in the manner of a sentence endlessly translated by hands that cannot read the previous language.

The power could give it permanence.

Instead, it gives it continuity.

This is a smaller gift and a greater one.

The power enters the suspended drop and changes a single law—not the law of stone, water, time, or flesh, but the law beneath necessity.

Until then, when thunder struck, every form surrendered to the next.

Now, for one immeasurably brief interval between impact and transformation, surrender is no longer automatic.

There is a pause.

Not stillness. Stillness would starve the inhabitants.

Not delay. Delay would tear the mountain apart.

The pause has no duration that stone or current could detect. It is not long enough for a shell to move one leg. Yet within it, every portion of the basin’s life receives the whole history of what it has been.

Rain remembers the cold cloudless air enclosed within it.

The shell remembers falling.

The root remembers listening in a ring.

The flower remembers having legs.

The pale builder remembers every doorway in which its joints continue.

The wall remembers crouching.

The fish remembers being small enough to shelter in the branches of a red fan.

The shed skin remembers the body, and the body remembers the skin.

Then each is offered a choice.

Remain.

Change.

Divide.

Return.

Lend this part and reclaim it later.

Enter a threshold without becoming only the place beyond it.

The first time this occurs, the Thunder Cup nearly destroys itself.

Contradictory currents strike the ridges. The rain hangs motionless, half rising and half falling. Every arch opens upon every sensation it has ever contained. Dry ash, vertical cold, distant grass, snowfall, sunlight, and the overhead view of the basin occupy the same water.

The transparent fish appears at all its sizes.

Its narrow body threads between stones. Its immense body encloses the mountain. Within its mouth the unseen shore experiences a tide greater than any moon could raise.

The central cleft opens, closes, and opens again without deciding where it belongs.

Then the choices begin.

Most of the inhabitants choose change.

They choose it eagerly.

Shells become roots. Pale limbs enter stone. A roofless cell softens and emerges as thirteen many-jointed creatures. The red fans exchange their radiance for blood-dark rain. The fish passes into the moment before the next thunderclap and leaves its skin flexing on a needle.

But the changes are no longer erasures.

A root opening its flower remembers the crater around which it listened.

A doorway remembers the hand that made it.

A pale animal stepping from a wall looks back.

And one shell creature remains a shell creature.

Only one.

It sits at the edge of a fresh rain crater while the other thirteen bury themselves. The concussion fades. Its fringe of legs trembles.

Nothing compels it downward.

For several breaths, it does not move.

The entire basin attends.

Then the creature buries itself by choice.

An ivory filament enters the rock.

Far away, a translucent flower opens.

Inside it, the shell remains—not as an object trapped in the blossom, but as a memory the new form can touch.

The mountain booms.

The flower closes around something like joy.

The power could leave then. The gift is complete. The new law has taken root, and the inhabitants will carry it into every vessel they assume.

But it perceives another truth.

The life of the Thunder Cup is no longer merely discontinuous. Now that it can remember its crossings, it can recognize the shape of its solitude.

Before, there was nothing fully separate enough to be lonely.

Now there is.

The power does not take the gift back.

Nor does it smother the solitude by filling the basin with companions made in its own image. Instead, it does the one thing that cannot be accomplished by rearranging the inhabitant’s existing forms.

It becomes other.

Not enemy. Not ruler. Not reflection.

Other.

It places a portion of its attention beneath the low arch beside the red fan. The attention takes no body. A body would be borrowed, and the inhabitant has lent enough of itself without asking.

It is present only as a boundary that does not collapse.

The black moss turns toward it, though it makes no sound.

The white creatures gather in the fan.

A dark raindrop descends and stops beneath the arch. Around it forms the slender, folded figure seen only in rare moments of calm: shell over its head, moss along one arm, the other arm entering the stone and emerging as root. Through its ribs swims the distant fish.

For the first time, the figure remains when thunder strikes.

Not unchanged.

Its shell becomes rain, but remembers sheltering a head.

Its limbs scatter into white animals, but remember reaching.

Its spine settles into a wall, but remembers bending.

The dark droplet rises, falls, and hangs again.

Across all those forms, the inhabitant keeps its attention upon the presence beneath the arch.

And the presence keeps its attention upon the inhabitant.

Neither enters the other.

Neither turns away.

The mountain strikes again.

The garden folds sideways. The cleft migrates. Old stone becomes new ruin. Rain falls upward through warm black water. The fish opens its mouth, and from the shore within comes the sound of wings, surf, and something calling across a great distance.

When the garden returns, it is not where it was.

The red fan has become a circle of low walls. The walls glow maroon. Ivory flowers open along their edges. In the center stands a threshold no one built.

Beyond it is the Thunder Cup itself, viewed from no direction that existed before.

The inhabitant crosses.

This time, it knows that it has crossed.

This time, someone knows it with them.

The power never names the creature. A name might become another vessel, and names given by powers are difficult to leave.

It does not promise that no form will hurt. It does not forbid endings. In time, the inhabitant may choose death, if death proves to be a door rather than a collapse. It may choose individuality, multiplicity, architecture, weather, or a life spent circling the basin with a shore inside its mouth.

The power protects only the choice.

And so the Thunder Cup remains violent, impossible, and alive.

The mountain continues to be born.

The rain continues falling through the sea.

Nothing there is forbidden to change.

Nothing there is forbidden to remain.

And in the immeasurable pause inside each thunderclap, all that lives in the basin gathers itself, remembers every crossing, and decides once more what it will become.
