The power did not arrive from the sky.

That would have been too loud, and the city already possessed more than enough noise.

It came by boat.

The vessel was one of the smallest to reach the harbor, without mast or sail, carrying no jars, no cordage, no flour, no timber, and no visible crew except an old traveler seated in the stern. The hull rode high despite the warm chop. No one saw an oar. It entered between the pale breakwaters at the turn of the tide and touched the harbor steps without striking them.

The traveler stepped ashore barefoot.

No herald named them. No bird fell silent. The springs continued their subterranean roaring, the roof gutters overflowed, the shell hammers rang, and two fishers on the upper quay continued an argument so furious in sign that neither noticed the stranger pass between them.

This suited the power.

It had not come to be recognized. Recognition changes what people ask for.

For many days it walked through the city.

It watched a girl clear a gutter with a shell scraper almost worn through. It watched three households open a wall to free a conduit that none of them owned and all of them required. It watched copper custodians oil an adze, wrap it, unwrap it, inspect it, record its lending, and finally place it in the hands of a worker whose own child had drowned because a public stair had crusted smooth.

It watched the markets divide a green leaf into portions smaller than fingernails.

It watched an old man recut the name of his mother on clay, copying from a tablet copied from a tablet copied from a tablet. He knew the marks but no longer knew how her voice had sounded. When he finished, he placed the old tablet in a basin and let the water take it. By the next day its letters had begun to whiten.

The power went beneath houses where the dead stood enclosed in the foundations. It listened to bones becoming stone. It entered the central meeting house and saw attendants knocking mineral curtains from the ceiling. One attendant had done this work for forty years. His shoulders had grown uneven beneath the hammer’s weight.

Still the power did nothing.

It could have stopped the springs.

The thought lasted less than a moment. Without the springs the shallow ecology would alter, the newborn shelf would begin to fail, channels would sour, fish would leave, and the city—freed from its labor—would become a ruin for the first time.

It could have made trees grow.

But roots would split cisterns and lift paving. Leaves would foul the reservoirs. Wood, made common, would destroy old exchanges and create new dominions. Green things might drink deeply from the fresh seepage and leave salt behind. A forest was not merely a collection of gifts. It was a nation of hungers.

It could have hardened the whole shelf at once.

Then no new stone would mend what the sea broke.

It could have softened the stone so that children might cut it.

Then the sea would cut it too.

It could have quieted the birds, calmed the waves, lowered the springs, banished the crabs, filled the black trenches, or taught every wall to remain forever as built. Each mercy, followed far enough, became an injury.

So the power continued to walk.

Because it wore an old body, people eventually began giving it work. This was how the city accepted strangers. A room could be refused; work could not.

The traveler carried shell fragments from a cracking court to a mortar yard. It held colored harbor slabs above its head while a grain boat entered through mist. It learned to skim larvae from the milky pools without taking the young fish. It learned the signs for brine, debt, danger beneath crust, copper requested, copper denied, and bird eggs not yet to be touched.

Its first attempts at signing amused the children. The sign for *deep water* became, through a misplaced thumb, *pregnant pig*. For some time the traveler was known by that error.

At last it was invited into a reservoir council.

A vent beneath the eastern quarter had narrowed. Pressure was forcing mineral water through house floors. One faction wanted to cut the vent wider. Another feared that doing so would redirect the spring and starve three drinking basins. The arguments were shouted and signed together until the chamber seemed full of struggling hands.

The traveler watched.

Then it raised both palms.

Its signing was no longer clumsy.

**If you could ask the water for one kindness, what would it be?**

The council assumed this was a way of discussing the vent.

A fisher asked that the water reveal where fish would gather.

A cistern keeper asked that salt and fresh water divide cleanly.

A mother asked that no crust ever again resemble safe ground when there was depth beneath it.

A copper custodian asked that brine no longer eat copper.

A householder asked that the spring beneath his sleeping room move beneath someone else’s.

This began another argument.

The traveler repeated the question in other districts.

Some asked for dry cloth. Some for doors that never narrowed. Some for an end to deafness. Some for stone that yielded only to human tools. Some for a channel to the distant green coasts so broad and safe that grain might arrive every day.

Children asked for fruit.

The very young asked that the birds stop stealing food.

One child asked that birds be made unable to defecate. The power considered this with appropriate seriousness.

At last the traveler brought the question to an old woman who kept records in a chamber behind the meeting hall. She had not heard ordinary sound for many years. Her fingers were bent, and three of them could no longer fully close, but her signing was exact.

She answered:

**Do not ask the water to become other than water.**

Then:

**Ask it to let us stop once in a while.**

The power sat with her for a long time.

This was the first request that did not try to perfect the city by ending it.

That night the traveler went alone to the oldest public spring. No one knew it was the oldest. Its visible rim had been rebuilt countless times, and earlier rims lay below it like cups nested inside one another. The power placed one hand in the water.

It did not command.

It made an agreement.

Far beneath the shelf, where warm water passed through darkness and gathered its burden of unborn stone, something altered—not forever, and not greatly.

The next change came without announcement.

The water continued to rise. It remained warm. It filled the gutters, trays, pools, and ducts as before. But one day a reservoir keeper lifted her dipping shell and saw that no white line dried along its edge.

A stair cutter struck the threshold of a house and found no new crust there.

On the eastern causeway, workers scraped a channel and gathered almost nothing.

At first the city was terrified.

Crews ran to the spring mouths. Ritual specialists tasted runoff and stared at one another. Fishers examined the pools. Children were ordered indoors, then immediately sent out again because they fit into the inspection ducts.

Nothing had failed. The water flowed with its usual pressure. Fish turned silver in the trenches. Shrimp moved across the flats. The stone beneath the city remained sound.

Only the deposition had ceased.

Not diminished. Ceased.

For the length of one customary waking, the water passed through the city without trying to close it.

No one rested.

They were too frightened.

Every hammer was made ready. Copper tools were unwrapped. Boats were loaded in case the shelf began to dissolve. Councils remained in session while messengers crossed the roofs.

Then, as people slept uneasily, the stone began forming again.

A white rim appeared on a forgotten cup.

By the next waking, gutters had their familiar skins. The city exhaled, although no one could hear it do so.

The traveler was gone.

Its little boat had left no rope stiffening at the quay, no cargo tally, and no debt.

The interval returned after three hundred and sixty reservoir clearings.

By then many doubted the first had occurred. Some called it contamination in the springs. Some called it a failure of observation. Some claimed their district had predicted it.

But it returned.

This time the ritual specialists had marked thousands of surfaces with dark clay. All remained bare for the same span. The spring water again laid down no stone.

People still did not rest. They performed experiments.

Potters filled jars. Channel cutters drove stakes into runoff. Children were given cups of every shape. Fishers brought shells, bones, ropes, baskets, and broken tools and submerged them in watched basins.

Nothing hardened.

Then the old record keeper came out of her chamber carrying no tablet and no scraper. She walked to the center of the largest court and lay down on the warm pavement.

Those nearby thought she had fallen.

She signed above her chest:

**If you spend a mercy measuring it, it becomes another task.**

Then she closed her eyes.

Others sat beside her.

By the third return, the interval had a name. It was called the Open Hand.

Work did not wholly stop. Water still had to be carried. Fish still spoiled. Infants still required feeding, pigs broke pens, boats arrived, and birds observed no holy days. But no one chipped public stone unless there was danger. Copper remained wrapped. Records were not copied. Debts could be stated but not collected.

Thick hides were hung across the entrances of the meeting house, and people entered in small groups to feel what quiet could be made when thousands of hammers were absent.

They ate stored dates.

Fresh water was poured without ceremony because everyone was considered a host.

People told stories that were not genealogies, laws, channel claims, boat losses, or disaster accounts. They told stories that had no practical reason to survive. Children acted out the arrival of the traveler called Pregnant Pig. Each year the performance became less accurate and more beloved.

Eventually the Open Hand became the city’s measure of age.

A child who had seen four Open Hands was no longer carried across causeways. At twelve, a child received a personal hammer. Marriages and copper custodianships began on the day after. The names of the dead were recited on the day before, so that the interval itself remained free even of remembrance as obligation.

The power had given the city a season, but not one of heat, cold, storm, migration, leaf, or fruit.

It had given a season of non-accumulation.

For one waking, the present did not harden.

For one waking, neglect had no consequence.

The gift did not make the people idle. It taught them that they were more than the labor required to keep their world open. This was difficult knowledge. Some resisted it. Some secretly scraped thresholds during the interval and felt virtuous. Some councils attempted to schedule civic instruction, tool inventories, and mandatory games until the old woman’s successors forbade all useful planning.

Over generations, the Open Hand changed the city in ways the power had foreseen and in ways it had not.

Songs developed—not the clipped calls used over water, but songs made for hands resting on another person’s ribs, felt through bone.

Games were invented that taught nothing.

Once each interval, the deaf and the hearing gathered under the same hanging hides, and no one’s way of understanding was treated as secondary.

People began keeping certain objects not because they could be reused, traded, burned, eaten, copied, or incorporated into walls, but because they were beautiful and useless. The first was said to be a thin green leaf sealed between two pieces of imported glass. It survived only seven Open Hands, but many claimed to remember it afterward.

The springs never weakened.

The city still had to be chipped free of itself. The old laws remained. Blockage was still a grave offense. Boats still brought the concentrated fragrance of distant vegetation. Birds still screamed, crabs still entered kitchens, and the dead still became foundations.

The power did not return, at least not recognizably.

Perhaps it understood that a benevolent act should not require perpetual gratitude. Perhaps it came often, under other errors and other names. There were always strangers seeking work.

Far below, the water carried its lime toward the light. For most of the year it built blindly, generously, dangerously. It sealed abandoned rooms and strengthened harbor steps. It took baskets, bones, jars, thresholds, mistakes, and histories into its pale body.

Then came the Open Hand.

The springs flowed clear.

Hammers lay untouched.

And throughout the crowded mineral city, people placed their palms upon the stone they had spent their lives resisting and felt, for a little while, that it was holding them instead.
