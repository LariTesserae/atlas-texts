At first, the chamber seems inhabited only by the things that can endure without proceeding.

The translucent scale-mats are among these. Each colony is a single animal in the loosest sense, though it resembles a scatter of shed fingernails pressed flat against the stone. The scales share warmth and moisture through their touching edges. If one is lifted, the others tighten imperceptibly around the gap, but they do not close it. Their feeding is equally incomplete: minerals enter them, salts appear around them, yet the colonies neither spread nor diminish. Their substance remains almost transparent, as though they have not finished deciding what color living matter should be.

The pale crawlers beneath the ledges are another kind of arrested life. They are called crawlers only because their many soft feet are made for crawling. No one has seen one change position.

Their bodies are enclosed in pliant shells with faint concentric ridges. These are not growth rings. Every ridge is the beginning of the same first ring, repeated because the shell cannot move on to the second. Underneath, the gripping feet clasp the stone with the solemn strength of hands around a precipice. Where a head should be, the feet simply become smaller until they vanish into a puckered seam.

Occasionally one is found under a ledge where none was found before. It does not arrive. It becomes noticeable.

The wiry stems near the entrance are less certainly plants. Their joints are hollow, and inside each hollow is a bead of clear liquid that cannot pass into the next segment. Their roots do not branch. Instead, they end in clusters of pale, sensitive tips, all touching the rock without penetrating it. When a person crosses the chamber, the stems incline a fraction toward the pool. When the person leaves, they do not straighten.

Over many visits, they have bent into shallow bows.

The small stone shelters were built for none of these.

Something occupies them, though it is rarely visible. A hand placed near an arch feels a minute warmth inside, warmer than the chamber and cooler than blood. The water in the clay cup develops a shallow hollow at its center, as if touched by a mouth too small to see. Sometimes the balanced flakes bear faint pressure marks: five slender impressions on one side, five on the other.

The shelter-dwellers have never emerged completely.

They are not miniature people, although those who made the arches may once have believed so. They have no fixed number of limbs or features. In one instant of touch, a patient finger may feel a smooth back curled within the arch; in another, a cluster of knuckles; in another, the ridged coil of something shelled. Their bodies remain in the state before a body chooses its arrangement. They are soft, warm packets of possible anatomy, gathering themselves beneath the stones.

There are three because the chamber can hold only three such gatherings apart from itself. If one shelter is dismantled, its resident does not flee. The warmth disperses through the surrounding floor, and within days—or years; the difference is uncertain there—a new pale nodule swells nearby. If an arch is rebuilt over it, the warmth collects again.

The cups are not for drinking. They give the shelter-dwellers a surface upon which to practice having faces. In absolute darkness, the water sometimes takes a shallow dent like a nostril, then the crease of a shut eye, then the small round pressure of lips. No face becomes whole enough to reflect.

The people who built the shelters called these beings the Nearborn.

They came from the lowland marshes, carrying reeds, clay, food, firestone, and pregnant animals. At first they believed the chamber preserved life. Fruit did not spoil. Cut reeds remained supple. Blood ceased flowing almost as soon as skin was broken. They thought they had found a refuge from death.

Only afterward did they understand that the place did not preserve what had been completed. It preserved what had begun.

An old wound brought inside continued to ache. A fresh wound remained forever fresh. Hunger could be interrupted but not satisfied. Sleep began readily but never deepened into rest. Most dangerous of all, thoughts formed with unusual clarity and then failed to resolve. A visitor might spend months preparing to speak the same sentence.

The attendants therefore worked in pairs joined by a cord. One remained nearer the entrance and pulled at fixed intervals. The other tended the cups, touched the warm patch, counted the shelters, and returned at the first tug, whether the work seemed finished or not. No task was allowed to feel complete. Completion was the one reliable sign that the chamber had begun working upon the mind.

The hollows pecked into the wall record these watches. Seven marks for the person who went inward. Seven for the person who waited near the slit. The blank interval between them is not punctuation. It represents the pull of the cord.

There should have been many more rows.

The last pair did not leave.

They are still present, though not as bodies that can be found. One can be felt in the smooth palm-places along the wall. Those patches are warmer when touched, and beneath the toucher’s hand there is sometimes the unmistakable answering pressure of another palm, stone-thin and trembling. The other remains associated with the woven platform. The reed fibers bow beneath a weight that never settles. The bone needle occasionally stands at a different angle, always poised above the twist of fiber, never piercing it.

They had been preparing something from the clay packet when the chamber took them.

Within the packet is a human tooth, very small and without roots. It belonged to neither attendant. They had brought it down from the warm patch, where it had pushed close enough to the surface to be teased free. They intended to wrap it, carry it outside, and learn whether an unfinished thing could become complete beyond the mountain.

One attendant pressed a thumb into the clay.

The other threaded the needle.

Neither action reached its end.

The chamber’s deepest inhabitant is not any of these separate lives. It has no central body unless the warm patch is counted as one. The pool may be its open eye; the crowded nodules may be its teeth; the motionless water in the cracks may be a circulatory system that has not yet learned to flow. But these comparisons make it seem more formed than it is.

It is a creature in the act of originating.

The black escarpment grew around its first impulse long before ordinary animals appeared in the lowlands. That impulse was neither egg nor seed nor thought, but had something in common with all three. It began to make a body and discovered that beginning was inexhaustible. Each structure it attempted divided into further possibilities. A limb became stone buds, clay folds, quartz threads. A mouth became the pool, the cups, the tiny puckered seams of the crawlers. A skin became the whole sweating chamber. Rather than choosing one form, it retained every first suggestion.

It does not stop time. Time passes through the mountain normally. Outside, rain cuts channels, cliffs collapse, reeds yellow, children become elders. The inhabitant simply cannot use time for change. Every moment reaches it as a fresh commencement.

That is why water appears but does not fall. Falling would be water’s second act.

That is why fire gives heat but little light. Burning begins, but illumination is what burning does afterward.

The single low note from the mountain is the inhabitant trying to continue. It comes at intervals no human calendar has measured. Each time, pressure gathers through all the embryonic forms. The droplets swell. The roots tense. The shell-feet tighten. Beneath the hand-sized patch, teeth, tools, bones, crystals, and unnamed organs press outward together.

Then the effort passes.

Nothing emerges.

The Nearborn in their three arches are its nearest attempts at children, or perhaps at thoughts of children. The attendants are becoming two more thoughts: one of reaching inward, one of drawing back. Even visitors who escape carry a small beginning away with them. For years afterward, they may wake with a word behind their teeth that they cannot say, or find that every cup of still water briefly holds the indentation of an unseen mouth.

Nothing in the nursery is dead.

Nothing there has fully achieved the condition of being alive.

The chamber itself waits between those statements, warm and crowded, endlessly making inhabitants it cannot release.
