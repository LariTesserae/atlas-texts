The first inhabitants are not the ones who appear in censuses.

They live inside the stone, where warm mineral water moves through pores too narrow for a finger. They are films, stains, opalescent slicks visible only when light catches a pipe at the correct angle. Some feed on sulfur, some on iron, some on gases rising from the plateau’s volcanic depth. Together they lay down the city’s true pasture: a skin spread through fissures, flooded basements, boiler jackets, aqueducts, cistern walls, and the dark sides of foundation blocks.

Everything else lives by grazing this invisible abundance, directly or through many intervening mouths.

The gutter crabs are the most public of the small citizens. Pale, numerous, and perpetually sorting themselves, they occupy curbs, drain mouths, wheel ruts, and the shallow aprons beneath leaking valves. Individually they are wary and almost delicate. In their thousands they behave like a granular fluid. They pour away from footsteps, bank against grates, divide around bridge piers, and reform behind passing carts. During heavy runoff they raise their bodies on narrow legs and let the current bring food to them. When pressure bells ring belowground, whole streets of crabs may turn at once, presenting a brief white glitter of shells before vanishing into the drainage system.

Children learn their migrations before they learn district boundaries. A red mark painted beside a curb does not mean danger to pedestrians but a crab crossing expected after the third night rain. Shopkeepers place low ramps over thresholds. Sweepers use soft-bristled paddles, moving the animals aside without interrupting the flow that feeds them.

The wall grazers are quieter. They cling to masonry in overlapping ranks, each broad as a hand, armored in dark plates polished by rain. Their feeding parts make a faint rasping sound. In enclosed courts, where tens of thousands work over the walls at once, the sound resembles distant sand being poured. They remove the microbial films so evenly that old inscriptions sometimes emerge beneath them, letter by letter, after centuries of concealment. Archivists follow certain colonies with notebooks and lanterns, waiting to see what the animals uncover.

The grazers are not owned, though particular buildings may claim particular populations. There are legal disputes over them. One guild may accuse another of redirecting warm seepage to entice a valuable colony across a party wall. Hospitals cultivate small, clean-shelled strains in their condensation chambers. Foundries favor thick-plated varieties able to endure hot mineral runoff. In poor districts, people read the condition of the walls by the animals: tightly closed shells mean chemical imbalance; pale edges mean hunger; a sudden descent toward street level may warn of overheating pipes above.

Deeper in the city live creatures known chiefly by what rises from the water.

Glass eels thread the submerged conduits. They are almost transparent except for a dark line of organs and a faint blue pulse near the head. During migration they fill observation windows like moving cracks in ice. Filter worms extend feathered mouths into currents from ceramic pipes. Soft-bodied cistern colonies inflate and contract together, making entire pools appear to breathe. Blind amphibious hunters inhabit pressure vaults and the cracks beneath reservoir steps. Some wait for months with their mouths exposed, indistinguishable from mineral-rimmed holes until something touches the water.

No complete inventory exists. Every draining reveals another population adapted to a temperature, pressure, or chemical mixture found nowhere else. Engineers speak cautiously of “resident conditions” before entering an old system. A sealed conduit is never presumed empty. Listening rods are applied to its walls. Water samples are taken over a full seasonal cycle. Sometimes a planned repair is abandoned because the interior has become a spawning chamber for an animal known from no other district.

The chimney moths belong to heat.

They pass most of their lives as pale, segmented larvae inside warm service shafts, feeding on films and the softened remains carried there by ventilation currents. Their adult lives are brief and mouthless. When the correct combination of rain, pressure, and furnace temperature occurs, they emerge in such numbers that the city seems to exhale them.

They cover pipes in trembling fur. They crowd lamps until the light turns dim and brown. Their wings make traction unreliable, obscure signal lenses, and gather in drifts against doors. Yet the emergence is not regarded as an infestation. Rail lines close. Weddings are scheduled for it. Families climb to covered galleries to watch the clouds rise from a hundred thousand shafts.

By morning the adults are dead. Sweepers move through the streets with broad copper scoops, collecting the bodies for rendering. Their fat becomes lamp fuel, sealant, industrial wax, and a sharp-smelling medicinal salve. Nothing organic remains long enough to rot.

The roof fliers consume the moths in the air.

They are tall when standing, narrow-bodied, gray as wet metal, with jointed wings that fold into several severe angles. Their feet can grip hot chimney rims and vibrating trusses. A transparent inner lid protects each eye from rain. They may remain motionless for hours, facing into the weather, until one bird makes a short metallic note and an entire parapet unfolds.

Their descents are abrupt but coordinated. They skim between cables, engines, and bridge arches without collision, taking insects from the air and shell-creatures from flooded roofs. Their calls resemble struck tools: clicks, pings, hollow clanks, and long descending scrapes. In districts with large colonies, residents distinguish local flocks by rhythm. Some imitate pressure bells closely enough to confuse visitors. Others incorporate hammer patterns from nearby workshops into their courtship exchanges.

The fliers are not tame, but they are deeply entangled with domestic life. Households leave mineral dishes near nesting ledges. Masons build roughened cornices where they want colonies to settle, because the cement formed beneath their nests seals roof joints better than most manufactured compounds. A nesting pair may be inherited with a building and named across several human generations. Disturbing an established nest is both unlawful and unlucky.

Their agitation is one of the city’s fastest warning systems. They react to pressure failures, gas leaks, unusual ground vibration, and changes in electrical charge before instruments do. A roof suddenly emptied of birds can halt work in every factory beneath it.

The great carriers occupy the ground with comparable authority.

They stand on six columnar legs, their broad feet spreading over wet paving without slipping. Their hides are thick, wrinkled, and often patterned by pale mineral deposits. Valved nostrils close against steam and floodwater. The head hangs low, protected by a heavy shelf of bone used to overturn broken masonry or push open obstructed routes. They eat wall grazers, soft shell-creatures, rendered feed, and mineral crusts that would splinter ordinary teeth.

Domestic carriers are fitted with padded harness frames rather than collars. They pull low freight sledges, move turbine parts through passages too narrow for engines, and stand patiently while cargo is transferred around them. Their handlers seldom command by force. A carrier remembers mistreatment, changes in street grade, the location of warm drains, and every door through which it has once been permitted to pass. Most are guided with pressure taps against the flanks and low notes sounded through a metal tube.

The wild herds are larger and less predictable. They move through abandoned industrial boroughs according to routes older than the present buildings. Walls have been rebuilt around those routes. Markets include broad central aisles not for customers but for the possibility that a herd may arrive. When they do, stalls close and traffic stops. The animals proceed through steam and rain, pausing to scrape at a favored retaining wall or crack shell prey against a curb.

Attempts to redirect them have taught the city patience. A herd prevented from reaching an old mineral lick may lean collectively against whatever blocks it. They do not charge. They simply apply weight until architecture reconsiders.

Among these animals live the people of the plateau.

They are listeners first. From infancy they learn to separate useful sounds from the city’s continuous thunder: the ordinary stroke of a neighborhood pump from the doubled impact that means air in the chamber; rain on roofing from water inside a wall; the courtship clatter of roof fliers from their alarm call; the harmless groan of settling stone from the sharp internal report preceding a collapse.

A person may wake because a familiar vibration has ceased.

Their clothing is layered, close-fitting, and made to shed water without expecting complete dryness. Outer garments are waxed with moth fat or mineral oil. Boots have broad, replaceable soles for slick stone and slotted sides through which shallow water can pass rather than collect. Metal fittings are avoided near high-voltage districts. Hair is commonly cut short or wrapped, not as fashion alone but because condensation gathers everywhere.

Their hands show the city’s occupations: pale scoring from mineral salts, blackened nail beds from wet iron, calluses formed by valve wheels and sounding rods. Many carry hooks for clearing shell masses, folding masks for steam leaks, and small cups with which to taste or smell runoff. Water is read almost as writing. Its warmth, metallic edge, cloudiness, and the animals present in it reveal where it has traveled.

The people divide themselves less by wealth or ancestry than outsiders might expect, and more by relation to flow. There are intake families, reservoir cooperatives, wall keepers, pipe listeners, herd guides, moth renderers, roof wardens, pressure clerks, and foundation surveyors. Some lineages have tended the same furnace for centuries, inheriting not the furnace itself but the obligation to keep its temperature within the narrow range upon which several subterranean ecosystems depend.

Engineers here are part mechanic, part naturalist, part historian. They do not ask only whether a machine works. They ask what has come to live in its warmth, what feeds in its discharge, which migrations use its vibration as a signal, and what will happen if it falls silent. Apprentices memorize species along with valve types. A senior pump keeper can identify an obstruction by the change in shell noise inside a pipe.

There are also specialists who negotiate transitions. When a reservoir must be lowered, they spend years opening retreat channels and establishing new warm pools. When a factory is to close, they maintain ghost heat in sections of its system while resident creatures move away. When an old district is reclaimed from flooding, they mark which rooms may be drained, which must remain aquatic, and which walls will fail if their living films die.

Their work rarely produces dramatic victories. Success is an alteration so gradual that animals, stone, and machinery remain calm.

Homes are arranged around seepage. A household may possess a trickle descending one interior wall through a carved channel. Cups are placed beneath it, or shallow basins where small shell animals keep the surface clean. Warm pipes pass through sleeping rooms. Windows open not for dryness but to balance condensation. Furniture has stone feet, metal frames, or hanging supports. Nothing valuable rests directly on a floor.

Food comes from the same hidden chemistry as the rest of the city’s life. Cistern eels are raised in partitioned pools. Shell grazers are steamed, ground, or fermented. Carrier milk, in districts where domestic breeds produce it, is mineral-rich and nearly gray. Moth meal thickens broths. Filter colonies yield translucent flesh with a sharp iron taste. Fungi and other non-green growths may be cultivated in controlled chambers, but no leaf, seed, or fruit belongs to the ordinary experience of most residents.

Green is therefore an abstract color: known from pigments, mineral flames, imported glass, and old descriptions. Children sometimes paint imaginary vegetation as clusters of shells on stalks or as roof fliers with roots. The idea that a living thing could stand motionless in the open, feeding directly on light, seems to them more fantastic than any blind creature found beneath a cistern.

They measure time by recurrence. The return of glass eels. The emergence of chimney moths. A roof colony’s nesting season. The slow opening of a floodgate. The year a foundation stopped sinking. A person’s age may be recalled by which reservoir transfer was underway at their birth.

Patience is not merely a virtue but a civic reflex. Conversations pause for engines. Ceremonies wait while herds cross. Legal decisions may be postponed until a wet season reveals the actual direction of seepage. Ownership itself is provisional where water and animals pass through: one may possess a room, but not necessarily the pipe behind it, the colony in its wall, the birds above it, or the ancient flow descending beside its door.

The dead also remain part of circulation. Bodies are rarely sealed away in dry ground, which is scarce and structurally dangerous. Depending on district and custom, they are rendered, placed in warm dissolution chambers, lowered into designated cisterns, or given to colonies maintained for that purpose. Mineral remnants may be mixed into repair mortar. Memorial plaques are set near pumps or conduits whose operation the dead tended in life. To be remembered is often to become attached to a continuing function: a gate cycle, a pressure bell, an annual opening for migrating eels.

And there are inhabitants less easily classified.

Old machines acquire names, temperaments, and recognized needs. A pump that has operated for four hundred years may support more life than a natural basin and require a staff larger than a village. Its pauses affect drains kilometers away. Its waste heat determines when moths emerge. Roof fliers time their hunting to its exhaust cycle. Generations of mechanics have replaced every component, yet everyone speaks of it as one continuous being.

Some machines are said to dislike abrupt loads. Some gates “wake badly.” Certain turbines are soothed by running water through them before engagement, even when the manuals claim this unnecessary. Such language may be metaphor, accumulated practical knowledge, or a form of respect too old to separate from engineering.

The city itself encourages the ambiguity. Its systems breathe, feed, foul, recover, age, and host descendants. Animals clean them; people repair them; microbes make use of every warm interface. No participant is independent enough to be called the sole master.

At night, when the pumps begin their sequential work, the inhabitants respond by scale.

Films brighten invisibly in newly warmed channels. Filter mouths open. Eels turn into the current. Crabs climb from dry gutters and arrange themselves along the arriving water. Moths stir against hot metal. Roof fliers lift their heads. Carriers change direction in the lower avenues. People pause over meals or tools, listening to determine whether the impacts are correctly spaced.

Then the water reaches the next district, and the response begins again.

What lives on the plateau is not simply a population surrounded by infrastructure, nor an ecosystem occupying a city. It is a single, distributed arrangement of appetites and obligations: stone feeding films, films feeding shells, shells feeding wings, furnaces feeding cisterns, animals maintaining drains, people tending machines that have become habitats, and water passing through all of them without ever belonging wholly to any one.

Nothing green grows there. Almost everything else is alive.
