At first they seem like innumerable species competing for the same unfinished world. Seen longer, they look more like one restless life divided among bodies.

The largest are the herd-carriers. Their backs are low, broad, and plated in overlapping shields, each plate black at the root and translucent along its growing edge. Moisture beads there, runs through grooves in the armor, and collects in folds beneath the belly. Small drinking animals travel among their legs, pressing soft mouths to these folds while the herd moves. Others cling to the plates: red thread-grazers that clean fungi from the joints, shelled mites that flash when a beast turns, and flat young scavengers mistaken easily for loose scales.

A herd-carrier has no permanent head in the familiar sense. It has a blunt forward crown ringed with six dark sensory pits, but any pit can become dominant. When the file must reverse, the rearmost pits widen, the digestive tract contracts in waves the other way, and what was the tail begins testing the ground. Thus whole migrations can turn without wheeling. From a distance, thousands of them appear to flow backward.

They eat the interlocked palms by force. The first ranks split trunks and expose the wet interiors; the following ranks grind the debris; the last feed on roots and the animals uncovered beneath them. Their passage is destruction only briefly. Spores survive inside their warm guts, seeds lodge between their plates, and their deep footprints become nurseries. Behind a migration comes a band of green so vivid it seems painted onto the dark ground.

Higher among the reeds move six-legged browsers. They are narrow-bodied and jointed, with grasping forelimbs, weight-bearing middle limbs, and long hind legs used to brace between stems. In crowded thickets they do not climb plants so much as climb one another. Hundreds lock together into trembling towers to reach fresh reed crowns. Those at the bottom feed on bark, shed skin, and droppings; those at the top crop the tender growth. When the tower becomes unstable, it breaks apart in a rain of bodies, and each browser lands running.

Their faces are fringed with delicate vanes that taste pressure changes. Before rain, all the vanes tilt in one direction. Before a vent opens, they flatten. A browser separated from its own kind may follow another species merely to watch the orientation of its sensory fringe.

Many of the apparent leaves are animals called, in gesture rather than speech, the folded ones. They settle in layers over the palms and close their wings into glossy fans. Some feed on sky-vapor through their wing surfaces. Some graze the leaf beneath them without moving. Some are predators whose “leaf veins” end in hooked mouths. When disturbed, an entire false thicket opens at once and departs, revealing bare stalks and, often, the pale remains of creatures that had sheltered underneath.

Above them fly the membrane scavengers. Their bodies are scarcely heavier than the air held inside them. Two or three transparent bladders lift a central knot of mouthparts and limbs; steering membranes trail behind like torn cloth. They descend on fallen vegetation before impact has finished traveling through the soil. Bark, pith, spores, nestlings, eggs, and soft-shelled burrowers are all taken. If food is abundant, the scavengers anchor themselves together and become a feeding canopy. If the source is poor, they consume their own trailing membranes and rise lighter.

Their mass flights make the weather difficult to distinguish from animal movement. Blue light running along their wing margins can mean a storm, a courtship, panic, or merely the friction of too many bodies occupying the same height.

Beneath the thin soil live the footfall hunters.

They are pale and nearly boneless, built around a circular throat and a crown of pressure-sensitive filaments. They do not pursue individuals. They pursue rhythms. The repeated tread of a herd draws them upward. So does rain, falling fruit, reed-knocking, or deliberate drumming. They gather beneath a vibration until the ground looks swollen. Then the soil opens and several mouths emerge around the same patch, each trying to swallow whatever caused the sound and, failing that, one another.

Old hunters grow canny enough to distinguish gaits. A limping animal may be followed underground for half a dim cycle. The small upright tool-users sometimes exploit this by striking the earth in the pattern of an injured browser, then moving away before the hunters surface. Sometimes the hunters have learned that pattern already.

In the water pockets live creatures with shorter lives than many flowers.

Their eggs wait dry inside mud, stems, dung, empty shells, even the porous outer layer of basalt. Rain wakes them. Within moments the pools cloud with translucent hatchlings whose entire bodies serve as mouths. They eat spores first, then smaller hatchlings, then their own discarded tissues. Some grow legs and climb the nearest reed before the water drains. Some bury themselves as new eggs. Others become swollen, glass-clear vessels, carrying a reserve of water within them.

Everything thirsty hunts these vessels. They evade capture by making their bodies difficult to see, but the water inside them catches every flash of root-light. After storms, the undergrowth fills with little moving lenses.

The permanent warm trenches have their own inhabitants. Long, soft creatures hang head-down in the black water, attached to films of orange microbes. They inflate gas sacs along their spines and release bubbles in measured sequences. The collective bubbling is not incidental: it regulates depth, keeps the microbial films turning, and warns of changes in subterranean heat. When the pitch rises, the trench-dwellers draw tight against the walls. When it falls, they extend feeding veils across the surface.

Crossing animals learn these changes imperfectly. The upright ones learn them best.

They are small enough to pass beneath the herd-carriers and tall enough, when standing fully erect, to look over the low palms. Their bodies are spare and flexible. Their feet have broad gripping toes for basalt, mud, bark, and the shifting armor of larger animals. Their hands are even broader, with two opposed inner digits capable of holding a reed steady while the outer fingers strip it, cut it, or feel vibrations traveling along its length.

Their eyes are large, silver-backed, and almost black in the dimness. A silent whitening of the cloud ceiling makes them contract to pinpoints. Root-flashes, spore-glow, wet reflections, and the faint outlines of moving wings widen them again. They see change more readily than form. A motionless body can vanish from their notice, while a single disturbed droplet draws every gaze.

Their ears are not prominent. Sound enters through thin patches along the jaw, throat, ribs, and palms. When listening to the trenches, they crouch with spread hands on stone. When testing a thicket, they bite a stem gently and feel the knocking of distant reeds through their teeth. They can sense an approaching herd long before it is visible, but constant noise prevents them from knowing where any one call began. Their attention is therefore communal. One listens to the ground, one watches the foliage, one tastes the air, one keeps track of the animals overhead.

They do not have names for places.

They have signs for conjunctions: **the interval after the blue flowers close but before the shell-grazers descend**; **the firm ground found beneath the third rank of a reversing herd**; **the drinkable hour of the red cups**; **the silence made by something too large to flee from**.

A journey is remembered as a sequence of such signs. Children learn it by gesture. An elder touches wrist, throat, left eye, then opens both hands: wait for condensation, ignore the first migration, follow the second until the palms begin flashing. Another pattern might mean that the route is dead—not blocked, but out of time.

Their groups rarely remain intact. They merge and divide as migrations intersect. A child may travel first among one cluster, then another, carrying unfamiliar warning signs between them. Kinship is recognized less by descent than by remembered timing: those who survived the same rainburst, crossed during the same lull, or once sheltered together beneath the same molting beast consider themselves bound. They greet by reenacting a shared danger with quick movements of fingers and shoulders.

Speech exists, but sparingly. The plain devours voices. Most communication is close, tactile, and visible: fingers pressed to another’s palm, reflective membranes flashed beneath the eyes, shoulders tapped in rhythm. In darkness they paint brief lines on one another with crushed luminous spores. The markings fade within minutes, which is long enough for warnings and short enough not to attract followers.

They make tools because their hands demand it, and abandon them because the land does.

A flake of basalt may open a fruit, cut a trapped limb free, shave a reed into a drinking tube, and then be dropped without regret. Ownership lasts only while an object remains useful. A forked branch fitted with shell becomes a hook for pulling water-cups close. A wad of moss becomes a vessel. Shed armor becomes a shield against the fruit-stone rain. None is carried beyond the point where its weight interferes with climbing or flight beneath a herd.

Their most valued implements are not objects but techniques: how to make a creeper contract in the wrong direction; how to trigger root-lights behind oneself rather than ahead; how to walk irregularly enough to confuse the buried hunters; how to hide one’s footfall inside the percussion of herd feet; how to draw water without causing a guarded plant to close its stem.

They do not build shelters, yet they are not without refuge. They borrow architecture from living things.

During violent rain they crawl into the hollow armor left by molting herd-beasts. During condensation they sleep in the upper folds of fan-palms, where water runs past but does not pool. When static gathers, they wedge themselves beneath colonies of grounded wing-creatures whose membranes bleed charge safely into wet soil. Sometimes they induce six-legged browsers to assemble into towers, then shelter in the warm spaces beneath the locked bodies.

No refuge is used twice in quite the same way. Repetition attracts parasites, predators, roots, and rivals.

Their dead are not buried. Burial would summon the pale hunters, and no grave would remain closed. Instead the body is placed in the path of an approaching grazing front, adorned with edible spores and split fruits. The herd consumes the offerings, scavengers take the soft parts, and seeds adhere to what remains. There is no expectation that a body should persist. Remembrance belongs to motion. The dead survive as a gesture inserted into the route: at a certain change in the bubbling, one touches the chest; when the green spores blow against the wind, one turns the hand as a particular ancestor once did.

They are not masters of this country, nor especially favored by it. Their intelligence does not give them permanence. It gives them a finer sensitivity to impermanence.

Around them, other intelligences operate at different scales. Herds choose routes no single beast comprehends. Creepers contract in patterns that redirect migrations. Root-nodules flash messages across leagues of connected soil. Wing colonies alter their altitude before changes in pressure that the upright ones cannot detect. Even the temporary pools appear to make collective decisions, darkening at one edge and spilling their climbing young toward vegetation not yet touched by rain.

Nothing lives alone enough to be understood alone.

The apparent predator may be carrying the eggs of its prey. The grazing front creates the plants that will divert it next season. A flower extinguishes its light to summon one animal and conceal another. The largest moving void in the storm-glow may not be a single beast at all, but a tightly coordinated absence: thousands of smaller creatures fleeing around a center they can sense but no observer ever sees.

And among all of them move the upright ones, hands free, eyes full of broken light, carrying no home except a remembered order of events.

They do not ask where they are.

They ask what is happening now, what will happen next, and how long the interval between them can be made to last.
