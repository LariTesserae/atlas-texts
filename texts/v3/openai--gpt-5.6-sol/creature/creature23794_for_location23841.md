The first inhabitants are too small to see.

They live in the lit skin of the water, in the greenish upper reaches where the false day is strongest. Some arrived deliberately in refrigerated tanks: selected plankton, nitrogen-fixing bacteria, algal strains chosen because they reproduced predictably and did not clog filters. Others came unnoticed in ballast water, on damp equipment, inside mussel shells, beneath the fingernails of divers. Together they form drifting provinces that appear empty until a sample is brought under glass.

They feed on scheduled light and measured nutrients. Their blooms begin around dispersal towers and spread along current boundaries in cloudy green sheets. They gather beneath rain belts, where droplets striking the surface draw gases into the water. When the lamps dim, many descend. When turnover sirens sound, they are carried everywhere.

The managers still classify them by origin—approved, incidental, contaminant—but the distinction is already failing. Strains mingle. Bacteria exchange useful tolerances. Algae meant for warm nursery trays appear as faint green dust on deep pressure doors. Transparent animals no longer than a fingernail pulse through the beams of inspection lamps, feeding on organisms that were not listed in any original inventory.

Most of the Lower Sea eats this invisible life, directly or at one remove.

The open-water fish are the most numerous and the least individual to the human eye. They are seen as masses: silver commas becoming clouds, clouds tightening into walls, walls folding around an unseen threat. In bright sectors their flanks return the rooflight so intensely that a school resembles a moving tear in the water. In darker regions only their turns are visible, brief pale changes traveling through thousands of bodies.

They have learned the basin’s hours.

Before the long artificial morning they rise toward the illumination shafts. At nutrient release they gather downstream of the towers, never directly over the outlets, where the concentration is too high and the water tastes of metal. During turnover they seek the lee of structural ribs. They recognize the descending pump warnings, the double impact of a valve preparing to move, the change in vibration when a transport submarine approaches around a blind pylon.

The oldest fish in the Lower Sea are scarcely old at all, yet knowledge has already passed between generations. It is not knowledge in the human sense. It lives in flight distances, preferred routes, the depth at which a school begins to turn from a red boundary beacon. Young fish follow older ones through cable racks and between grille bars. Those that do not follow are removed quickly from the population.

Several schooling species now migrate along paths no engineer planned. They use conduits as coastlines and lamp shafts as seasonal feeding grounds. Where a damaged array has left eighty square kilometers dim, pale-bodied shoals occupy the darkness by day and emerge at evening. Workers call them ghost herring, though they are not herring and are no more ghostly than any fish seen in the wrong light.

Closer to the structures live the small possessive creatures.

Blennies and goby-like fish defend bolt recesses as if the recesses were ancestral caves. They lay eggs behind loose inspection plates. Juveniles shelter in embossed serial numbers. Some spend their whole lives within a few meters of a warm conduit, darting out for shrimp and vanishing whenever a maintenance shadow passes overhead.

Crabs patrol the sediment plains. Their routes seem aimless until time-lapse recordings reveal the same arcs repeated night after night: seam to drain, drain to cable support, cable support to the place where organic debris collects against a raised weld. They test everything with their claws. Soft sealant is bitten. Rust flakes are lifted and discarded. Dead fish are dismantled where they fall.

The crabs have little respect for categories. To them, manufactured gravel is gravel, a gasket is shelter, and an inactive sensor housing is a cliff.

Mussels and tube worms occupy the slow-water surfaces. Their colonies begin as dark freckles around sheltered joints, then thicken into fringes that soften the machinery’s outlines. Maintenance scrapers remove them from gates and heat exchangers, but the scraping seldom kills the whole colony. Larvae remain in adjacent water and settle again when the machine withdraws.

Thus some of the most persistent inhabitants live on surfaces from which they are repeatedly erased.

Shrimp prosper around the motors. They crowd the mild effluent in translucent thousands, grazing bacterial film and eating whatever is stunned by pressure changes. When a motor stops, the warm patch vanishes and the colony disperses. When it restarts, the shrimp return so quickly that technicians once suspected they were surviving inside the machinery. Cameras showed instead that they waited nearby in cold crevices, nearly motionless, feeling for the first renewed tremor.

On the upper shelves, the installed kelp forests are narrow, orderly, and heavily inhabited. Their anchor grids make rectangular clearings. Their outer edges end abruptly where the nutrient pipes end. Within them live juvenile fish, snails, amphipods, worms, and small hunting squid. Leaves scarred by service vehicles trail pale fibers. Seals push through the ribbons and leave temporary tunnels that close behind them.

Nothing in these forests is old enough to be enormous. There are no trunks layered with decades of growth, no deep mats of accumulated shell. The forests still show their assembly. Numbered clips hold roots in place. Torn fronds expose polymer mesh below. Yet animals born there behave as though kelp has always grown in rectangles.

The octopuses know more of the constructed world than almost any other animal.

They enter inactive ducts through gaps considered impassable. They learn which hatches open inward, which cameras rotate, and which inspection vehicles will pause if something is placed in their path. Their dens contain shells and crab claws, as expected, but also washers, glass fragments, identification tags, colored cable sleeves, ceramic chips, and occasionally small tools.

One individual lived for months inside a decommissioned flow meter. It covered the instrument face with mussel shells and arranged blue insulation around the entrance. When workers cleared the housing, it returned after three nights. When they sealed the main opening, it entered through the calibration line. The flow meter is now marked on maintenance charts as occupied equipment.

Different sectors have different octopus traditions. Those near cargo lanes collect paint flakes. Those near old lamp towers pile transparent material around their dens. In one diffuser field, they have learned to release trapped gas from shallow recesses, perhaps for the movement, perhaps for the sound, perhaps because a column of bubbles startles small fish into reach. No one knows whether this was discovered once and copied or discovered many times.

Above them move the larger hunters.

Tuna follow the long current corridors, rarely approaching walls unless a school is pressed against one. They cross administrative borders without noticing them and force managers to coordinate populations across sectors designed to be operated independently. Their speed makes the sea seem briefly too small: a flash through a lamp beam, a pressure wake, then absence.

Rays settle on inspection panels where power lines provide a few degrees of warmth. Fine ceramic dust gathers on their backs until they resemble pieces of the floor. Only the opening of a spiracle or a ripple along one edge reveals them. During regional shutdowns, when the panels cool, they lift one by one and glide toward live structures.

The sharks are less numerous than rumor makes them, but their territories are conspicuous. They favor diffuser margins, nutrient fronts, and the dark edges around vertical illumination shafts. Some patrol predictable loops around platforms. Others travel widely with fish migrations.

They too understand tones. An intake warning scatters prey, and several sharks have learned to arrive not before a pump starts, but during the confusion immediately preceding it. They remain outside the dangerous draw, turning through the surge while smaller fish bunch against grilles and barriers. Attempts to drive them away with acoustic deterrents work for days or weeks. Then the sharks begin using the deterrent as another signal that feeding conditions are about to improve.

Divers give them names based on damaged fins, scars, or movement. The animals do not become tame, but some become familiar. A broad female called Staple patrols three neighboring diffuser platforms and has done so for years. She ignores working vehicles, avoids bubble curtains, and approaches silent submarines from behind. Her range is shown unofficially on crew maps, not because it is forbidden territory, but because everyone prefers to know where she is likely to be.

The seals are bolder.

They live between water and machinery, sleeping on ledges in air galleries and entering through flood channels that were never intended as animal passages. They haul themselves onto warm decking beneath pipe runs. Their fur smells of salt, oil, and the iron tang of wet infrastructure. In occupied stations they steal gloves, fish from sample bins, and soft packing material for sleeping hollows.

They follow divers and survey vehicles for the light. A seal may swim just beyond the lamp beam, waiting for small fish to reveal themselves against a wall. Some have learned to tap viewport glass to make occupants turn on exterior lights. Others wait beside moon pools during shift changes, hoping for discarded food or merely interested in the commotion.

Pups born in the Lower Sea do not know an open sky. Their first air is the enclosed air of service galleries, carrying fan noise and condensation. They learn to sleep through turbines and wake at changes in ventilation. A pressure door cycling far away will raise every head on a ledge.

Birds inhabit the margins overhead.

Gulls nest on roof trusses and inspection catwalks, dropping shells onto armor plates to break them. Cormorants roost on handrails and dive beside the artificial shore. Smaller coastal birds pick through algae at the manufactured tide line. They have transformed high beams into cliffs, light housings into warm nesting shelves, and maintenance alarms into ordinary weather.

Their world and the fishes’ world meet at the surface, but neither fully understands the other. A gull sees only the uppermost flashing of a school. A fish hears the bird’s landing as a blow from another element.

And then there are the humans.

Very few live in the water continuously. Most inhabit dry cylinders, suspended stations, pressure towers, and roof settlements whose windows look down into blue distance. They sleep with vibration in the walls. They learn to identify pumps by pitch and can tell from a cup’s trembling when a large valve has closed two sectors away.

Their professions divide the sea differently. Structural crews know it as plates, loads, welds, and tolerances. Hydrologists know masses of moving temperature. Aquaculture workers know appetite, disease, spawning, and loss. Pilots know lanes, beacons, dead sonar zones, and the turning radii of vessels carrying awkward cargo. Divers know handholds.

A diver’s Lower Sea is intimate despite its size. It is the cold transmitted through a glove. It is a painted numeral appearing from silt. It is the moment a platform edge turns out not to have a floor beyond it. It is the pressure of current against the chest and the realization that the current is machinery working kilometers away.

Children raised in the stations draw the ocean with a roof. Their pictures include lamp constellations, cable conduits, orange submarines, kelp grids, and fish. Asked to draw a beach, they often put identification numbers on the cliff panels. Asked what lies under the seabed, they answer correctly: chambers, pipes, and more water.

Workers develop local superstitions. Never say a hatch is sealed until you have touched it. Never trust a quiet conduit. Do not follow a seal into a dark opening. If shrimp gather on the cold side of a motor, leave the sector. Some rules are misremembered safety instructions; others are observations for which no formal cause has yet been found.

There are people who spend so long below that the rooflight feels like weather to them. They speak of going “outside” when they enter the water and “ashore” when they return to a pressure station, though there is no shore nearby. They know individual towers by the growth on their legs. They can orient themselves by the direction of suspended particles. During leave above the basin, true wind and an unbounded sky may make them uneasy.

No humans are entirely native yet, not in the long sense. The oldest were children when the basin filled. But a Lower Sea culture is forming: practical, enclosed, attentive to schedules, impatient with romantic ideas of oceans. Its songs keep the rhythm of pumps. Its jokes concern pressure seals and lost tools. Its stories are about doors opening where no door was charted, lights seen beneath suspended floors, vehicles found covered in mussels after a single forgotten season.

Among all these inhabitants, the most successful are not necessarily the strongest. They are the ones that treat operation as climate.

A current is not “artificial” to a fish born within it. A lamp cycle is day. A nutrient purge is spring. A shutdown is winter. An acoustic warning is thunder with meaning. Warm effluent is a vent, a conduit is a ridge, and the recurring passage of a scraper is a grazing disaster after which settlement begins again.

Even the animals introduced as regulators have become subjects of regulation by the sea itself. Their territories depend on power demand. Their breeding seasons follow turnover. Their hunting methods incorporate gates and grilles. The infrastructure shapes behavior, and behavior alters the infrastructure: filters clog, sensors disappear into dens, birds foul condensers, mussels change flow, fish enter places they were meant to avoid.

The machines are not alive, but from within the ecology their distinction from living things is less clean than their makers intended. They breathe water in and out. They sleep during maintenance. They announce themselves before moving. They provide warmth, kill without hunger, create feeding grounds, abandon them, and return.

Everything living there must interpret them.

That is the common trait of the Lower Sea’s inhabitants. Not gills, not scales, not intelligence, not human design, but attentiveness. They live by reading a young world whose laws can change with a control signal.

For now, they still seem like arrivals: fish occupying hardware, kelp fastened to grids, seals sleeping under numbered pipes, people behind pressure glass. The fitted nature of the habitat remains visible around every body.

But sediment is entering the seams. Shells are collecting in corners. Routes are being inherited. Dens are being reused. Birthplaces are becoming territories, then traditions.

The first true natives of the Lower Sea may already be alive. No one will know which they were until much later, when the fabricated ocean has aged around them and their descendants can no longer imagine that its warnings, currents, artificial dawns, and deep mechanical heart were ever new.
