Nothing in the underland lives alone.

What first seems to be an animal is often a procession: a shell carrying a nursery, a nursery carrying parasites, parasites cultivating films of edible bacteria, all of them attended by scavengers small enough to shelter in the pits of the host’s armor. Bodies are habitats here. A large creature may die by degrees, continuing to walk while one chamber after another is colonized. A carcass does not become empty; it merely changes populations.

The smallest and most numerous inhabitants are the glassmouths, the gray multitudes that make distant slopes appear furred. Each has six short legs arranged around a soft, sagging body. Their mouths are harder than the rest of them: paired mineral blades that grow continuously and wear down against the rock. They do not eat stone exactly. They harvest the invisible skins laid over it by heat, moisture, salts, and microbial life, taking some minerals into themselves and excreting others in altered forms.

A lone glassmouth is nearly helpless. In millions, they are geological agents. Their feeding fronts advance in broad bands, every animal touching several neighbors with fine sensory hairs. Information passes through the swarm as pressure: heat ahead, poison to the left, eggs behind, danger beneath. A warning may travel a mile without any one animal knowing more than the next body’s shiver.

They hatch clear. As they feed, their coiled guts stain them copper, green, yellow, or black according to the district. Underlanders read these colors as forecasts. A green-bellied migration means damp stone. Black coils mean sulfur exposure. A sudden wash of white through a feeding swarm means the animals have emptied themselves and are preparing to flee.

Behind them come the plated herds.

These creatures begin life soft enough to be mistaken for swollen grubs. For their first season they eat constantly, choosing among mineral beds with an instinct so exact that different broods develop different armor. In iron country their overlapping plates grow red and weighty. Near gypsum springs they become pale, high-ridged, and almost translucent. Those raised on pyrite glitter like ceremonial objects until dampness roughens them.

The adults are broad, many-legged, and patient only when sleeping. They travel in files because the first animal breaks the crust and the rest deepen the path. Each step sounds like crockery struck against stone. Their eyes are distributed in rows beneath the edges of their plates, dozens of dull lenses that see movement but little shape. They recognize one another by vibration and by the mineral taste of the herd.

Some species carry their young in warm dorsal chambers. From a distance, these nurseries resemble raised scars along the spine. At danger, the chambers seal. If the parent dies, they open anyway, releasing hundreds of pale offspring into the cooling body. Other plated animals trail their eggs beneath their tails in adhesive ropes. They cross one another’s paths and become entangled, sometimes binding several adults into a single furious, many-headed knot.

Predators exploit every such inconvenience.

The wet-stones are among the simplest and most successful. They crouch around pools with their limbs folded beneath them and their soft dorsal tissue spread flat. Silt settles over them. Condensation beads on their backs. Small swimmers and thirsty land animals cross what appears to be harmless rock until whiskers hidden along the predator’s rim detect contact. Then the body folds upward like a closing hand.

A wet-stone may remain shut for days while it digests. During that time scavengers gather on its back, grazing whatever was crushed outside its mouth. Larger wet-stones have been found with old footpaths crossing them and shell markers piled on their hides. There are stories of entire camps built upon sleeping individuals. The stories are probably true.

Above, the thermal flocks are less like birds than scraps of living membrane. Their bodies are little more than a mouth, a reproductive sac, and a framework of hollow struts stretched between wings. They cannot fly strongly. They climb warm air, spread themselves, and sift the cavern atmosphere. Their feeding fringes trap dust, mites, fungal motes, droplets, and the drifting larvae of pool creatures.

They breed in the ceiling fractures. When condensation is plentiful, their young descend in translucent threads and remain airborne for weeks. When the thermals fail, adults rain onto the landscape in numbers sufficient to bury smaller animals. Most recover when the warm currents return. Some do not, and the fallen become temporary reefs of wing and cartilage.

Everything eats them.

The upright inhabitants eat them too, though they do not call themselves people in any simple sense. Their own name is made by striking the breast and throat together while one foot remains on the ground. It cannot be spoken without a resonant floor. Translated poorly, it means something between **those who hear before moving** and **those who are warned from below**.

They are not all of one shape.

This is partly because their bodies continue to reorganize after maturity. A hatchling begins low to the ground, with eight nearly equal limbs and a flexible hide. As it grows, mineral-rich food stiffens portions of its internal frame. The rear limbs become pillars. The middle pair shorten into bracing legs used on unstable ground. The front pairs divide in function: one grows strong for carrying and climbing; the other becomes fine, many-jointed, and dexterous enough to braid tendon or chip glass.

An adult at rest may seem four-legged. Standing to work, it becomes upright. Running, it drops again and uses every available limb.

Its head is narrow and roofed by overlapping sensory plates. There are eyes, but they are secondary organs: dark clusters adapted to glare, motion, and the silvery distortions of wet surfaces. More important are the whisker-fans around the jaw, which taste air currents, and the paired heel-bones that conduct vibration from the ground into chambers in the chest.

An underlander does not merely hear a noise beneath its feet. It feels the direction, depth, and texture of it throughout the body. Herd movement thrums in the lower ribs. Cracking crust scratches behind the shoulders. Pressurized water produces a cold flutter under the breastplate. Deep stone movement arrives as a tone in the teeth.

Their speech uses all these surfaces. Close conversation consists of clicks, throat pulses, hand signs, and changes in posture. At a distance they stamp patterns through the floor or strike suspended shell. Names are rhythmic rather than verbal. A person’s intimate name is usually a short vibration sequence that resembles some event surrounding their hatching: rain on a hollow plate, the gait of a migrating herd, a particular fracture opening.

Such names must change when the stone changes. A rhythm that carried clearly through one ledge may be distorted after a ridge rises or a fissure fills with water. To keep an old name despite altered ground is an expression of mourning, stubbornness, or rank.

Their skin is soft in the joints and naturally armored across the back, shoulders, and crown. Unlike the great herd animals, they cannot grow all the minerals they need. They supplement themselves. Thin plates cut from carcasses are bound over vulnerable places; shells are fitted to the shins; copper scraps are worn near egg pouches because biting mites dislike them. Over time these additions become difficult to distinguish from the body beneath. Scar tissue grows through drilled holes. Mineral seepage cements straps into place. An elder may carry the remains of several species as a second skeleton.

No garment stays merely decorative. Bright markings indicate toxicity, brood status, safe passage, or a promise of food. Strings of hollow beads are tuned to rattle at dangerous frequencies when the wearer crosses unstable crust. A mantle of thermal-flock membrane serves as rain covering, larval sieve, shade, sling, and emergency water bladder. Ornament is permitted, but only if it can also warn, catch, bind, carry, repel, or be eaten.

Their settlements are temporary without being careless.

They build beneath overhangs not because the stone is permanent, but because its failure can be predicted. Each dwelling has several exits and no interior corner deep enough for a wet-stone or egg thief to conceal itself. Sleeping racks are suspended from braided cords and surrounded by shallow troughs filled with bitter mineral paste. Food is hung in hard shell cages. Water receives the greatest protection of all: nested vessels, copper hooks, tight lids, and guardian animals small enough to eat invading larvae without fouling the supply.

Every settlement keeps useful scavengers. Red bridge-mites clean tendon rope and abandon it before the fibers fail. Palm-sized clatterers eat eggs laid in bedding and produce a sharp alarm when something large approaches. Certain blind ribbon-swimmers are kept in cisterns because they knot themselves violently before a pressure change. They are not pets as the surface might understand pets. They are co-inhabitants bound into reciprocal danger.

Nor are the plated animals domesticated. Nothing so large and migratory could truly be owned. A group may travel beside a herd for a season, patching nursery chambers with resin, cutting away parasites, and steering the animals from poisonous basins with copper disks. In return they take shed plates, abandoned eggs, dung rich in usable salts, and occasionally a sick adult. The relationship ends when the herd turns toward ground the listeners consider unsafe. Sometimes the herd is right instead.

Young underlanders hatch in communal shell chambers warmed from below. A brood may contain offspring from many parents, and kinship is determined less by descent than by who listened over the eggs. The first adult to detect a hatchling’s tapping rhythm becomes its ground-parent. The one who opens the shell becomes its air-parent. The one who feeds it its first mineral paste becomes its body-parent. These roles may belong to three individuals, one individual, or an entire traveling group.

The young are reckless because they cannot yet distinguish deep vibration from nearby commotion. To them, a ridge rising and a herd approaching feel much the same. They are tethered with gut cord and taught through games: identify the filled shell among the empty ones, cross a crust without waking the burrowers, remain still while a false alarm beats through the floor. Their most important lesson is never “be quiet.” Quiet has little value here. The lesson is **know which noise contains you**.

Adulthood is marked by a listening vigil. The initiate enters a narrow post alone and places bone needles against several cracks. For one full moisture cycle, they name what passes beneath and around them. Glassmouth front. Water pocket. Two plated adults, one limping. Burrowing colony. Thermal collapse. Settling roof. Unknown deep movement.

No one is expected to identify everything. The test is whether they know when they do not know.

Those who pretend certainty are barred from guiding others.

The best listeners become route-keepers, but their knowledge is not recorded as fixed geography. A place-name describes a condition: **Ledge That Holds While Warm**, **Pool Safe Before the Pale Flock Falls**, **Third Shelter After Bitter Eggs**, **Bridge Where Red Mites Must Be Present**. Routes are sung as sequences of events. A competent traveler may have no idea how far away a destination lies, yet know exactly which migrations, smells, tremors, and condensation patterns must occur before it can be reached safely.

The dead rarely remain available for ceremony. A body attracts too much life. Some groups surrender their dead deliberately to glassmouth swarms, believing that a person should be distributed widely through the land. Others place them in newborn fissures so their armor may become part of a ridge. Listeners are sometimes preserved differently: the resonant chambers of their chests are cleaned and mounted in towers, where changing air pressure makes them sound.

Thus the dead continue to warn.

Their greatest fear is not predation. Predators are legible. Hunger has habits. Even collapse gives signs to those trained to perceive them. What they fear are the white basins, because nothing living explains them.

Scouts approaching such a basin feel their own footfalls weaken. Chest chambers cease to resonate. Warning taps vanish into the floor as though the stone were swallowing them. The empty shells around the rim bear no tooth marks, no dissolution, no fracture. Whatever left them did so without feeding.

The amber light now opening beneath the basins has altered the underlanders’ oldest routes. Settlements are dismantled earlier each season. Listening posts have been multiplied. At night—if “night” can be applied to a land whose ceiling never dims—groups gather with their feet pressed to common slabs and wait for the distant sympathetic notes.

Some interpret the clicking below as hatching.

Some say it is speech, slowed by the thickness of the earth.

Some believe the basins are not empty at all, but occupied by something that has no parasites, no scavengers, no young, and no place in the endless exchanges of the underland. To creatures for whom every body is a habitat and every death a migration, such independence is more monstrous than any mouth.

Yet when the amber cracks widen, the first beings to approach are always the glassmouths. They gather at the margins in gray, trembling crowds. Behind them come scavengers, egg-layers, hunters, winged sieves, plated herds, and, at a careful distance, the upright listeners carrying copper hooks and bone needles.

They are afraid.

They are also inhabitants of a world where bare stone is an invitation, where catastrophe exposes fresh minerals, and where nothing unoccupied remains so for long.

Whatever is coming through the white basins will emerge into a land already gathering to eat it, flee it, shelter inside it, learn its rhythms, make tools from its remains, and give it a name through the floor.
