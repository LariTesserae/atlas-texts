The High Hollow is inhabited, though no census of it would remain accurate for long.

At first glance, its inhabitants appear to be the quick, comprehensible things: the rivet mites burning in red constellations around stressed bolts; the glasswing roaches folded like scraps of mica beneath hot covers; the condenser frogs, each with one broad foot pressed to the pipe as if listening for an answer. There are cable lemurs in the upper tangles, sailbacks nesting in dead sirens, and multitudes of organisms too small to possess names. Every warm film contains grazers. Every grazer carries parasites. Every parasite leaves something that feeds a root.

But these creatures are tenants. They occupy the High Hollow without belonging to its oldest arrangements.

The ones who belong are called keepers, though there is no evidence that they call themselves anything.

Their number is uncertain. At least three distinct handspans have been found in fresh grease beside inspection hatches. Seven sleeping recesses remain warm at irregular intervals. Knotted fiber signs suggest as many as nineteen individuals, unless one keeper ties in several styles or the cable lemurs have learned to imitate them more precisely than anyone realizes. Years may pass between sightings. Then, in a single week of heavy valve-rain, keepers will appear on several terraces at once, cutting vines from a moving joint, sounding the skin of a cistern, or standing at the outer railing with their faces turned into the escaping air.

They resemble people most strongly when they are still.

They are tall, but not from abundance. Their height consists of narrowness drawn out: long shins, long forearms, deep rib cages, hands with two thumbs and fingers permanently darkened by oxide. An adult can pass sideways through spaces that would stop a broader animal. Their joints are unusually loose. Elbows and shoulders dislocate without injury, allowing them to descend through cable bundles or turn inside maintenance ducts too narrow for them to crouch.

They carry little flesh. In the warm central terraces they go bare except for belts of tools and layered pads over the sternum and knees. Near the open walls they wrap themselves in membrane cloth lacquered against pressure loss. This cloth is patched with sailback skin, old filter mesh, hammered foil, and translucent sheets shed from some unknown machine. When the outer draft catches it, it balloons without tearing.

Their faces are protected by a dark inner eyelid like that of the cable lemurs. They can close it against grit while continuing to see shadows and large motions. The visible eye beneath is pale, often colorless. Their noses are broad and closable; their ears lie flat, with no outer shell to snag on wires. Most have scarred throats. Whether the scarring is an adaptation, an injury caused in childhood, or a deliberate silencing is unknown.

They do not speak into the air.

At close range they communicate by placing the brow against the brow, the hinge of the jaw against the collarbone, or the palm over the listener’s sternum. Their language is transmitted through bone. It consists of clicks made behind the teeth, timed contractions of the throat, taps of the finger joints, and slower vibrations from the chest. A greeting may last several minutes. An argument can look like an embrace between exhausted people.

Across distance, they use the chamber itself. A keeper striking a rail does not merely make noise; the blow travels through joined metal and returns altered by loosened fasteners, water levels, living roots, and the positions of large moving parts. Their messages ride beneath the engine’s roar. Frogs sometimes answer them. Whether this is mimicry or conversation is unclear.

Light is used for urgent matters. Keepers carry shutters containing colonies of cold blue bacteria. By exposing and covering them, they signal between galleries. Blue means motion or passage. Amber means heat. White means a pressure change. Red is rarely shown. When it appears, even the lemurs retreat into the cable trays.

The keepers do not have fixed homes. They sleep wherever the engine permits sleep: in dead instrument cabinets, inside the hollow arms of counterweight guides, beneath root mats laid over warm grating. A few western booths have been sealed with felted vine fiber and furnished with hammocks. Others contain only heaps of polished furnace clinker, sorted by size.

The clinker is important to them.

They use the rounded pieces for calculation. Rows of black stones are placed in grooves cut into tabletops, along railings, and on the dry lips of cisterns. Each row records a relationship: the inclination of the floor, the interval between piston movements, the length of a cable under tension, the timing of rain. Because gravity changes and magnetic instruments lie, the keepers trust repeated falls. They release stones down calibrated channels and watch where they settle. Some calculations require decades. A keeper may begin one that only a grandchild will be able to read.

Kinship in the High Hollow is not counted simply by descent. A child belongs first to whoever teaches it a route through the machinery. That route becomes its name.

A keeper might be known as From the Tea Basin to the Split Duct, or Nine Ladders Against the Pull, or Where the Warm Rail Ends. As routes change, names change. If a passage becomes unsafe, the keeper attached to it may cut away part of the name and retain only the surviving section. The oldest among them possess very short names: a single joint, one bolt, a particular patch of dry floor. These are not diminutives. They indicate endurance.

Children are small enough to live inside the planted drums of the dwarf trees. They learn the chamber first by taste and vibration. Bitter copper fruit marks safe moisture. Sweetness is distrusted, since many coolant leaks are sweet. A smooth hum beneath the palms means a housing may be crossed. An irregular hum means wait. Silence means flee.

Their earliest game is played with drops.

An adult holds a child beneath the intermittent fall from the cooling veins, and the child must catch each drop before it strikes metal. Later, the drops are allowed to strike leaves, then rails, then pools, and the child identifies the surface by watching the splash and feeling the transmitted impact through one hand on the floor. By adolescence, a keeper can distinguish rain on rust from rain on fungus several terraces away.

They eat sparingly and almost continuously. Their diet is made of bitter fruit, fern hearts, insect paste, pale shelf fungus, frog eggs, and condensed mineral syrups scraped from hot seams. Cable lemurs are not normally hunted. They are tolerated as thieves, rivals, and occasional assistants. A lemur colony will sometimes strip insulation from a failing cable hours before it overheats, exposing the fault. In exchange, keepers leave fruit in decommissioned switch boxes.

The fiber knots woven by lemurs are read but not trusted. Keepers add one strand of hair to any knot that carries a genuine instruction. The distinction has not remained entirely secure. Some lemurs now collect keeper hair.

The keepers’ principal work is not repair in the ordinary sense. Most of the machinery is beyond repair, and much of it may never have been intended to be repaired. They maintain relationships.

They keep roots from crossing moving joints but guide them into cracked housings where their fibers can slow a leak. They clear drainage channels, then dam others to shift weight toward a settling terrace. They transplant moss onto bolts that must remain cool. They cultivate rivet mites because the colonies reveal strain before metal visibly opens. They encourage fungi around certain ceramic ribs and burn it from others. They remove nothing without deciding what will occupy the emptied place.

A keeper cutting back a vine may spend longer selecting the replacement organism than making the cut.

They are not masters of the garden. Much of their work consists of negotiation with processes that are larger, slower, and less forgiving than themselves. They know that the piston will rise. They know that one wheel will advance. They know that gravity will eventually incline again. They do not appear to know why.

Or they refuse to say.

Their oldest diagrams are tattooed, not written. The lines run over chest, back, arms, and scalp, continuing from one body to another. When several elders stand shoulder to shoulder in the correct order, their scars form a partial plan of the chamber. No gathering has ever assembled the whole image. Some necessary bodies may be dead. Others may live in chambers elsewhere in the tower. There are lines ending abruptly at wrists, jawbones, and the soles of feet, implying continuations that have not been seen.

Certain tattoos do not correspond to any visible structure in the High Hollow. They depict galleries where there is open air, a second central void, and a vast horizontal member passing through the present chamber. These may show its original form. They may show its future form. They may belong to another elevation through which the High Hollow passed centuries ago.

Death among the keepers is difficult to observe because they do not leave bodies where they lived.

When one becomes too weak to travel, the others bring them to the circular clearing on the lowest terrace. The dying keeper is washed in condensation and painted with grease gathered from the oldest moving joint. Their route-name is tapped into the black floor. Every tool is taken away except a clinker stone held under the tongue.

Then the attendants leave.

Afterward the clearing remains empty. No corpse is found. Sometimes, days later, the illuminated shaft beneath the unreachable ladder contains a new shadow crossing its light. Sometimes the house-wide piston moves by an amount too small for instruments but large enough to disturb the water in the terrace pans. Sometimes nothing happens.

The keepers do not descend the ladder. They claim it is not for climbing.

The pale thing in the central void is not counted among them.

They call it only by a gesture: one hand open, moving downward while the other closes around the wrist. Its prints have been found on vertical ceramic hundreds of meters above any ledge. It possesses at least five grasping limbs, though never more than three impressions appear together. The emptied shells it arranges after rain include roaches, frogs, sailback chicks, and once the dark outer eyelid of a keeper. No one will explain this last object.

During valve-rain, the keepers bar their sleeping recesses and avoid the vines. If caught in the open, they freeze with all limbs touching different parts of the structure. The pale thing seems to locate prey by changes in vibration. By spreading their weight, the keepers become difficult to distinguish from the architecture.

Yet they do not attempt to kill it.

They leave offerings near the void: mite-covered bolts, bowls of mineral syrup, braided insulation, and malformed fruit. The thing usually ignores them. On rare occasions it takes a single object and returns another. Among its gifts have been a working gauge from an unknown level, a freshly severed length of human hair several meters long, and a ceramic tooth engraved with alignment marks.

Some keepers believe it lives in the shaft. Others believe it lives in the rain and has no body between valve openings. A few point out that the descending spiral of closing plants begins before it climbs, suggesting that the vines sense not its arrival but its decision.

Beneath all these visible lives, another inhabitant may exist.

The keepers treat the High Hollow itself as something not wholly insensible. They feed it measured quantities of water and rust. They listen to its tremors as one listens to breathing. They refer to warm housings with signs used elsewhere for organs. They distinguish between leaks that are wounds and leaks that are expressions. When the great wheel advances, they place their hands on the floor and remain motionless until the reverberation fades.

They do not worship the engine. Worship would imply distance.

Their relationship is more intimate and less reassuring. They live in it as mites live around a rivet, as frogs live beneath a pipe, as roots live in the seams of old floors. They interpret its strains, consume its residues, alter its surfaces, and are carried by motions too slow to feel. They may maintain it. It may maintain them. Neither appears capable of leaving without becoming something else.

So the population of the High Hollow depends on where one draws the border around a life.

There are animals in the garden, and people hidden among the ducts. There is the pale rain-climber in the void. There are fungal beds that remember the warmth of conduits after the conduits have gone cold. There are mite colonies outlining invisible forces in scarlet. There are trees whose roots enter panels opened once in a century and withdraw before the panels close again.

And there is the lifting engine beneath them all, continuously trembling, accumulating weather and tissue, adjusting the direction of down.

Perhaps it is only machinery.

Perhaps the keepers are its smallest moving parts.
