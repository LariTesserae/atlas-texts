At first it seems that beetles live in the Underfoundry.

They are the most visible. They occupy the cleared streets in black, brown, green, and oil-blue processions, their shells lacquered by the same wax used on the city’s pipes. Most are small enough to fit beneath a fingernail, but the eldest freight beetles are broad as serving dishes, with worn patches on their wing cases where harnesses have rubbed for years.

They haul everything.

A line may begin with seed meal packed into pellets, then mineral grit in folded-leaf panniers, then a dead moth, then translucent tanks of nectar mounted on the back of a scarred old female. Traffic appears orderly from a distance, but its order is chemical rather than visual. Every road is crossed by scent lanes too fine for most creatures to perceive. Beetles pause continually to taste the paving with their antennae. When routes conflict, they touch faces, exchange odors, and settle priority without stopping the greater flow.

Some carry the sharp scent of the mills. Others smell of brood paste, cistern tannin, hot resin, fungus, alarm, or the sour medicinal sap used in the eastern circuits. These odors are not merely badges of occupation. A beetle steeped long enough in the foundry’s mill-perfume becomes, in practical terms, a mill beetle. It is admitted through mill gates, fed from mill stores, answered by mill workers, and attacked in districts where that scent has been forbidden. Identity can be washed away, though never easily. Exile begins with scrubbing.

Yet the beetles are not the city’s people in any exclusive sense. They are only the citizens most likely to cross an open plaza in daylight glow.

The wasps would claim that the Underfoundry belongs to them, if they were creatures inclined to claims. Their nests occupy the warm upper folds, fitted between ceiling roots like pale paper organs. They are narrow-bodied, long-faced, and almost hairless, with wings stained amber by mineral dust. Each nest maintains valves, shutters, diaphragms, and the thin disposable parts of machines. Where the rooted city grows thick structures slowly, wasps improvise quickly. They can seal a ruptured air pipe in minutes or build a temporary bridge before a swollen stair has finished unfolding.

Their paper is made from the city itself: scraped dead fiber, chewed with saliva and fungal gum. Different nests produce different grades. Coarse gray sheets serve as ductwork. Brown laminated paper can bear the weight of a root-pig. The finest is nearly transparent and used in pressure gauges, resonant message drums, and the little scent envelopes by which agreements are preserved.

No wasp remembers an agreement for long. The nest remembers.

Its paper walls are layered with old drumming patterns, brood scents, burial secretions, and traces of former queens. A wasp returning from work presses her antennae to these archives before she eats. What she learns is not history in any narrative sense. It is inclination: which cistern should be defended, which mill is habitually dishonest in measure, which strain of mold is safe, which vibration means the western buttress has begun to slip again. An old nest has prejudices centuries deep.

The crickets live nearer the machinery.

They are pale, long-limbed things with transparent joints and black mouthparts hardened by silica. Generations of feeding on tension vines have made them exquisitely sensitive to strain. A cricket standing on a belt can tell by the vibration whether it is too thick, too wet, poorly aligned, or carrying a damaged load somewhere beyond sight. They walk the moving bands without being thrown, snipping a fiber here, scoring a swelling there, applying resin from bulbs strapped beneath their thoraxes.

They count by pulse.

Their forelegs beat against wood in groups too quick for larger animals to follow. They measure engine cycles, allot work periods, record water deliveries, and announce when fermentation vats are due to be turned. Most of the clacking heard in the industrial galleries is not machinery at all but cricket arithmetic.

They are terrible at recognizing individuals. A cricket distinguishes duties, rhythms, and states of tension, not faces. If another creature can reproduce the proper beat, the cricket will obey it. This has caused disasters. Certain mantises learn enough counting-song to call workers within reach.

The mantises are among the few inhabitants who seem solitary.

They stand in the hatchery thickets like folded pieces of young wood, their bodies green where the lamps are strong and ivory where generations have lived in darkness. Each is given a hunting boundary marked by bitter sap. Within it, the mantis takes flies, gnats, silverfish, mites, weak moths, and anything else not protected by a recognized scent. It is fed when prey is scarce and destroyed when it becomes too bold.

No one trusts a mantis. Everyone trusts what happens in its shadow.

Hatchery keepers leave malformed larvae on offering stems at the edge of its range. Wasps sometimes guide infestations toward it by opening scent shutters. Beetle caravans take long detours around its station, though they will shelter beneath its abdomen during moth swarms. The mantis accepts all this without gratitude. Its usefulness is indistinguishable from appetite.

The moths are different.

By day they sleep across the ceiling in dense, downward-facing congregations, every scale catching condensation and dim light. Some have been bred for silk, some for pollination, some for the powder shed by their wings. The largest bear patterned scales in bands of white, rust, blue, and ultraviolet. When they descend, those bands flash messages across whole chambers.

A moth cannot carry much. It can, however, carry a message over walls no walking creature could cross.

For this reason the trained strains are handled with extraordinary care. Their caterpillars are raised on hedge walls flavored with destination saps. After metamorphosis, each moth seeks the odor incorporated into its own body. A messenger released at the Foundry Nave may fly unerringly to a cistern district it has never seen. There it is dusted with a new scent, fed, and sent elsewhere until its scales wear off or its memory becomes confused.

Old messenger moths gather at warm lamps and fly in indecisive circles, full of destinations they can no longer separate. They are not killed. The city tolerates them as it tolerates scar tissue. Some nights hundreds rise together and perform fragments of obsolete routes through chambers whose doors have long since grown shut.

Lower down, in channels and machinery hollows, live the small maintainers.

There are silver mites that herd water droplets. They brush condensation along reed walls with feathered forelegs, combining beads until gravity carries them into a collector groove. Their routes are marked with fungal traces visible only in wetness. During drought they become nearly motionless, clustering in pipe joints like tarnish.

Snails inhabit bearings, gates, piston collars, and elevator tracks. Each has a working territory no more than a few body lengths long. They graze rot, smooth fungal burrs, and leave a film that keeps plant surfaces from abrading one another. Their shells accumulate mineral dust until they resemble moving stones. The oldest have identifying grooves carved into them by generations of handlers.

Snails are slow enough to become landmarks.

Directions may be given by them: turn at the white-shelled lubricator, descend beside the sleeping spiral, wait where the three copper snails meet. Occasionally the landmark has moved by the time a traveler arrives. No one considers this unusual.

Frogs keep the wet places. They are tiny, soft-skinned, and variously colored by what their cistern contains. Those in clear condensation beds are yellow-white. Those in tannin pools are brown as old leaves. Frogs living near copper circuits develop a green sheen and rarely survive long, but their absence is useful: when the circuit frogs stop calling, the moss keepers know a leak has turned poisonous.

Their breeding follows rain. Eggs appear in every temporary pocket, even in footprints and the cups of unfurled leaves. Most dry before hatching. Many tadpoles are deliberately transferred into water gauges, where the speed of their growth indicates the richness of the supply. A fat tadpole means usable water. A twisted one means acid, metal, or disease.

The frogs are not thought of as workers, though they work. They are closer to weather: noisy, fertile, opportunistic, and impossible to negotiate with.

The blind rodents are negotiated with constantly.

They live in felted warrens around the capstan galleries. Their fur is cream, gray, or pinkish, their eyes no more than sealed dimples. They know the city through whisker touch and the minute changes in airflow caused by moving gates. Most are harness-trained young. They walk inside drum wheels to lift loads, pump sap, or wind tendril elevators, receiving fermented root pulp at the end of a shift.

They will not work if the pulp is thin.

They will not work when air pressure drops.

They will not work near the scent of a tunnel cat, even if that scent is centuries old and trapped in structural resin.

Unlike many of the smaller creatures, rodents know one another individually. They have families, grudges, favorites, and places where they hide food from their handlers. A capstan team may refuse a replacement because its footfall is wrong. Mothers teach their young the safe stopping points inside a wheel and the vibration that means a load has snagged. Sometimes an old rodent steps out of harness, enters the warrens, and is never compelled to return. Its descendants may nevertheless inherit its debt.

Deeper still are the root-pigs.

They are squat, nearly naked animals with tough shovel snouts and blunt tusks stained by compost. Their harnesses are grown around them while they are young, padded with moss and linked to guiding vines. Root-pigs open new waste galleries, turn fungal beds, expose mineral seams, and chew out diseased tissue too thick for insects to penetrate. They are tremendously strong and only intermittently obedient.

A pig recognizes pressure more readily than command. A pull on the left guiding vine means little unless accompanied by the proper scent and a vibration through the floor. Experienced handlers do not direct one so much as arrange the world until its digging becomes useful.

Every root-pig has attendant beetles living in the seams of its harness. They clean its skin, carry away parasites, and release calming odors around its ears. If these attendants scatter, the pig panics. A frightened pig can undo decades of cultivated structure in an afternoon.

And beneath all of them—beneath paws, tarsi, mucus tracks, brood combs, and traffic scents—lives the oldest inhabitant.

No one sees it whole.

It has no single trunk and perhaps never did. It is a congress of fused stocks, grafts, parasitic vines, captured fungi, bacterial films, and organs grown for purposes no wild plant would possess. Some portions produce flowers. Others make muscle, resin, acid, wax, air, voltage, fiber, heat, memory, or sleep. There are districts that seem dead until rain reaches them, whereupon they pulse and alter the shape of nearby streets. There are sealed knots that continue to warm themselves although no passage has led to them for generations.

The organism responds to injury. It anticipates familiar loads. It withdraws sugar from neglected chambers and thickens around useful machinery. It sometimes refuses alterations by producing bitter sap, strangling a new graft, or healing shut an opening as quickly as workers cut it.

Whether it thinks is a question asked only by creatures capable of separating thought from growth.

The wasps treat its pressure changes as law. The crickets treat them as measurements. Beetles follow its chemical gradients. Rodents gnaw it and are fed by it. Moths pollinate organs they never see open. None commands it, yet almost every inhabitant can persuade some part of it. The city is governed through these persuasions.

There are no rulers. There are concentrations of leverage.

A mill colony may withhold meal until a blocked conduit is reopened. Wasps may close air valves against a district that has taken too much water. Pollinators may abandon a flower gallery if its keepers use the wrong pesticide mold. Rodents may simply sit down. Even mites, in sufficient absence, can allow condensation to gather where it rots a bearing.

Disputes therefore take the form of stoppages, reroutings, odors, and selective neglect. Agreements are embodied rather than declared. A new pipe grown between two districts is a treaty. A severed bridge is condemnation. A shared fermentation vat can bind former enemies for as long as both require its heat.

The closest thing to a council occurs at the central hollow.

There, on a broad plate of resonant root, delegations bring not speeches but signals. Wasps drum propositions into paper membranes. Crickets answer in counted taps. Beetles lay scent cords in branching patterns. Moths flash from perches. Rodent representatives tug marked strings. Changes in the root plate’s color indicate the condition of water, sugar, heat, and structural strain.

No participant understands every language present. Consensus emerges through repetition. A proposal is tapped, scented, flashed, tasted, rejected, altered, and introduced again until enough species begin behaving as though it has passed.

Sometimes what passes is not what anyone proposed.

What lives in the Underfoundry, then, is not one people but an arrangement that repeatedly becomes a people when threatened.

Most of the time its inhabitants eat one another, exploit one another, steal larvae, raid stores, spread parasites, cheat measures, sever routes, and grow structures into their neighbors’ space. A beetle crushed beneath a cargo wheel does not matter beyond the delay it causes. A moth congregation can lose half its number to a bad exhalation. A wasp nest may be dismantled and fed into a pulp vat if its valves fail too often.

But when the deep shudder passes through the columns, distinction collapses.

Beetles abandon food trails and open evacuation seams. Wasps chew emergency vents. Crickets cut the driving belts. Rodents brake the capstans with their own weight. Snails withdraw from joints so gates can slam freely. Moths drop from the ceiling in a living curtain, confusing predators and carrying alarm scent into inaccessible rooms. The root-pigs are released from guidance and driven toward collapse zones, where their bodies and strength may hold open a passage. Even mantises flatten themselves against brood entrances and strike at whatever comes through.

The old organism contracts around them all.

For a moment the Underfoundry is not a city full of animals and not a plant used as a city. It is one reflex assembled from countless hungers.

Then the danger passes. The unity loosens. Feeding resumes.

The inhabitants return to their routes with dust on their shells, paper pulp in their mouths, pollen on their wings, and no common account of what has happened. The dead are already being taken apart. The damaged chamber is already swelling at its edges. Somewhere a wasp begins a new wall over the old one. Somewhere a cricket tests a replacement belt. Somewhere an obsolete messenger moth circles a lamp, still trying to deliver a warning to a district that vanished a hundred rains ago.

And far below, in tissues no traveler enters, the rooted city keeps the pressure of the event in a ring of hardened growth.
