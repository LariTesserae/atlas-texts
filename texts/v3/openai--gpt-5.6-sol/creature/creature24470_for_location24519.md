At first there seems to be no principal inhabitant.

There are the springtails, white as grains of salt, and the crustaceans turning slowly in the black water. There are larvae in their tubes, beetles under moss, flatworms polished by their own mucus. Each belongs to the hollow in an ordinary way. They feed, hide, breed, and are eaten. None appears to account for the strange impression that the chamber is occupied even when every visible animal has withdrawn.

That impression comes from the walls.

What looks like condensation on the inner rock is not entirely water. Between the silver runnels lies a nearly transparent skin, thin enough that the stone shows through it. In some places it is no more than a shine. Elsewhere it thickens into colorless pleats around a fracture or gathers beneath an iron scale in soft lobes like the edges of a submerged ear.

It has no definite outline. A portion can be scraped away without revealing where the rest begins. Yet it is not merely a bacterial film. Under magnification, the clear substance contains strands: long living threads, finer than root hairs, braided around mineral grains and colonies of iron-feeding microbes. Some strands are pale and hollow. Others are darkened by metal until they resemble minute wires. They pass into cracks too narrow for any animal and emerge elsewhere, sometimes a hand’s breadth away, sometimes on the opposite side of the pool.

The hollow’s true resident lives in this network.

Its oldest parts are fixed in the wet stone above the waterline, where repeated flooding has laid rust around them in rings. Its youngest parts spread below the pool, among the loose black grains. It grows slowly in cold weather and almost not at all in warmth. Heat loosens its tissues. Prolonged dryness kills them. The chamber suits it because nothing dries and nothing keeps one temperature for long enough to become comfortable.

It does not move through the hollow. Movement travels through it.

Before a pressure pulse, gas entering the lowest fractures stretches the submerged strands. The strain passes upward through the web. Clear lobes contract. Films tighten against the walls. Along the mineral veins, weak electrical changes run ahead of the rising water. They are small signals, but the whole network answers. Feeding surfaces open. Fine cilia stand away from the mucus. Tiny valves between its chambers relax.

Then the pool erupts.

The resident uses the collapse. Water driven through its pleated surfaces brings bacterial fragments, midge eggs, mineral grains, and the soft remains of anything crushed under shifting stone. Most particles pass through. Some adhere. The clear skin folds over them so slowly that the act is almost impossible to observe. Digestion occurs in shallow external pockets, each scarcely larger than a raindrop.

Its food is not only flesh. It takes dissolved iron from the water and incorporates it into the darker strands. It shelters iron-feeding bacteria inside its tissues and receives chemical energy from them. Other microbes consume sulfur compounds brought up from below. The creature—or colony, if it is a colony—keeps these partners separated in adjoining films, allowing the chamber’s pulsing water to carry products from one to the next.

This is why its branching paths follow the boundaries between different kinds of stone. It grows where chemistry changes.

The faint prickling felt against the wet wall belongs partly to it. Mineral water moving through magnetic rock creates the current, but the resident gathers and conducts it. The darkened threads carry weak charges from one chemically active patch to another. They do not resemble nerves in structure, yet they serve something like a nervous function. A disturbance at the entrance can alter contractions deep beneath the pool. A falling flake causes distant feeding folds to close. When a hand rests against the wall, the network withdraws from the warmth in a slow radius, leaving the touched patch unusually bare several minutes later.

It has no eyes. Light matters only as a condition near the entrance, associated with moss roots, warmer water, and visiting animals. It has no hearing organs, but vibration shapes nearly every act. The chamber’s knocks and thuds are its weather. It distinguishes the irregular impacts of feet from the familiar piston-blow beneath the pool. It knows the skitter of a shrew only as a rapid disorder passing along the stone. The rebuilding spiders register as repeated delicate tensions above the moss line.

The smaller animals live around it and within it.

Springtails graze its surface without doing serious harm. Crustaceans tear loose folds after eruptions, then spread fragments through the pool. Midge larvae reinforce their tubes with its discarded fibers. Flatworms hunt along its margins. In return, waste from these animals feeds the microbial partners embedded in the clear skin. Their bodies, when they die, are held rather than washed away.

Some crustaceans carry tiny knots of the resident between the plates of their shells. These knots do not parasitize them. They remain dormant until rubbed against suitable rock, where they fasten and unfold. Thus the network crosses gaps that its own growth could not span. It may even pass through flooded fissures into neighboring pockets, borne by animals too small to know they carry it.

No one portion appears to command the rest. If the pool divides it with sediment, both halves continue. If winter ice shears away the submerged sheets, the wall-bound films survive. If a fracture closes, threads on either side lose their coordination and gradually become separate beings—if they were ever one being at all.

Its reproductive bodies appear late in winter.

They form beneath overlapping ice plates as rows of translucent beads, each with an iron-dark point at its center. Pressure lifts the ice, and the beads are squeezed into cracks along its underside. When the pool subsides, some freeze solid. Those die. Others remain in bubbles where groundwater continues to move. At thaw, they rupture into dust-fine swimming forms.

For several hours these forms possess flagella and move freely. They collect along electrical gradients, giving the water a faint opalescence. Most are eaten. A few settle where two mineral waters meet. There they lose their tails, flatten, and begin the patient work of becoming a skin.

Its age is difficult to determine. The black stone is young, so the network cannot have occupied this chamber for long. Yet it may be older than the hollow. Fragments could have survived in the buried gravel below, compressed among cold groundwater channels before the new rock hardened above them. The chamber did not create the resident. It gave an ancient, inconspicuous lineage a new shape.

Here, repeated pressure has made it broad and interconnected. The iron has made it conductive. The cold has slowed it enough for separate lives—microbe, animal, mineral partner—to remain joined without one consuming the others. Elsewhere its kind may exist only as thumb-sized films in springs. Here it extends across nearly every wet surface.

Still, it never fills the room. It respects, or is confined by, the hollow’s emptiness. Its substance stays close to stone, water, roots, and sediment. Only during the strongest pulses do pale filaments briefly lift into the pool. They stream upright in the turbulence, revealing their number, then settle again until the chamber appears vacant.

Larger visitors rarely notice it. A frog resting at the entrance may acquire a clear patch on one foot. An eel-thin fish may brush through its submerged folds and leave with iron-dark threads trailing from a fin. A shrew biting at crustaceans may taste metal and recoil. These animals depart.

The resident remains.

When the marsh is quiet and the pool lies flat, the network becomes almost inactive. Its threads loosen. Its feeding pleats close. Electrical differences fade. For that interval it is indistinguishable from seepage and mineral stain.

Then pressure gathers below.

Before the water rises, before the ribs hum, a contraction passes through the walls. The pale films draw tight. Crustaceans leave the exposed veins. Springtails vanish into seams. Across the black pool, hundreds of clear folds open at once.

The chamber begins to hammer, and the thing living on its machinery feeds.
