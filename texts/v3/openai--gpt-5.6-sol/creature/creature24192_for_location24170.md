At first, what lives there seems to live by hiding.

The small creatures are easiest to mistake for accidents of water and stone. In the warm grooves of the limestone, pale amphibious bodies lie folded into shapes no thicker than a finger. They have no eyes, and no fixed front. At rest they adhere along their whole length, exchanging gases and minerals through skin as clear as soaked parchment. Rain wakes them. They climb onto exposed rock, arrange themselves around resonant cavities, and open their radial mouths upward.

They are not feeding on rain alone. Each drop carries a minute condition of the sky—temperature, charge, pressure, traces of distant stone—and the creatures receive these differences with their entire bodies. Their flesh darkens in traveling bands. Neighboring individuals answer with bands of their own until a whole limestone shelf flickers with quiet, coordinated color.

When the rain ends, they press themselves back into the grooves. Some dissolve a little at the edges, leaving living films inside the rock. Others emerge later bearing patterns that belonged to individuals several kilometers away. Whether they are animals using the mineral lattice to communicate, or temporary organs extruded by the basin to taste the weather, cannot be decided.

There are thread-thin things beneath the dead grass. Their light is electric blue, but no one body produces it. The glow runs along buried filaments, vanishes, then reappears in another mat as though crossing the intervening ground by a route shorter than distance. Dug up, the threads resemble fungal hyphae, hair roots, nerves, and capillaries in equal measure. They knot around the clear seedlike nodules in which old scents and molecules are preserved. During changes in pressure, they pierce those nodules and release their contents selectively.

A patch may exhale the chemistry of a vanished flowering season. Another releases the alarm musk of prey extinct before the present marsh formed. The filaments respond by branching, recoiling, or producing tiny bead-shaped bodies that crawl away on cilia. In this manner the basin appears to rehearse former ecologies without restoring them completely. It samples what has lived there. It remembers by germinating fragments.

The vermilion growths inside sinkholes are less passive. Each resembles a fungus only from above. Below the lip, the red tissue continues as muscular fans laid against the shaft walls. They contract when stones fall past and open wider for particular echoes. Their spores are hard, hollow polyhedra that do not drift on air. They are carried downward by water, then return years later inside amber blisters on distant rocks.

If an amber blister is touched before it retreats, something taps back from under the surface. Not once, but in a sequence matching the visitor’s pulse and then departing from it.

The long-legged birds are the most clearly separate inhabitants, though even they seem to borrow part of themselves from the place.

They come one at a time through the lowest cloud. Their feathers are black only at a distance; nearby, each feather holds a dull, depthless reflection, like a strip cut from one of the altered pools. Their bodies are heavy and narrow-winged. They do not beat those wings enough to remain aloft. Instead they wait for a resonance to travel through the stone, open their feathers at the correct instant, and are lifted by a change that is not quite air.

On the ground they stand with one foot in water and one on rock. The submerged foot spreads into dozens of sensitive threads. The other stiffens against the limestone. In this posture the bird bridges water and mineral, listening to the difference between them.

It feeds by sudden, precise thrusts of its beak, but what it draws out is rarely prey. Sometimes it lifts a strand of clear gel that trembles in shapes like sound. Sometimes a small silver object, jointed but not visibly alive, which the bird swallows while it is still rearranging itself. Once in a long while it pulls up a pale point of light from water far deeper than the beak could have reached. After swallowing such a light, the bird’s shadow continues standing for several moments after the bird has flown.

No nests have been found. During the basin-wide white flowering, however, the birds gather beyond sight and call below the threshold of hearing. The mineral beds answer. Weeks later, black eggs may be discovered sealed inside limestone cavities with no opening large enough to admit them. These eggs contain rainwater, a twist of dark filament, and a fully formed memory of flight before they contain an embryo.

The six-footed walker is known almost entirely by its tracks.

Its prints are broad and deep, arranged in two groups of three. The spacing suggests a creature as massive as a large grazing animal, but the mud around each print is not pushed outward. It has been removed cleanly, as though the foot drank the ground beneath it. The tracks cross flooded flats, climb near-vertical slabs, and terminate where the stone is unbroken.

On rare occasions, low vegetation is seen parting in six places at once. A pressure moves through it. Nothing occupies the space above the impressions except a distortion like heat over cold water. The movement is slow and deliberate. It pauses at the boundaries where local durations meet, putting three feet on one side and three on the other.

There it may remain for hours.

During these pauses, doubled sounds become single. Rain that has begun to rise falls normally again. Contradictory shadows align. The walker seems to brace adjacent versions of the basin against one another until their differences subside. Its weight may not be bodily weight at all, but the load of keeping two conditions continuous.

The tracks end at rock because the creature does not enter it. Rather, at the point of contact, whatever is called the creature ceases to be concentrated. A faint sixfold vibration spreads into the buried lattice and travels outward.

The largest moving inhabitant remains beneath the deepest channels.

It does not resemble a fish, though observers inevitably imagine one because water marks its passage. It may have no permanent outline. Soundings taken before its arrival find a vertical shaft; soundings afterward find a broad cavern; later both measurements prove true. The pale points in the depths gather ahead of it, outlining portions of a body too extensive to perceive at once: a curved flank one night, a procession of internal arches another, a slow opening that could be a mouth or a gate through the substrate.

It consumes neither reeds nor visible animals. It consumes gradients.

Where warm runoff enters cold depth, it passes. Where acidic peat water meets mineral seepage, it passes. Where one pool reflects the ordinary cloud deck and another reflects the star-filled lower sky, it moves between them and leaves both temporarily dark. Its approach draws reeds inward because the water’s possible directions are narrowing around it. After it has gone, streams run uphill while the basin restores the differences on which the creature fed.

The birds avoid it, but not as prey avoids a predator. They leave with the haste of instruments removed before a powerful current is switched on.

Sometimes the submerged presence rises near enough that the surface becomes perfectly smooth. Under that smoothness appears not a back but a landscape: ridges, branching channels, tiny moving weather. For several breaths, an entire other wetland can be seen folded across it. Then the surface bends downward, and the image descends without breaking.

It is possible that this being is ancient. It is equally possible that it forms anew whenever enough of the basin’s discrepancies collect in one place.

There are also inhabitants that cannot be counted as bodies.

A voice circulates in the perforated stone after thunder. At first it is only the storm broken into smaller reverberations. Over hours, certain fragments recur. They exchange positions. One sound waits while another completes itself. The resulting murmur has syntax without a stable language.

It does not speak about the basin. It performs adjustments.

A low phrase passes beneath a peat shelf, and drainage holes close with swelling mineral membranes. A repeated click travels under a field of sedge, and the roots release stored water. A long harmonic enters the sideways trunks of the dwarf willows; days later, white flowers open across a distant district. The voice is distributed among hollows, water columns, living fibers, and the wet metallic leaves hidden at microscopic scales inside the rock. Silence in any one place does not interrupt it.

Whether this is one mind is the wrong kind of question. It can divide without becoming plural and converge without becoming singular. A pool may hold a brief, local intention: delay this reflection, retain this scent, direct this animal. A limestone rib may answer slowly over centuries. A root mat may decide only under frost. Together these acts compose something coherent, but coherence here does not require a central self.

The basin’s most intimate inhabitants may be these temporary selves.

They arise where processes meet. One lives for the duration of a rainstorm in the exchange between cloud charge, amphibian skin, and conductive stone. Another persists for thirty years as a network of willow trunks, microbial terraces, and a buried warm seep. A third is born when a bird places one foot in a pool and one on limestone; it exists only while the circuit remains closed. Each has sensation, memory, and effect, but none has an interior wholly its own.

Even a visitor would not remain entirely outside this arrangement.

A heartbeat watched by the pale points below becomes a pattern in their motion. Warmth from a palm enters the hinged channels of the rock and returns as shaped pressure. Footsteps alter drainage; breath condenses on fog stems; attention changes which hidden structures open under magnification. Gradually the basin constructs a working model of the intruder—not merely an image, but a distributed counterpart made of delayed echoes, mineral stresses, displaced water, and roots adjusting around prints.

For some time after the visitor leaves, that counterpart continues.

It walks once more where the original walked. It pauses where the original hesitated. Pools retain portions of the face but assign them to different skies. A call moves through stone and may return to the place before the caller arrives there. In rare cases the counterpart persists long enough to be met by its source.

Those who encounter it do not see a double standing in the marsh. They notice instead that the reeds lean toward an empty place with the familiarity of recognition, that six-footed tracks have begun following their own, or that a black bird is listening to the rock in their voice.

No single creature rules this country. The great submerged form is not its god; the hidden walker is not its keeper; the birds are not messengers from elsewhere. Even the mineral intelligence is not sovereign. It depends upon rain, roots, decay, moon-pull, temperature, and the innumerable small lives that enter its pores. Damage one part and the others reorganize, not to defend an owner but to preserve the conversation.

What lives there is therefore difficult to separate from where it lives.

There are animals in the basin. There are plants, fungi, membranes, lights, and slow beings without names. There are memories capable of taking root and weather patterns that acquire habits. There are transient minds assembled by contact. Beneath them all—or among them, or briefly produced by them—the low country senses itself.

Not continuously. Not everywhere at once.

It wakes in districts.

It attends through water.

It thinks most clearly during rain.
