No single creature owns the plain. Ownership belongs to layers.

The first inhabitants are films: bacteria in the warm water, yeasts on fallen fruit, algae glazing submerged stems, fungi spreading through wood and soil in threads too fine to see. They inhabit surfaces before anything larger arrives. A leaf touches mud and is already being entered. A carcass falls and, beneath the first flies, another population wakes from invisibility and begins separating hide from flesh, flesh from mineral, mineral from root.

Above them live the nations of the small.

Ants keep the driest chambers they can find inside root crowns and abandoned mounds. Some cut vegetation and feed it to pale fungal gardens. Others herd soft-bodied insects along young stems, stroking them for sweet secretions and carrying them underground when rain comes. Predatory species move in temporary fronts, flooding logs and grass tussocks with blind, biting workers. Creatures that cannot flee these fronts climb; creatures that cannot climb seal themselves in; creatures that can do neither are reduced and carried away.

Termites occupy the plain more deeply. Their true settlements are not the weathered humps visible aboveground but the galleries beneath them, descending toward reliable dampness and spreading outward through buried wood. The workers are soft, nearly colorless things that would die quickly in open air. They travel inside covered roads of chewed soil, extending the darkness of the nest wherever they go. Soldiers wait at breaks with enlarged heads and jaws. Winged adults emerge only on certain wet nights, when the air itself seems to loosen and fill with fragile bodies. By morning most have been eaten. A few pairs have found softened earth, shed their wings, and begun another hidden city.

Beetles fill the remaining trades. There are borers, grazers, scavengers, hunters, pollinators, seed-eaters, fungus-eaters, and beetles that live only in the nests of other insects. Some carry horns disproportionate to their bodies. Some resemble chips of bark or wet seeds. The water beetles take bubbles of air below the surface. Dung beetles navigate the dark with their burden braced between their hind legs, removing a heap piece by piece until only a stained patch remains.

The flying insects are less a cloud than a changing population of specialists. Mosquitoes rise from temporary water; moths emerge from cocoons under leaves; midges assemble over pools; small metallic flies visit wounds, eyes, nostrils, flowers, and dung. Their abundance supports everything above them. Bats comb them from the air. Frogs take them from stems. Spiders stretch nets across the narrow clearings between reeds, rebuilding after each animal tears through.

Many of the spiders never make webs. They wait inside folded leaves or hunt over bark with the same broken colors as lichen. The largest shelter in tree hollows and flooded burrows, where they endure long intervals without feeding. Scorpions live in the drier seams of old mounds. Centipedes inhabit the rotten interior of the plain, passing through wet wood with a smoothness that makes them seem poured rather than jointed.

The frogs are everywhere water has recently been.

Some are no larger than a thumbnail and complete their development in hoofprints before the sun drinks them dry. Their eggs hatch quickly; their tadpoles feed desperately; the first young leave the shrinking water with tails not yet fully absorbed. Others attach foamy masses to leaves above permanent pools. Heavy-bodied burrowers remain below hardened mud through the dry interval, enclosed in layers of shed skin, and surface after rain as if made by it. Tree frogs cling to cane with widened toes and translucent bellies. They call sparingly, because every call is also an announcement to snakes, bats, and other frogs.

The reptiles are creatures of restraint.

Small lizards travel through the vegetation in quick, papery bursts, stopping so completely that the eye loses them. Turtles push along the black channels, their shells furred with algae and marked by old tooth scores. Snakes occupy every level: narrow green hunters among leaves, mud-colored ambushers beside game paths, glossy burrowers pursuing frogs underground. Some can remain coiled in one root hollow for days, conserving themselves until warmth, scent, and vibration resolve into a single strike.

In deeper water lie the crocodilians. They are not constantly active and seldom visibly dramatic. Most of their existence consists of suspension: nostrils above water, feet resting in silt, hide accepting the temperature of the pool. Fish shelter near them until one movement changes the arrangement. At night they cross between channels along routes pressed smooth by their bellies. The old ones bear entire small habitats on their backs—algae, leeches, water snails, sometimes seedlings that survive until the animal next submerges.

Fish persist wherever water persists, and some persist where it almost does not. Slender species enter grass during floods and feed among drowned roots. Armored bottom-feeders rasp films from wood. Air-breathing fish rise with a soft kiss at the surface or bury themselves when pools contract, sealing their bodies in mud until the rains reconnect the plain. In isolated hollows, predation reduces whole generations to a few large survivors. When water spreads again, those survivors become the founders of new populations.

Birds inhabit the vertical openings.

Small ground birds follow grazers and scratch through newly exposed mud. Rails pass through reeds without moving their upper stems. Kingfishers wait over channels that seem too shallow to hold prey. Herons and storks arrive when pools shrink, each placing its feet with the slowness of roots growing. In the canopy, fruit-eaters swallow berries whole and deposit their seeds on distant mounds. Nectar-feeders defend flowering trees for the few days those trees are useful. Owls hunt the margins of paths, while nightjars lie lengthwise on branches, indistinguishable from broken wood until they open their mouths around a moth.

The bats are the plain’s most numerous large fliers. They sleep in hollow trunks, beneath loose bark, inside abandoned termite chambers, and in dense roosts under leaning trees. Insect hunters skim pools closely enough to drink without landing. Fruit bats travel farther, carrying pulp and seeds between isolated crowns. Some listen for frogs. Others take fish from the surface. Their flight ties together places that, to a ground animal, are separated by impassable thickets and water.

The climbing mammals seldom descend. They are light-bodied, long-fingered, and cautious, moving through vines as though the tangled canopy were a second ground laid over the first. They eat fruit, eggs, insects, young leaves, and one another’s young. Many rely less on speed than on stillness. Their fur repeats the dull contrasts of moss, bark, and shadow. Their large eyes disclose them only when light strikes directly.

Below them, heavier mammals maintain the roads.

The most consequential are the broad-footed grazers: low-headed herd animals with splayed hooves and dense, water-shedding coats. Their hides are dark, nearly bare along the flanks, and usually lacquered with mud. They feed on sedge, cane shoots, floating plants, and the short growth along old paths. Each herd moves in single file where the vegetation closes, reopening passages that may be older than any living member. Calves learn the route by following the body ahead. In floodwater, the adults proceed by memory through places where no trail can be seen.

Their importance exceeds their number. Their feet deepen some hollows into seasonal ponds and trample others shut. Their grazing preserves strips of low vegetation. Seeds travel in their dung and mud-caked hair. Turtles, insects, fish, frogs, and birds all occupy features produced accidentally by their movement. When an old grazer dies, it becomes a sudden center. Predators open it. Flies find it. Beetles undermine it. Fungi enter from below. Within days it is no longer one body but thousands.

Along firmer ground move taller browsers with narrow heads and prehensile lips. They strip leaves from thorny thickets and pull vines down from young trees, creating openings used by smaller animals. Males carry scarred shoulders from contests conducted more by pressure than impact. They appear solitary, but scent marks and low calls connect them across distances. Each knows where mineral-rich mud can be found, which pools remain deep late in the dry interval, and which old termite swells will bear weight after rain.

There are burrowers too: blunt-clawed mammals that enlarge termite galleries, rodents that store seeds above the waterline, and heavy nocturnal diggers that turn over broad patches of earth in search of roots and grubs. Their abandoned shelters rarely remain empty for a night. Frogs enter first for moisture. Snakes follow the frogs. Small mammals line upper chambers with grass. Beetles settle in the refuse. The burrow becomes another layered settlement.

Predators are fewer, but their presence shapes every corridor.

The largest land hunter is a long-bodied cat, darkly patterned rather than truly black. In open light its coat shows mottled bars and broken rosettes; at night it becomes a moving absence. It walks the raised paths because its prey does, but leaves them whenever the reeds offer cover. Its broad paws spread on soft soil. It can climb when floods divide the ground and swim when they do not. Most hunts end without contact. It survives by abandoning failure early and waiting for the next opportunity.

Smaller cats specialize in rodents, birds, and frogs. Long-snouted scavengers move in family groups and test everything with their noses. Packs of lean, high-legged hunters sometimes cross the open sedge, relying on endurance and cooperation, but they avoid the deepest thickets where a charge can break their formation. Around water, the crocodilians take what comes close. In trees, constricting snakes and climbing carnivores patrol the same branches as sleeping mammals.

None of these predators is entirely masterful. Cats carry ticks in their ears and worms in their gut. Crocodilians lose eggs to lizards and small mammals. Snakes become prey when young. Even the largest hunter must guard a kill from insects, heat, competitors, and decay. Flesh cannot be possessed for long here.

Parasites may be the plain’s most intimate inhabitants. Leeches wait in water and wet litter. Ticks climb grass stems and extend their forelegs toward passing heat. Flies place larvae beneath skin or in wounds. Worms occupy blood, liver, lung, and intestine. Mites live among feathers and scales. Some wasps lay eggs inside caterpillars; their young consume the host without killing it until the final stage. Fungi enter insects and alter their movement, urging them toward the conditions in which spores disperse best. Every visible animal carries a smaller, less visible fauna with it.

The plants are occupied in the same way. Galls swell around insect larvae. Ants nest in hollow thorns. Frogs sleep in water held by leaf bases. Beetles live inside flowers and are dusted with pollen as they feed. Vines use trunks for height; strangling roots use them for shape; fungi enter wounds; moss preserves dampness against bark. A mature tree may support more lives above the soil than it does through its own roots.

There is no final inhabitant beneath all the others, no hidden queen or mind directing the exchange. The nearest thing to a ruling creature is the whole association: water retained by roots, paths maintained by herds, timber hollowed by termites, seeds carried by guts, bodies returned by fungi. Each life alters the conditions for another and is altered in return.

The plain’s residents are therefore difficult to count as separate things. A grazer includes its gut microbes, its ticks, the seeds in its coat, the flies following it, the pool made by its foot, and the fish that may later breed there. A tree includes the fungus joined to its roots, the ants in its hollow stems, the bats under its bark, and the seedlings waiting in its shade. Even a predator’s absence is inhabited: prey feed differently where its scent has faded, and plants grow differently where prey linger.

Nothing lives there alone.

What lives there is a multitude folded inward—life inside life, shelter inside shelter, hunger contained by greater hunger. It breathes through vents and nostrils, drinks through roots and mouths, and advances without a front. In darkness it does not present itself as a crowd. It presents itself as pressure: a leaf bending under an unseen weight, a channel widening around a submerged back, a stem trembling after something has already passed.

Then the stem becomes still, and the plain appears empty again.
