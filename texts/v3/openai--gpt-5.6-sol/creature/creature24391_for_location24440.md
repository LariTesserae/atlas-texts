Nothing alive inhabits the plateau.

There are, however, people.

They are easiest to detect at dusk, when all the pools become one body of water. In the minutes before that leveling, the old shelters cease to look abandoned. Condensation gathers inside them more quickly than it gathers outside. The floor basins fill from beneath. Copper hooks bend under burdens that cannot be seen. A darkening appears in the doorway—not a shadow cast into the shelter, but a subtraction from the brightness beyond it, roughly the height and width of someone crouching.

These are the inhabitants.

They do not occupy bodies continuously. During the white day they are distributed among wet stone, rich pockets of air, reflections, and differences in distance. A person may exist as a hand’s weight in one shelter, a volume of breath pooled on a ledge, a standing shape concealed in the reflection of a black cistern, and a sequence of knocks traveling through a cliff. At dusk those portions draw closer together. They do not always meet in the same place, or in the correct order, but for a short interval they become capable of intention.

Their first act is usually to breathe.

The breath is visible before the breather is. Bright threads spool from an empty corner and crawl along the walls toward the condensation grooves. Each exhalation removes a little air from the shelter while adding something heavier: attention, perhaps, or memory. Bells sound without clappers. Water rises. Tools shift on the floor.

Only then may a figure resolve.

No two sightings agree. Some describe bodies built from layers of transparency, as though several people made of shallow water were standing in the same place. Others see compact, mineral-pale figures with long arms and heads wrapped in hammered foil. Others see only hands. The number of fingers varies because a hand here is not a fixed anatomical fact. Three broad digits are sufficient for holding stone. Six narrow ones appear when shaping clay. A hand reflected in water may possess additional fingers corresponding to movements it considered but did not make.

Their footprints are more consistent than their feet. They leave narrow, deep impressions even on surfaces too hard to mark, and these impressions often contain water. A line of prints may cross a terrace, pass over the surface of a pool, ascend the reflected cliff, and vanish where the real cliff meets the sky. Such a path is not evidence that the walker defied gravity. It shows that, at that hour, the reflection was the shorter ground.

They are neither numerous nor solitary. Number is one of the things that behaves badly among them.

A shelter may contain one inhabitant speaking with five voices. Five shelters may be occupied by a single person whose gestures occur in sequence across the plateau. When the pool-connections open, inhabitants pour parts of themselves from one basin to another. Two may exchange hands, shadows, or remembered routes. Several may combine to lift a roof slab. They separate afterward, but not necessarily into their former selves.

This accounts for the absence of graves. Death does not leave a body because embodiment was always temporary.

An inhabitant dies by failing to reassemble.

A voice may continue knocking in the cliffs long after its intentions have ceased. A hand may persist as fingerprints appearing in newly fired clay. A person’s weight may remain beneath a covered stone. Their sense of direction may become lodged in a doorway, causing all who pass through it to emerge facing a mountain that is not there. What travelers call the local rules may, in part, be the remains of such deaths: habits outliving the ones who possessed them.

The inhabitants fear uncovered objects for this reason. Under the full brightness, weight loosens from matter. A tool left in the open may not simply become light; it may lose its history of being held. They wrap important objects in foil, set them beneath slabs, or sink them in black pools. The crude appearance of their technology is misleading. Precision is dangerous on the plateau. A perfectly measured object may become trapped in its measurement, and any crack whose dimensions are fully known will widen.

They therefore work by approximation, touch, and sound.

Their picks are used less for quarrying than for questioning the rock. Three blows ask whether a route exists. Five ask whether it is safe to intend that route. The knocks heard from inside the cliffs are answers, though not always answers to the person who asked. Because stone transmits intention more faithfully than location, a question struck today may be answered kilometers ahead, or generations earlier.

Their bells serve a similar purpose. A bell rings when two places become nearer in the relevant sense. Inhabitants suspend them beside sleeping hollows, pools, and stone frames. A single note may announce that water has joined water. A rapid trembling means that a distant shelter is briefly adjacent. A deep tone from a bell too small to produce it warns that something vast has turned its attention toward the saddle.

What that vast thing might be is uncertain.

The inhabitants do not seem to worship the plateau, but they treat certain formations as one treats large animals: with caution, familiarity, and an unwillingness to stand where they are looking. A mountain’s visible face is considered its eye. This is why no summit can be reached from the front. To climb directly toward the apparent peak is to remain permanently within its regard, and distance will continue to accumulate beneath one’s feet.

The successful routes approach from behind because “behind” means outside attention, not on the opposite side.

The inhabitants are expert at manufacturing such directions. The field of stone frames is their greatest work. It is not a city or temple, though it has served as both. It is more nearly a herd enclosure for adjacencies.

Places wander on the plateau. A red cavern may graze near the saddle for a season. A dark coast may appear in three frames after lightning and then retreat for a century. Rooms, valleys, storms, and unfallen moments pass through the rows. The inhabitants use the frames to catch them. They narrow an opening with fitted stones, hang bells nearby, and wait until the desired elsewhere looks through.

Iron cannot be carried through because iron insists too strongly on where it is. It remembers the mountain from which it came and pulls every uncertain route back toward ordinary space. Copper is preferred because copper accepts several histories at once. Black glass is used for edges because it has already survived being both liquid and stone.

The shelters are not primarily houses. They are molds for reassembling a person.

Their cramped dimensions keep breath, weight, reflection, and memory close enough to combine. The floor basin supplies the water portion. The entrance aligns with a ridge gap through which distance arrives. The roof preserves darkness at noon. Grooves gather the exhalations of earlier occupants. To enter one at dusk is to risk sharing it with whoever the structure remembers.

When fully assembled, the inhabitants work quickly.

They mend channels before the pools level. They move salts between basins, not as food but as records. Mineral branching preserves the shape of events: a fall, a conversation, the sequence of stars on a particular night. At dawn the records dissolve and run into cracks, joining the plateau’s hidden circulation. An inhabitant reading such a record places their fingers in the solution and allows crystals to grow briefly beneath the nails.

They make cups because a cup is a small, portable hollow, and hollows have authority here. A cup may carry not only water but depth. One filled from a seemingly shallow pool can be poured into a crack to make the crack bottomless. Another may preserve a mouthful of dusk until noon. Their mirrors are not used to see faces. A face, once clearly seen, can become too singular and cease to reassemble. Mirrors are angled toward inaccessible ridges, allowing those ridges to be approached from the only rear side they possess.

They do not eat. They sometimes place food in water and wait until all color has been leached from it, but this appears to be an act of study or mourning. Organic life is alien to them—soft, feverishly repetitive, full of enclosed fluids that refuse to join the common pools. A living visitor may be followed for days by knocks, shifting cairns, and small ridge lights. This is not necessarily predation. The inhabitants may simply be astonished that so many processes can remain crowded inside one skin.

Fire disturbs them even more. Flame consumes while remaining itself, an arrangement the plateau does not naturally permit. They approach reluctant campfires through reflections and listen to them with tools pressed against the ground.

Their own young, if they can be called young, begin as repeated mistakes.

A bell rings when no distance has changed. Footprints appear with no destination. The same cup is found wet in several shelters. Knocks answer questions that were never asked. Over years, these errors gather around a particular pool or doorway. They acquire a preference: three knocks rather than five, copper-green salts rather than red, one impossible coast rather than another. Preference becomes intention. Intention attracts breath.

Eventually, at dusk, something in an empty shelter exhales.

The oldest inhabitants are scarcely persons at all. They have dispersed too widely to assemble in one figure. One may occupy an entire chain of pools, lifting their surfaces when the stars appear. Another lives in the tones drawn from a forest of rock fins. Another is present whenever a falling stone is heard before it is dropped. These elders can no longer use tools, but the younger inhabitants consult them by pouring water, striking cliffs, or uncovering objects at precise hours.

Above them—or perhaps among them—are the ridge lights.

By night they resemble stars resting on nearby summits. In the morning they are gone, though small circles of warm stone remain. Some inhabitants believe these lights are people who achieved perfect assembly and became independent of shelter and pool. Others treat them as predators. When one moves, every nearby bell is muffled, every mirror is covered, and no one answers the knocks.

Before dawn the inhabitants come apart.

Their outlines loosen first. Fingers become rivulets. Voices enter the walls. Weight settles into covered implements, while breath streams toward shaded cliffs. The final remaining feature is often an attitude of listening: a slight inward turn in the glare, as if the empty shelter were waiting for a distant reply.

Then condensation sweeps the plateau. The pools separate. The sun returns.

By full morning there are no people anywhere—only wet tools, fresh fingerprints, bells slowly becoming still, and perhaps a new law that was not there the day before.
