At first, what lives there seems to be only the green skin of the summits.

It clings rather than grows. Each patch is a common body made from many species: moss binding the stone dust, sedges stitching through moss, fern rhizomes fastening both, and creeping vines tightening when the ground begins to lift. Pulled loose by a gust, a whole mat folds instead of scattering. Its pale underside contracts around trapped soil and water. Sometimes the wind rolls such a piece across the plateau until it catches in a hollow, roots downward, and becomes a wandering garden made stationary again.

Inside these mats is the greater part of the animal country.

The six-footed grazers—called fold-ears by some of the summit families—make warm tunnels between root and rock. Their front pair of limbs widens into toothed scoops; the other four brace against sudden pressure changes. They eat from below, shaving the living mat without exposing themselves to the sky. Colonies maintain several sealed chambers and open them according to the wind. Before severe weather they retreat to the deepest pockets and pack the entrances with chewed fiber. Their stillness is one of the surest warnings of a coming storm. When no vibration comes from beneath the turf, people begin fastening roofs.

In rare intervals of dead air, fold-ears emerge by the hundreds. They are glossy, broad, and no higher than a hand. They graze in radiating lines around their burrows, communicating through taps of the feet. At the first tremor they vanish almost together, and the meadow appears empty again.

Other small lives depend on being carried away.

Seed mites inflate transparent bladders and rise into the wind. Thread-bodied hunters trail lengths of adhesive silk from the rims of leaves. Rain-basin larvae mature quickly, sometimes hatching, feeding, mating, and sealing their eggs into mud during the few hours that a pool exists. Many creatures spend years dormant. A single wet afternoon may be their entire visible lifetime.

The pale gliders belong to the gulfs more than to any plateau. Seen at rest, they are awkward things: long-boned, soft-bellied, with four folding sails running from wrist to flank and a smaller steering membrane between the hind limbs. Their eyes are protected by clear inner lids. The mouth opens sideways so that they can rake insects and seeds from the moving air without turning the head.

They seldom choose where they land. Instead they choose which current will eventually release them.

Some remain aloft until pale films of lichen begin to grow along their backs. They sleep one half of the brain at a time and drink by entering cloud. When several meet in an updraft they circle without touching, exchanging long cries too low for human hearing but strong enough to shiver water in shallow bowls. No plateau claims them. Their shed sail-skins, when found snagged on stone, are cut into precious coverings for maps and water vessels.

The shell-backed browsers are harder to account for.

A browser may be seen only once in a generation. It is immense by summit standards, though low to the ground: a slow wedge of horn, stone-colored hide, and overlapping plates. Moss, flowering vine, and even small trees take root in seams along its shell. Fold-ears colonize the soil accumulated on its back. Birds—or creatures sufficiently like birds to be called that—follow it for the insects dislodged by its feeding.

Its route is never direct. It circles pools that have not yet formed and avoids ground that will collapse weeks later. The people say it can feel the old fractures below the plateau through the soft pads of its feet. Whether it breeds is indeed unknown. Some hold that the browsers are young fragments of the land, not animals at all, and that when one finally stops moving it becomes the first stone of a new wind court.

They are not hunted. Meat can be scarce, but no family wishes to kill something that may know where the ground will be tomorrow.

The human inhabitants are likewise low to the earth, though not in stature. Generations in the roaring high country have given them a characteristic way of carrying themselves: knees flexed, feet set wide, shoulders turned obliquely to the wind. They walk with an economy that strangers sometimes mistake for fear. Even children do not run freely across open ground. They move from grip to grip, testing the turf with hooked staffs and keeping one hand available for a rope.

Their clothing has no loose edge. Inner layers are woven from softened root fiber; outer garments are made of overlapping flaps that close tighter when struck by wind. Hoods fit close around brow and jaw. Hair is braided flat against the scalp and threaded with small beads of shell or glass. These beads are not chiefly decorative. Their tremor against the skull warns of shifts in the air that the ear may miss.

Most wear a cord wound several times around the waist. It serves as belt, lifeline, measure, tourniquet, snare, bridge repair, and proof of adulthood. A child is given a short cord when first trusted beyond the shelter ring. At maturity, that cord is spliced into a longer one by relatives and neighbors. At death, a person’s cord is unbraided. Its fibers are divided among the household ropes, so the dead continue as knots, handholds, roof ties, and path lines.

No one is buried in deep ground. There is too little soil, and fissures cannot be trusted. Bodies are wrapped into the living root mats at sheltered margins, where moss and vine close over them. A family may say that an ancestor has “taken hold” when a particular patch resists a gale that strips everything around it.

Their settlements are small because shelter cannot safely become monumental. A village is often no more than six or eight low houses gathered behind a ridge of stone, connected by crawl trenches and hand ropes. From above it resembles a patch of darker ground. Entrances face sideways to the prevailing wind and bend twice before reaching the interior. The rooms are sunk where possible. Sleeping platforms lie directly on the warm rock, and possessions occupy fitted recesses that can be sealed before a storm.

The center of a household is not a hearth but a water wall: a cool, porous surface honeycombed with grooves. Night condensation gathers there and runs into covered jars. The first waking person touches the wall before greeting anyone. Its wetness decides the day’s labor, diet, and travel.

They eat tubers taken from under the turf, sour fern hearts, fold-ear meat, fungus paste, smoked larvae, and the eggs of rain-basin creatures gathered before the pools disappear. Nothing is boiled uncovered. Steam is water escaping. Cooking pots have weighted lids and narrow recovery tubes that lead condensed vapor back into the vessel.

Because the wind steals ordinary speech, their language has several forms.

Indoors they speak softly, often with the forehead or cheek touching the listener’s. In open country they use the hands, elbows, posture, and the angle of a staff. These signs are large enough to be read at a distance but compact enough not to unbalance the body. Names are signed as well as spoken. A person may have one name for the shelter, another for the open ground, and a third pattern tapped through a taut rope.

Their rope-speech is the most private. Pressure, pauses, and short sequences of plucked vibrations can pass around a house during a storm or along a path hidden by cloud. Simple messages travel far: **water found**, **hold fast**, **crossing unsafe**, **someone approaches**, **not our plateau**. Skilled speakers can identify one another by touch alone.

They do not count age primarily in years. Lives are measured by approaches.

Someone may be described as born when the Split Table came near, married during the three-night joining with Moss-Crowned Shelf, or old enough to remember when the western horizon held five summits instead of four. These are not exact dates. Exactness would imply that the same event looked the same from every place, and the inhabitants know better.

Kinship follows the same uncertainty. Blood relatives matter, but so do those delivered by a temporary bridge, found after a night of convergence, or trapped when their home plateau recedes. Such arrivals are called weather-kin. They are not treated as guests, because a guest is expected to leave by a known road. Weather-kin are given work, water rights, and a place in the shelter until the gulfs make another decision.

There are stories of entire communities arriving this way. A storm ends, and an inhabited table stands close enough for signals. For several days the two peoples exchange seed, tools, marriage promises, news, and corrections to their tendency maps. Then the gulf opens again. Descendants may maintain obligations to a place no living person has visited. A portion of every harvest may be sealed in a cave for cousins whose plateau has not approached in two centuries.

Their maps are bundles rather than sheets. Knotted cords represent sequences of weather; pierced bone slats record recognizable wind tones; thin glider skin carries painted profiles of distant rims. None gives a route in the lowland sense. To read one is to learn patience: go to the eastern court under a hot updraft, wait for three answering notes, watch whether the black-rooted shelf sharpens or blurs, and travel only if cloud begins spilling upward rather than down.

Thus the most honored navigators are not the swiftest walkers. They are those who can wait without persuading themselves that waiting has become useless.

The wind-court keepers hold a related authority, though they are neither rulers nor priests in any simple way. They learn the underground voices of stone vents and maintain the low rings that shape them. Their work requires an exact memory for tones, pressure, season, and the bodily sensation of sound. Many become partially deaf. This is not considered a disability in their vocation. A keeper is expected to listen through palms, ribs, knees, and teeth.

They never say that a court commands the wind. It asks questions of pressure and makes the answers large enough for human bodies to understand.

Yet some tones are preserved without interpretation. At abandoned courts, keepers replace fallen stones and clear roots from vents even when no practical reading survives. Asked why, they say that an unanswered voice is not the same as a meaningless one.

Their ceremonies are correspondingly restrained. There are few tall markers, no banners, no processions that would expose people unnecessarily. For a joining, two families braid ropes together and stretch them across a court while the wind sounds beneath. For mourning, a dead person’s final cord is plucked once from each path they maintained. For the arrival of weather-kin, water is poured into a common shallow bowl and allowed to tremble until all present have touched its rim.

The gravest wrongdoing is to falsify safety: to retie a warning chime so that it gives the wrong motion, to conceal a dry condensation trench, to claim that a bridge is stable when it is not, or to alter a tendency map out of pride. Theft is serious, but deceptive certainty is worse. In this country everyone survives by trusting descriptions of what cannot yet be seen.

Their stories rarely praise conquest. The recurring hero is the one who notices a changed note, loosens a rope before it tears, abandons a destination, or recognizes a stranger’s plateau from the taste of rain. Cleverness consists of revision.

Children are taught early that the world does not owe them continuity. A house may be rebuilt, a pool may leave, a path may widen into sky. They practice dropping flat at shouted signs. They learn to sleep through thunder and wake at the cessation of a familiar hum. They memorize every handhold between home and the nearest court. As a game, they arrange stones to represent the visible plateaus and alter the arrangement whenever cloud hides the horizon. The winner is not the child whose model remains correct, but the one who changes it first without distress.

Still, they are not a grim people.

When the air briefly calms, entire villages come out onto the open green. Fiber streamers are untied from bark and allowed to whisper rather than roar. Children release seed bladders with colored threads attached. Adults lie on their backs, an indulgent and vulnerable posture, and watch the gliders describe invisible rivers overhead. Music is made with drums pressed directly to the ground, so that rhythm travels through the body more reliably than through the air.

Their humor concerns misjudgment: the traveler who packed for a month and arrived before supper; the court keeper who mistook a snoring browser for a deep-pressure warning; the proud navigator who waited six days for a plateau that was only a cloud. Laughter, difficult in the wind, is often shown by shutting the eyes and striking one’s chest with an open hand.

And there may be another inhabitant, though the summit people resist naming it.

It is not the stone, which they insist is fixed. It is not the wind, which merely passes, nor cloud, nor heat. It is the changing interval between things: the gulf in its acts of narrowing and expansion. Some keepers claim that different gulfs have different temperaments. One brings living creatures but never tools. One carries echoes from generations past. One closes readily in summer and opens wider whenever blood is spilled near its rim. Another has not permitted a crossing within any preserved memory, yet answers every wind court at dusk.

No one openly worships these intervals. No offerings are thrown over the edge; to waste anything in that fashion would be foolish. But houses are built with one empty sleeping recess. Meals sometimes leave one covered portion untouched until morning. When an unfamiliar object appears after a convergence, it is not used immediately. It is placed in a court and allowed to sound through one full change of weather.

This caution is partly for strangers. Occasionally the gulfs deliver people.

They arrive drenched, bruised, and certain that their own plateau touched this one only a few steps behind them. Sometimes they speak a recognizable language altered by centuries. Sometimes they carry tools made by methods long forgotten. A few insist they have crossed from a summit whose profile appears on no map, not even among the inherited tendencies.

More rarely, a shelter is found newly near the rim after a storm, its roof pinned, its water wall wet, its cooking vessel still warm—yet no tracks lead to or from it. Such places are left untouched until a court keeper has listened to their foundations.

For all their acceptance of change, the inhabitants fear one possibility: not that their plateau will collapse, but that it will cease to approach any other. Isolation without end is their image of extinction. A land can remain green, watered, and habitable, they say, and still die if no new voice ever answers across the gulf.

So every morning someone goes to the rim.

They inspect the haze, taste droplets from the grass, watch the angle of gliders, and set a palm against the rock. They look not only for danger but for company: a dark underside sharpening through cloud, a grove where empty sky stood yesterday, smoke dragged flat over some newly adjacent green.

If another plateau has come near, the watcher does not shout. Shouting is wasteful, and the wind would take the news.

They turn toward the settlement and make the broad sign for **within reach**.

Then ropes are brought out. Water is divided. Old obligations are remembered. Someone searches the tendency bundles for a profile that may not have been seen in a lifetime, while others stand at the edge and wait to discover what kind of neighbors the weather has made of them.
