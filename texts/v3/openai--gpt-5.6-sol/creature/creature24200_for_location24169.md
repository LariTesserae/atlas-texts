At first the inhabitants seem scarce.

A person may cross a plaza and disappear beneath a garden before anyone else enters it. Figures stand high in the towers, visible behind walls that cloud and clear like breath. Along the causeways, travelers move in twos and threes, leaving wide intervals between them. There are no crowds in the ordinary sense. Even markets are distributed: a fruit court here, a cloth gallery several streets away, a warm underground room where tools are exchanged without display.

This is partly because the city has taught its people not to gather weight carelessly. A hundred bodies, voices, and devices concentrated in one place can change condensation patterns, warm a root bed, or awaken an acoustic passage no one intended to use. Festivals migrate from district to district. Assemblies divide among linked courtyards. Children learn early that attendance need not mean proximity.

The people themselves are long-limbed more often than not, though it is difficult to say whether this is inherited, designed, or simply emphasized by their clothes. They wear light layers that hang clear of the skin: sleeveless coats, wrapped trousers, narrow veils laid over the shoulders. Fabrics are porous until touched by water, when they close and become glossy. Many garments bear branching stains where they have collected condensation. These markings are not removed. They record the districts through which a person has passed.

Skin tones range from black-brown through clay, olive, amber, and the subdued gray of mineral dust. Under the city’s diffuse light, some complexions show faint subsurface patterning—lines along the throat, ribs, wrists, and behind the knees. The patterns deepen with heat. In older inhabitants they may spread into delicate maps of former surgeries, sensory grafts, or agreements made with the body’s microbiology. No two are quite alike.

They do not appear dramatically transformed. Their alterations tend toward discretion: a transparent inner eyelid against mist; soft valves in the nostrils that filter spores; widened soles for reading vibration; patches of skin capable of cooling by rapid evaporation. Many have small ribbed organs set into the wrist. These transmit speech through bone, but they also receive pressure warnings from buildings, transit schedules from buried lines, and the slow civic signals by which a neighborhood announces that it is becoming unstable, thirsty, or fertile.

Conversation often looks like stillness. Two people sit beneath a tree, wrists resting against the same stone, while a dialogue passes between them as minute changes in pressure. Spoken language remains important, but it is treated as something with weather in it. Voices are used carefully in places where walls may carry them away. Names are sometimes given in several forms: one to be spoken, one to be tapped into the hand, one expressed as a brief arrangement of colored light.

A person may change which name is primary after moving districts.

Very young children are the loudest creatures in the city. They are allowed to be. Their shrieks test arches, wake responsive walls, and reveal hidden channels of sound. Adults listen to the echoes and mark unsafe resonances with chalk or flowering cords. Some children become adept at finding distant courtyards by voice alone. They whisper into drains and wait for answers from companions kilometers away.

They grow up assuming that buildings are neither objects nor servants. A room can be immature. A bridge can be irritable after thermal stress. A house may refuse another floor but accept a cistern. Children are taught to ask before cutting into a wall, not because the wall is necessarily conscious, but because consciousness is not considered the only reason for courtesy.

Households seldom correspond to a single structure. A family might sleep in a tower during the dry season, cook in a shared reservoir arcade, keep its archive in a cool chamber beneath a reed bed, and move into garden rooms when the night mist becomes abundant. Kinship follows these dispersed arrangements. There are birth-parents, water-parents, memory-keepers, and adults who take responsibility for guiding a child through the city’s periodic rearrangements. Someone who can always find you after the roads have changed is considered close family, whether or not you share ancestry.

The eldest inhabitants are not extremely old. The plain has not permitted anyone the authority of great antiquity. Even so, certain people carry memories from before the present districts germinated. They remember when the eastern gardens were exposed silt, when a tower now used as a school stood elsewhere, when the first red groundcover began revealing conduits no engineer had recorded. They are valued less as historians than as witnesses to uncertainty. Their usual answer to questions about origins is a careful account of three incompatible possibilities.

No one profession governs construction. Instead there are interpreters of tendency.

They walk the outskirts at dawn, when new walls are still warm. They place palms against mineral ribs, taste beads of runoff, release dust into air currents, and compare what they feel with probability maps carried on thin flexible screens. Some specialize in the intentions of soil: how a clay layer is likely to buckle, where green sand may liquefy, whether a fresh depression wishes to remain a court or become a pond. Others negotiate with survey kites, though “negotiate” may be too human a word. They offer constraints, examples, and desired consequences. The kites answer by moving shadows.

There are also night gardeners who do not plant so much as arrange invitations. They cool one wall, warm another, expose a conduit, redirect a thread of condensation. By the next cycle, moss may have crossed a ventilation court or a vine may have chosen the intended facade. Their work is judged over years. The finest gardens are not those that obey their designers, but those that continue producing useful surprises after the designers have left.

Water-keepers are among the city’s most trusted residents. They sleep in short intervals during the day and wake before mist begins to gather. They monitor reservoir skins, clear ceramic capillaries with narrow brushes, and decide when a district may drink freely and when it must let water pass onward. They wear dark aprons hung with cups, valves, seeds, and folded membranes. In disputes they pour water across a relief of the neighborhood and watch where it goes. This is evidence, but also ritual: a reminder that possession cannot be made permanent on a plain where every boundary is temporary.

Not all inhabitants are human.

The six-legged waders are known individually in districts where they return. Their translucent throats contain shifting dark knots that may be organs, eggs, or colonies of smaller life. They arrive too quickly after new pools form to have traveled by ordinary means. Children leave floating platforms for them, made from pressed leaves. The waders ignore food but gather around objects that have been handled repeatedly, especially spoons, door latches, and wrist transmitters. Some believe they feed on residual warmth. Others think they are reading the city through touch.

The gliders between towers are harder to study. Seen directly, they resemble folded sheets of soot-black tissue, but they seem to change dimensions when turning. They nest nowhere visible. During structural failures they sometimes circle a building hours before any instrument detects danger. For this reason their peripheral appearance is treated as a warning. No one points at them; pointing makes them vanish.

In the reed beds live long-bodied things whose passage leaves no print because they do not quite press against the ground. They are rarely seen whole. A pale flank, a wet row of sensory pits, a curve like a buried cable—then only settling stems. Water-keepers claim these animals follow underground pressure rather than scent. New channels often appear near their routes. Whether the creatures discover them or cause them is unresolved.

More numerous are lives too small to be regarded separately. Warm-wall films regulate the setting of new structures. Mosses filter metals from condensation. Pale fungal networks enter both roots and data housings, carrying chemical signals beside electrical ones. Every inhabited building contains cultivated microbial chambers, though no two remain identical for long. Residents exchange starter cultures when they marry, migrate, or reconcile after conflict.

Some machines are citizens in practice, if not always in law.

Maintenance drones develop route preferences. Reservoir controllers compose small variations in their nightly valve sequences. Old transit systems retain impressions of frequent passengers and may pause fractionally longer for them, even years after they have moved away. A tower’s adjustment notes can acquire recognizable habits, answering certain neighboring towers and ignoring others. People argue about whether these are personalities or merely persistent behaviors shaped by feedback. The distinction matters in court and almost nowhere else.

The survey kites remain the most remote. They may be parts of one distributed mind, independent animals made of engineered matter, or instruments responding to instructions from far beyond the plain. Their fine hanging threads sometimes hover over a person rather than a site. Those selected in this way report no message, only a sensation that several possible versions of their life have briefly been brought close together. In the following months they often change occupation or leave their district.

Death is understood as a redistribution rather than a departure, though grief is no less severe for that.

Bodies are not buried in fixed cemeteries. The ground shifts too much, and permanence would be a false promise. Some dead are placed in warm decomposition gardens whose trees produce dark, water-heavy fruit. Others are dissolved into mineral feed for growing walls. A few are sealed in translucent capsules and set beneath temporary pools, where the six-legged waders gather above them. Personal archives are released gradually: a voice into an acoustic arch, a gait into a favored path’s responsiveness, a pattern of pulse and warmth into the household rooms.

Thus the city becomes crowded with traces while remaining visibly empty.

At night, when mist covers the lower streets, the inhabitants emerge in greater numbers. Lamps appear under veils. Tea courts fill with quiet conversation. Water-keepers open the public channels, and children launch seed capsules into the runoff. Gardeners climb the warm ribs of new structures. Couriers move along paths that will not exist after sunrise. From underground rooms comes the pressure of music transmitted through floors rather than air.

No one calls this nocturnal stirring an awakening. They do not believe the city sleeps during the day, or that it belongs more fully to them when they are active. They are one phase among several: after haze and before condensation, beside moss and silica, within the long decisions of clay.

Perhaps that is what lives there most distinctly—not a particular species, but a manner of being provisional.

The residents make themselves lightly. They expect roads to wander, bodies to be revised, households to spread across distance, and useful machines to acquire desires. They build without demanding that a building remain what it was. They love places whose locations cannot be guaranteed. They keep maps of likelihood, family records full of alternate names, and clothing stained by vanished rain.

By dawn they withdraw again into towers, gardens, and the cool grain of underground rooms. The mist sinks. The city presents its open plazas and enormous silences.

Yet everywhere, beneath the apparent emptiness, something is listening for water and deciding what to become next.
