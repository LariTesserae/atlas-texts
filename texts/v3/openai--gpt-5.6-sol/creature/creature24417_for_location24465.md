At first the inhabitants seem smaller than they are.

This is partly the scale of the place. A single person crossing one of the assembly courts appears diminished by all the empty seating, the open stairs, the long walls waiting for roofs. It is also their way of moving. They seldom occupy the center of a street. They keep close to channels and arcades, stepping where the stone has already declared itself sound. They pause often—not timidly, but to listen. Before entering a courtyard, someone may stand with one hand against the wall, feeling for the pulse of falling water beneath it.

They are human, though strangers sometimes mistake their reed mantles and mineral-whitened clothing for moss-covered masonry. Most are lean, broad-footed, and strong through the shoulders. Their hands are marked by wet labor: fingertips cracked from mineral salts, nails stained green or orange, palms polished by rope and wooden tool handles. Older people often have one shoulder higher than the other from carrying water gates, baskets of clay, and sling loads along steep paths.

Their clothing is layered rather than heavy. Inner garments are woven from grass fiber and soft bark thread. Over these they wear short wool or felt tunics acquired by exchange with people below. The outermost layer is usually a cape of overlapping reeds, supple in mist and easily replaced when it mildews. Almost everyone carries a length of waxed cord, a bronze or stone-edged knife, and several wedges tucked into a belt. Even children have small measuring sticks.

Nothing they wear is expected to remain whole. Sleeves are pieced from different fabrics. Sandals have replaceable soles. A person’s cloak may contain strips cut from those of parents and grandparents, not as ornament but because sound material is never discarded. On festival days they wash the mineral stains from their faces but leave the pale crust along their cuffs. It is considered evidence of useful work.

They do not divide themselves cleanly into builders, gardeners, water keepers, or householders. Everyone must know enough of each craft to prevent a local failure. A cook can reset a channel stone. A child carrying herbs can identify the first signs of a clogged drain. The person recording measurements in the assembly court may leave the tablets at once to climb a scaffold and retension a survey line.

Nevertheless, some abilities are held in particular esteem.

There are listeners who can distinguish the hum of a healthy wall from the note of a cavity about to close. They walk the perimeter before dawn, when wind and temperature are most consistent, carrying small whistles and hollow clay cups. They do not command others, but their judgments can move an entire household from one quarter to another.

There are level-readers, usually older people with extraordinary patience, who maintain the cords stretched between survey posts. They keep records not merely of height but of tendency: which stones rise evenly, which tilt toward a new spring, which seem stable only because the ground around them is catching up. Their tablets are full of lines continuing beyond the edges, copied from one slab to the next over generations.

Most honored are the adjusters. An adjuster is called when a door will no longer close, when a wall begins to bear weight it was never meant to carry, or when two channels exchange their gradients. Their work is rarely dramatic. They loosen, shim, lift, drain, trim, and wait. A good adjustment may look like almost nothing. The highest praise given to such work is that the building “did not notice.”

Households are equally provisional. They are not defined by one dwelling, since no family can expect to remain under the same roof for long. A household is a group sharing an oven turn, sleeping mats, water obligations, and an inherited sequence of works. Its members may occupy three houses in different quarters. During a wet year they gather on the southern wall; during a dry one they move uphill near the spring. Kinship matters, but adoption is common, especially adoption into a task. An apprentice who accepts responsibility for an unfinished stair becomes kin to those who began it, whether or not blood joins them.

What passes from one generation to the next is therefore less often property than intention.

A child may inherit the western face of a storehouse, together with the knowledge that the ground beneath it is expected to rise another handspan. Another inherits six silver-barked trees and the obligation to keep their roots from entering a cistern. Someone else inherits a doorway suspended above an old threshold and the prediction that it will become usable in forty years.

They speak of these obligations as if speaking of absent relatives.

“The north stair is impatient.”

“The red court has begun turning.”

“The third tower will meet us soon.”

A building that cannot yet be occupied is not considered unreal. Nor is an old room lost beneath mineral stone considered destroyed. It has merely passed out of use and into the mountain’s keeping.

Children learn the city first by sound. Before they can name every lane, they can identify the cistern impacts beneath their sleeping quarter and recognize the note made by wind at the nearest gate. Games consist of being blindfolded and led through arcades, then asked to point toward the spring. They float bits of dyed bark in channels to learn flow times. They press wet clay over cracks and return days later to see whether the marks have stretched.

Their toys resemble instruments: miniature lifting frames, clay pipes, knotted cords, small walls made to be dismantled. They build little settlements beside runnels and delight when mineral deposits cement them into unusable lumps. Adults do not correct these constructions. The ruined model is itself a lesson.

There are few children at any one time. The settlement cannot support many mouths, and births are planned around garden capacity and the expected opening of new terraces. This gives each child an unusual visibility. Their footprints are noticed. Their illnesses reorganize work. When one laughs in an empty gallery, the sound may travel through half the district, and people smile in rooms the child cannot see.

The inhabitants are neither solemn nor especially mystical, though outsiders often imagine they must worship the mountain. They are too familiar with its inconveniences for simple reverence. They curse blocked gutters, stubborn moss, badly timed seepage, and soft stone that crumbles beneath a wedge. Yet they do not think of the ground as inert. To them, stone is not the opposite of growth. It is growth at another speed.

Their rites are practical acts extended until they become communal.

When a new spring appears, no one drinks from it immediately. For several days they observe its color, warmth, smell, and the kinds of film it leaves on clay. A shallow channel is cut, and one of the translucent crustaceans from an established watercourse is placed within it. If the creature survives, the spring is welcomed. People touch the water to their eyelids, then begin arguing over grades and distribution.

When a structure must be abandoned, its useful panels and timbers are removed, but one wall is usually left standing. On it they paint the former floor level and the names of those who slept there. Later mineral skin covers the writing. No effort is made to restore it. Memory, they say, should not be kept drier than anything else.

The dead cannot be buried deeply. There is too little soil, and the young rock resists excavation. Bodies are wrapped in woven mats and placed for a season in high, airy chambers along the southern wall. Afterward the bones are cleaned and divided. Some are ground into clay used to seal the household’s water jars. Larger pieces are set into unfinished walls, never as visible decoration but deep within loose courses where they do not bear weight. A dead person thus becomes part of a structure without being asked to support it.

This distinction matters to them. They dislike metaphors of duty that sound like load-bearing. No one should be made the foundation of everyone else. Foundations move.

Their assembly has no fixed ruler. Authority passes according to the matter at hand: the spring keeper speaks for water, the level-reader for gradients, gardeners for roots, oven tenders for fuel and smoke. Decisions are marked on clay and placed in public niches. Disputes are usually about timing. Almost everyone agrees on what must eventually be done; they quarrel over whether the mountain is ready.

The gravest offense is not theft but concealment of change. A person who notices a leaning post, sour water, a new crack, or a shift in the wall’s tone must mark it at once. To hide such a thing because repairing it would burden one’s household is to endanger people years hence. Their strongest condemnations are reserved for those who pretend stability.

They trade with the valleys below, though reluctantly. Small parties descend carrying mineral pigments, medicinal mosses, fired pipe fittings, and records of spring temperatures. They return with wool, grain, bronze, salt, resin, and news. Lowlanders find them frustrating merchants. They inspect every wooden object for hidden damp and reject metal tools that cannot be repaired with local means. They will trade generously for seed, cordage, and stories of floods, but show little interest in jewelry.

Those who leave the shelf for long periods often suffer from the stillness below. Floors that remain level seem unnatural. Silent walls make sleep difficult. Rivers, with their committed courses, appear almost aggressive. Some emigrants settle in the valleys, but many return thin and restless, saying that old land gives no warning because it has forgotten how to speak.

Visitors are received kindly and watched continuously. This attention is not suspicion so much as concern. A stranger may step on a mineral lip too young to bear weight, close a door meant to ventilate a room, or place a heavy pack on a floor awaiting adjustment. Guests are given bright cords to wear so they can be found quickly in cloud. They are taught three sounds on their first day: the warning whistle of ice, the hollow knock of an opening cistern, and the rapid clay rattle that means everyone must move uphill.

The settlement has no soldiers. Its remoteness is defense enough, and there is little portable wealth. The broad courts and walls can look formidable from afar, but many parapets are garden beds, and several towers would collapse under the weight of armed occupants. Their weapons are hunting spears kept mainly for the rare goat or fox that enters the plantings. They are handled without ceremony and used reluctantly. Large animal blood is almost unknown there; when spilled, it seems shockingly bright against the pale stone.

The creatures of the shelf are treated less as resources than as fellow indicators.

The black-winged birds are tolerated in vent towers because changes in their nesting height often precede colder seasons. Pale moths gather beneath bridge stones, where their disappearance may reveal contamination before people can taste it. The tiny crustaceans in the channels are watched with particular care. Children count them through clear clay cups. A channel in which they vanish is shut even if the water looks pure.

No one claims to understand where these creatures came from. Some say they have always lived inside the mountain and emerge only when water approaches the air. Others believe they are remnants from an older landscape buried beneath the rising shelf. Their red inner thread is depicted on water jars as a single wavering line.

At night the inhabitants become difficult to distinguish from the city’s mechanisms. A lamp moves through an arcade. Someone taps a pipe and waits for its answer. Two figures lift a wall panel to let damp escape. In the upper quarter, a spring keeper sleeps beside the basin with one hand resting on the floor, waking when its faint vibration changes.

They do not imagine that the city will one day be finished and filled. Completion is not the promise that sustains them. They expect future people to alter their plans, abandon some towers, open streets no one has yet conceived, and laugh at measurements now treated with solemn care.

What they hope to pass on is not a completed place but a place still capable of being changed.

This may explain the city’s extravagant emptiness. Its plazas are not waiting for a lost population, nor necessarily for a multitude to come. The open space is room for correction. The unused galleries are allowances for water not yet risen. The extra doors admit futures from several possible heights.

The people live sparsely because they do not regard emptiness as absence. Empty courts hold drainage. Empty rooms let walls breathe. Unoccupied streets preserve paths for movement when lower ones close. Gaps between households allow the ground to declare its next shape.

Even among themselves they value this room. Conversation contains long pauses. Advice is often given as a question. Children are not pressed early into a single craft. A person may leave an inherited work untouched for years if the stone has not yet answered.

Seen from outside, they are builders.

Seen more closely, they are attendants to beginnings: gardeners of thin soil, readers of unfinished rock, citizens of a city whose chief civic virtue is the willingness to move a door.
