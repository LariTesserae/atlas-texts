The people who live there are not, at first glance, much altered by the place. They have not become eyeless, nor translucent, nor small enough to pass through the rootwork. Visitors expecting cave creatures are disappointed. The citizens are recognizably human.

Only after watching them for a while do the differences gather.

They are narrow people, for the most part—not uniformly short, but spare through the shoulder and practiced at turning sideways. Children learn early to lead with one arm when entering a passage, protecting the face while feeling for hot metal, thorns, or another body approaching in the dark. Adults duck without seeming to duck. They step over pipes in mid-conversation. Even in an empty room, they stand as if sharing it with machinery.

Their palms are thickened along the edges rather than at the center, from testing walls and handrails before taking hold. Many have pale heat scars on the forearms and a stippling of mineral burns around the wrists. Hair is cut close, braided flat, or sealed beneath cloth caps soaked in aromatic resin. Beards are uncommon in the hotter districts. Eyebrows and lashes tend to be singed unevenly, giving some faces a skeptical expression they do not intend.

They sweat carefully.

Clothing is loose where air must move and tight where it might catch. The common garment is a sleeveless inner shift beneath overlapping aprons of woven mineral fiber, with pockets stitched vertically so their contents do not fall out when the wearer climbs. Cork-soled sandals are wrapped to the ankle with damp-resistant cord. Everyone carries a strip of insulating cloth, a ceramic cup with a fitted lid, and several small wedges for holding swollen doors open. A well-equipped citizen also carries chalk, pollen brushes, a pinch of salt, and a loop of soft wire. Knives are less useful than scrapers.

Water has shaped their manners more deeply than law has.

No one drinks while walking. To do so would risk spilling, and also implies that the drink was obtained too easily. Cups are held in both hands even when nearly empty. Before drinking, a person touches one wet finger to the base of the throat—not as an offering, they insist, but to cool the pulse and judge the body’s need. Children are taught not to lick condensation from pipes until they can identify the metals by taste. Guests are offered steam before water: a warmed cloth held over the mouth and nose, fragrant with bitter leaves. The gesture means, *You may take something from this room without diminishing it.*

Wastefulness is treated less as sin than as evidence of dangerous inattention. Someone who leaves a cistern lid ajar will be watched for days afterward. Are they ill? Grieving? Beginning to lose their memory? Punishment comes only if carelessness is deliberate. Accidents are repaired collectively and remembered precisely.

Names often record such repairs. A woman may be called Mara-of-the-Sealed-Fault though she did not seal it herself; the name belongs to her household. Others are named for gradients, valves, stair turns, or the season in which they were born: Senn Under-Seven-Degrees, Oru Pale-Bloom, Little Tavi at the Third Relay. These descriptions change as people move. A fixed name is used only in contracts and funerary inventories.

They speak softly, not from solemnity but because loudness is inefficient. Their language has many words for silence: the silence of stopped machinery, the silence before dripping begins, the muffled silence of fungal walls, the uneasy silence caused by a missing engine pulse. They can distinguish a healthy pipe by pressing the jaw against it. Mechanics sometimes listen with their teeth.

Conversation proceeds in short exchanges, with pauses long enough to trouble an outsider. A question may not be answered until the speaker and listener have climbed two flights and crossed into a cooler district. Interruptions are rare. Repetition is rarer still, because repeated words might enter the pipes and return stripped of context.

They do not answer the returns, but they listen.

Some keep private records of them on thin ceramic slips: the phrase heard, the vent from which it came, the temperature of the wall, whether the listener was alone. Officially these archives are used to diagnose pressure faults. Unofficially, people search them for dead relatives, forgotten routes, warnings, and proof that the settlement has intentions.

There are citizens who claim they can recognize a true return from a meaningless one. They are called listeners, though most are plumbers by trade. They sit beside suspect ducts with one ear covered, counting breaths between syllables. Their status is ambiguous. Engineers consult them and mock them afterward. Families pay them in dried fungus to determine whether a phrase is ancestral or merely mechanical.

The most respected workers are those who notice slow events.

A piston keeper may spend an entire shift observing four movements of a rod. A wall-reader marks the advance of a crack with glass dust and returns six months later. Gradient stewards walk the same circuits at the same hour, laying their palms against ceramic plaques and recording how warmth has shifted. Rootwrights train living cables through masonry, sometimes beginning a brace they know their grandchildren will finish.

Haste is associated with catastrophe. The person running through a corridor is either a child, a fool, or the bearer of news that will change the route of heat. Even emergencies are addressed with a peculiar restraint. Valves are closed in sequence. Doors are wetted around their rims. The injured are moved only after the floor ahead has been touched and tested.

Their doctors understand the body as another warm, crowded structure with limited water and no convenient place for debris. They favor drainage, compression, patient cooling, and fungal medicines grown in little sealed cupboards. Wounds are stitched with fibers that slowly mineralize and remain beneath the skin as pale branching lines. Fever is mapped directly on the body in washable clay: red marks where heat pools, white where it leaves. A prolonged illness can cover a patient in diagrams.

Birth is difficult there, though not especially rare. Children are usually born in the cooler rooms that migrate from household to household over the decades. For this reason, families do not own birthing chambers; they own claims upon a temperature. When a suitable cool patch moves behind a pantry wall or into a neighbor’s stairwell, the room’s function follows it.

Infants are carried high on the chest, above hot railings and low pipes. They sleep in suspended ceramic cradles lined with fungal felt. Before they are given a household name, a newborn is brought near the New Well—not over the opening, but close enough to feel its draft. If the child turns toward the smell of rain, this is considered fortunate. If it turns away, no one says what that means.

Children are among the few creatures permitted to be noisy. They crawl through retired ducts, trade moth wings, and dare one another to touch doors said to have rooms behind them. They learn the city by obsolete routes. Because they are smaller, they can still enter passages abandoned to roots, and sometimes return with objects from former houses: a spoon green with copper, a glazed tile bearing half a face, a cup still sealed around a thimbleful of ancient water.

Adults discourage this in public and examine every recovered object in private.

There are elderly people who have outlived the useful accuracy of their own memories. The stair they recall has tilted; the workshop has been enclosed; the cool bedroom of childhood is now a vat of red algae. Such elders often become route-tellers. They describe not where a place is, but what lies inside what: the lamp room around the old kitchen around the storeroom with the blue door. Builders value them because they remember which walls are walls and which are merely the hardened exteriors of forgotten machines.

The dead are not buried. There is no soil to spare and nowhere sufficiently outside the settlement.

Bodies are dried in circulating air, wrapped in rootcloth, and placed in cavities within new construction. A person may become the quiet core of a retaining wall, a stair landing, or the base of a cistern. Names are inscribed inward, facing the sealed room, so that no public surface is taken up by memorials. The dead are said to “hold” the structure. Whether this is metaphor depends on whom one asks. Rootwrights report that brace roots often thicken around occupied walls.

Nothing useful is placed with a body. The dead receive one extravagant thing only: an open cup. It is always empty.

Not everyone living there is fully counted as a citizen.

There are the tenders of sealed flower rooms, who spend so much time avoiding human breath that they become shy of company. They communicate through slates passed beneath doors. Their skin smells of wax and cold pollen. Some are said to sleep in air cupboards and go days without speaking.

There are under-grate workers who maintain the old passages and slow machinery. They emerge lacquered in black condensation, their clothes shining, their eyes reddened by mineral vapor. Because sound travels unpredictably below, they use taps made directly against the bones of the wrist. They know of chambers absent from every map. They also report roots that withdraw when approached and pipes warm with no measurable contents.

There are those who live unofficially in enclosed rooms: debtors, widows who surrendered their gradient rights, lovers hiding from household contracts, and people who simply prefer walls with only one door. Their existence is tolerated so long as they contribute labor and do not interfere with water. Food appears near their vents. Repairs are completed at night. Sometimes one is known only by a hand seen passing a finished valve through a gap.

And there may be others.

The white lizard has adherents despite the absence of proof. They leave moth husks on high pipes and tie loose strips of insulation where thermal movement might carry them away. To them, the creature is not a beast but the city’s only true wanderer, capable of passing through districts without owing anyone heat, labor, or water. Children claim it has fingers. Under-grate workers say it has no head. Once, during a Tight Time, something long and pale crossed three translucent cooling tubes in succession, against the direction of flow. For years afterward, infants born in that district were given a white thread around one ankle.

The plants, too, are not regarded as passive crops. Each cultivated line belongs to a working category—brace, wick, filter, shade, food, seal—but old specimens often exceed their assigned purpose. A ceiling garden may remember a demolished stair and continue lowering leaves toward the space where people once climbed. Reservoir plants swell before leaks are detected. Pollination flowers turn toward unopened doors. Root bundles sometimes grow around a failing pipe without being trained.

Citizens speak of these events without deciding whether they indicate intelligence. Intelligence is not a particularly useful distinction there. What matters is whether a thing participates, whether it alters the shared balance, whether its needs can be predicted, and whether it remembers.

By that measure, the settlement itself is among the living.

It breathes through cooling shafts. It sweats. It closes routes and opens others. It consumes structures without destroying them. Its oldest machines pulse more slowly than any animal heart, while its newest cracks remain bright as wounds. The people do not worship it, exactly. They quarrel with it, repair it, prune it, inherit portions of its warmth, and complain when it changes without notice. Their relationship is less like devotion than family: intimate, indispensable, and full of obligations no one remembers agreeing to.

During the upward rain, all these lives draw close—the engineers with their ceramic maps, the pollen tenders in their masks, the black-shining under-grate crews, children lifted onto shoulders, elders who remember when the Well was only a crack. Roots hang among them. Moths settle under dimmed lamps. Somewhere below, a piston completes its patient stroke.

No one speaks as the droplets rise.

Perhaps they are listening for the water. Perhaps for the stone. Perhaps for some return that has not yet reached them: a message spoken when the cavern was first broken open, still traveling through brick and pipe and root, becoming slower and stranger on its way.

When the rain ends, they go back to work. They press the water into new seams. They reopen valves. They resume shaping metal without blows and growing walls around what cannot be removed.

They live there by becoming difficult to separate from where they live.
