What lives in the Green Mill is not a collection of creatures so much as a single life distributed among many bodies.

Its largest body is the mill organism itself. No one part of it is the plant. The ribbed trunk, pumping drums, revolving pods, reservoirs, valves, and miles of roots beneath the marsh are organs of something whose boundaries cannot be found. The broad leaves above may be its foliage, or merely a crop it tends. The fungi in its chambers may be symbionts, or tissues that learned to travel under their own power. Even the warm water passing through it leaves carrying enough spores, cells, and dissolved sap that the outflow seems less like drainage than a diluted continuation of the mill.

It is ancient without being old. Nothing in it survives unchanged; bark wears away, valve lips split, chambers soften and are digested. Yet every loss is replaced before it becomes absence. Its identity persists not in matter but in proportion and rhythm. It is the interval between contractions, the maintained depth of each pool, the exact pressure at which a seed-pod begins to turn. If it thinks, its thoughts are balances.

The blue discharges in its root hairs are the nearest thing it has to nerves. They pass through wet tissues in slow, branching waves, summoning contractions, closing overflow membranes, altering the sweetness of a lure-flower. In the hidden recesses, filament fans register tremors and shadows. Seed capsules answer with patterned openings. The organism counts without numbers and remembers without storing images. A drought might be retained as a thicker wall in one buried reservoir. A vanished insect might survive as the spacing between two flowers.

The metallic beetles are among its most visible inhabitants. They are low, heavy creatures, their backs rounded like drops of hardened oil—green, bronze, or blue according to the angle of the light. Each beetle spends its life walking one groove. Its flattened feet fit that groove precisely. As it advances, it scrapes away softened bark and fungal crust, polishing the channel while feeding. At the end it descends through a slit and reappears much later near the beginning, washed clean and faintly warm. No beetle seems to know the circuit, but the circuit knows the beetle. Where one dies, larvae mature beneath the bark and the groove is occupied again.

The frogs live in chambers shaped to their bodies and voices. They are broad-faced, damp, and nearly the color of new leaves, with translucent skin at the throat. Each has a hollow: a woody cup grown around a pocket of air. When the mill’s pressure reaches a certain point, cool sap beads along the rim. The frog feels this through its feet, inflates its throat, and calls. Its note is magnified by the cavity and travels through the roots as a clean concussion.

The frogs do not appear to call to one another. Their calls regulate something deeper. A note may release a valve, dislodge a clot of pollen, or signal a contraction in a drum beyond the chamber. Young frogs begin with uncertain voices, striking too early or too softly. Their hollows slowly alter around them, narrowing until even a poor call produces the required tone. Thus the mill trains them by changing the world in which they speak.

Emerald snakes wait above the frog hollows. They are slender, almost leaf-veins given muscle, with pale mouths and eyes like wet seeds. They feed rarely. A snake may lie across the same branch for weeks, accepting the warmth that passes through the wood. Then one frog’s hollow remains silent. The snake swallows, the mill adjusts, and a smaller frog eventually emerges from a water pocket in the wall. The snakes are predators, but also pauses: living gaps inserted into the rhythm so that the chorus never grows dense enough to blur its separate notes.

The pale crabs are custodians of softer matter. Their shells are cream-colored and porous, often greened by moss. One claw is narrow enough to tease fibers from a clogged lattice; the other is blunt, suited to tamping them into digestive clefts. They work without urgency. Bits of leaf, drowned larvae, shed frog skin, and strips of failing membrane pass through their claws. Crabs that wander from the maintained channels become sluggish, as if deprived of some necessary current. Those that remain at their stations may live long enough for rootlets to grow harmlessly through the holes in their shells.

In the deeper runnels are transparent fish, visible chiefly by their eyes and the fine silver line of their spines. They face perpetually upstream. Their fins make corrections too small to see, holding them in the same place while water, pollen, bacteria, and mineral dust move through their open mouths. Their bodies are living sieves. Colored granules accumulate in specialized sacs along their flanks, and at intervals the mill brushes these sacs with root hairs, harvesting what the fish have sorted. The fish do not flee the touch. They tremble, empty themselves, and resume their stations.

Mosquitoes inhabit the vertical air. They rise in narrow columns above warm pools, each swarm bounded as sharply as a stalk of smoke. Their larvae graze the bacterial films below; the adults carry droplets between flowers that may be separated by no more than a few handspans but are chemically incompatible without them. The carnivorous flowers remove those that stray, weaken, or multiply beyond the width of the column. Their traps close softly and reopen clean.

Ants occupy the hinges.

They are minute, black, and glossy, and their nests lie in dry capillaries threaded through the living valves. When molds encroach on a moving seam, the ants pour out and cut them back to a precise margin. They do not clear all fungus. They preserve a narrow felt of it, enough to lubricate the hinge without obstructing it. Their dead are carried to warm pockets where pale fungal knobs consume them. From those knobs the colony later feeds. Whether the ants cultivate the fungus or the fungus cultivates the ants is not a distinction the mill appears to make.

The cicadas are less like animals than installed voices. Their nymphs live for years inside the walls, mouths fixed to vascular threads. When mature, they do not emerge into the marsh. They crawl only as far as resonant bark chambers and fasten themselves there. Their wings remain folded. Their continuous whine changes pitch with the electrical state of the wet roots, turning the chamber’s chemistry into sound. A listener might eventually learn to hear salinity, pressure, or approaching decay in their harmonics. The mill may hear them through vibration. Or the cicadas may simply be its hearing made external.

Fungi pervade everything. Some appear as silver fur under ledges, some as black fans inside fermentation bulbs, some only as a smell of bread or bruised fruit. They dissolve what the roots cannot, precipitate metals into dark nodules, and pass nutrients from dying structures into new ones. Their threads cross the plant’s tissues so completely that no clean division remains. In places they form pale cords like tendons. In others they bloom into delicate cups that catch single falling drops, each impact releasing a measured cloud of spores.

Bacteria inhabit the thinnest surfaces: rainbow skins on pools, rust-colored lines along channels, blue-green light in sealed bladders. Individually they are beneath the scale of the mill’s visible order, yet much of its warmth and current begins with them. They sour sugars, bind nitrogen, plate minerals onto roots, and generate the faint voltages carried by the filaments. They are the innumerable inner weather of the place.

There may also be a rarer inhabitant, though it is difficult to say whether it is an animal at all.

Something moves through the mill without displacing water. It is noticed only when several activities coincide: beetles halt with one foot raised, mosquito columns narrow, the cicadas’ pitch falls, and the translucent fans in the recesses flush from green to violet. No body appears. Instead, attention seems to pass through the chamber. Valves delay their closing by a fraction. Frogs turn their bright eyes toward an empty corridor. Seed capsules open in a sequence that travels away into the roots.

Perhaps this is the mill’s thought moving from organ to organ. Perhaps it is a small, soft creature that lives in the vascular dark and has never needed to enter open air. Perhaps every animal feels it because each contains some trace of the same fungus, sap, or electrical pulse.

Whatever it is, it does not command. Nothing in the Green Mill commands. The plant constrains the beetles, the beetles shape the bark, the frogs time the valves, the valves preserve the pools, the pools breed the insects, and the insects carry the plant into its own flowers. Predator and prey, organ and tenant, machine and operator are temporary names for positions in a cycle.

The inhabitants are born into grooves already fitted to them. As they grow, the grooves widen. When they die, the grooves close or receive another body. None can leave without becoming something else, and nothing enters without being sorted, fed upon, dissolved, and incorporated.

What lives there is therefore one creature and a multitude: a marsh-sized plant attended by animals, an animal society enclosed in a plant, a machine whose moving parts hatch from eggs, and an ecology so complete that it has become an individual.

Its pulse is audible in every resident.

**Thrum** in the trunk.  
**Clack** in shell, valve, jaw, and hinge.  
**Rush** in blood, sap, water, and wing.

Then again.

And again.
