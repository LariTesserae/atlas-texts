No creature lives continuously in the Hollow Ward.

Life there is intermittent. It opens, feeds, crosses some brief interval of the city’s activity, and closes again. Even the things that appear motionless are usually waiting for a particular arrangement of water, heat, gravity, and sound. To inhabit the Ward is not merely to occupy space. It is to keep an appointment with a cycle.

The smallest inhabitants are therefore the most numerous, though for much of the time they are indistinguishable from residue.

In the Sink, the black silt is threaded with dormant eggs, cysts, folded membranes, and organisms dried to translucence. A handful of it placed in water would seem at first to contain only salt and powdered masonry. Then threads would uncurl. Minute jaws would begin working. Fine-legged bodies, clear except for rust-red digestive tracts, would separate themselves from the grains.

The largest of these are the eyeless basin crustaceans. They emerge with the condensation shower, sometimes while drops are still striking the Sink. Their bodies are almost flat, each segment fringed with combs that let them swim through water scarcely deeper than their own thickness. Where gravity lies sideways across a pool, they move along its vertical surface as though grazing a wall of glass.

They eat the brief abundance left by the city: microbial skin, softened moss spores, iron bacteria, the organic dust sifted from ventilation shafts. They also eat their own dead. Nothing edible remains exposed when the pools contract.

As evaporation begins, the crustaceans gather in shallow pits and knot themselves together. The outer animals die first, making a damp shell around those within. The survivors lay eggs into the shelter of their relatives’ bodies before burrowing beneath the salt membrane. In a poor cycle an entire generation may hatch only to become insulation for the next.

Some eggs wait decades. A few are carried upward when the gravity routes change, embedded in flakes of salt that drift through the chamber. These may land in rooftop gutters, abandoned cisterns, or cups of broken ornament. Thus tiny populations appear far above the Sink, live for three days in a handspan of brown water, and vanish again for half a lifetime.

Around persistent pools, life becomes less hurried.

Amber microbial mats spread beneath the water in slow concentric stains. They draw energy from heat, dissolved metals, and the faint electrical gradients leaking through buried conduits. Their upper layers are golden and oxygen-bearing; underneath, they darken through violet to black. Bubbles form in them and remain trapped, often pointing in several different directions according to the local fields.

The mats remember disturbances. A foot pressed into one may leave a dark print that remains after the shape itself has healed. Branching marks appear where sailer droppings fell, where a pipe discharged copper-rich water, or where some long-ago machine opened beneath the basin. In the oldest pools, these records make the bottom resemble a map of streets that do not correspond to any district now visible.

Small condensation insects graze there as larvae. As adults they become little more than wings: pale, soft-bodied things whose organs are distributed through their veins. They hatch during the rain and climb the falling droplets, taking advantage of opposing gravity routes. In certain light, thousands can be seen ascending through the shower while the water descends around them.

Most live for less than one roaring cycle.

When the engines begin, the insects seek cracks in the masonry. Those caught in open air are shaken apart or driven into the central hollow, where they become food for the sailers. Their powder-fine bodies also collect in resonant seams, and from those seams the black mosses grow.

The moss fans are the Ward’s quietest animals, if they are animals at all.

From above they resemble patches of soot arranged in overlapping semicircles. During the internal rain they unfold joint by joint. Their silver undersides catch the infrastructure light and send it deeper into the fan, where dark filaments manufacture sugars. Between the filaments live colonies of mobile cells that move like blood but can leave one fan and enter another through a film of water.

A moss fan can be divided into many viable pieces. Separate fans can also merge. What looks like a hundred individual growths along a condensation seam may be one dispersed organism, while a single large fan may contain several incompatible colonies maintaining a tense border down its center.

They close before the roar reaches full strength. This is not merely protection. The folded fans become resonators. They take in the vibration of the Ward through their attachment points and store a minute electrical charge in mineral plates beneath their skin. After the engines fall silent, the charge powers slow growth.

Old fans have heard centuries of machinery. Mineral deposits preserve their former edges in pale rings, so that the wall behind one resembles the cross-section of a tree. Those rings do not mark years but noise: broad layers for long resonances, thin dark lines for quiet periods, fractured bands for changes in pitch. In this way the mosses contain a history of the Ward that no inhabitant has learned to read completely.

The pale shaft-vines are less passive.

They begin as translucent threads rooted in warm, wet cracks. A thread grows across bare stone until it encounters the chemical trace of corrosion. Then it follows that trace toward an electrical junction, moving so slowly that its movement is visible only in the accumulation of shed skins behind it.

Upon reaching current, the vine builds a cage.

Several stems arch around the junction without touching its hottest parts. Fine root hairs enter screw holes and seams in the insulation. The plant feeds on heat differences, trace metals, and the weak magnetic field. Pulses travel through its stems at intervals ranging from minutes to months.

A lone vine pulses irregularly. A grove becomes synchronized.

The cages then begin to resemble simple nervous systems. They respond to opened doors, active lamps, changing gravity, and approaching bodies. A traveler may pass beneath a vine grove and see one pulse follow them from stem to stem. Minutes later, another grove several districts away may answer.

Whether this is communication is uncertain. The vines may merely be reacting to fluctuations in the same buried circuit. Yet service apertures enclosed by mature groves tend to remain functional. Conduits beneath them suffer less corrosion. Once, when a municipal line burned out, vines from three shafts entered the damaged housing and bridged the break with iron-rich tissue. The repair lasted nine cycles before the machinery regrew a proper conductor around them.

For this reason, those who cross the Ward do not cut the vines.

The sailers are the most visible inhabitants and the least often seen.

At rest, a sailer could be mistaken for a bundle of black cloth wedged in a niche. Its six limbs fold around a narrow central body; the steering membranes collapse into pleats; a ring of gripping hooks closes over its soft underside. In this state it may sleep through several gravity changes, waking only when the stone beneath it begins to vibrate in a particular way.

Unfolded, a mature sailer can span three or four meters. Its skin is not truly black but a deep, oily blue patterned with heat-sensing pits. It has no eyes. Along the leading edge of each membrane are rows of fine hairs that detect pressure, acoustic vibration, airborne particles, and distortions in active gravity routes.

A sailer does not fly. It chooses how to fall.

It launches into a known gradient, spreads its limbs, and allows the city to take it. Small alterations in the membranes move it from one route to another. It may descend sideways along a wall, curve outward over the Sink, then enter an upward freight field and rise toward the opposite district. Some circuits require days. During them the sailer sleeps in brief intervals, half its limbs steering while the others slacken.

Its mouth is a long slit beneath the body. Condensation insects and spores are combed from the air and passed inward by cilia. Larger sailers also graze suspended dust sheets, leaving clean arcs through them.

They are solitary in stillness but social in motion. Two entering the same route will match their membranes and circle one another without contact. A third may join. Their overlapping bodies alternately conceal and reveal the distant lights, making a turning pattern in the dark. The formation can persist for hours before the route divides them.

Sailers mate only during the deepest resonance. They anchor on opposite sides of a vibrating structure and release clouds of motile cells into cracks carrying the correct pitch. The resulting young grow inside the wall, feeding on mineral biofilms until they are broad enough to catch a gravity current. Their first emergence is dangerous. The floors beneath known nesting sites are littered with dried juveniles that opened their membranes too early or chose routes that had shifted since the previous generation.

Old sailers learn the Ward’s changes. Scars on their membranes show where obsolete gradients carried them into masonry. The oldest have lost limbs and compensate by attaching strips of moss, mineral film, or shed membrane to the damaged edge. One immense five-limbed animal has been glimpsed for more than a century. It follows a route so long that it appears only every seven years, crossing the central hollow from the sealed gate toward a balcony that no longer exists. At the end of that passage it falls into darkness and is not seen again until the route returns it.

The Ward also contains inhabitants that are harder to classify because they are not always alive at the same time as themselves.

These are the recurrent travelers.

They are few—perhaps three, perhaps twenty, perhaps only one seen at different stages of a route. They leave no camps and seldom remain anywhere for an entire cycle. Their evidence consists of touched surfaces, repaired markers, folded lengths of cable, and narrow ceramic vessels placed where condensation will fill them.

A recurrent traveler dresses for conflicting directions of weight. Straps cross the body from shoulder to thigh. Tools are fixed at both ends. The hood closes around the face not as protection from air but against sudden acceleration and the cutting grit of dust routes. Soles, knees, elbows, and palms carry gripping pads. Small metal vanes worn along the arms tremble before a gravity boundary changes.

They travel slowly even when moving fast.

At a ledge, one may wait hours before releasing a pinch of powder. The grains fall in several directions. From this, the traveler chooses a path, clips a line to the rail, and steps into the hollow. There may be no visible destination. The city catches them. They turn, diminish, and disappear among the distant lights.

Their journeys are seldom direct. A route from one gate to the other may include days spent orbiting the same tower, a sideways fall through an avenue, and a period of near-motionlessness in which each step takes the traveler only a few centimeters against stored momentum. Some routes close behind them for years.

This is one reason the Ward seems empty. Its people may be present but delayed.

A traveler who entered an old freight gradient a generation ago might still be describing its immense circuit through the chamber. They may cross an inhabited ledge for seconds, touch a handrail to turn themselves, and be gone before anyone can speak. The clean place on the rail remains. The person does not return for another eleven years.

Some recurrent travelers carry route-lamps. They wake the dim lamps of vertical avenues in sequence, not to illuminate where they are going, but to tell the Ward where they wish to be sent. The municipal system reads the procession as a request. Sometimes it complies. Sometimes the lamps continue after the traveler has vanished, tracing an alternative journey with no body in it.

No one knows whether the travelers maintain the Hollow Ward or merely negotiate with it. They clear certain drains, tend vine cages, and place warning marks near unstable boundaries. Yet they also open sealed rooms, remove components, and leave offerings of conductive salts at resonance pylons. Their signs combine technical notation with gestures of courtesy.

A circle crossed by six short lines means that a route is awake.

A hooked spiral means that momentum will return.

An open hand means do not shout.

They do not build permanent homes. The closest things to dwellings are waiting niches cut into route junctions. Each contains anchor loops, a dry shelf, a catchment bowl, and often a packet of dormant food wrapped in mineral cloth. Objects left in these niches are treated as communal, except for bells.

Every traveler carries at least one bell, though the bells are rarely rung.

They are made in mismatched shapes and produce tones too low or too high for easy hearing. Before entering an uncertain route, a traveler may strike one and watch what the sound does. If it departs normally, the route is safe enough. If it returns at once, the path is closed. If it fails to return, the traveler waits.

The most dangerous answer is a note arriving before the bell is struck.

There are other presences in the Ward that may be errors, natural effects, or inhabitants adapted beyond recognition.

During prolonged silence, movement is sometimes seen on the opposite wall: a procession of small lights traveling along avenues believed to be sealed. Magnification shows no bodies carrying them. Doors open ahead of the lights and close behind. Vine groves pulse as they pass.

In the Sink, geometric ripples occasionally avoid a particular patch of water, as though breaking around an invisible standing figure.

Delayed echoes return altered. A shouted name may come back days later in several voices, or a footstep may return with a second step following it. Most travelers attribute this to old acoustic routing. Nevertheless, they leave food in certain empty stations and greet some echoes before continuing.

They call these phenomena the Late.

The Late are not ghosts in the ordinary sense. They may be pieces of movement that the Ward has not yet delivered: a reflection separated from its body, a sound held in a resonance circuit, the pressure wake of a traveler whose passage is still working its way through the architecture. Over centuries, such remnants might accumulate and coincide. A gesture, a footfall, a warmth against a rail, and the opening of a door could all return together, briefly assembling the impression of a person.

Whether anything experiences that assembly from within is unknown.

The Ward itself does not distinguish sharply between a living body and the motion of one. Both are mass entering a route. Both create heat, pressure, noise, and demand. The old systems respond with the same grave attention. Lamps kindle. Fields bend. Doors unlock into darkness. Somewhere, engines alter their note.

For this reason, the greatest inhabitant of the Hollow Ward may be neither animal nor traveler, but the municipal process continuing through them all.

Its metabolism is erosion and redeposition. Its circulation is routed gravity. The roar is not a voice, though it behaves like breathing. Its slow repairs are not intention, though they preserve it. It draws rust from one district and grows red glass in another. It carries dissolved walls upward and makes terraces beneath roofs. It accepts moss into its power lines, crustacean eggs into its channels, sailers into its freight paths, and travelers into circuits older than their languages.

Everything in the Hollow Ward survives by becoming, in some measure, part of that process.

The crustaceans entrust their future to its rain.  
The mosses feed on its thunder.  
The vines enter its nerves.  
The sailers surrender themselves to its idea of falling.  
The travelers ask permission to move.

And the Late—if they live at all—are what remains when the Ward has kept the permission but forgotten the traveler.
