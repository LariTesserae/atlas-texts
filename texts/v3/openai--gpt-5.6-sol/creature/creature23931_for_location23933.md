At first, the Cinder Conservatory appears uninhabited because almost everything alive there has mistaken stillness for safety.

The broad silver leaves are the easiest to recognize as living things. They fold against heat, reopen with the reversal of the chamber’s breath, and slowly turn their dull violet faces toward favorable vapors. Yet even these movements are not quick enough to seem animate unless watched across an afternoon. A visitor might shelter beneath one for hours before realizing that its canopy has lowered around them by the width of a hand.

The older fan-trees are less like trees than small climates. Their roots penetrate sulfur seams and carbon-rich stone, drawing separate gases into chambers beneath the paving. Inside their hollow trunks, these gases pass through porous baffles lined with catalytic tissue. The amber fluid that circulates through them is neither blood nor sap. It is a warm, tarry solvent carrying dissolved sulfur, simple carbon compounds, mineral grains, and chains of reactive oils. It moves not by a heartbeat but by daily pressure: descending when the plain heats, rising when the city exhales.

A fan-tree may possess only a few dozen leaves, but each can roof a courtyard. Its trunk thickens by adding fired layers from within. The oldest individuals are therefore hollowed by age, their living tissue confined to a thin inner lining while the dead exterior serves as column, chimney, and dwelling wall. Streets have been laid through some of them. Ovens nest in their abandoned chambers. Their roots lift foundations without breaking them, opening neat slots through which new stems rise.

Around these giants live the red filament colonies.

What looks like moss is a society of tensile threads. Each filament is nearly mindless by itself. It grows toward warmth, contracts in alkali vapor, loosens in sulfurous air, and seals any wound with a bead of black resin. In company, however, the threads behave with disquieting competence. They lace loose bricks together. They suspend falling roof panels. They weave across a crack before it widens. When fire passes through them, their combustible tissues burn away and leave a delicate silica scaffold. The next generation climbs that scaffold rather than beginning again from the ground.

Thus a red curtain remembers its former shape.

Some curtains are older than the walls they support. Their memories are visible as nested skeletons: pale meshes inside red meshes, each recording an earlier position of a building. When a tower leans, the living threads do not restore it upright. They redistribute themselves until the new angle becomes stable. The Conservatory is full of such negotiated deformations.

Beneath the stones are the resin bulbs, slow and pale, with no surface organs except blunt breathing pores. They can remain dormant for decades. Pressure wakes them. A widening fissure pinches one side of the bulb; the tissue responds by digesting mineral dust and swelling into the gap. Its outer layers harden into a sulfurous ceramic while the interior withdraws and grows elsewhere. In this way cracks are plugged by bodies that turn their own dead skin into mortar.

The bulbs also make the nearest thing the Conservatory has to offspring with mobility. When a bulb has accumulated enough reactive oil, it forms hard nodules in its outer wall. These lie dormant until struck by a pressure wave. Then they rupture, throwing thimble-sized husks into gutters and ventilation shafts. A husk may roll for years, carried back and forth by the chamber’s alternating winds, before lodging in a useful fracture. Most become pavement grit. A few wake and begin to feed.

The cup-flowers are more dramatic. They are not ornaments and perhaps are not flowers in the strict sense. Each is a mineral reaction vessel grown by several species together. A fan-tree supplies the cup and its channels. Red threads adjust its mouth to the wind. Soot lichen seeds the central crystal. When the correct mixture of gases arrives, the crystal glows and the tissues around it manufacture spores, resin, or heat. Different cups respond to different mixtures, so waves of color pass through the gardens as the composition of the chamber changes: green-white along the ammonia walls, blue near sulfur vents, ember-red in the carbon groves.

Their pollen is not carried gently. Nothing here visits a blossom.

Instead, reproductive pods tighten as they warm. Their walls accumulate strain over days or months, then fail all at once. The spores shoot outward as glassy needles, often hot enough to score soft salt brick. They are built to survive impact. Many species require it: the shock cracks an outer casing and mixes chemicals that had been stored apart. A spore that merely settles remains inert. A spore that strikes stone hard enough begins to live.

For this reason, the chamber’s vast empty volume is not wasted space. It is reproductive distance. The long bridges, isolated platforms, and apparently purposeless façades interrupt trajectories, gather spores, and cast them into new currents. A blank wall can be a nursery. A ladder ending in darkness may support a single cup whose seeds need hours of free fall before they are ready to germinate.

The city’s architecture makes more sense when seen from the point of view of things that travel as dust.

Whether these organisms are separate kinds is difficult to determine. Their tissues pass into one another too readily. The red filaments enter the roots of fan-trees and emerge from their leaf joints. Bulbs exchange oils with soot lichen through mineral pores. Cup-flowers combine organs from three or four growths. Even dead structures remain chemically active and may be taken up generations later.

They are not one creature. Nor are they quite a forest. They resemble a long argument among compatible bodies, conducted through pressure, heat, and the exchange of catalysts.

They perceive the world in those terms.

A silver leaf senses not an object but a shadow of reduced radiation. A root knows another root as a change in the bitterness of the stone. The red threads register tension across whole streets. Soot colonies in the dome detect the arrival of deep gases long before those gases reach the floor. No part sees the Conservatory entire, yet information travels through it. A tightened cord alters a vane; the vane redirects a current; the current cools a grove; the grove unfolds its leaves and changes the pressure again.

This communication is slow until the great release from the central pit.

Then the whole city responds in a single instant.

Every mature organism has some tissue tuned to that pressure pulse. Leaves close. Pods brace. Resin valves open. Filaments contract. Dormant bulbs admit a measured breath of hot gas. The apparent simultaneity may be only a shared reflex—but it is the closest the Conservatory comes to waking.

The sealed buildings around the pit complicate matters.

They contain no residents now. Behind their narrow doors are shallow chambers lined with grooves, molds, scrapers, wedges, and bowls blackened by reactive oils. Some tools fit no hand that can be imagined from the surviving life. Others resemble hardened plant parts too closely to be confidently called tools. A grooved stone might have drawn fiber into cord. It might also have served as a trellis around which a vanished tendril contracted. The clay molds resemble the cavities left inside old bulbs. The glass scrapers bear polished patches like those made by repeated gripping, but no finger bones have ever been found.

Perhaps mobile gardeners once lived here and died when some atmospheric component vanished. Perhaps they cultivated the Conservatory, choosing which roots should meet and which vapors should be diverted.

Another possibility is that the gardeners were not a separate people.

At rare intervals in the chamber’s ancient past, conditions may have allowed the gardens to produce temporary, mobile bodies: dry-jointed things assembled from fiber, resin, and ceramic plates, animated by pressure bladders rather than muscle. Such bodies could have cut, tied, wedged, and fired. They might have lived only for a season, tending the rooted forms from which they had budded before stiffening into tools, walls, or fuel. The little houses would then be neither homes nor tombs, but places where the Conservatory grew hands.

Nothing proves this. Yet some façades have sockets at their bases like empty joints. Some ladders are rooted. In the oldest workshops, chipped scrapers are fused to black cellulose strands that enter the floor.

Now there are no walkers. The city tends itself by slower means.

Its inhabitants brace walls because pressure favors growth along strain. They clear channels because fire follows resin. They sort themselves into districts because each lineage poisons or feeds the next. What looks like foresight may be the accumulated survival of forms that happened to preserve the chamber. What looks like instinct may be architecture repeating through biology.

Still, the Conservatory is not passive. It does not merely endure its environment. It tunes it.

The vents sing at frequencies that shake mature spores loose but leave young pods intact. Screens sift mineral dust by grain size. Leaf canopies direct useful gases into lower streets. Empty courtyards allow dangerous mixtures to disperse. The central pit’s release resets pressures throughout the roots and walls. Over centuries, every organism that lived more successfully because of these arrangements helped maintain them. Garden and city became indistinguishable.

So what lives in the Cinder Conservatory is not hidden in its houses.

It is the shade cast by a wall, if that shade cools a violet leaf. It is the tension in a cord that tells a red colony the tower has shifted. It is the black lining of a hollow pillar, the bulb beneath a broken step, the glowing crystal in a cup-flower, the dormant needles falling through the central dark. It is a population distributed through structures, and structures behaving like inherited organs.

For most of the day, these lives seem solitary and sparse: one great fan-tree, one curtain of filaments, one sealed patch of lichen high in the dome.

Then the chamber breathes.

Leaves turn together. Curtains tighten. Cups kindle along the streets. The bridges hum with stored strain, and something too diffuse to possess a voice adjusts itself around the emptiness.

The Conservatory has no citizens.

It has, instead, a civic life.
