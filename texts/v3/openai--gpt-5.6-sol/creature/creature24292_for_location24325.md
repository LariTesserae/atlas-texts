At first the inhabitants seem to be lights.

They kindle wherever the mountain is wounded or wet: emerald beads moving against the bark, amber organs swelling and dimming beneath translucent wings, white sparks that scatter when a branch shudders. Some are fungi. Some are insects carrying fungal gardens in pits along their bodies. Some are eyes, though no traveler can safely assume which.

The smallest lives inhabit the bark’s layered country. Needle-jawed mites tunnel between scales, drinking the turquoise sap before it hardens. Six-legged combers follow behind them and harvest the crust. There are transparent worms inside the combers, and smaller black things attached to the worms. A hand laid on apparently bare wood may cover a population greater than that of any settlement.

These creatures live quickly. A fresh split in the mountain is discovered within minutes. Warmth gathers; eggs open; feeding colonies spread in colored bands. They graze until the wound seals or drowns them in resin. Their descendants depart as dust, clinging to wings, fur, tools, and the hair of travelers.

Where moisture persists, life becomes soft-bodied and patient. The eyeless pool-swimmers are silver-gray, with mouths arranged like the petals of a flower. They filter pollen until the water begins to retreat. Then the petals close into a cutting beak. Fins become legs. The swimmers climb in files from the shrinking pool, each touching the one ahead with a filament extended from its brow.

They can smell water through several walls of living tissue. This does not mean they always reach it.

Lichen-mimics gather along their routes. A resting mimic is broad, thin, and almost without depth, its blood moving in branching green lines that resemble leaf veins. When a swimmer steps onto it, the entire animal folds upward. Later it spreads itself again, a little larger and a slightly different color. Old mimics may cover the side of a dwelling without being noticed. The height people test suspicious patches with hot needles; a true lichen curls away from heat, while a mimic curls toward it.

Above the pools are the bark herds.

These are long animals, some no thicker than rope and some large enough to carry a shelter on the back. Rings of feet encircle the body at regular intervals. Half grip while half advance, so the animal flows over vertical surfaces without appearing to step. Their blunt heads scrape nutritious films from the bark. Their rear segments lay down glue for the young to follow.

They communicate by knocking their armored throats against wood. A herd spread across miles of trunk can pass a warning faster than a runner. Different dangers produce different rhythms: three close knocks for a gliding hunter, a rolling beat for splitting bark, an irregular tattoo for fire. Settlers sometimes imitate these signals, and old herds learn to answer. Whether this is understanding or merely alarm is disputed.

The pale gliders rarely touch the mountain except to breed. They have narrow bodies and many-jointed ribs supporting dark membranes. In flight they resemble torn leaves. They drift for days through the open chambers, feeding on spores and small aerial animals. Their mouths open upward, toward whatever settles from the canopy.

A glider finds its way by feeling pressure through the ribs. It knows the shape of an unseen trunk from the bend it gives the air and detects an opening flower by the minute warmth of its breath. Storms confuse them. After violent inward rain, thousands may cling to the same cable, folded wing over folded wing, until the branch looks clothed in pale scales.

Their eggs are carried by neither parent. Each egg bears a hook and a bladder of luminous oil. Released into the dark, it shines and drifts until it catches in hairlike foliage. Many of the cold white flashes seen in the abyss are not flying animals at all, but unborn ones searching blindly for somewhere to fasten.

The narrow furred hunters follow the gliders and the migrating swimmers. Their bodies are all spine and tendon, with four gripping forelimbs and a fan of stiff tail-hairs that can seize the bark like a hand. Their eyes are small. The hollows inside their skulls are large.

They hunt by listening through their feet.

A hunter can distinguish a footstep from sap movement, and a healthy animal from a lame one, through the vibrations of a branch. Packs spread over several adjoining surfaces, sometimes with open air between them. They coordinate by scratching signals too faint for most other creatures to notice. Only after a kill do they make audible sound. Then each animal lifts its muzzle from the carcass and sings into the wood, producing a shrill descending phrase. Other packs answer from remote ridges until the mountain seems full of mourning.

The height people consider the song neither celebration nor grief. They say the hunters are measuring how much country remains between one hunger and the next.

The greatest grazers have no agreed name. Every community knows a different part of them.

Those living near the high crowns speak of warm, blunt heads nosing among the leaves. Pool settlements tell of legs descending through the dark like slowly swinging towers. Travelers have found sheets of shed skin stretched across root forks, each pore large enough to shelter in. One old account describes an eye opening beyond the edge of a suspended forest and reflecting the whole forest in its pupil.

They appear to have broad gas-filled bodies and many trailing limbs. Colonies of luminous parasites map their flanks in shifting constellations. Smaller animals nest in the folds around their breathing vents, feeding on spores caught there. When the grazer exhales, these commensals erupt in clouds.

Some dwellers believe the giants never land. Others insist that far below, where falling branches vanish, they walk upon a buried floor.

The people themselves are small, though not exceptionally so among the bark fauna. An adult can stand beneath the curled rim of a common leaf. Their limbs are long, the fingers and toes divided almost to the wrist and ankle so they can wrap around cables. Dense gripping ridges cross their palms. Children are born with a strong clenching reflex and are carried for their first years in netted slings; an infant startled in sleep may seize the mesh hard enough that each finger must be pried loose separately.

They have broad chests and slow hearts. Their ears are protected by movable folds, but the bones behind the jaw are sensitive to vibration. When listening to distant activity, they press the side of the face to the bark. Conversation often stops this way: a whole gathering turns, kneels, and listens to something passing deep inside the mountain.

Their eyes are suited to blue dimness and bioluminescent points. Bright flame hurts them. Color, as they describe it, includes distinctions other peoples might call brightness, pulse, temperature, or freshness. They have several names for turquoise sap according to how long it has been exposed, and no single word for black.

They do not regard themselves as masters of the heights. Mastery is a stone-country idea, encountered only in fragments of old stories. They are tenants of surfaces that grow, routes that close, and chambers that may decide—without intention or malice—to kill everyone inside them.

Their smallest social unit is not the family but the line: those who share one set of anchors.

A line maintains bridges, ladders, sleeping slings, water cords, and the resin marks along a particular route. Membership can be inherited, married into, purchased by labor, or granted after rescue. To neglect an anchor endangers everyone. Deliberate damage to one is the nearest thing they have to an unforgivable crime.

Settlements seldom number more than a few dozen people. Larger communities produce too much heat, odor, waste, and vibration. They attract parasites; they exhaust nearby films; they fail together when growth closes a path. When a village becomes prosperous, it divides. Half the inhabitants carry embers, eggs, cordage, dried swimmer-flesh, and cuttings of useful fungi to another junction. Separation is celebrated with knocking songs that may continue between the two settlements for generations.

Almost everything they own was once alive.

Clothing is woven from shed glider membrane, inner bark, fur-hunter hair, and the silk of blossom insects. Water vessels are varnished seed husks. Helmets are nut shells lined with felt. Armor consists of overlapping bark scales that must be boiled free of the mountain’s scent or grazing animals will try to eat the wearer.

Metal is too rare for common use. A soft-metal hook may belong to an entire settlement and have a personal name. Stone blades are more precious still. Black splinters found in roots are polished, hafted, inherited, lost, recovered, and sung about. Since stone does not swell, heal, sprout, soften, or close, it is associated with honesty and death.

Firekeepers form a distinct profession. They tend clay-lined ember pods, trim dangerous sparks, and inspect every resin seal. Their eyebrows and head hair are shaved. A firekeeper who smells smoke where none should be will cut loose a house, bridge, or storage chamber without waiting for permission. The falling structure may contain a family’s wealth. No one argues until the danger has passed.

More common than flame are heat-beetles: fist-sized, placid insects with coppery shells and hot abdomens. They are fed sweet pulp and kept in ventilated baskets. Children sleep around them during freezes. Cooks arrange them beneath stone slivers or metal cups. Breeding lines are prized for steadiness; an overheated beetle can become frantic, burn through its basket, and disappear into the wall.

Knowledge belongs to listeners.

These specialists memorize the tones of surfaces under tension. They can hear rain entering a vessel far above, detect the gnawing of a hidden parasite colony, and judge whether a bud will open inward or outward. They make maps, but not primarily of shape. Their maps are arrangements of strings, knots, hollow beads, and dried membranes. Touched in sequence, they record the pitch of safe paths, the rhythm of water, the seasonal pulse of flowering fronts.

A drawn map would become false as the mountain grew. A song can be changed.

Their dead are seldom buried. There is nowhere that remains earth, and enclosed corpses invite rot into living tissue. Some are wrapped and lowered into the void. Some are placed in drying chambers to be consumed by pale, clean beetles. The bones are then divided among the line: long bones for tools, finger bones for bridge charms, the small bones of the ear for listeners in training.

The oldest or most honored may be given to a fresh wound. The body is pressed into exposed fiber and bound there until the mountain seals over it. Years later a swelling marks the place. Families visit these knots and listen. Internal fluids, root pressure, and the settling of bone produce faint clicks.

No one claims the dead are speaking.

No one willingly says they are not.

Religion, if it is religion, begins with the breath. Some believe each mountain-trunk is an animal whose thoughts are too slow to distinguish from seasons. Some say the whole range is one juvenile body, not yet aware of the settlements inside it. Others teach that there is no mind in the wood—that reverence is owed precisely because shelter need not love those it shelters.

During the great inhalation, all argument ceases.

People lash themselves at branch junctions. Bark herds flatten. Fur hunters hook their tails into cracks. Gliders fold wherever they can. Pool-swimmers dive into mud. Even the luminous life dims as if darkness itself were a form of gripping.

Then the air moves.

Afterward, survivors count not only their own. They note which herds answer, which pools remain, which nests still shine across the chamber. Long knocking messages travel through the ribs of the country: names, losses, warnings, requests for cord and medicine.

Sometimes the reply comes from a settlement known to them.

Sometimes it comes from far deeper in the range, in a rhythm no living listener recognizes.

And sometimes, after every inhabited place has fallen silent, something vast knocks once from within the mountain—too slow for an animal, too deliberate for splitting wood—and all the small lives on the bark wait together for the second blow.
