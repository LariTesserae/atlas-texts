What lives in the Thunder Cup cannot be counted.

At first there seem to be many kinds of life: the ring-making shell creatures, the white many-jointed animals inside the red fans, the transparent fish, the flexing skins, and something unseen that moves the stones. Given time, these distinctions become difficult to defend. They may be separate species. They may be stages of one life. They may be organs temporarily misplaced from a single creature too large—or too discontinuous—to appear all at once.

The smallest are born where the dark rain strikes.

They rise from fresh craters in groups of thirteen or fourteen, each enclosed in a shell shaped like a cupped hand. Beneath it is a soft body with no apparent front. Fine legs surround it in a fringe and move in sequences, so that the animal seems to revolve without turning. After an impact, the creatures arrange themselves around the crater’s lip with their shell openings directed inward.

They are not feeding. They are listening.

Each catches a different portion of the fading concussion. Their bodies shiver, and the sound passes around the ring from shell to shell, diminishing until it becomes a pulse too low to hear. Then, in perfect unison, they bury themselves. If the crater is broken open afterward, nothing lies beneath it but threads of ivory growth entering the rock.

Whether the shell creatures become those filaments is unknown. The filaments do, however, contain tiny chambers matching the shells in shape and size. When thunder forces their translucent flowers open, pale jointed limbs can sometimes be seen folded inside.

The animals sheltered by the red fans are larger, though perhaps only because distance behaves badly around them. They resemble white crustaceans assembled from the joints of several incompatible bodies. One has a narrow torso and twenty delicate knees but no feet. Another carries a round, featureless head between two pairs of hips. Their limbs terminate in transparent pads that hold the water without stirring it.

They never leave the fans while visible.

During darkness, however, something crosses the basin floor. It travels behind pressure rather than through water. Sediment rises in a line; black moss turns to follow it; thresholds tremble as it passes. By the time the green haze returns, one red fan may stand empty and another may contain twice as many pale bodies as before.

Sometimes there are footprints between them.

The prints are long and shallow, with the impression of five fingers at both ends.

These creatures appear to be the builders, if building is what occurs. No one sees them lift a stone. Instead they crouch near a collapsed wall and place their hands—or feet—against the floor. At the next deep boom, the stones answer. They roll, rise, and stack themselves around the crouching form. The builder remains inside until the last rock settles. When it emerges, a new arch stands where it had been, and one segment of the creature’s body has hardened into black basalt.

With each construction, the builder becomes simpler.

It loses a joint, a limb, an opening. The structure gains one.

This may explain why the cells feel inhabited even when empty. Their doorways are the width of pale shoulders. Their low walls curve like folded spines. Some arches contract faintly after loud reports. If touched, their stones contain a slow, irregular vibration resembling a heartbeat heard through many rooms.

The inhabitants do not build shelter from the Thunder Cup. They build additional bodies.

A doorway is a mouth through which they can enter another condition. A wall is a limb made too patient to move. A circle of stones may be an ear. The changes visible beyond each threshold are not views of other places but sensations belonging to the creature that made it: dryness remembered by one organ, cold arranged by another, the impossible overhead vision of an animal whose eyes were never attached to its flesh.

The oldest inhabitants may no longer move at all. They persist as architecture, wearing erosion that has not happened yet.

The long transparent fish is associated with them, though its nature is less clear. It circles whenever many structures have fallen. Seen nearby, its ribs resemble a row of narrow arches. Tiny shell creatures cling to their inner surfaces. Red tissue spreads between them in fans. Within its open mouth there is always a shore: waves withdrawing over pebbles, wind flattening grass, occasionally the far call of an animal breathing air.

Nothing in the basin is known to enter that mouth. Yet after the fish passes, one of the roofless cells is often gone.

Its inconsistent size may result from ordinary distortion. More likely, size is simply the part of it that lives elsewhere. The narrow creature glimpsed between stone fins may be only a finger. The immense shape eclipsing the ridge may be the whole animal briefly remembering itself.

When the cleft closes, the fish does not flee through stone. It passes into the instant before the collision. For several breaths it can be seen as a pale line trapped in the newborn basalt. Then the floor splits, and the line is gone. In its place lies a shed skin, still flexing, several meters long or several centuries old.

The skins are not empty.

If one is approached during total darkness, something inside it approaches in return. A pressure passes through the observer’s bones, testing joints, cavities, and old injuries. The skin moves without swimming and wraps itself around a nearby stone needle. For a moment it takes on the shape of whoever watches it—not their outward form, but the arrangement of spaces inside them. Lungs become clear bladders. The mouth becomes a tunnel full of surf. The skull opens like a low arch.

Then thunder shakes the resemblance apart.

It may be that the true inhabitant of the Thunder Cup is this capacity to pass from one vessel into another. The pale animals, fish, roots, shells, ruins, and rain may be temporary anatomies assumed by a life that has no preferred material. It occupies differences: the boundary of hot and cold, the surface of a droplet, the instant between noise and impact, the threshold where one basin becomes another.

It cannot live in anything settled.

When the mountain rests, even briefly, the inhabitants grow agitated. Shell rings form without rain. White bodies crowd the red fans until the branches glow. Walls dismantle themselves faster. The fish opens its mouth wider, and the sound of surf becomes a roar. The entire population seems to wait for the next rupture with something like hunger.

Thunder restores them.

At each concussion, forms exchange. A rain creature disappears and an ivory flower opens. A builder loses a hand and a doorway gains a side passage. A stone wall softens; a pale animal emerges from it carrying centuries of shell growth on its back. Nothing eats because nourishment here is not matter. Nothing dies because death would require remaining one thing long enough to end.

In the still pockets behind the ridges, one may occasionally see an inhabitant almost complete.

It crouches beneath a low arch beside a red fan. It is slender, white, and roughly the height of a person, though folded through too many directions. A translucent shell covers its head. Black moss grows along one arm. The other arm enters the stone at the wrist and emerges as an ivory root several paces away. Through its open ribs swims the distant fish.

It appears to be tending the garden.

At the passing of a deep tremor, it turns—not toward the sound, but toward the place the sound will make. Beneath the shell there is no face. There is a small dark droplet containing lightning, suspended in cold air.

For that moment, the creature seems solitary and ancient. It seems possible that all the arches were made for it, all the fans planted by it, all the skins shed from its changing body.

Then the mountain strikes.

The shell becomes rain. The limbs scatter into white animals. The spine settles into a wall already worn smooth. Its dark interior rises through the water toward an absent sky.

When the basin grows still again, nothing remains beneath the arch except a new threshold—and, just beyond it, the impression that someone has crossed to this side.
