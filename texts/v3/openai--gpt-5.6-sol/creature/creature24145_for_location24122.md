At first, the only inhabitants appear to be the black skins in the fractures.

They are thinner than lichen and less distinct than soot. A blade passed beneath one finds no underside, only a gradual deepening of color until the stain becomes indistinguishable from the mineral beneath it. They do not lie upon the rock so much as persuade its outermost crystals to darken.

Each skin begins along a boundary: where two salts touch, where glass cools against charcoal stone, where a warm plane meets a cooler rib. It spreads not toward light or heat but along difference. A uniform surface starves it. A divided surface invites it onward.

For long intervals, nothing about the skins suggests more than chemistry. They expand by fractions, contract during tremors, and leave behind delicate branching patterns. But when the sky becomes charged, their behavior changes.

Separate patches tighten at nearly the same instant. Lines appear between them—too faint to be called filaments, visible only as changes in sheen—and the scattered skins become parts of a larger figure. This figure may extend across one shelf or over an entire system of ridges. It does not resemble a body. It resembles a diagram drawn over the mountains: loops around warm fissures, forks at salt blooms, long dark strokes leading toward the roofless chambers.

These are the inhabitants.

They have no fixed substance. A single one may consist, for a time, of a hundred black films, several conductive glass seams, the charge held in a bank of dust, and one cold enclosure of leaning slabs. Hours later the same being may occupy different material. Parts of it are buried, raised, or sealed into a cliff, while new parts condense farther upslope.

Their boundaries are uncertain even to themselves. Two may share a salt column and respond as one during a tremor, then divide when the column branches. One may split across a closing basin and remain two thereafter. Several may join under a strong electrical storm, becoming briefly capable of an attention too broad for any of their separate forms.

They do not move through the land.

They move by changing which portion of the land is alive.

A dark skin contracts on one ridge and fails to reopen. Far away, another flattens beside a fresh seam. Between the two lies no trail, no transferred matter, only a sequence of pressures through the stone. The inhabitant has crossed the distance in the way a fracture crosses a plate: not by carrying itself forward, but by continuing.

Their slowest organs are the stone chambers.

The chambers are not houses. Nothing enters them. The inhabitants have no sheltered bodies to place inside, and the cold darkness would interrupt the reactions by which they persist. The empty interior is itself the useful part.

Within that narrow absence, heat gradients become still. Dust does not interfere. Minute changes in local charge can accumulate without being dispersed into the warm air. Crystals placed there turn toward active uplift because the chamber is listening to stress in the range. Its aligned stones form a crude directional sense, registering pressures too deep and gradual to be perceived along exposed surfaces.

A young inhabitant possesses no chamber. It exists only as a responsive film around a forming salt rib, contracting from shocks and opening again. As it grows, it begins to influence condensation. It cannot lift a slab or choose one as an animal might. Instead, over many cycles, it alters where salts form and where electrical stress gathers. A crack opens beside one stone rather than beneath it. Dust becomes glass along one edge. A rising shelf tilts another slab inward. What appears to be construction is a chain of exceedingly small preferences imposed upon an already convulsing landscape.

Eventually, several upright stones lean together around a strip of undisturbed ground.

The inhabitant has made its first hollow.

With this organ it can anticipate the terrain. Before, it reacted only to events already passing through it. Now it feels the pressure gathering under a peak that has not yet begun to rise. It withdraws from surfaces soon to be folded shut and extends toward places where fresh mineral will be exposed. Its black films may darken days in advance along the route of a future fissure.

This foresight is limited. The land remains stronger, faster, and more complicated than anything living upon it. Chambers are buried. Networks are divided. Whole beings are carried sideways inside migrating escarpments and sealed beyond contact with the sky.

Yet burial does not always kill them.

A chamber enclosed within a cliff can remain cold for ages. Its stones continue to hold slight inequalities of charge. When a later fracture intersects it, blackness emerges along the new crack—not the old skin restored, but the old pattern resumed. Such beings wake with portions of the mountains missing and other portions newly made. They do not remember places as images. They remember directions of pressure, sequences of mineral taste, and intervals between tremors. Their past is an arrangement of tendencies.

The oldest among them are therefore difficult to locate. Their visible skins may be young, while their buried hollows pass through the interiors of several ridges. Some have been folded so many times that their chambers form chains of rectangular cavities kilometers deep. During strong atmospheric charges, these hidden structures answer one another. A pulse enters at one cold void and emerges from another moments later, causing every black skin between them to contract.

For those few moments, an ancient inhabitant becomes coherent.

It perceives the range not as peaks and valleys but as a body of approaching changes. Rising stone is pressure. Salt is flavor. Glass is stored brightness. A stable shelf is silence. An opening fissure is both wound and road.

The green sheets in the upper air are its nearest equivalent to daylight. When they pass overhead, charge pours through every sharp edge. The inhabitants unfold to their greatest extent. Their skins become mirror-black. Salt fans blossom around them, guided into complex combs. Dust rises in spirals and hangs in branching veils.

They signal through these structures.

The signals are not carried as sounds. One being changes the angle at which a salt rib grows. Another, encountering the altered field, answers by clouding a seam of red glass. Across many shelves the exchange may take years, though during intense charge it accelerates until an entire mountainside flickers with slow, coordinated responses.

What they communicate is inseparable from what they are: a rising place felt from within. A coming thrust. A cooling seam. A chamber lost. A chamber opened. The approach of another network. The discovery of a new kind of mineral difference upon which life can spread.

They neither hunt nor flee. There is nothing here to eat them, and they consume no substance in the ordinary sense. Their metabolism is the relaxation of imbalance. Heat moves toward cold; charge passes between unlike crystals; vapor becomes solid. The inhabitants insert themselves into these transitions and retain a minute portion of each change as structure.

Their waste is order.

Behind an active skin, salts lie in regular bands. Glass cools into repeating internal planes. Charged grains settle according to size and conductivity. Over long periods, an inhabited fissure becomes more organized than an empty one. Its branching ribs echo earlier branches. Its crystals preserve the rhythm of tremors that passed while they formed.

Thus every inhabitant writes itself into stone, though it has no language and intends no record.

Reproduction occurs when a living pattern becomes too large to answer as one. A new ridge rises through it. Communication across the ridge slows. The skins on either side begin responding to different chambers, different pressures, different futures. Eventually a charge storm comes, and the two halves fail to tighten together.

Afterward there are two inhabitants.

For a long time they remain similar. They grow the same salt geometries and darken in the same rhythm. As the terrain between them changes, resemblance fades. One may become a narrow being stretched along a glass seam. Another may spread across a basin and develop many small chambers. A third may descend through fractures until nearly all of it lies buried, surfacing only as a single black ring around a hollow column.

There may also be one far greater inhabitant.

During the largest disturbances, when airborne minerals rotate across the whole sky and every ridge shines violet, all the smaller networks appear to align. Crystals in distant chambers turn toward different peaks, yet their movement begins and ends together. Buried cavities transmit pulses through the mountains. Black films from one end of the province to the other contract in a sequence too orderly to be accidental.

Perhaps this is only shared response to a common force.

Or perhaps the scattered beings are temporary organs of something whose complete body is the range itself.

If so, it is still being born.

The rising peaks are not its skeleton, because they do not support it. The chambers are not its mind, because no single chamber contains it. It lives in the growing correspondence between them: in the fact that one fracture can answer another, that a salt bloom can preserve a tremor, that a hollow can point toward a mountain before the mountain rises.

It has never known water, weathering, or decay. It cannot conceive of being worn down. Injury means only isolation; death means perfect uniformity, the end of every gradient on which its blackness feeds.

For now, uniformity never comes.

The interior breathes mineral vapor. The shelves tilt. New differences open everywhere. Each convulsion gives the inhabitants more surfaces, more boundaries, more cold hollows from which to sense the unfinished world.

In the immense quiet between upheavals, they lie spread thinly over the first edges.

They wait without sleep for the next thing to become possible.
