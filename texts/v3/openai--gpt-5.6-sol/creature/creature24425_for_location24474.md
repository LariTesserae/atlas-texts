What lives in the High Pelagic does not, at first, look alive.

The most obvious inhabitants are the anchored ribbons. They begin as a discoloration along a mineral edge: a green-brown seam no wider than tarnish, occupying the narrow interval where lucid water touches newly condensed stone. For a long time they remain almost indistinguishable from the chemistry that feeds them. Then the seam loosens into blades.

Each blade is translucent and only a few cells thick. There is no stem in the familiar sense, no root seeking soil. Its base infiltrates the stone’s pores with a web of adhesive cells, testing the young mineral for iron, nickel, phosphorus, and charge. Above this holdfast the blade divides repeatedly, forming a flat radial fan. The oldest colonies resemble soot-dark flowers pressed against black glass. Their bottle-green surfaces show violet where they store metals and smoke-yellow where they have been injured by excessive light.

They do not sway merely with the current. A colony can feel a pressure change moving through the ocean days before its arrival. The entire fan then alters its profile. Blades overlap, flatten, and turn their thin edges toward the coming shear. Microscopic chambers empty of water so that the plant becomes denser and grips its ledge more closely. When electrical disturbance approaches, conductive pigments retreat into protected layers. From a distance, the response passes across a shelf as a gradual dimming, though each colony may lie kilometers from the next.

Their lives are long only by accident.

Nothing eats them. No parasite burrows into their tissues; no animal tears away their blades. A ribbon can persist for centuries in an undisturbed eddy, adding one nearly transparent layer after another until its base becomes a laminated record of vanished currents. Yet most are destroyed young. The stone beneath them dissolves, or gravity tilts, or an entire pressure stratum slides sideways. A mature fan may be peeled from its anchor in one piece and carried into open water like a dark flag.

Such a plant does not die immediately. Along every torn edge are cells capable of becoming holdfasts. Stretched by contrary currents, the fan separates into threads. Each thread may travel for hundreds of kilometers, dormant and almost colorless, until it brushes a compatible mineral surface. Most never do. They thin, rupture, and become dissolved carbon in the lucid emptiness. But occasionally, long after the parent shelf has ceased to exist, a single fragment catches on a newborn needle of stone. Green gathers there again.

Their reproduction is similarly unhurried. They cast no dense clouds of spores. There would be little advantage in filling so much sterile water with short-lived offspring. Instead they release large, durable packets one at a time. Each is a many-layered grain containing stored minerals, folded photosynthetic membranes, and a minute internal crystal that responds to local gravity. The grain can change its density by moving ions through its shell. It rises, descends, or drifts sideways—not purposefully enough to be called swimming, but with enough discrimination to avoid the most barren currents.

Some remain in transit for generations.

Farther from stone live the veils. No single veil can be seen. It is a population so dispersed that neighboring cells may be separated by meters, sometimes hundreds of meters, yet the cells behave as parts of one loose body. Each is a folded disc of living membrane around a clear interior. In strong light it opens to many times its resting area, becoming nearly two-dimensional. In darkness or electrical danger it pleats itself into a compact spindle.

Their connection is the water between them.

An opening cell alters the charge of nearby molecules. That change travels outward, weak but persistent, and other cells answer. Over immense distances the population opens in successive regions, not all at once but in broad fronts, like a transparent flower whose petals are countries apart. When a storm writes conductive channels through the sea, the veils fold along the approaching pathways. Seen only through instruments—or through the way light changes around them—whole provinces of ocean appear to blink.

The veils migrate without going anywhere individually. Cells in unfavorable regions close, sink across one gravitational gradient, reopen in another, and divide. Cells elsewhere become inert and dissolve. Over decades the population shifts thousands of kilometers. It can pass through a stationary mineral range while almost none of its original members survive the crossing. What continues is not a body but an arrangement: a broad, faint attentiveness carried from cell to cell.

There are other growths less easily separated from the minerals they occupy.

In nickel foams, thin films inhabit the boundaries between crystal grains. They have no exposed surface. Light reaches them only after being refracted through stone, and they live on a dim metallic radiance. Their metabolism is extremely slow. A line of cells advances through a formation perhaps the width of a hand in a century, dissolving one crystal face and depositing another behind itself. The stone does not visibly decay. It changes orientation.

Around such colonies, fractures tend to branch differently. Stress is directed away from inhabited seams; pores open where useful currents pass; trace metals gather in layered nodes. This might be an adaptation of the film, using mineral growth as an external skeleton. It might instead be the ocean’s own corrective behavior occurring near living tissue. The distinction cannot be made cleanly. Remove the cells and the crystal continues some of their habits. Break the crystal and surviving cells reconstruct portions of its geometry elsewhere.

The oldest colonies are therefore not plants attached to rocks. They are green traces distributed through moving masses of rock and water, with no obvious boundary around the organism. A particular mineral shelf may be shattered, dispersed, precipitated again, and still retain the bias imposed by life: the angles at which it cracks, the isotopes it gathers, the frequencies at which it resonates. Long after the final cell is gone, the formation continues to behave as though expecting its return.

In the open reaches, where no visible vegetation interrupts the water, there are living processes thinner still.

Some pressure waves carry self-preserving chemical patterns. They pass through dissolved organics and induce them to assemble, briefly, into membranes. The membranes persist for seconds or hours, correcting irregularities in the wave that formed them. When the pressure train weakens, they come apart. Later, thousands of kilometers away, the same pattern may produce the same structures from entirely different matter.

It is difficult to say what the individual is. The transient membrane is not; it has no continuity. The wave is not, for its water is continually replaced. The pattern may be, though it can divide around obstacles, overlap with another version of itself, or vanish for years into the resonant structure of a mineral plain. Some patterns return altered. They propagate more accurately through turbulence than before. They avoid frequencies that storms erase. They induce more durable membranes.

Nothing hunts them. Their struggle is against corruption.

The planet’s loudest events tear patterns apart, while certain crystal ranges preserve them as standing vibrations. Thus a black ridge may contain, in its prolonged humming, more inherited variation than all the visible green life on its surface. When the ridge fractures, those vibrations enter the sea. Most decay into noise. A few encounter suitable chemistry and become active again.

Perhaps these are only self-maintaining reactions. Perhaps they are the High Pelagic’s dominant life.

If so, its creatures have no stable bodies. Their anatomy consists of temporary relationships among current, pressure, dissolved mineral, and light. They are born whenever those relationships close into a loop and cease whenever the loop opens. Their generations may last the duration of a lightning pathway or the lifetime of a floating mountain. They have no senses localized into organs. Instead, changes in the ocean alter them directly. To perceive pressure is to bend; to perceive light is to unfold; to perceive damage is to rebuild differently.

There is no animal awareness among them. Nothing looks outward from the stone. Nothing is frightened by the approaching roar. Yet the world contains memory.

A ribbon colony remembers storms as thickened bands in its base. A veil remembers them in the timing of its folding. A mineral film remembers in crystal orientation. A traveling pressure pattern remembers by changing the form it attempts to preserve. At greater scales, currents repeatedly favor arrangements that survived previous rearrangements. Mountain after mountain condenses with subtle improvements in flexibility, conductivity, or resonance, though no mountain plans the next.

The ocean itself may be the broadest inhabitant, but it is not a single mind asleep beneath the weather. It has no center from which to regard its extent. Its responsiveness is divided among countless temporary coherences. One region stores; another compares; a third dismantles what the first two have made. Signals cross but do not necessarily agree. A repair here becomes destruction elsewhere. The whole system is less like an intelligence than a medium in which intelligence is perpetually about to occur.

At rare intervals, enough of these processes align.

A storm’s conductive branches join distant mineral shelves. Pressure tones synchronize through the surrounding water. Veils fold in vast concentric sequences. Buried films release ions from crystal seams, and anchored ribbons alter the light passing across their surfaces. For several hours, perhaps days, events separated by hundreds of kilometers respond to one another with unusual precision. The region behaves as though it were one immense, distributed creature turning toward something.

There is no face to that turning and no object of attention. No message follows. The alignment disperses as the storm moves on. Ranges fall out of resonance. The veils open. Green blades lift from the rock.

What remains is a slight change in the water—a pattern held in currents, corrected as it travels.

Far away, a newborn escarpment begins to sing in answer.
