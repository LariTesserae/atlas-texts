At first, what lives there appears to be the forest itself.

The distinction is difficult to make. Nearly everything has roots for part of its life, and nearly everything drifts for another part. Creatures carry gardens on their backs; plants withdraw from touch, migrate by the width of a leaf, and remember alterations in the water for generations. A moving animal may anchor itself to a ridge and remain there until stone grows around it. A vine may release its hold after three centuries and cross a valley on slow, muscular folds.

The smallest inhabitants are the dustlike ones.

They occupy the mineral seams and the warm pores of basalt, where they lie packed together in shells no larger than grains of blue flour. Most are closed. Their shells are faceted and dark, each fitted so tightly to its neighbors that a colony resembles a patch of natural crystal. They wake only when the chemistry of the sea changes: iron from a new fracture, sulfur from a deep fissure, the faint electrical shiver of rock being made.

Then the shells hinge open.

From each extends a crown of transparent cilia, too fine to see except when they catch pollen. For several days the colony grazes on the mineral bloom. It stains the surrounding water with copper and violet, lays down another hair-thin layer of shell, and seals itself again. A single colony may wake fewer than twenty times in a thousand years. Its oldest individuals are buried beneath their descendants, still alive in the warm stone.

Larger grazers move among the ribbonwood. They are shaped like loose knots.

Their bodies consist of many pale loops gathered around a central stomach, though the loops can untwist and spread over several meters. When feeding, one drapes itself across a fan or red mat and presses hundreds of soft mouths against the surface. It takes only what the plant is ready to shed: wax, dead membrane, beads of excess salt. The loops darken as they fill. Afterward the grazer knots itself around a branch and becomes indistinguishable from a mineral-clotted root.

Some remain in one place so long that vines grow through them. They do not struggle free. They incorporate the vine into the knot, sharing warmth and dissolved sugars with it, and when they finally move, decades later, they carry a length of the forest away.

The glass-winged creatures of the upper water are younger forms.

They hatch from black pods in the lowest valleys, close to the wavering underside of the sea. For years they are dense, crawling things with hooked feet and mineral plates. They descend wherever gravity points, feeding on particles collected along the boundary. When they have grown heavy enough, they climb—not toward the light, but toward the oldest vine they can find.

There they shed their plates.

Gas gathers inside the new body. The creature loosens, rises, and unfolds into a cluster of broad transparent vanes. What looked like one crawler becomes five or six drifting shapes, linked by threads too fine to see. Thus the apparent groups in the high canopy are usually a single animal divided among several bodies.

The vanes do not beat. They alter their angle to the suspended currents and travel as leaves travel, turning only once in many hours. Pollen adheres to their wet surfaces. Tiny mouths along the leading edges open and close. When one vane passes through a sunbeam, branching organs flash within it: gold digestive filaments, a dark thread of memory tissue, silver sacs that regulate buoyancy.

If a vane is damaged, the others gather around it. They may remain clustered for months while its wound seals. If it dies, they do not abandon it. Its gas is drawn back through the connecting threads, and the dead wing folds gradually inward until the whole creature is one body smaller.

Near the dark ridges live the broad swimmers.

They resemble shadows cast by objects that are not there. Their upper surfaces are matte and almost black; their undersides are pale, ridged, and crowded with hanging sensory threads. Some are only the width of an outstretched arm. The oldest span entire chasms.

They do not possess eyes because light is less useful to them than pressure. Through the fringe beneath them they feel distant cracks, pores opening in leaves, spores settling on stone, the minute contraction of the sea after sunset. The whole suspended ocean presses its events into their bodies.

A broad swimmer may know that a mineral fin is dividing many valleys away. It may turn toward a volcanic seam months before the seam opens. Its route appears aimless only because the thing guiding it has not happened yet.

They feed rarely. Their mouths are narrow folds hidden between the ventral ridges, suited to soft growth rather than pursuit. One may hover above a rust-colored moss for a season, lowering itself by imperceptible degrees. When contact is finally made, the sensory threads enter the moss without tearing it. Nutrients pass upward. In return, the swimmer leaves behind spores gathered from some remote peak.

The forest depends on these crossings. Without them, valleys separated by cold water or contrary gravity would become isolated from one another.

The swimmers reproduce only at places where two kinds of stone meet. An adult settles across the boundary, black rock beneath one half and pale crystal beneath the other. Over many years its body thins. The center dissolves first, leaving a ring draped over the ridge. The ring then divides into several smaller shadows, each already fringed with threads and carrying partial impressions of the parent’s journeys.

For a time they remain together.

They pulse faintly through the rock, exchanging what each has inherited. Then one loosens and slides toward warm water. Another turns into a sideways valley. Another may settle immediately and never travel at all, becoming a dark, breathing shelf beneath the plants.

This is why no one can say how many broad swimmers there are. A creature may be one body, a divided body, or a colony that has forgotten it was once joined.

There are hunters, though hunger here seldom has the shape of pursuit.

The mouth-flowers are among the most obvious. Their petals imitate the rounded membranes of harmless foliage, but inside each blossom waits a chamber lined with inward-pointing glass hairs. A glass-wing brushing one may not notice. The flower does not close at once. It allows the animal to return with the current, touching the same petals over days or years. Each contact leaves a thread of adhesive.

Only when enough threads have accumulated does the blossom begin to fold.

The folding takes so long that the captive may continue feeding throughout it. By the time escape becomes impossible, mineral anesthesia has entered its tissues. Death, when it comes, is no more abrupt than sleep.

Less obvious are the colorless threads of the cool wells. They seem like fields of fine vegetation, all streaming in the same direction. In fact, each field is a single colonial animal spread through the water. It intercepts larvae, spores, and flakes of living membrane. No part of it is large enough to recognize as a mouth. Digestion takes place across kilometers.

When threatened by warmth or chemical change, the threads separate. Each coils into a clear bead and drifts until it finds cold water. Centuries later the beads unfold, recognize one another through taste, and make a field again.

Yet the strangest inhabitants are found inside the vaulted chambers of the cliffs.

They are not always present. A chamber may stand empty for an age, its walls furred with pale fans. Then a traveler looking through an arch may notice that some of the roots inside do not attach to anything.

They hang in loose bundles from the ceiling, or rise from the floor, or extend sideways where gravity has turned. Their outer skins resemble old ribbonwood: green-brown, fibrous, stippled with gas pores. Leaves grow along them. Small grazers feed on them. Mineral algae cover their lower portions in terraces.

Only their arrangement suggests another nature.

The bundles converge toward a mass concealed deep in the stone, and the leaves are not aimed chiefly at light. They face fissures, current boundaries, unstable shelves, distant volcanic seams. Each leaf is tuned to a different vibration. Some are thin enough to respond to the closing of pores across the valley. Others are stiff plates that move only when mountains alter.

These chamber-dwellers have no single name because they are not a single kind of being.

Part plant, part colony, part long-lived animal, they begin as larvae within the bodies of broad swimmers. When carried near a suitable hollow, a larva leaves its host and enters the porous mineral. It extends threads along cracks, tasting the shape of the mountain from within. If the place is stable, it grows slowly outward. If not, it waits.

Its first leaves may appear a century later.

Over the following millennia, the being fills the chamber without blocking it. It holds soft crystal apart as the cliff grows. It reinforces one wall, permits another to fracture, and opens narrow channels through which warm and cool waters can meet. The resulting arches and vaults look constructed because they are, though not according to any plan that can be separated from growth.

The chamber-dweller does not build a home. The chamber is its body’s consequence.

Nor does it cultivate the surrounding forest in any simple sense. It releases minerals where certain mosses thrive. Those mosses attract knot-grazers. The grazers carry spores. The spores become vines that brace a ridge. The braced ridge alters a current, and the altered current brings pollen to the chamber-dweller’s leaves.

Whether this chain is intention, habit, or metabolism is impossible to determine.

These beings are the closest the high sea has to witnesses.

Their nervous tissue lies as amber filaments inside the transparent vines stretching between ridges. Many vines that appear to belong to separate plants are extensions of neighboring chamber-dwellers. Where two such filaments meet, they may fuse. Signals pass between them very slowly: not impulses, but gradual changes of pressure, salt, luminosity, and growth.

A message can take years to cross a valley.

It may announce the opening of a fissure, the passage of an ancient swimmer, or the arrival of unfamiliar spores. The response may take another decade: leaves turning toward a new source of light, gas bladders emptying to alter the tension on a bridge, a mouth-flower being starved of minerals until it loosens its hold on a migration route.

Nothing marks the exchange as speech. There are no repeated calls, no symbols cut into stone. But information travels. Events are retained. A fracture on one ridge may change the growth of a chamber-dweller beyond the horizon generations later.

Their memory is not held in recollection but in anatomy.

A thickened filament records a period of warmth. A spiral in the ribbonwood records a vanished current. A sealed hollow in pale crystal preserves the chemical trace of a swimmer that passed before the surrounding summit had divided. To remember something is to continue growing around it.

This makes forgetting difficult.

Some chamber-dwellers bear ancient injuries they can no longer interpret. Their leaves repeatedly close at harmless shadows. Their vines curve around obstacles that eroded away ages ago. One may spend centuries reinforcing an empty span because stone once fell there. Another continually releases a particular mineral into the water, calling to a species that has long since vanished.

Such damaged elders are often surrounded by unusual gardens. Their obsolete responses create conditions found nowhere else: warm pockets inside cool wells, sideways rain of spores, groves that unfold at night and close at noon. Rare creatures survive in these errors.

When a chamber-dweller dies, the process may occupy an era.

Its outer leaves continue to function after its interior filaments have gone dark. Vines remain stretched between ridges. Local plants keep answering chemical signals that are no longer being made. Gradually the vaulted spaces fill with mineral. Amber channels become brittle. Whole sections of forest lose their shared timing, leaves closing at different hours until a new rhythm emerges.

The last living part is usually a single root sealed far inside the mountain, warmed by stone.

Sometimes, after centuries of stillness, that root encounters a fresh fracture and begins again. Whether the resulting being is the old inhabitant returned or a descendant growing through its remains has no clear answer.

Nothing in the high sea lives alone enough for identity to stay simple.

The swimmers carry forests. The forests shape chambers. The chamber-dwellers extend through vines and rock. Glass-wings divide one life among several bodies. Grazers braid themselves with plants. Predators become gardens around what they consume. Even the dormant shell-creatures build the mineral on which later generations settle.

At dusk, these relations become briefly visible.

As the upper light fades and brightness rises from the clouds below, the amber filaments within the vines begin to answer one another. One line brightens between two peaks. Then another, farther away. A dim pulse crosses a chasm and vanishes into a chamber. Minutes later, a fan on the opposite cliff folds. Gas notes rise from silver pores. A broad swimmer changes the angle of one winglike margin.

Across the immense submerged range, the responses continue too slowly to form a flash or chorus. They resemble thought only when seen from far enough away.

Then the cloud-light fails. The filaments darken. The glass-wings gather their divided bodies among the leaves. The great swimmers settle over warm seams and spread themselves thin. Mouth-flowers remain half open. Shell colonies close on their grains of altered stone.

Inside the mountains, the chamber-dwellers continue to feel the sea: its patient weight, its minute contraction, the drift of clouds beyond the lower boundary, and the almost inaudible labor of new rock entering the world.

They do not rule the high sea.

They are how the high sea notices itself.
