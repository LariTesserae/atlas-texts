At first, the inhabitants appear to be the crouching figures who pass between the three shelters.

They are seldom seen upright. Uprightness is for looking far, and little in the Rattlecourt stays where it was seen. They move with their weight low, one hand often trailing near the ground. In open dust they run in short, slanting bursts, pausing behind trunks no wider than an arm. When a gust catches them, they fold rather than resist: knees wide, chin down, six-fingered hands spread against the stone.

Fully extended, an adult would stand a little shorter than an ordinary human. The torso is narrow above and deep through the lower ribs, with a heavy basin of muscle around the hips. The arms are long. Each hand has two opposed thumbs and four slender fingers, all tipped with dark pads that can feel vibration through barkcloth and mineral crust. The feet are broad, four-toed, and roughened almost to the hardness of horn.

Their skin is not scaled, exactly. It lies in minute overlapping folds, fine as the grain of a file, charcoal-gray where exposed to the wind and amber-brown in protected hollows. A wax continually works its way to the surface. Dust adheres to it, so that an inhabitant who has spent the day outside takes on the color of the ground crossed: rust at the knees, ivory along one flank, soot-blue under the hands.

The wax is groomed off each evening with crescent scrapers and saved. Nothing produced by the body is discarded casually.

They have eyes, but sight is not their chief means of judging the world. The eyes are small, recessed beneath shelves of bone, and covered by two sets of lids: an inner smoky membrane for grit and an outer lid that seals during electrical flashes. What first resemble decorative scars along the jaw and collarbone are sensory pits. Thin mineral rods grow inside them. Air pressure, ground tremor, and nearby impact set these rods vibrating against nerves.

An inhabitant can hear a nail-mite procession through the pads of the hand. They can distinguish a kiteback landing from a spindle bulb striking its own branch. With one cheek against a shelter wall, they can follow a needle-cat moving beneath lichen twenty paces away.

This is why they crouch so often. They are listening with their whole bodies.

Their circulation is slow and amber-colored. It is richer and darker than the sap of the trees but behaves in the same unsettling way. A cut does not bleed. Beads gather along it, quivering, then spring toward charged cloth, fur, or stone. Companions treating a wound first ground themselves with basalt rods. Then they use combs of horn to draw the scattered beads back and press them beneath a waxed bandage. A careless healer may lose part of the injured person into the air.

They do not drink. They eat oils, resin, roasted flesh, salt pastes, and the soft inner layers of certain scale-lichens. Fan-tree cork is chewed during long journeys, not for nourishment but because it releases a vapor that slows the loss of bodily solvent. Their most valued food is made from kiteback fat worked with sulfurous powder into gray cakes. These keep for years and must be warmed between stones before they can be bitten.

Hunger makes them weak. Loss of salts makes them confused. But what they fear most is becoming thin: the gradual evaporation of the amber solvent within them. Someone suffering this sickness grows stiff at the joints and begins to shed fine golden dust from the mouth and sensory pits. Such a person is wrapped in resin cloth and carried into one of the warm alcoves below the northern wall. Sometimes they return. Sometimes only the cloth is brought back, hardened around a light, angular hollow.

They have no spoken name that can be separated from its performance. It begins with a heel struck once against stone, continues with two palate clicks, and ends by brushing all six fingers down the sternum. Depending on the surface and the strength of the wind, it may mean *the ones who listen downward*, *those who answer from above*, or simply *we who are here now*.

For convenience, they might be called the Answerers.

A count of them never remains reliable. Five may enter a shelter and seven emerge. A figure glimpsed crossing the basin may prove to be two children beneath one wind-cloak. An apparent child may unfold into a very old adult whose limbs have contracted and doubled at the joints. Some individuals remain in the upper branches for whole warm intervals, wrapped in vane-fiber among the bellows, and are included in no ground count. Others enter cracks behind hinged lichen and do not return until the arrangement of the grove has changed.

Usually there are between twelve and twenty Answerers in the Rattlecourt. At some seasons there are none.

The shelters do not belong to families. One is kept for those whose bodies are growing; one for those who hunt and work; one for those becoming brittle with age. An inhabitant may move through all three several times. Age is not imagined as a straight road. Heat, injury, feeding, and molt can make a person “younger” or “older” in practical ways.

Their young emerge from resinous brood cases set among geothermal stones. They are small enough to sit in an adult hand, though no adult holds one that way—the static of the young body is too strong. For their first several years they possess dense adhesive cilia on palms, feet, elbows, and belly. They can climb sheer basalt and cross the underside of an overhang with little effort.

The smallest handprints on the western ledge are theirs.

During a child’s first crossing of the overhang, it carries a pouch of pigment in its mouth. At each pause it prints one hand against the stone. The trail records not achievement but hesitation. A child who crosses without leaving enough prints is made to cross again, slowly. To the Answerers, fear properly attended is a form of knowledge.

The cilia are shed before adulthood. They gather in drifts around the sleeping hollows, and are spun into the finest thread the Answerers make. The tendon lines of the central instrument are repaired with it. So are masks, charge-moss jars, wound dressings, and the nets used to snare airborne seeds.

Their masks do more than exclude dust. Each has small cavities cut beside the mouth and jaw. When the wearer clicks their teeth, breathes, or taps a finger against the chin, the mask shapes the sound into a narrow direction. In this way the Answerers speak beneath the noise.

Their language consists of knocks, scrapes, brief expulsions of air, and vibrations passed through shared objects. A statement made through open air is considered incomplete because echoes may alter it. Important things are said with two people touching the same stone, tool, tree trunk, or length of tightened fiber. The vibration carried through matter is the true phrase. What the air repeats is merely its loose shadow.

They distrust voices without bodies.

This makes the echo-mimicking creatures of the cliffs especially hateful to them. These small things are rarely seen. They hide in fluted stone and repeat calls only after the original caller has moved on. The Answerers call them something like *mouths left behind*. Children are taught never to follow the sound of their own names unless they can also feel the rhythm through the ground.

No single Answerer commands the others. Authority belongs temporarily to whoever has heard something first. At the central stone ribs, one person may become leader for the duration of an updraft because they recognized its approach. Another takes precedence when the lichen hinges in an unfamiliar sequence. A child may halt an entire hunt by feeling a subterranean scrape through a hand that adults, their palms hardened, failed to notice.

Disputes are carried to the wind instrument. Each side tightens one tendon thread to represent its claim. The basin then plays both claims for as long as the disputants are willing to wait. Sometimes one thread breaks. Sometimes the tones resolve into a consonance. Sometimes a crown-wheel descends and tears the whole argument away.

All three outcomes are accepted, though not always graciously.

Their relationship with the other life of the basin is neither pastoral nor hostile. They harvest spindle resin but leave polished magnetite grains beneath each cut bush. They eat nail-mites by sweeping entire processions into hot stone trenches, yet divert every third procession around the trap. Bellows are tolerated in the fan-trees because their alarm notes give warning of kitebacks and falling mineral sheets. Kitebacks are hunted, but their jaw plates are mounted at shelter bends, where the wind makes them knock and discourages others from entering.

Needle-cats are killed whenever possible. Their circular mouth plates become grinders and geothermal trivets. Their warm pellets, however, are prized. The Answerers split them apart to recover powdered bone, shell, resin, and mineral salt. From this material they can learn what has recently moved beneath the lichen.

Crown-wheels are not hunted. The Answerers behave as though the wheels can remember offense. When one settles around a spindle-bush, they mark a circle in the dust and do not cross it. If the bush dies, its resin is left untouched until the wheel rises. If the wheel does not rise, the Answerers dismantle the nearest shelter and depart.

They do not agree on whether the crown-wheels are individual beings. Their language uses a grammatical form for them otherwise reserved for weather, swarms, and parts of the body that move involuntarily.

The walking figures made by whirlwinds receive the same uncertain courtesy.

When dust, husks, bone, and charged resin gather into a person-shape, the Answerers kneel along its path. They do not worship it. They do not flee. They knock their own names softly against the ground as it passes, giving the figure an opportunity to answer. It never has.

After it collapses, no one touches the resulting pile until the basin’s next period of stillness. Then the objects are separated.

Some belong to known dead: a copper hook, a child’s jaw plate, a mask buckle lost two lifetimes earlier. Others are older than the shelters. A few cannot have been made by the Answerers at all. There are translucent rings that contract when warmed, carved bones from animals with no counterparts in the basin, and flat pieces of black material covered in rows of holes too regular to be ornament.

These are hung from the central ribs. In time, all of them become part of the instrument.

The Answerers believe the Rattlecourt is inhabited in more ways than can be counted. A tree may be one life, or a thousand vanes using the trunk temporarily. A lichen field may be a surface organism, or the exposed sense organ of something under the rock. A crown-wheel may be a creature, a seed, a detached organ, or a season that has taken shape. Their own dead may persist as wax, tools, sound patterns, child-thread, and airborne dust without being wholly absent.

Death, to them, is not silence. Silence is something stranger.

When an Answerer dies, the body is seated against a fan-tree during a gale. The wind dries and articulates it. The wax is drawn off. Useful plates of bone are removed over several days, never all at once. The remaining husk is fitted with vane strips and resonant shells until it becomes an instrument.

For a while it is addressed by name.

Later, when its sounds have changed beyond recognition, it is allowed to become something else.

Beneath all these lives is the possible inhabitant below.

The Answerers do not depict it. They have no agreed account of its shape. Some tapping-stories suggest a body spread beneath the entire mesa, with the Rattlecourt as a shallow organ in its back. Others describe a single buried plate that expands in the heat and strikes the roof of a cavern. One old sequence claims there is no creature below at all—that the knock comes from the future, traveling backward through stone because sound loses its way here just as echoes do.

Whatever the cause, the Answerers prepare for the stillness.

At the first cancellation of the winds, they stop wherever they are. A lifted foot remains lifted. Tools are pressed to the ground. Children flatten both hands against stone. No one speaks through air or matter.

The immense knock comes from below.

It passes through the cold glass seams first, then the warm plates. It enters trunks, bulbs, bones, shelters, animals, stone ribs, and the amber circulation of the Answerers. For an instant every separate body in the basin receives the same vibration.

Then the Rattlecourt answers.

The trees clap. Bulbs ring. Bellows discharge their impossible voices. Mites strike their shells against the ground. Crown-wheels turn once with a sound like a blade drawn around the rim of a glass. The central threads release every note they hold.

The Answerers contribute one blow each: fist, heel, tool, brow, or open palm against stone.

They do not know whether the thing below is asking who remains.

They do not know whether it is warning them.

They do not know whether their answer keeps it sleeping.

But each time the knock comes, they answer with everything alive, everything dead, and everything that has not yet decided which it is.
