What lives here is difficult to divide into creatures.

The smallest inhabitants make this plain. Every wet surface carries a skin of life: films that turn black stone green, translucent colonies inside beads of condensation, threads growing through the pores of clay before anything resembling soil has formed. The seep water is clouded not by sediment but by minute swimmers. They gather around mineral warmth, separate when a shadow passes, and close again as though the water possessed a granular consciousness.

Fungi inhabit the same surfaces from beneath. Their pale fans and violet cups are merely the visible edges of a network passing through fallen wood, dung, shed scales, empty eggs, and the soft bodies of the newly dead. They do not seem to consume these things so much as translate them. A moth’s wing pattern reappears in the marbling of a fungal shelf. The yellow of a flower passes into a beetle, then into a bird’s throat patch, then shines again from a cluster of spores. Nothing keeps its color for long, though no color is lost.

The insects are beyond counting. Many are armored against perpetual wetness, with overlapping plates from which droplets roll. Others cultivate moisture: long-backed flies carry glistening beads between ridges on their bodies, drinking from them in flight. Beetles with spade-shaped heads work beneath root mats, not excavating lasting tunnels but opening temporary pockets that close perfectly after them. Thin, jointed hunters walk on the surface film of hidden pools. Their legs are so widely spread that they resemble mobile cracks in the reflected sky.

There are moths heavier than some birds. At rest they lie one over another on tree trunks, their wings patterned in rust, cream, basalt black, and the dull sheen of wet clay. When disturbed, they do not scatter at once. The outermost layer lifts, then the next, then the next, until the trunk itself appears to come apart into wings. By the time they settle elsewhere, other moths have taken their former places.

The amphibians are among the basin’s most characteristic inhabitants. They are thick-bodied, patient animals with broad mouths and eyes protected by clear inner lids. Many sit so long in the same hollows that algae grows across their backs and tiny plants root in folds of skin without harming them. Their calls pass through mud and roots more efficiently than through air. An animal may answer a neighbor it never sees, half the basin away, by sounding a low note into a hollow stem.

Some carry their young in shallow basins along their flanks. Eggs, tadpoles, and newly formed froglets may occupy the same parent at once. The young feed on mucus, trapped gnats, and the green growth of the parent’s back. When they climb down, other young are already present. No empty stage can be observed.

The lizards belong to the branches and to the wet air surrounding them. Their toes spread into soft fringes that grip bark without scarring it. Leaf-shaped species hang beneath foliage with their throats pulsing slowly; sail-backed species stand above the leaves when the vapor is thickest, letting condensation gather along the ribs of their crests. Narrow grooves conduct the droplets to their mouths. They drink without lowering their heads.

A few seem nearly winged. Skin stretches between their limbs and flanks, allowing them to glide through the short spaces between crowded crowns. They rarely travel far enough to lose sight of their starting branch. The forest offers no long fall and no open air, only a succession of nearby surfaces.

The snakes are correspondingly difficult to define. Some mimic vines in color and posture. Some go further: moss adheres to scales roughened for that purpose, and small insects shelter on them. A resting snake may support its own fragment of the forest. Its head alone reveals it, and sometimes not even then. There are species with blunt faces that feed on eggs and fungus-softened carrion, and long-jawed hunters that take frogs from pools without disturbing the floating plants around them. Venom is common, but frantic pursuit is not. In such abundance, hunger seldom requires haste.

Birds fill the levels above.

The lowest are bright, quick gleaners that perch on the backs of larger animals and move over them like sparks. They eat flies, mites, loose algae, and the minute pale worms found in folds of warm skin. They are tolerated even near eyes and nostrils. Their nests are cups sealed with wax and fungus fiber against the wet. Within a single nest, fresh eggs may lie beside naked young, feathered young, and the clean halves of shells. The parents react to each according to its need, showing no confusion.

Higher in the crowns, broad-winged birds move slowly from tree to tree. Their feathers have oily, close-set surfaces, and rain beads on them as it does on leaves. Some possess bare, brilliantly colored faces that serve where sunlight cannot: blue wattles, red eye rings, yellow throat skin, pale crests that show through vapor. Their displays are made for short distances. A flash of color vanishes behind a fan leaf and appears immediately in another crown, answered by a low wooden knock.

The waders patrol root mats and mineral slicks. They have long toes but heavy bodies, and their legs flex deeply with every careful step. They feed on whatever the ground presents—beetles, frogs, fish exposed in a hoofprint—without probing or reshaping it. Their paths never become paths. When one lifts its foot, water shines beneath it, and the crowded surface seems to close.

The fish live in a country with almost no visible rivers. They pass beneath leaves, roots, and mats of floating vegetation through channels scarcely deeper than their bodies. Some are silver, some transparent, some dark enough to disappear against basalt. Their fins are low and rounded for pushing through submerged growth. During moments when a channel seems to end, they wriggle through wet root fiber or vanish into porous stone. A few possess small muscular fins with which they cross the slick margin between one hidden flow and the next. They do not migrate toward any larger water. There may be no larger water.

Among the middle-sized animals are soft-footed grazers, browsers, grub-eaters, and fruit-crushers. Many combine traits that elsewhere would belong to separate kinds. A low, piglike animal may have a flexible browsing lip, webbed feet, and a coat sparse enough to grow green in patches. Another has the sloping back and muscular haunches of a runner, though there is nowhere to run; it uses its strength instead to shoulder gently through reeds that rise again behind it.

They move in herds so dense that, seen through vapor, the herd resembles a dark current. Individuals maintain contact by touch: flank against flank, muzzle against rump, whiskers laid along a neighbor’s side. Calves walk beneath adults, hidden among many sets of legs. No leader is evident. If the foremost animal turns aside from soft ground, the entire mass curves around the place and closes beyond it.

They browse lightly. Their mouths take a leaf here, a fruit there, the end of a fern, a mat of floating green. Nothing is stripped. Whether this restraint is instinct or simply the effect of inexhaustible abundance cannot be determined. Behind them, the vegetation looks untouched, though their bellies remain full.

Predators move within this abundance rather than outside it.

The branch-cats are long, shallow-chested animals with flexible paws and fur that lies in dark waterproof ridges. They rest stretched along horizontal limbs, tails hanging among vines. Their eyes are large but not luminous; there is too much diffuse light for true darkness, and too little contrast for dazzle. They take birds, lizards, and the young of herd animals, usually at arm’s length. A paw descends, a body is gathered upward, and the surrounding life closes around the brief absence.

Larger hunters follow the herds. They are compact, broad-headed, and deliberate, placing their feet in the prints of their prey. Yet no chase produces an enduring gap in the multitude. A taken animal disappears under wings, beetles, roots, and fungal bloom. Elsewhere—sometimes immediately beside the kill—a newborn presses itself upright beneath its mother. The herd’s shape does not alter.

This is the quality shared by everything that lives in the basin: no creature manages to become a consequence.

They eat but do not reduce. They breed but do not increase. They kill without creating scarcity and die without accumulating remains. Their bodies change continuously, yet the life of the place has no before and after. There are old-looking animals, scarred and mossed and heavy-lidded, but no sign that they were ever young. There are young everywhere, but none can be watched passing fully into age. Growth occurs locally—a feather opening, a larva splitting its skin, a fruit swelling around a seed—while the whole remains fixed.

They are not immortal. Death is common and unremarkable. Nor are they ghosts, machines, or frozen images. They hunger genuinely. They startle. They choose mates, defend resting places, search for missing young, and struggle in the jaws of predators. Pain belongs to them. So do appetite, curiosity, irritation, comfort, and the small pleasures of warmth and touch.

What they lack is history.

Memory exists only at the scale required for living. A bird remembers which branch supports its nest, but never converts repeated flight into a route. A predator recognizes the smell of prey, but does not make a cache. Herd animals know one another, perhaps intimately, yet no herd develops a tradition. An amphibian returns to a particular hollow without deepening or improving it. A creature may use a stick to pull an insect closer, but when the insect is eaten the stick is released as though the relation between hand and object had never occurred.

Nothing takes the second step from use to making.

Thus no inhabitant becomes master of the basin. There is no central builder, no keeper of the equilibrium, no hidden intelligence pruning excess. The balance appears to arise from every mouth, womb, spore, root tip, and hunting eye at once. Each creature behaves as if the world were abundant and mutable. Together they produce a world in which abundance never changes and mutation leaves no record.

Seen from within, the basin’s true inhabitant may not be any species at all. It may be the arrangement itself: a single vast life expressed temporarily as bird, fungus, snake, herd, fly, root, and fish. Bodies are its moving parts, and their exchanges are circulations within something that neither develops nor decays.

Yet the animals do not behave like fragments of a collective mind. The frog resists the snake. The moth flees the bird. The calf cries when separated from the press of warm bodies. Each is wholly itself while it lives.

That is what makes the place stranger than a paradise and less merciful than one. Nothing is lost from the whole, but everything can be lost by the individual. The basin preserves life perfectly while preserving no particular life from fear, hunger, or death. It grants endless renewal without progress, endless motion without escape.

Its inhabitants live in the first instant after creation, when every niche is already filled but no deed has yet hardened into a past. They crowd the wet stone, brilliant and busy, forever arriving in a world that has never had time to remember them.
