The first inhabitants one notices are the trees, though “trees” implies individuals more clearly bounded than these.

A vault tree begins as a dark seed lodged in a crack, often scarcely wider than a hair. Its first root does not descend. It travels sideways, searching the rock for moss, old fiber, or another root from which moisture can be drawn. Only after securing several such contacts does it put up leaves: thick, nearly black fans with pale undersides, held close together against the cold.

As it ages, the tree divides. Its trunk separates into load-bearing strands, some rooted in stone, some in the remains of older plants, and some grafted into neighboring trunks. Where two branches press together, their bark thins. In time the tissues beneath mingle. Sap passes across the join. A branch fed by one root system may flower from another. After centuries, it is impossible to say whether a vault belongs to one organism, a grove, or a succession of trees that have inherited one another’s living wood.

These old composites are the great residents of the central thicket. They are hollow almost throughout. Their sound wood forms shells around chambers packed with roots, trapped leaves, fungal felt, and warm, wet air. They grow slowly outward and rapidly inward. Every cavity becomes another protected place in which a seed may germinate. Their youngest descendants are therefore often born inside them, sending pale shoots through cracks toward the canopy and roots downward through the body of their ancestors.

On the windward rim live the cloud-combers. They are shorter and harsher, many-stemmed plants with needles like black glass. Ice shears their crowns into level tables. Their outermost twigs die continually, but the dead tips remain attached, making dense combs that strip water from passing mist. Droplets run along grooves in the needles and converge at swollen joints. There small cups open and close according to the weight of water, tipping their contents into channels that lead toward the basin.

A cloud-comber’s interior is seldom frozen. Its dead outer layers shelter a narrow core of living tissue, and beneath its bark are galleries occupied by dark beetles no larger than seeds. The beetles graze on molds and lay their eggs in frost-killed twigs. Their larvae are white, flat, and almost motionless. During hard freezes they fill their bodies with bitter sugars and become rigid without dying. A larva may take several summers to grow, waking for only a few damp weeks each year.

Higher still are the red spires. What appears from below to be a forest of narrow trunks is one of the thicket’s least divisible beings. Each spire grows upright until wind bends it against another. Contact prompts both to shed bark at the meeting place and fuse. Some are joined at dozens of points. Others have been entirely engulfed, leaving only red seams within a thicker trunk.

The spires exchange water, stored sugars, and injury. When frost opens one stem, neighboring stems thicken around the wound. When a crown loses its light, it may cease making leaves but remain alive for decades, fed through the lattice. Their flowers are minute and nearly transparent. They open inside pockets of still air between the trunks, where pollen falls rather than flies. Often the flower of one apparent tree is fertilized by another stem of the same interwoven body.

Below them live the bridge vines. They are among the few inhabitants capable of visible haste. Their growing tips circle through the air, recoiling from cold stone and fastening to warm bark. Fine hairs along the tip can distinguish living wood from dead by its nightly heat. Once anchored, a vine contracts, drawing two structures closer. Older vines become ropes thick enough to bear whole gardens. Their interiors then decay, leaving flexible tubes through which roots, water, and small animals pass.

The vine’s seed pods wait for frost. A sudden freeze tightens their outer walls while the moist inner layer remains swollen. At dawn they split with a crack and throw hooked seeds through the galleries. Most lodge in moss. Some catch in fur—but there is little fur here—and many instead fasten to other vines, traveling only the length of a bridge before beginning again.

The true majority of the Crown Thicket are smaller beings.

Mosses occupy every interval between larger lives. Some form deep black cushions that drink meltwater and release it over days. Others are translucent, each leaf only a few cells thick, and vanish almost entirely when dry. Thread ferns grow in the exhalations of hollow trunks. Liverworts seal wet wood with overlapping scales. Bead plants hang beneath bridges, storing droplets in round leaves until their own weight tears them loose. Fallen beads roll into lower chambers and root there.

None of these carpets is uniform. A hand’s breadth may contain dozens of competing colonies, their borders marked by changes in sheen, texture, or the direction of tiny leaves. They overgrow one another without pause. Beneath the visible green lies a compressed record of former surfaces: brown moss, pale roots, blackened leaf skins, fungal threads, then older moss again. The apparent floor is a living crust over generations of buried rooms.

Fungi pass through all districts. They are the thicket’s inheritors and its internal weather. Their threads penetrate dead boughs before the last leaves have fallen. They soften overloaded joints, hollow trunks, and turn collapsed bridges into dark fiber in which roots can move. Some bind themselves to nearly every mature plant, carrying minerals from wet rock in exchange for sugars. Others make thin luminous skins in the deepest cavities. Their light is weak and intermittent, visible chiefly after the eyes have ceased expecting to see.

The shelf fungi projecting from walls are only their briefest organs. They emerge after rain, layered like pale balconies, shed dust-fine spores, and are eaten from beneath by the slow grazers.

These grazers are soft, long-bodied creatures with no visible eyes. Their skin is ridged like wet bark and mottled with algae that continue growing on them. They move by expansions of the whole underside, so slowly that moss may begin to overtake a resting animal. A single hollow trunk can support a lineage for thousands of generations. They know its cisterns, its fungal seasons, and the warm seams where grafted wood carries sap. If the trunk collapses, most perish within the familiar chambers. A few cross the new ruin during rain and enter whatever the ruin has joined.

In the concealed pools live the water-striders: pale jointed animals whose legs end in broad, unwettable fans. They do not see the roof above them. They read tremors. A falling droplet sends them outward; the writhing of a trapped beetle draws them inward. Their eggs are attached beneath floating leaves, beyond the reach of surface frost. In winter the adults gather around rootlets where faint warmth leaks from living wood, standing motionless in crowded rings.

There are rarer inhabitants too. Pinhead mites graze the lichens sealing newly exposed stone. Clear worms inhabit the water channels and are carried from district to district whenever cisterns overflow. Minute white springing creatures gather in thousands beneath folded leaves, then scatter like spray when the leaf opens. High in the crowns, frost moths sometimes emerge wingless, females climbing red bark to lay eggs while smaller males are borne uncertainly on the wind.

Birds are visitors rather than citizens. They arrive bright-eyed and loud, disturbing a place where nearly all life communicates by pressure, moisture, chemical trace, or the slow redistribution of shade. They eat the conspicuous berries and depart. Seeds pass through them to other mountains, but few return. To the thicket, a bird is less an inhabitant than a brief mobile season.

No single creature rules the Crown Thicket. If it possesses anything like a ruling life, it lies in the grafts: the innumerable places where one body has accepted another without fully becoming it. Water crosses those unions. Sugars cross. Fungal filaments thread them all. Damage in one quarter alters growth in another. A fallen vault opens light; distant mats flower. A dry cistern causes vines above it to tighten. The cloud-combers gather mist that feeds plants they never touch, while the fungi return the dead to structures not yet born.

Thus the city’s inhabitants are not merely housed by the city. They are its rooms, its closures, its accidents of passage. They live by surrounding one another and by being surrounded in turn.

The oldest among them are broad and plural. The youngest begin in darkness, in cracks no larger than their seeds. Between these conditions lies the whole life of the Crown Thicket: to find another surface, fasten to it, alter it, and eventually become the surface upon which something else begins.
