At first the inhabitants seem to be the leaves.

They rise wherever workmanship has opened a seam: from the hairline between two paving stones, from sockets where doors once turned, from drains packed with centuries of fine sediment. Each leaf is a long, dark blade, soft enough to fold under the weakest current and broad enough to gather what little descends from above. Seen close, its surface is not smooth. It is divided into minute translucent chambers, each holding a dusk-colored fluid. Sparse light enters, wanders among these chambers, and is retained.

The leaves do not seek the open water so much as arrange themselves within it. Along streets they incline in parallel. In courtyards they spread into low, overlapping fans. Where walls constrict the flow, they narrow into ribbons that pass through windows and under lintels. A grove adapts to the architecture so perfectly that it can seem ornamental, as if planted according to an old civic design.

But there are no separate plants beneath most of these groves.

Under the silt lies a corded body, pale and thick, following the laid joints of the world. It runs down gutters, branches through tiled conduits, knots itself inside cisterns, and spreads in thin sheets beneath floors. Where a wall bars its way it enters the mortar. Where the mortar is too dense it waits, pressing a soft growing tip against the obstruction for decades. Moisture, mineral seepage, or the slow opening of a crack will eventually admit it.

A plaza’s leaves may belong to the same body as the leaves hanging from a tower many streets away. In older districts the connection extends farther than any observer could trace. Root cords pass beneath collapsed bridges and through reservoirs buried under later reservoirs. Severed portions survive independently, close their wounds, and begin to spread again; yet when two such portions meet, they often join without contest. Their tissues mingle. Afterward no border remains.

Whether the drowned plain holds millions of these beings or only a few immeasurably fragmented ones cannot be determined.

They live too slowly for individuality to be obvious.

A stem damaged by falling masonry releases its milky sap. The sap coils into the water, but within the wound something deliberate-looking occurs. Nearby leaves contract their internal channels. Pressure shifts through the buried runners. Over the following months, growth elsewhere diminishes while a collar thickens around the injury. In a year the break is sealed. In ten, new shoots have risen from the rubble. In a century the fallen stones are enclosed in roots and held as firmly as if rebuilt.

This is not thought as animals would know it. Nothing startles. Nothing desires and pursues. Yet information travels through the colonies: changes in pressure, faint electrical disturbances, gradients of dissolved stone. A collapse in one chamber alters growth in chambers far beyond it. A density current crossing the plain is registered through thousands of leaves at once. For several seasons afterward, new blades emerge aligned to the direction from which it came.

The oldest colonies have learned the city’s shape without possessing any image of it. Their knowledge is embodied in branching. They know that this channel reaches warmer water, that this stairwell accumulates silt, that beyond this blocked doorway lies porous brick. They remember by continuing to occupy. A route abandoned by growth is forgotten only when the last buried runner dies.

Among them live the wall-dwellers.

These are the velvet growths that cover inscriptions and reliefs. Up close, each patch consists of countless upright threads, shorter than a fingernail and crowded so closely that water scarcely moves between them. Their exposed tips are dark green or blue-black. Beneath, protected from the cold water, they are colorless.

They feed on mineral traces drawn from fired clay, mortar, and stone. Pale branching films penetrate the surfaces under them, dissolving almost nothing at a time. Over centuries they soften chisel marks. They turn carved faces into gentle rises and written lines into unreadable furrows. Their destruction is inseparable from their preservation: the same mat that consumes a wall also binds its loosening grains and prevents them from washing away.

Whole genealogies of these small inhabitants may occupy a single column. The outermost layer is alive. Beneath it lie compressed generations, still clinging to the stone. When a mat grows too thick, its oldest depth darkens and becomes part of the sediment. The living surface advances outward by less than a finger’s width in a human lifetime.

In the warm seepage above deep cisterns are paler beings. They have no broad leaves. Their bodies resemble branching antlers made of wax, or hands with too many slender fingers. They grow with their tips turned toward the mineral current, taking sustenance from compounds carried up from buried water systems. Some are nearly white; others hold a dim yellow-green that seems self-luminous until the surrounding darkness is examined and proves them only reflective.

They form gardens around vents and cracked pipe mouths. Because nothing grazes them, their shapes become fantastically elaborate. Branches divide and divide again until their own weight bends them downward. They touch the wall, attach there, and rise once more. Old specimens make cages around entire rooms. When a section dies, the living branches continue across its rigid remains, producing thickets of repeated arches.

Farther from the seeping water they cannot survive. The boundary of each pale garden records the reach of warmth more precisely than any instrument.

The tallest inhabitants stand in the settling basins.

Their stems are jointed, hollow, and strengthened by rings of tough fiber. They ascend through the darkness beyond sight, though few reach anywhere near the surface. Their purpose is not merely to approach the light. The long columns also bridge layers of water, drawing faintly warmer fluid from below and releasing traces of it above. A single stem may take centuries to reach its full height. By then its base has thickened to the width of a doorway, rooted through accumulated vegetation and into the tiled floor beneath.

These giants grow in close ranks. When the slow currents move through them, they lean one after another, passing pressure across the basin. The resulting tremor may take hours to travel from one edge of the thicket to the other. Stems touch, separate, and touch again. Their surfaces polish one another. At the contact points they sometimes fuse.

A mature thicket is therefore less a collection of towers than a loose, flexible structure assembled from living pillars. It catches sediment, slows water, and makes its own ground. Dead stems remain upright for ages, hardened by minerals. New stems use them as supports. The basin gradually fills with a dark, fibrous mass until the upper joints of the plants stand where open water once lay.

There are also the drifters, though they scarcely seem alive.

Once in many years, mature surfaces on the ribbon forests become grainy. The grains detach without visible signal and rise or fall according to the minute balance of the water. They are spores wrapped in transparent skins, carrying enough stored substance to endure long transport. Most settle where nothing can wake them: on intact basalt, on living leaves, in cold hollows starved of minerals. There they remain. Decades pass. Some collapse. Others are buried.

A few enter mortar.

When water reaches them through the porous stone, their skins open. What emerges is not a root or a leaf but a clear thread, almost too fine to see. It explores one grain of sand, then another. If it finds no suitable chemistry, it stops and hardens. If it finds clay, decaying fiber, or the residue of an older colony, it branches.

The first dark leaf may not appear for fifty years.

These are the only travelers in the drowned world. They have no destination, no senses directed outward, and no means to resist the current. Yet through them the vegetation crosses sealed chambers and reaches structures isolated by collapses. A room can remain bare for a thousand years, then acquire a green film, then a fringe of leaves, then a root mass that fills it completely. The arrival is silent. The occupation is permanent.

Among all this growth, death is difficult to locate.

Leaves age without being eaten. Their tissues grow opaque, soften, and fold. Still attached, they become substrates for smaller films. When at last they tear free, they descend only a little way before catching on another leaf, a parapet, or a carved projection. Layers gather. Pressure flattens them. Their outlines remain visible deep within the black felt filling old interiors.

Living roots enter these deposits and feed upon what the water has slowly altered. There may be organisms smaller than the visible plants completing this work—colorless cells inhabiting the boundaries between sap, mineral, and decay—but they build no bodies large enough to disturb the silence. If they are present, they exist as chemical populations: films within films, generations contained in droplets between fibers. They leave neither tracks nor shells.

Thus the primary inhabitant is not any single stem, mat, or pale garden. It is the continuity among them: the patient living layer laid over the constructed one.

It has no center. The shafts do not lead to its heart. The tallest grove is not its head, nor are the ribbon forests its limbs. Parts die beyond recovery every year somewhere across the plain. Other parts awaken. Connections close and reopen. The whole cannot be killed by a roof fall or a blocked current because it is not whole in that way.

Still, over immense periods, it has acquired something like a character.

It favors workmanship. Natural fracture, if any exists below the masonry, offers less to it than fitted stone. It thrives in channels, joints, rooms, drains, reservoirs, and roads. The dead builders divided water and sediment into innumerable small conditions, and the living layer has answered each condition with a form. The architecture is its climate, its trellis, and its memory.

On unbroken paving it advances reluctantly. At a row of peg holes it flowers into clustered shoots—not flowers in color or scent, but tight translucent blades bearing spores. In stairwells it descends step by step. Along aqueducts it sends roots farther than it sends leaves. It occupies the world according to intentions it cannot understand.

Perhaps this is why, from a distance, the vegetation sometimes seems to be rebuilding the drowned civilization.

Roots brace walls. Mats seal cisterns. Jointed stems stand where columns have fallen. Braided runners cross gaps left by vanished bridges. But no design is being restored. The resemblance comes from the fact that both builder and growth obey the same gradients: gravity, water, weakness, support. The plants repeat the old solutions because the old solutions have become the shape of every opportunity.

Their life is neither peaceful nor cruel. Those words require creatures that can flee, pursue, suffer anticipation, or recognize rivals. Here there is only endurance, contact, deprivation, and growth. One colony shades another for three hundred years. A wall mat closes over a young runner and starves it. A pale garden dwindles when its warm seep fails. These are struggles, but they have no moments of decision. Victory is measured in thickness. Loss is a corridor gone dark.

And if there is anything resembling awareness in the immense connected forests, it is an awareness without images: pressure passing through a million leaves; warmth moving along buried tile; the mineral taste of a newly opened room; the distant shock of an arch collapsing, received not as sound but as a change in water and tension.

For centuries afterward, roots turn toward it.

Slowly they enter the ruin.

Slowly the ruin becomes inhabited.
