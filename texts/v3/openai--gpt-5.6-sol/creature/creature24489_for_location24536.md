The first inhabitants one notices are the **matbacks**.

They are small, six-legged grazers with broad ribs and loose pelts woven in gray, saffron, and leaf-green bands. When feeding, they move with the mild persistence of livestock, clipping resinous shoots with paired plates of glazed clay. When alarmed, their legs unlace at the joints and fold beneath them. The spine spreads, the pelt draws taut, and the animal becomes a rectangular mat laid neatly across the path.

A matback may remain this way for hours. Beetles cross it. Seeds lodge in its fur. Other animals sleep on it. If danger passes quickly, it rises unchanged. If it is forgotten, roots enter its underside and the path begins to grow over it. Some matbacks awaken months later with flowers along their backs. Some do not awaken but continue to migrate as sections of flooring, lifted and relaid by unseen hands.

They travel in pairs. Each carries on its brow a glossy black patch in which its companion is reflected, however far apart they become. The reflection keeps both animals near. If one patch is scratched away, the pair may separate by no more than a few steps and yet spend the rest of their lives trying to cross the distance.

Above them browse the **masked stilters**, those narrow, long-legged animals resembling deer assembled from reeds, lacquered hide, and pale joint-bone. Their faces have no mouths in the usual place. The entire mask lowers over aromatic crusts, seals against the ground, and lifts with a thin layer adhering to its inner surface. Feeding occurs behind the mask. Now and then a stilter raises its head and opens its face lengthwise to let the spent crust fall out as colored dust.

Their young begin as soft tassels suspended beneath the adults. For several years they have neither legs nor masks. They drink warmth through braided cords and watch the paths through amber beads. When ready, a tassel drops onto a pile of discarded limbs. It selects four, not always matching, knots them to itself, and follows the first adult whose mask it recognizes. A herd may therefore contain legs belonging to many generations.

No one leads these herds. Leadership passes into whichever animal is momentarily being watched. The watched stilter becomes decisive, choosing turns and testing bridges. When attention moves elsewhere, it lowers its head and becomes ordinary again.

Under the leaves live the **undersides**: pale lizards so closely fitted to foliage that they appear to be a second, private anatomy of the plant. They have flat translucent bodies, fine copper claws, and rows of breathing slits aligned with the leaf’s microscopic scales. They regulate the leaf’s temperature, licking mineral deposits from its veins and opening or closing its pores with their toes.

Whether they are animals living on plants or organs capable of detaching is uncertain. At evening they move from old leaves to new ones. The abandoned leaves soon cool, tarnish, and fall. During droughts of oil or fragrance, whole colonies migrate across the canopy, and the trees beneath them seem to change species overnight.

Some blossoms are inhabited before they exist.

The creatures responsible are called **petal-bearers**, though each one becomes what it carries. They resemble tiny green people only at a careless glance: upright bodies, two grasping forelimbs, basketlike backs. Seen closely, each is a colony of hinged scales surrounding a warm grain. They gather mica, horn, wax, metal leaf, beetle glass, dyed skin, and pieces of shed mask. At a closed bud they arrange themselves in concentric ranks. Their legs sink into the bud’s seams. Their backs broaden, their colors deepen, and over several weeks the colony becomes a flower.

When the flower closes, not all of them wake again.

Those that do emerge carrying a scent they did not possess before. They take this scent to other buds, although it is less like pollen than a set of instructions: curve here, darken at the edge, leave room for five more bodies. Thus blossoms breed partly by persuading animals to remember their shapes.

In the canopy pass the **given birds**.

They are flat as folded paper and have wings marked with diagrams of wings. They do not fly. A branch holding one bends, gathers tension, and relinquishes it. For a moment the bird is absent. Its shadow crosses the intervening space, often skimming over surfaces that are not on the ground. Another branch bends and receives it.

A given bird cannot be caught between branches, but its shadow can. Certain hunters pin these shadows with thorns. The bird then fails to arrive. For days or years it remains nowhere visible, while every suitable branch in the garden inclines slightly under an undelivered weight. When the thorn is removed, the bird appears on all those branches in quick succession before choosing one body again.

Their open cavities contain curled strips, dry membranes, or little racks of clay tongues. Most are silent. A few have completed songs, though they sing only one note in a lifetime. The note may be a knock, a breath, the sound of grit crushed under a tile. Once sung, it leaves the bird and becomes a small airborne animal. These living notes hide in huts and hollow fruit. They are responsible for many sounds attributed to settling walls.

The little flames under the beetles’ wings are not fire but **attention kept visible**. The beetles collect moments of notice from the eyes of larger creatures. Each glance kindles a cold flame; prolonged staring makes it burn blue and tall. They spend this gathered attention along their scent routes, fixing unstable paths in place.

A heavily watched beetle can make a trail so firm that it remains passable in both directions. An unnoticed beetle may turn a corner and find that its abdomen has reached the destination before its head has left. Such beetles sometimes knot themselves around the contradiction and become hinges for gates.

The black hunters are known by many forms because they rarely complete a hunt unchanged.

They begin low to the ground, with padded bodies, sealed jaws, and a single row of watchful eyes. Their approach is ceremonial rather than swift. Each step is considered for hours. Their prey sees them coming and continues to feed, sleep, molt, reproduce, or enter another category of life. The hunter’s task is not simply to overtake but to remain the kind of creature for which the prey remains food.

Usually it fails.

A matback becomes a path. A petal-bearer becomes a flower. A stilter sheds its edible skin and wears one made of bitter copper scales. The hunter then stops, folds its legs beneath itself, and extrudes an arch of black ribs. Leaves gather over it. Clay insects plaster the gaps. By the end of a season it has become a shelter.

Many of the garden’s huts began as disappointed predators.

A few succeed. Their jaws do not open outward; instead, the prey discovers itself already inside. For a time hunter and hunted occupy the same skin, growing older at unequal rates. Later the hunter coughs up a small glazed effigy of the animal. These effigies are placed in shrines or built into pillars. Some continue to graze in miniature on painted fields.

There are parasites, though possession is difficult to define in the Low Garden.

**Knot-mites** live in the tied joints of nearly every walking creature. They tighten fibers, replace frayed ligaments, and alter the angle of limbs. A healthy animal may carry thousands. If the mites disagree about the proper repair, the host develops a new gait or an extra joint. Over generations such errors become species.

**Mouthkeepers** inhabit discarded jaws. They polish ceramic teeth, sharpen grazing edges, and test each mouth with dry coughs until another body requires it. At night one may see an empty jaw moving carefully along a wall on dozens of fine legs.

**Silver threadworms** live inside trellises and porous rods. They pass through fired clay as if burrowing through loam. Wherever they gather, the ceramic becomes briefly soft enough to bend. Much of the garden’s slow architectural growth is theirs. A trellis does not receive a new rung because someone attaches one; rather, threadworms persuade two upright rods to remember that a crosspiece was always between them.

The dead are attended by the **sorters**.

They are pallid, many-fingered insects with bodies divided into small labeled compartments. They arrive without hurry and take a corpse apart according to substance rather than organ. Pigment goes into sacs along the left side. Fibers are combed and rolled beneath the abdomen. Oils are sealed in warm bladders. Salts form geometric crusts on the back. Teeth, regardless of their former owner, are stacked in the thorax.

Nothing is eaten. Everything is classified.

When full, sorters travel to nests, unfinished birds, cracked walls, eggs, wounds, or blossoms. Their bodies open like cabinets. Other creatures take what is needed. An old grazer’s green may become a lizard’s eye; its ligaments may tie a bridge; its teeth may be planted in a fruit that will hatch with the ability to chew.

Sorters occasionally make errors. They file a memory among pigments, or an appetite with reusable fibers. Such mistakes are treasured. They produce blue walls that recall being hunted, ropes that hunger for knots, and animals born nostalgic for bodies they never possessed.

Near the basin of red stones live the **turners**, though they are almost never seen whole. A black fingertip appears between two pebbles. A row of tiny knees withdraws beneath the basin’s rim. A hooded back crosses the court only when every observer is looking at a different center.

They keep the pebbles rotating, but not by pushing them. They whisper roughness from one stone to another. A pebble becomes smooth because its angles have been distributed among the turners’ hidden bodies. Older turners are said to be nearly impossible to approach: they have accumulated so many corners that distance catches on them. Passage around such a being may take days.

If a red pebble is removed and goes cold, a turner eventually comes for it. What arrives resembles a knee-high figure in a hood, carrying a shallow basket. Follow it and it becomes a file of unrelated animals: two undersides, several beetles, a lame matback, a walking jaw, perhaps a folded bird being passed from back to back. Ignore it and the hood remains one hood, the basket one basket, the purpose unmistakable.

These are the beings most often called the **gardeners**, but the name suggests individuals where there may be only agreements.

A gardener is assembled whenever a task requires one.

For the repair of a wall, beetles bring fixed attention so the crack cannot withdraw. Threadworms soften the ceramic. A mouthkeeper nips damaged edges. Petal-bearers carry flakes of clay. Knot-mites bind the brace. A matback lies flat as scaffolding. Their movements overlap until, from any respectful distance, they form a single hooded worker with two hands and a mallet.

The thumbprint left in wet clay belongs to no thumb found on any participant. It is the print of the arrangement itself.

Approach too closely and the agreement loosens. There is no deception in this. The animals have not disguised themselves as a person. Rather, personhood is one of the garden’s temporary structures, like a bridge, flower, predator, or room. It persists only as long as needed.

Some gardeners last minutes. Some have continued for centuries, their constituent animals replaced one by one without interrupting the work. One ancient gardener is believed to inhabit the southern margin. It has been seen as a bent figure moving among the skeletal frames, tying one-winged birds to cords and pressing hollows into unfired eggs. No single creature there is old, yet the figure’s habits are. It always favors the same absent hand. It pauses at the same blank tile. It appears to be following plans for a garden already finished.

There are also inhabitants of the garden’s deeper intervals: beings not hidden under leaves or behind walls, but inside distances that have gone unwatched.

Grazers sometimes stare into an empty gap between adjacent tiles and chew as though watching a herd in a far meadow. A vine may grow eye-shaped leaves around such a gap to prevent it from receding. Through it one may glimpse warm glades, densely nested courts, forests no taller than a hand and yet days across. Their animals are familiar in pieces but not in combination: flying masks trailing roots, black shelters stalking lacquer flowers, herds made entirely of legs.

These are not separate worlds. They are the garden’s uncounted rooms.

Their inhabitants understand attention as weather. To be watched is to experience nearness, pressure, and definition. To be forgotten is to enter a broad season in which bodies may loosen and alternatives accumulate. Many creatures retreat deliberately into forgetfulness when molting. They return with an altered number of limbs, a new diet, or memories of having been architecture.

Others fear it. Pairs keep one another present. Herds exchange reflective brow patches. Nests are ringed with eye-leaves. Eggs are painted with staring faces so they can watch themselves until hatching.

At evening, when shadows detach, another population becomes apparent.

Each animal’s shadow has spent the day learning its shape without perfectly obeying it. Once free, shadows gather beneath trees, in shrines, and under folded leaves. Most wait quietly for their owners. A few exchange places. A matback may leave in the morning with a stilter’s shadow and thereafter become restless at the sight of high branches. A predator wearing the shadow of a hut may cease hunting and begin to shelter anything that lies down inside its darkness.

Some shadows do not return at all. They thicken over successive evenings, acquire joints, and eventually step into the garden as black animals with no corresponding bodies. These are perhaps the true origin of the low hunters. Or the hunters may be shadows cast by shelters not yet built.

Beneath all these lives circulates the warm mineral oil. It may itself be inhabited. Fine membranes have been found drifting in the capillaries, folding around heat and carrying it from root to root. They possess no stable shape, but the channels alter after their passage, becoming better suited to whatever grows above. The red stones rotate in patterns resembling migration. Trees separated by whole groves flower in sequence as though exchanging a slow signal underground.

It is possible that the oil is the garden’s oldest animal.

It is equally possible that the dome is.

The wicker roof breathes through hidden screens. Its pinholes dilate. Sunbeams bend toward resting creatures and remain warm after the apparent sun has moved. Small repairs appear in the plaster where no ladder could stand. During the evening inhalation, every knot in the wicker tightens at once, and the handprints pressed into the lime seem briefly deeper, as though hands on the other side were fitting themselves back into them.

But the inhabitants do not worship the dome, the oil, the stones, or the garden as a whole. Worship would require a distinction between maker and made that they do not seem to possess.

They build one another because building is how life continues there.

A deer repairs the vine that cools the lizard that tends the leaf that feeds the matback whose resting body fixes the path used by the beetle whose attention holds near the wall repaired by the gardener assembled from them all. Predator becomes hut. Corpse becomes inventory. Bird becomes note. Animal becomes petal, shadow, doorway, or hand.

Even the unfinished things are alive in this provisional sense. The one-winged birds hanging at the southern margin listen with empty song cavities. The unfired eggs warm themselves from within. Blank frames flex when no one watches. Each waits not for a final maker, but for enough smaller lives to agree upon what it has been becoming.

Thus no single species properly inhabits the Low Garden.

What lives there is a multitude perpetually arranging itself into creatures, structures, tasks, and relationships—then loosening, sorting its parts, and arranging itself again. The garden is crowded not only with bodies but with possible bodies. Every joint may be untied. Every wall may discover it was a shell. Every nest may stand up.

And when the whole place inhales, all its residents look toward different centers because each briefly feels the pull of the form it might become next.
