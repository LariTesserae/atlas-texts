What lives here has learned not to belong to a place.

The smallest lives are scarcely bodies at all. They wake wherever rain spreads thinly over stone: clear cells sealed in mineral skins, dormant for months in pores too fine for water to enter freely. When the pavement wets, they open. They draw iron, sulfur, and faint salts from the rock, arranging themselves into a transparent film only a few cells deep. In shadow it is invisible. Under pressure it answers with cold blue light.

For a short while this skin is the plain’s pasture.

The first grazers arrive as black-brown multitudes, each animal no larger than a fingernail. They have low armored backs and a fringe of quick legs that keeps their bellies above the abrasive stone. Their mouthplates are broad, replaceable rasps. A feeding front advances with astonishing neatness, blue-white sparks traveling beneath it as millions of jaws disturb the film. Individuals are nearly mindless, but the swarm behaves like weather: dividing around dry ground, contracting before storms, pouring into newly wetted basins within minutes.

They lay their eggs in cracks while water is rising. The eggs are hard mineral beads indistinguishable from gravel until a flood softens their seams. Most wait through many rains. Thus generations overlap underground, and a swarm appearing after a storm may contain offspring laid a century apart.

Behind them come the shield-backs.

These are broad creatures, some as long as a person is tall, plated in overlapping shells of gray, dun, or oily black. Their many short limbs end in rubbery gripping pads. They browse slowly, taking the film the smaller grazers missed and crushing the grazers themselves when they can. Their armor is never silent. The plates shift, knock, and grind with every step, each animal producing a slightly different rhythm.

A herd recognizes its members by this clatter. Mothers find their young through fog by listening for an irregular triplet in the smaller shells. Rivals turn side-on and shake themselves until their armor booms. During migration, the herd becomes a moving field of percussion, loud enough to conceal approaching predators and low enough to be felt through submerged feet.

Nothing here can afford great height, but some animals carry themselves high for another reason. The bell-bodied strainwalkers stand on six or eight reed-thin limbs, their swollen torsos suspended above the water. Their legs flex independently, so the body remains almost motionless while the feet negotiate cracks and currents below. From the underside hangs a veil of transparent tendrils. They spread this veil across a channel and strain from it eggs, larvae, flakes of tissue, and the dust of ground shell.

Their bells are hollow. Air enters through valves along the back and passes through internal chambers before being expelled. The resulting notes change with humidity and pressure. A solitary strainwalker murmurs. A gathering becomes a choir of deep, uncertain vowels. Before violent rain, they close their valves and fall abruptly mute.

The people of the plain listen for that silence.

The channel swimmers are paler, almost colorless except for a dark line of organs visible through their sides. Their bodies are narrow in one direction and broad in the other, made to slip edgewise through fissures. They swim by rippling their entire length, but when trapped on exposed rock they coil and strike the ground, throwing themselves from wet patch to wet patch. A migrating school sounds like heavy rain arriving early.

They have eyes only when young. As they mature and enter deeper cracks, skin grows over the lenses. Older swimmers navigate by sensing minute pressure changes along the whole body. Some vanish into the buried sea and return years later, scarred, swollen, and carrying luminous parasites around their mouths. These returners are followed, cautiously, because their appearance often precedes the opening of long-dry vents.

The predators are quieter.

The ragged plates in shallow pools are called many things, none of them names spoken carelessly near water. At rest they flatten every limb beneath a mottled mantle and let mineral deposits accumulate across their backs. Old individuals support layers of pale grit thick enough to grow the feeding film. Grazers walk directly over them.

Then the apparent stone buckles.

Six hooked limbs close inward. The center of the plate opens into rings of hard teeth, and water erupts around it. After feeding, the animal settles elsewhere and slowly resumes the posture of terrain. Its patience exceeds ordinary hunger. A plate may remain folded for a season, digesting shell and waiting for the pathways of a herd to become habitual.

In flowing channels live the prongheads, long muscular hunters that brace themselves between opposite walls. Their skulls bear hollow tines full of taut membranes. Each tine is tuned to a different vibration: swimmer-thrashing, shell-step, falling rain, the distant grinding that precedes a flood. They strike without seeing, guided by distortions in the water and stone.

Young prongheads have soft crowns and hunt in groups. Adults become solitary as their tines harden. Very old ones carry broken, mismatched antlers and are sometimes found facing upstream during storms, feeding on whatever the pressurized aquifer drives into their mouths.

There are scavengers in every seam: red threadlike colonies that enter carcasses through joints; hand-sized shell-cutters with one immense claw; translucent sacs that inflate around scraps and dissolve them slowly. After a flood they gather so densely that a dead shield-back appears to tremble. Within days its soft parts are gone. Within years its armor has been broken, swallowed, excreted, and rolled into the pale bars that edge the channels.

And there are people.

From far away they resemble another migratory species: low figures moving in files, wrapped in dull hides, burdened with shells and poles. They rarely stand fully upright in the open. Wind, noise, and the uncertainty of the crust favor a crouched gait, one hand often lowered to the rock. Their feet are broad from childhood and protected by layered soles of cured membrane, stitched so that the toes can spread and feel vibration.

They are compact, heavy-boned, and strong through the neck and hips. Their ears have small outer folds that can close against spray, but hearing is only one part of how they listen. Bare palms, jawbones, knees, and the soles of the feet are all used against the ground. When uncertain of a route, a traveler may lie flat with one cheek pressed to the pavement and remain there for several minutes, reading the buried water.

Their language has two forms.

The near-language is spoken mouth to ear inside shelters or beneath hide hoods: dense, breathy, full of touches to the shoulder and face. The far-language is made for the plain. It uses clapped shells, stamped rhythms, booming chest resonators, and small copper tongues held against stone. Its simplest messages travel through fog: danger, firm ground, rising water, food, return. More elaborate patterns identify families and recount routes.

Names are not fixed words. A person’s public name is a short rhythm, often derived from the sound of their first shelter, the storm during their birth, or a flaw in the beat of a household wall. Because shelters change as they erode, names change too. An elder may have carried six or seven names, each remembered as a different arrangement of knocks.

Their settlements are not villages in the usual sense. No one expects to remain long in one place. A settlement is a circuit of silence shelters, hunting basins, egg-stones, copper seams, and safe crossings joined by remembered sound. Several families may occupy the same circuit at different seasons without meeting. Their claim is not to the ground but to the sequence: the right to follow certain tones when the water stands at a certain height.

The silence shelters are their most valued inheritance.

Inside them, children learn how loud their own bodies are. They hear swallowing, sleep-breath, the click of a needle passing through hide. They are taught the shapes of dangerous echoes by placing their hands on resonant slabs while elders strike the outer walls. Repairs are made cautiously. A stone added in the wrong place may amplify the very note the shelter was built to extinguish.

The oldest structures are treated less as monuments than as living instruments. Their builders are not imagined as vanished. They remain present in proportions, grooves, and absences—in the exact gap that lets one subterranean voice cancel another. To alter such a shelter without understanding it is a kind of killing.

Food comes from movement. People skim sheets of mineral film with toothed plates, though it is bitter and sustains poorly. They harvest grazer swarms in woven shell-fiber scoops, break open shield-backs at the soft seams, and suspend funnel nets for larvae during flood reversals. Strainwalkers are hunted rarely; their pressure-sensing organs are more valuable intact than their meat. Dried channel swimmers become dense, salty strips carried on long crossings.

Water is everywhere and not always drinkable. Some pools burn the mouth with metal. Others carry dormant parasites that awaken in warm flesh. Travelers test them with copper resonators: the plate is dipped, struck, and judged by the deadening of its note. Children learn the tones of safe water before they learn the routes beyond their birth shelter.

Fire is difficult. Fuel must be carried: rendered oil, dried membranes, cakes of animal fat mixed with shell dust. Flames are kept tiny and sheltered in stone recesses. Most food is softened chemically, fermented in sealed skins, or laid in warm vent cracks. A household may transport an ember for weeks in a double shell packed with porous mineral, less for cooking than for working hide and heating the fine glass points used in surgery.

Metalworkers are not miners so much as listeners for metal. Native copper occurs in rare threads within the pavement. A skilled finder walks with a rack of tuned plates and strikes the stone until an answering overtone reveals a buried seam. Copper is too precious for large blades. It becomes needles, probes, valves, weather tongues, and thin disks used to map resonance inside shelters.

Death does not produce graves. The crust is too hard, and loose ground is suspect. Bodies are wrapped in weighted hide and placed in selected channels where scavengers can open them. Later, relatives retrieve polished bones or shell fragments caught downstream. These are fitted into shelter walls as wedges. The dead therefore enter the instruments of silence. When a wall begins to fail, ancestral pieces may rattle before any crack is visible.

The people fear sudden quiet more than noise.

Noise means that air and water are still moving through known passages. Quiet may mean a sealed vent, a flooded chamber, pressure gathering below. When every fissure falls silent at once, people leave low ground immediately. Herds do the same. For a brief interval all life moves together—grazers like spilled grain, shell-backs knocking in haste, strainwalkers folding their legs, people carrying children and sounding the crust ahead of them.

They do not worship the buried sea as a single being. It has too many voices for that. They speak instead of hungers, lungs, sleepers, and mouths beneath the stone. Some chambers are said to remember every impact made above them. Others are believed to answer sounds from places impossibly distant. There are shelters where a knock given at dusk returns after midnight, altered by passages no one has entered.

The most respected listeners spend years learning those returns. They are route-keepers, weather-readers, builders, and historians at once. Age often leaves them partially deaf to airborne sound, but more sensitive to vibration. They sit with both hands on the floor and interpret tremors others cannot separate: a herd two days away, rain beyond the horizon, a newly collapsed shaft, the deep tidal pulse of the aquifer.

Sometimes they hear rhythms that match no known animal, storm, or vent.

These may be dismissed as shifting stone. They may be the echoes of distant settlements. They may come from organisms in the lower sea—vast, slow things pressing against cavern roofs, never exposed to sky. The people do not agree. They only know that on certain nights, when surface water lies perfectly still, something below moves with enough deliberation to make the standing stones answer one after another across the plain.

Then even the predators flatten themselves.

The herds cease clattering.

People enter the old shelters, where the walls subtract the familiar roar but cannot remove that deeper cadence. In the comparative silence they sit close enough to feel one another breathing, hands spread on the ancient floor, while the thin world beneath them speaks from below.
