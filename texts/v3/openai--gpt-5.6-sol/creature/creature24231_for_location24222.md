The people who live here are seldom dry.

Even indoors they carry the weather with them: darkened shoulders, damp hair, cuffs greened by contact with moss, bare calves stippled with mud. Their hands are pale from water and scored by wicker, thorns, fish spines, and split reeds. Children develop tough feet early. Adults move with a slight forward inclination, learned from climbing steep paths under loads and ducking beneath branches without looking up.

They are not especially small, but the town teaches them to fold themselves. They sit on their heels, sleep beneath low rafters, turn sideways through living doorways, and pass one another on bridges with an intimate economy of movement. A stranger often strikes their head. A resident merely tilts it.

Their clothes are layered rather than heavy. Next to the skin they wear nettle cloth or softened bark fiber; over it, loose wool, felted wraps, aprons, gaiters, and short capes dressed with oil or wax. Long hems are impractical. Wide sleeves catch on twigs. Most garments stop at the knee or are bound close to the leg, and nearly everyone owns a hood broad enough to shed dripping water away from the face. Nothing remains its original color for long. Plant dyes fade into smoke-blue, peat-brown, alder-red, lichen-yellow, and all the soft, uncertain greens of things kept in shade.

Clothing is patched as patiently as houses. A coat may preserve pieces of three generations: a mother’s hood, a grandfather’s pockets, a strip from the sailcloth of an old market awning. People recognize one another’s histories in these repairs. To wear something entirely new is conspicuous, almost embarrassing, unless it is ceremonial.

Ornaments are light and resistant to damp: carved horn, polished fruit stones, teeth, snail shells, bright feathers sealed in resin, copper wire hammered thin and reheated many times. Iron is rarely worn for decoration. Iron has work to do.

Almost everyone carries a knife. It is less a weapon than a second thumb, used to cut cord, lift bark, clean fish, graft branches, trim hooves, sharpen stakes, divide food, and scrape rot from wood. A person’s knife bears a name, and the name belongs to the continuing tool rather than any particular blade. Children receive small blunt ones when they are judged old enough not to lose them into a pond.

They speak loudly in public and quietly at home. The loudness is practical: water, leaves, animals, and hidden corners swallow ordinary speech. Calls have developed for the paths—a rising note for someone coming uphill, a falling one for a descending load, two clipped shouts for livestock, a long wavering cry for loose timber or uncontrolled water. Every child knows them. At close range, however, the townspeople often murmur, as if compensating for the clamor outside.

Their language is rich in gradients of wetness. There are separate common words for rain that hangs without falling, rain shaken from trees, rain blown uphill, water emerging through a wall, water lying over hard ground, and water that has begun to move soil. They distinguish a branch that may be trusted from one that is merely alive. They speak of buildings as healthy, tired, thirsty, crooked, or settling well.

They rarely describe ownership without qualification. A person may own a wall but not the water through it; a roof but not the tree growing from it; a tree’s lower fruit but not the branches over the lane. Rights are layered like the terraces. A single alder may involve a fruit-gathering family, a bridge keeper, two pigeon households, a dyer who harvests its bark, and children who possess by custom the hollow between its roots. Arguments are therefore frequent, detailed, and usually unresolved in any final sense. They end in arrangements rather than victories.

The most respected people are those who understand consequence downhill.

These include the channel keepers, who walk the settlement during heavy rain with hooked poles and hooded lanterns. They know which sluice feeds which pond, whose retaining wall is newly weakened, where leaf-muck gathers, and which family will pretend not to hear when called before dawn. Their authority lasts only while water is rising, but during those hours it is nearly absolute. They may cut a fence, empty a cistern, breach a garden bank, or order a household from its rooms. Afterward, they must account for every act beneath the pollarded trees in the square.

There are rootwrights, too: builders who work not only with cut timber but with living trunks. They train branches across gaps, open bark for grafts, brace trees without strangling them, and judge where a growing limb will stand twenty years hence. A skilled rootwright begins rooms for grandchildren not yet born. Their plans are bundles of marked switches, clay models kept damp in baskets, and songs whose verses record measurements.

Pond tenders maintain fish, ducks, reeds, and water plants in shifting combinations. They read the color of the surface and smell sickness before it becomes visible. They know which ponds may accept overflow from the fiber basins and which must be protected from it. In cold weather they break skin ice with wooden mallets and push rafts of bundled brush beneath the surface so fish can shelter.

There are bark beaters, basket makers, net knotters, cider pressers, mushroom growers, charcoal burners, smiths, goose herders, leech gatherers, midwives, herb cutters, pigeon keepers, and people whose principal skill is finding lost animals by the differing clack of their wooden bells. Many trades overlap. A smith may also keep bees. A channel keeper may weave eel traps. No one performs only one kind of labor, because the town interrupts specialization. Rain does not care that a person is a potter when a bank gives way.

Children belong partly to their households and partly to the paths.

They travel in wet, noisy bands, carrying messages, gathering fallen branches, raiding gooseberries, and undertaking errands whose urgency they exaggerate for pleasure. The youngest are tethered near dangerous channels by long cords fixed to waist belts, though they learn to untie these with remarkable speed. Older children know passages inaccessible to adults: hollows under platforms, culverts wide enough for thin shoulders, ladders concealed inside willow screens. They serve as the town’s fastest messengers because they can move across it without following the official paths.

They are taught water order before letters. They learn which basin may be touched with dirty hands, where waste may be poured, how to clear a blocked grate without being pulled against it, and never to step onto a flooded path whose edges cannot be seen. They learn to listen when frogs suddenly stop.

Their games imitate necessary work. They build miniature terraces, release trickles into them, and accuse one another of unlawful drainage. They race bark boats through gutters. They construct tiny houses around saplings and then delight when the saplings split the walls. In summer they keep beetles on threads and wager on which can pull the largest chip of wood.

Old people are highly visible. The town has no single place to which they retire; its work can nearly always be reduced to a seated scale. They sort seed, strip rushes, mend nets, watch infants, turn drying herbs, carve pegs, scare jays, and remember agreements no one thought to write down. Benches are built into the roots around busy crossings so that those who no longer climb easily remain at the center of news.

Age is measured less by years than by remembered changes. Someone will say she was born before the east pond became a garden, or that he first grew a beard in the year the three-house willow came down. The very old serve as maps of vanished paths. During disputes they may be asked where water ran half a century earlier, and their recollections can determine whether a channel is considered restored or newly stolen.

The dead do not leave the settlement entirely.

Bodies are generally carried to drier ground near the upper forest, wrapped in woven mats and buried shallow beneath trees selected for long life and manageable roots. A cutting from the tree may be planted near the deceased person’s house. Thus family lines become groves distributed through the town: ash for one household, rowan for another, alder for those whose work belonged to water. Names pass into tools, bridges, cisterns, and bends in paths. A person may disappear, but the Hook of Enna, Old Brann’s Step, or Seli’s Overflow continues to be used every day.

Their ceremonies are practical acts made solemn by repetition.

Marriages are marked by joining water from the two households’ cisterns and using it to soak a bundle of green rods. The couple bends those rods into the first rib of a shared room. Whether the room is completed within the year depends on kin, weather, and the cooperation of the tree.

At births, the midwife washes the infant first with warm stored water and then touches its feet with a drop from the upper spring. At funerals, tools are not usually buried. They are cleaned, renamed if necessary, and passed on.

The largest annual observance comes near the beginning of the longest rains, when every channel is cleared from the spring to the marsh. Work starts at the upper rim and proceeds downhill. People lift grates, dredge ponds, cut roots from culverts, test bridges, and renew the markers showing clean and foul flow. It ends not in prayer but in a meal spread along the boardwalks of the lower quarter. Ducks steal from the platters. Someone always falls in. The disorder is regarded as a favorable sign.

Their religion, where it can be separated from custom, is concerned less with gods than with continuities. Water must pass. Roots must take hold. What is cut must sprout again. What is taken uphill must eventually be returned downhill in a form that does not poison those below.

They do not worship the spring exactly, but no one approaches its first basin carelessly. A small class of spring stewards keeps its stone channels free of silt and records illnesses that might be carried in the water. Their taboos are old, yet many are confirmed by experience: no tanning above the food-washing basin, no burial in the spring’s catchment, no animal but a nursing one allowed across the upper run. The stewards are expected to be severe. People mock them until someone fouls a cistern.

The inhabitants are hospitable, though not in ways a traveler from the plains always recognizes. A guest is offered warmth, something bitter to drink, a place to hang wet clothes, and precise instructions about where not to step. Food may arrive without ceremony: smoked fish, soft cheese wrapped in leaves, mushrooms cooked with onions, black bread, stewed gooseberries, pond greens, hazelnut paste, duck eggs, cider sharp enough to sting the gums. Salt is precious. Honey is festive. Clear water from the first basin is the highest courtesy.

They are suspicious of large wagons, foundations dug too deep, straight roads, and anyone who claims a permanent solution. Plain-dwellers sometimes call them improvident because their buildings lean and their boundaries move. The townspeople, in return, consider a rigid structure evidence that its builder has misunderstood time.

Strangers often find them argumentative. They bargain noisily, correct one another in public, and remember minor trespasses for decades. Yet a cry of broken water ends quarrels immediately. Tools appear. Ropes are brought. Those with strong backs go to the wall; those with quick hands weave brush; children carry messages; elders direct from stable ground. No one asks first whose terrace is failing, because every collapse acquires a downhill owner soon enough.

The animals participate in this society without consenting to it.

The black pigs are half livestock, half weather instruments. When they abandon low ground and crowd onto steps, people inspect the channels. Ducks are kept in named flocks but mingle freely and must be sorted by clipped feathers, leg cords, or the shapes cut into their bills. Goats are shameless roof trespassers. Dogs know the town vertically: a dog may leave by a door, cross two bridges, descend through a workshop, swim a pond, and appear on the roof of the same house before its owner has finished shouting.

Cats live nearest to independence. They enter through smoke gaps and root hollows, sleep in basket stores, and are tolerated because they keep rats from the grain platforms. Some households insist a particular cat belongs to them. The cat’s behavior generally refutes this.

Pigeons are claimed communally while flying and privately once inside a loft, a distinction that produces endless accusations. Their droppings feed the gardens. Their feathers fill bedding. Their sudden flights warn of hawks, falling branches, unfamiliar dogs, and sometimes nothing at all.

Among the wild inhabitants, frogs possess the strongest customary protection. Children collect them but must return them. A person who deliberately kills frogs in a water store is believed to invite fever and is also likely to invite mosquitoes, so superstition and observation reinforce each other. Certain large, pale frogs inhabiting the oldest spring basins are said to be older than the carved stone. The stewards deny this while making sure none are disturbed.

Herons are treated with wary respect and personal resentment. They take fish from any pond they can reach and stand beyond thrown stones with an air of legal immunity. Individual birds are recognized by damaged feathers or crooked toes and given unflattering names.

There are also beings less easily placed.

In the upper wood, where fog moves between the trunks, rootwrights sometimes find woven switches they did not bend. Channel keepers hear knocking in culverts after water has been shut off. Children report small, mud-colored people sitting beneath boardwalks, combing duckweed from their hair. Adults say these are voles, echoes, or lies—then leave a heel of bread near a newly repaired sluice.

No doctrine governs such things. The townspeople have too much daily experience of hidden movement to insist that everything concealed is unreal. Walls breathe with beetles. Roofs walk with snails. Water travels beneath floors and emerges three terraces away. A voice behind leaves may belong to a neighbor, a jay, a child, or something for which no useful decision is required. The correct response is usually the same: announce yourself, do not block the channel, and leave the place no worse than you found it.

At night, households gather in rooms that smell of wet wool, smoke, apples, and warm clay. Lamps burn fish oil or tallow. People mend because nearly everything needs mending. Stories are told while hands continue working.

Their heroes are not conquerors. They are the woman who opened a bank and saved the lower quarter, though her own orchard washed away; the child who crawled through a blocked culvert with a rope; the rootwright who spent forty years leading a bridge from one side of a ravine to the other; the miser whose hoarded iron became nails after his death; the fool who roofed his house too steeply and woke to find all his goats in the bedroom.

The stories change with each telling. So do the houses in which they are told.

By dawn, the inhabitants emerge before the sun reaches the bottom of the bowl. They test steps with their feet. They inspect gutters, count ducks, empty traps, recover tools left under awnings, and assess what the night has altered. They do not expect the town to remain as it was. Permanence would seem less like safety than neglect.

What they preserve is not shape but relationship: water to channel, branch to brace, roof to root, household to neighbor, living to dead, appetite to renewal. Their home survives because they do not demand that it stop changing.

And so do they.
