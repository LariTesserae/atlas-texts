At first they are difficult to distinguish from what they have built.

A watcher looking down into a court sees gray walls, patched roofs, a stack of fuel cakes, and several bundled shapes sitting motionless in the sun. Only when the light leaves the stones do the shapes rise. One unfolds into an old woman winding wool from a spindle. Another proves to be a child with a pigeon tucked inside their coat. A third is not a person at all, but a long-haired goat chewing frost from its beard.

The people of the plateau are generally compact in build, though not uniformly small: deep through the chest, thick in the shoulders, with strong calves from stairs and steep ground. They walk without hurry. Lowlanders mistake this for solemnity until they try to keep pace and discover that the highlanders have learned never to spend breath twice. They do not call across a courtyard if they can descend to speak. They do not run except after animals, children, or water. When labor demands force, it is applied in rhythm—three pulls, a pause; six hammer strokes, a pause—so that lungs and hands tire together.

Cold gives them a layered appearance. Closest to the skin they wear soft felt or finely worked hide, then wool wrapped and belted rather than closely tailored. Outer garments are broad, stiff with grease at the shoulders, and easily opened to the sun. Sleeves extend over the fingers. Hoods rise high around the cheeks, leaving narrow weathered faces in shadow. Many wear veils of woven hair or thin leather against blown ice. Their boots have wide soles and removable linings; in old districts, where roofs and stairs are perpetually glazed, soles are bound with knotted rawhide for grip.

Clothing records a household’s animals. One family wears mostly black goat wool, another the brown-gray of upland sheep, another pale felt traded from the northern basins. Repairs accumulate until a coat becomes a genealogy of small pieces. A dead grandparent’s sleeve may survive as a child’s hood; a wedding mantle may end as boot linings, then window seals, then felt stuffing between roof beams. Nothing disappears merely because its first use has ended.

Their faces are marked less by a single ancestry than by the plateau itself. Skin darkens and roughens where wind reaches it. Eyes remain narrowed outdoors and water in the glare of snow. Lips split in winter and are greased with rendered fat, wax, or aromatic resin. Hair is commonly worn in tight braids, cords, or felted coils so that it will not snarl in the wind. Adults often shave or pluck a strip above the forehead where hood edges rub. Travelers from other basins can sometimes be recognized by the way they bind their hair, knot their belts, or fold the earflaps of their caps.

Infants are carried close beneath clothing, against a parent’s or older sibling’s body. They learn the city first as warmth, heartbeat, wool smell, and the muffled sound of feet on stairs. When they begin to walk, a loop of cord is often fastened around the waist—not as a leash in ordinary streets, but for roofs, bridges, cistern mouths, and cliffside paths. Children grow up knowing where a careless step kills. They also grow up among animals and acquire their footing early. A five-year-old may be trusted to cross an icy roof with a basket of dung chips while an adult lowlander is still crawling on hands and knees.

They are not quiet by nature. Quiet is a practical art.

Speech within a room can be quick, teasing, and full of interruption. At feasts, people sing until the rafters sweat. But outside, breath is measured and wind carries secrets unpredictably. Plateau languages favor close conversation: many soft consonants, short phrases, and meanings completed by the eyes or hands. There are extensive gestures for use across noisy waterworks or between separated roofs—open palm for release, closed fist for hold, two fingers split for divide the flow, a circling hand for animal trouble, both arms lowered for death.

Whistled signals travel farther than voices. Every district has its own patterns for fire, collapse, missing child, escaped herd, and sudden water. A stranger may hear three thin notes passed from ridge to ridge and see no reaction, while behind walls shutters are already being closed against a storm.

No one simply belongs to the city. They belong to a catchment, and within it to a chain of obligations.

A person introduces themselves by naming the water that raised them: the snow fence above a certain ridge, the spring under a certain shrine, the seventh cistern of a particular stair. Household names change when people marry or move; water names endure. Those born during drought may be named for endurance, shade, clay, or the patience of buried ice. Those born in wet years receive more extravagant names: Overflow, Silver Channel, Three Rains, Marsh-Lark.

Kinship is broad because survival requires it. A household may contain blood relatives, spouses from several basins, foster children, widowed herders, apprentices, and elderly people whose original quarters have collapsed or emptied. Membership is demonstrated through maintenance. Someone who clears the same channel year after year becomes kin in a way that a distant cousin who contributes nothing does not. Adoption often occurs publicly at a cistern: the new member pours a measured cup into the household jar and receives one back.

Authority belongs to those who can remember consequences.

The most respected figures are often old women and men who no longer perform the heaviest work but know how the neighborhood behaves under strain: which wall sweats before thaw, which lower court floods if the western spillway is opened, which spring tastes bitter after tremors, which family will deny taking more than its share. Water assessors carry marked rods and nested cups, but their true instruments are stories. They can recite the droughts of five generations and the quarrels produced by each one.

They are not rulers in the manner of lowland kings. They may be mocked, contradicted, or removed. Yet when one of them kneels beside a channel and places a cup beneath the flow, conversation stops. Measurement is among the plateau’s few sacred acts.

Children learn fractions through water before they learn them through trade. Half a run, a quarter jar, three households to one night gate. They learn to read scratches on sluice stones and knots on inspection cords. The best water keepers possess an exact bodily sense of volume. They can hear when a cistern is filling differently by the pitch of drops in darkness.

Other professions are similarly inseparable from the land. Builders are part mason, part geologist, part historian. Before adding a room, they examine old cracks, taste mineral dust, listen with an ear against the wall while someone strikes the far side, and ask what stood there before anyone living was born. They distrust straight lines extending too far. A long wall is made to yield in sections; a stair is allowed to settle independently from the houses beside it. Their finest work may appear irregular to outsiders because its elegance lies in how it fails: outward rather than inward, slowly rather than at once, one replaceable piece before the whole.

Rope makers command similar respect. Fiber is life on steep ground. A rope lifts stone, lowers a rescuer, restrains an animal, braces a roof, measures a channel, and secures a corpse against funeral winds. Good rope is inspected strand by strand. Families mark their work with distinctive splices, so that a cord found generations later may still be attributed.

Healers keep warm rooms near springs, dung stores, or crowded animal pens. They treat frost injury, snow blindness, infected cracks of the hands, broken ankles, smoke sickness, and the headaches of visitors who climb too quickly. Their remedies are modest and exact: hot stones wrapped in felt, bitter lichens, juniper smoke, rendered fats, fermented milk, salt, rest, darkness, and controlled descent. They know that some illnesses belong not to the body but to altitude, cold, hunger, or grief. For these they prescribe a different room, a different elevation, or a season with relatives beside another water.

Food is dense, hot when possible, and rarely elaborate in fuel. Grain is parched before storage so that it can be ground and eaten with little cooking. It becomes thick porridge, dumplings dropped into broth, or hard journey cakes softened under the tongue. Milk is soured, dried, churned, or frozen into winter stores. Meat is used sparingly in ordinary meals and lavishly when an animal dies at the proper season. Fat is prized above lean flesh. Blood is collected. Organs are eaten quickly. Bones go into broth before passing to the toolmaker.

People carry small lidded cups inside their coats. Hospitality begins with water, but never with an unmeasured amount. A host pours enough to acknowledge a guest, not enough to display waste. The second offering is usually something warm and salted. The greatest generosity is not a full cup but permission to remain beside the hearth through the night.

There are few open fires. Families gather around clay stoves, heated benches, braziers shielded beneath pierced covers, or stones warmed in communal ovens. Smoke and scarcity have shaped their social lives. Several households may bake together, brew together, or heat bathwater in sequence. Privacy is found not in isolation but in custom: a turned bowl means do not interrupt; a cord across a stair means mourning; boots placed outward beside a door mean the household is awake but receiving no one.

Their religion, where it can be separated from law and maintenance, is concerned with passage.

They attend to things that cross boundaries: snow becoming water, water entering stone, breath becoming cloud, the dead becoming food for birds, an animal becoming clothing, an old street becoming a cellar. They do not sharply divide the living city from the buried one. Ancestors are understood to remain beneath the household, not as ghosts continually watching but as weight, warmth, precedent, and obligation. When a floor is opened and an older doorway discovered, people may leave a cup of water there before sealing it again.

Shrines are small and numerous. Some occupy cracks where warm air rises. Some consist of a stone darkened by generations of touch. Others hold the horn of an exceptional herd animal, a piece of fallen iron, a bowl that has never been allowed to dry, or a bundle of cords cut from people rescued during a collapse. Grand temples exist in a few old basins, but even these are built around practical mysteries: an unfailing spring, a vent that breathes in winter, a rock face that gathers rime when the surrounding walls remain bare.

No one assumes the earth is still. Stability is treated as a temporary courtesy.

After tremors, residents emerge without shouting and stand in courtyards listening. They listen for falling stone, running water, animals under floors, and the tiny continuous sounds that mean a wall is still moving. Every household keeps digging boards, wedges, cord, lamps, and dust cloths near an exit. Children rehearse where to crouch. Goats and dogs are watched for unease, though no one agrees whether they truly foresee movement or merely hear it first.

Death by collapse is feared, but not considered unnatural. More troubling is death far from one’s water, where the proper household may not hear of it before thaw. Messengers therefore carry tokens: belt knots, horn toggles, braid rings, tool marks. Identity rests in worked things as much as in faces.

The dead are seldom buried deeply in ordinary soil, because soil is thin, frozen, or needed for cultivation. Customs vary by basin. Some bodies are placed on high stone platforms for cliff birds. Some are folded into old cisterns that will never again hold water. Some are sealed in wall chambers with a cup, a cord, and a fragment of household roof. Flesh may return to animals; bones, after weathering, may be gathered beneath the house. There is no contradiction in mourning the dead and allowing ravens to know the funeral paths.

Ravens are tolerated with wary respect. Pigeons are domestic opportunists, living half under human care and half beyond it. Their eggs and droppings are collected; their sudden flight warns of predators or falling masonry. Small birds nesting in walls are believed to keep cracks from becoming lonely. Children sometimes know individual birds by markings and feed them crumbs despite adult complaints.

The relationship with herd animals is more intimate and less sentimental. People assist at births, sleep beside sick animals, sing to them during storms, and slaughter them when needed. A valuable pack beast may have a name, lineage, decorated halter, and legal standing in compensation disputes. Sheep are recognized by ear cuts and coat patterns. Goats recognize doors and can become accomplished thieves, lifting hide latches with their horns. Dogs are bred for judgment rather than size: quiet animals that can separate strays, detect unstable snow, and lead a breathless person home through cloud.

Herders move through the city as its circulating blood. They arrive smelling of wool grease, snow, smoke, and open ground. They carry news between basins that otherwise share little. Their seasonal departures are watched carefully, for the timing of migration predicts pasture and thaw. Some spend most of their lives beyond roofs, sleeping in low hide shelters and navigating by ridges invisible to city dwellers. They may regard settled people as soft, though their own families usually occupy rooms in some crowded quarter and maintain rights to a cistern there.

There is no single plateau people. Across its breadth, dialects shift, gods acquire different names, roof shapes change, and strangers disagree over whether fermented milk should be sour, smoky, or nearly solid. Some districts descend from quarry settlements; others from herding camps, exiles, caravan stations, shrine communities, or refugees from the lower valleys. There are neighborhoods where nearly everyone works stone, neighborhoods known for rope, districts of hide dressers, pigeon keepers, metal repairers, bone carvers, singers, midwives, and keepers of underground storehouses.

What unites them is not ancestry but a common discipline: nothing useful moves without leaving an account of where it came from and where it must go next.

This applies to water most strictly, but also to dung, straw, bone, labor, shelter, and news. Waste is treated with disgust not because it is dirty, but because it breaks continuity. The shameful person is one who ends a chain: who lets meltwater vanish into a fouled drain, burns sound timber for a moment’s comfort, discards a repairable shoe, withholds warning of a cracked wall, or allows meat to spoil while neighbors hunger.

Their beauty follows the same ethic. Ornament is rarely without function. Bright braid reinforces a seam. Carved channels make door lintels shed water. Bone inlay strengthens the rim of a cup. Roof edges are patterned by alternating dark and pale stones that reveal displacement after tremors. A bride’s belt may display the colors of two watersheds joined, but its pendants are also needles, scrapers, fire strikers, and measuring spoons.

Metal ornaments are especially charged. A small iron bead may pass through twenty generations, no longer recognizable as part of any tool. Bronze fragments are worn smooth by handling. To give metal is to surrender stored possibility: the future knife, rivet, drill point, or surgical edge it could have become. Such gifts are made at marriages, reconciliations, adoptions, and the settlement of grave water offenses.

Their celebrations are warmer and more crowded than an outsider expects. Since open plazas are exposed, festivals spread through connected rooms and roofs. Food travels hand to hand. Drums are shallow frames of hide; flutes are bone or reed; singers use close harmonies that thicken in low ceilings until a small room seems enormous. Dancing is compact, built from stamping, shoulder turns, linked elbows, and intricate changes of weight. In summer it moves onto the roofs. In winter, it shakes dust from ancient beams.

Humor is dry, persistent, and often directed at certainty. Stories ridicule kings who order water uphill, merchants who carry useless luxuries but forget boot felt, and priests who mistake a blocked drain for divine displeasure. Builders tell jokes about straight walls. Herders joke about builders who cannot recognize weather until it enters the house. Every basin mocks the accents of its neighbors.

Visitors often remember the people as severe because negotiations are slow, measurements exact, and welcome initially restrained. Those who remain longer discover a society full of small indulgences: children warming beetles in their hands, old men keeping useless bright feathers, women cultivating tiny flowers in cracked bowls, lovers meeting in disused stair towers, workers setting aside the smoothest stones simply because they feel good beneath the thumb.

At sunset the inhabitants become more visible. Doors open to release cooking vapor. People carry heat stones home from ovens. Inspectors return from the channels. Herds compress themselves into pens and lanes. Pigeons crowd the vents; dogs take the outer stairs; ravens settle where funeral platforms catch the last light. Across thousands of districts, lamps appear one by one behind horn, hide, oiled cloth, and thin stone.

No lamp shines very far. None needs to.

The life of the plateau is not maintained by a great central fire, a palace, or a single commanding will. It persists as warmth persists in its masonry: held in innumerable chambers, passed from body to body, conserved through the long cold, and released only in careful measure.
