The first inhabitants one notices are not the largest but the ones nearest the skin of the city.

They live in the damp rind of things: translucent mites among moss stems, silver springtails on the undersides of stones, pale woodlice curled in the softened mortar. Minute snails graze the green film from inscriptions, following grooves cut by hands they could neither perceive nor survive. Beetle larvae tunnel where plaster has become chalky and roots have made it fibrous. In the wetter joints, tiny crabs brace themselves against the masonry with violet claws, retreating so deeply at a footfall that only a stirred bead of water shows where they were.

Many of these creatures are the color of their particular wall. There are moths like old limewash, mantises mottled like lichen, spiders whose abdomens resemble chips of blackened mosaic. They do not hide against the ruins so much as complete their surfaces. When they move, the stone seems briefly to crawl.

The pools have their own nations. Each cistern is an island turned inward, separated from the next by a few walls and, sometimes, centuries. One contains black tadpoles that rasp algae from submerged steps. Another holds thin, nearly colorless fish with broad fins, descended from stream fish swept through the drainage works during some ancient flood. In the darkest reservoirs their eyes are small and clouded, and they find insect larvae by following tremors along the water. They lay adhesive eggs beneath fallen tiles and survive dry intervals in the deepest cracks, where the water remains no more than a cool black ribbon.

The cistern frogs are more various still. Some are small enough to sit in a bromeliad cup. Some are heavy, long-legged animals that spend the day wedged into carved mouths where the stone stays damp. Their calls have changed to suit the chambers that contain them. Frogs in narrow shafts produce abrupt metallic notes; those in domed baths give low calls that roll around the ceiling and seem to come from every direction. Neighboring populations may be separated by only a courtyard, yet answer in different pitches, each shaped by the resonance of its enclosure.

After rain they travel. They climb overflow channels, cross flooded paving, and enter courts that were unreachable the day before. For a few hours the city becomes continuous to them. Then the water sinks, stranding eggs, adults, predators, and prey in new basins. Much of the life below the streets has spread through the ruins in this way: not by steady migration, but by brief watery rearrangements.

Above them are the ledge-dwellers.

Small lizards occupy the sunlit faces of towers. They are flat-bodied, long-toed, and quick enough to vanish into joints that appear too narrow to admit anything alive. Males display from worn reliefs, their throats flashing blue or amber against the pale stone. Insects rise from the canopy and strike the warm façades; the lizards catch them there, then retreat before the afternoon cloud removes the heat.

Snakes follow. The smallest are no thicker than roots and spend much of their lives inside drainage slots, feeding on geckos, frogs, nestlings, and sleeping bats. Larger ones lie along cornices where vines disguise the curve of their bodies. They know the regular passages of other animals. A polished lip on a stair, a branch touching a gallery, a hole used by swifts—each is a place where patience eventually becomes food.

The birds divide the city by altitude, light, and cavity size.

Swiftlets own the high interiors by day. They enter through broken windows at speed, turning through columns and roofless chambers without touching stone. In the deepest galleries they navigate by sharp clicks, their calls stitching momentary outlines through the darkness. Their nests cling to vaults in crowded shelves. Generations use the same chambers until the floors below are banked with guano, feathers, shells, dead insects, and the pale growths that feed on all four.

Parrots belong to the fruiting districts. Their flocks move noisily from fig tower to fig tower, carrying seeds in their crops and pulp on their bills. They are among the principal gardeners of the upper city. A parapet where parrots habitually gather becomes first a place of droppings, then a strip of weeds, then a thicket. Their preferred perches can determine which walls remain exposed to weather and which disappear beneath roots.

Quieter birds inhabit the understory streets. Long-legged antbirds follow raiding columns through halls and plazas, taking roaches, spiders, and larvae driven from the litter. Small dark thrushes nest in statue niches. Nightjars rest lengthwise on balustrades, indistinguishable from broken stone until their eyes open. On the meadow roofs above the canopy, compact mountain birds build nests between the uprights, using grass, moss, monkey hair, and strands pulled from old bat roosts.

The bats arrive in layers.

Tiny insect-eaters pack themselves into cracks near the upper vaults, where the evening air is open and cool. Broad-winged fruit bats hang lower, close to the canopy routes that lead between feeding trees. In buried chambers live slower bats with large ears and soft, probing flight. They glean moths from walls and pluck frogs from cistern margins. Each species leaves its own depth of darkness beneath it: thin dustings of droppings, wet black heaps, floors alive with beetles, pale fungi rising and collapsing in cycles.

At dusk they issue from the city in separate currents. The smallest emerge first, slipping through arrow slits and broken screens. Then the fruit bats push out from broad windows, their bodies briefly blotting the cloud-bright sky. Last come the deep-chamber hunters, moving low through streets and beneath arches. From a distance the departures look like smoke escaping from many levels of a mountain.

The monkeys are the most conspicuous residents, though even they possess only a fraction of the place.

They are lean, dark-limbed animals with long balancing tails and hands toughened by stone. The oldest individuals know routes invisible from below: a sequence of leaning columns, root bridges, surviving roof beams, and vines stout enough to bear weight. Troops can cross several districts without descending to the leaf-filled streets. Infants learn the city as a chain of gestures—jump here, duck there, do not put weight on that slab, wait before crossing the exposed ridge.

Their days follow the moving patches of food. At first light they raid flowers on the upper terraces. Later they descend into figs and palm crowns. During rain they shelter in galleries, grooming one another on dry ledges while water sheets down outside. They crack nuts against particular steps, and generations of use leave shallow cups in the stone. Some troops drop hard fruit onto roofs to split it, producing blows that travel through the chambers below like distant collapse.

They are not builders, but they modify their paths. They break obstructing twigs, pull loose vines into reach, and repeatedly leap from the same takeoff points. Seeds pass through them and germinate beneath their sleeping sites. Their roads become rows of fruiting trees. In this way their memory is written into the vegetation, though no individual understands the pattern.

There are larger animals, but they are seldom seen whole. A small forest cat moves through the middle terraces, leaving round prints in cistern silt and parrot feathers beneath arches. Thick-bodied herbivores enter the lower avenues at night to eat fallen fruit. Shaggy, long-snouted omnivores turn over the leaf mold on buried plazas, exposing tiles and insect chambers before the next rain smooths their work. Now and then something heavy passes through a distant arcade, visible only as a sequence of trembling leaves and startled birds.

No great predator reigns here. Hunting is intimate and opportunistic. A snake waits at a drain. A bat takes a moth from a column. A frog swallows a beetle beside a pool in which fish are eating its young. Ants overrun a nestling fallen from a gallery; antbirds follow the ants; a cat follows the antbirds. Death occurs continuously, mostly out of sight, and is incorporated quickly. Fur becomes nest lining. Bone vanishes under fungi. Shells dissolve into wet mortar. Guano feeds roots that loosen stones and make new cavities for the descendants of the animals that produced it.

Some inhabitants live almost entirely on the city’s accumulated life.

There are blind crickets in the debris beneath floors, feeding on fungal threads and bat remains. Pale cockroaches browse the damp bindings of root and plaster. Termites build covered roads up columns to reach timber buried in collapsed roofs. Their clay galleries resemble new veins laid over old carvings. Predatory beetles enter those galleries through breaches; frogs wait at the breaches for both beetles and termites. In the oldest guano beds, entire communities never see daylight and respond to the outer world only when rainwater seeps down or the roost overhead grows suddenly restless.

The plants, too, behave less like decoration than inhabitants with long, slow appetites.

Figs begin as seeds in cracks high above the ground. They send down roots that search the façade, splitting and rejoining around windows, hanging freely through halls, thickening wherever they touch damp stone. A mature tree may stand nowhere in the ordinary sense. Its crown occupies one roof, its trunk is distributed across several walls, and its roots descend through rooms to a buried court. Animals sleep, feed, mate, and hunt at different heights within the same individual.

Bromeliads make elevated ponds. Mosquitoes breed in them; frogs lay eggs there; drowned insects feed larvae; snakes inspect them for frogs. Orchids time their flowers to short bright intervals after the cloud lifts, and bees travel along exposed terraces in routes as regular as streets. Moss forests, only finger-deep, hold grazing snails, mites, eggs, spores, and the young of creatures that will later descend into larger worlds.

Fungi bind much of this together. They bloom from guano, dead wood, old nests, and the buried mats of leaves. Fine white threads run through mortar dust and root fiber. Bracket fungi protrude from doors that are now mostly soil. During the wettest nights, small luminous bodies appear in the lower courts, not bright enough to illuminate the chambers but numerous enough to confuse their dimensions. Beetles move among them carrying spores on their legs.

No single creature knows the settlement as a city. Each inhabits a different version of it.

To a frog it is a scattered chain of resonant wells joined briefly by rain. To a swiftlet it is a volume of dark air pierced by entrances. To a monkey it is a remembered route through crowns, ridges, and handholds. To a blind fish it is one pool and the trembling ceiling of that pool. To a fig it is a descending opportunity, stone holding moisture all the way down. To the termites it is food separated by mineral obstacles. To the bats it is shelter that opens nightly onto a basin full of insects.

Together, however, their separate maps overlap. Calls pass where bodies cannot. Seeds cross boundaries in stomachs and fur. Floodwater carries eggs between courts. Predators learn the roads of prey. Roots connect roofs to buried chambers. Droppings from creatures of the open air feed organisms that have never encountered the sky.

What lives there is not a hidden successor to the vanished builders. It is a multitude trained by borrowed forms.

Bodies have flattened to enter joints. Calls have altered to fit domes. Feet have learned wet stairs, sheer walls, moss, bark, and tile. Nesting seasons follow the filling of cisterns. Feeding routes follow parapets laid out for forgotten reasons. Instinct has bent itself around architecture until the distinction between adaptation to mountain and adaptation to city no longer matters.

The inhabitants do not restore the place, nor do they finish destroying it. They keep it permeable. They move seeds into towers, nutrients into pools, spores into vaults, blood into predators, and old stone gradually into living tissue. Their abundance is not orderly, but it is settled. Every cavity is a climate, every height a province, every wet interval a migration.

The city’s population is therefore never assembled in one view. It reveals itself as eyes in a doorway, claws on a cornice, a throat swelling beside black water, a thousand wings leaving a tower, a root tightening by a fraction around a wall. What lives there is the continuous exchange among all these things: a civilization without citizens, distributed through fur, leaf, scale, feather, chitin, mycelium, and stone.
