At first there seem to be no inhabitants.

The open flats disclose nothing that crosses them. Pools remain untroubled except by rain and the slow contraction of their rims. No small body darts when a membrane lifts; no eye withdraws into a crevice. Even the towers, which from a distance suggest colonies or monumental nests, contain only wind, water, and their own branching anatomy.

Closer examination does not restore the familiar world. It only changes the scale at which life must be sought.

The glossy hide of the plains is divided into low polygonal territories, each several meters across. A territory brightens, swells, drinks, and darkens as a unit. Along its edges run narrow bands of contractile tissue that draw the rain seams shut after floods. At the center are translucent pores, ordinarily mistaken for puddles, which open in coordinated spirals to exchange gases with the chambers below. When sunlight passes over the plain, these territories respond one after another. A faint paling travels across the plateau—not a shadow but a sequence of surfaces turning themselves toward the light.

Each territory could be called an individual. Cut free, it seals its margins and may remain alive for months. Given water and sufficient surface, it begins laying pale ribs beneath itself and may eventually produce a small raft.

Yet no territory remains truly separate. Fine conductive threads cross every seam. Sugars move from districts in sunlight toward districts under cloud. Chemical alarms travel outward from tears. A drying plate may be abandoned by its neighbors, its contents withdrawn until it becomes a weightless husk; another may be nourished long after its own photosynthetic surface has failed because its position is structurally important. Independence exists here, but only temporarily, like the independence of a finger.

Beneath these plates dwell the oldest living parts of the country.

They are not roots in the ordinary sense. They are pale, blind cables made from successive generations of fused growth. Some are dead at the center and alive only in concentric sheaths. Others contain slow rivers of sap, colonies of translucent cells drifting along their walls, attaching where reinforcement is needed. These wandering cells have no limbs and almost no fixed shape. Pressure carries them through the plateau. At damaged sites they gather, flatten, and begin secreting fiber. In a few hours they become a plug; in a season, a buttress. They are among the nearest things the plateau has to mobile inhabitants.

In the warm inner chambers live films of microbes so thoroughly domesticated that none can survive outside the tissues that contain them. They digest drowned layers, separate useful salts from windborne dust, and reduce failed structures to liquids the conduits can reclaim. Different chambers maintain different films: rust-colored ones in oxygenated cisterns, gray velvet in the deepest cavities, luminous green smears beneath skins thin enough to admit daylight.

They do not spread freely. The chamber walls feed them, prune them with antiseptic sap, and close passages when one film grows too vigorous. They are livestock only in the broadest sense, though there is no keeper. They are also gut, compost, immune system, and ancestry. Their lives take place entirely inside another life.

Above them, the monumental forms are less like trees than temporary generations of organs.

The hollow trunks that sing in the wind begin as soft uprisings on the plain. For several wet seasons they are fed by surrounding plates, growing without contributing much in return. Their walls separate into flutes. Their interiors are hollowed by controlled dissolution. Only when they reach sufficient height do their upper membranes unfold and begin condensing mist. Then water descends through them, and the districts that raised them are repaid.

A tower may serve for decades. During that time its uppermost edges are continually young. Thin green fingers extend into the air, touch neighboring fingers, and fuse into arches. Some fail and are absorbed. Some thicken into load-bearing ribs. Others remain as sensitive streamers that register changes in humidity and wind direction. The tower slowly reshapes itself around their reports.

No mind stands at its center. Its decisions are distributed through strain. A rib under dangerous tension releases a chemical that softens growth on one side and stiffens it on the other. A chamber resonating too strongly in the wind closes some apertures and widens others. Sections that receive no stress and transport no water cease to be fed. The finished-looking structure is merely the portion still being argued over.

The great hydraulic flowers are shorter-lived inhabitants. They rise abruptly, consume stored water, and often collapse within a single season. Their thick petals are reservoirs lined with heat-dark tissue. At noon they spread open, and the air above them trembles. Sap climbs through the warmed central column; cooler liquid descends around it. At night the petals fold over the remaining water, making sealed warm chambers in which pressure and dissolved gases can be adjusted.

Some flowers produce dust-fine reproductive bodies. These are not seeds so much as packets of compatibility: dry curls containing genes, signaling compounds, and a little adhesive food. Wind carries them across the open distances. Most are caught by wet surfaces and digested. A few land upon receptive seams, dissolve, and alter the growth there.

Thus one district enters another without replacing it.

This exchange helps explain the repeated proportions without requiring the plateau to be a single organism. It is neither one plant nor a forest. It is a federation of lineages that have grafted together so completely that their borders are expressed mainly as disagreements in form. One lineage favors narrow channels; another grows red load-bearing fibers; another responds quickly to wind but poorly to drought. Their boundaries migrate. A successful pattern passes through the shared tissues as living instruction, copied not by thought but by growth.

In some places those negotiations become visible. A ridge may have one flank pleated and the other smooth. Channels approach from opposite sides at different angles and meet in a knot of circular pools. Towers occasionally begin with two incompatible curves, producing a twisted lower frame before one growth rule prevails. The landscape’s apparent style is the compromise of innumerable vegetative selves.

The closest things to wanderers are found at fresh wounds.

When an escarpment unzips, tiny pale bodies emerge in the clear fluid beading on the face. They resemble grains of rice made from gel. Each is a compact cluster of unspecialized tissue wrapped in a slippery coat. They are extruded by the thousands, creep only by swelling and contracting against the wet wall, and collect in the deepest part of the tear. There they merge. Their boundaries disappear, and the resulting sheet greens in the light.

For a few hours each cluster behaves as an autonomous creature: avoiding excessive heat, adhering to fibers, consuming stored sugars, and following chemical gradients toward damage. Once joined, it becomes indistinguishable from the repair. Nothing here keeps its creaturehood for long.

Even reproduction occurs chiefly as catastrophe.

A raft section peeling from the plateau is not necessarily dead. As it falls into the cloud depths, its exposed ribs close around stored cisterns. Outer surfaces curl inward. Towers break away, sacrificing height to reduce drag. Long roots trail beneath the fragment, collecting moisture and airborne dust. If the fragment catches upon another living shelf—or remains buoyed in the dense wet air against an unseen updraft—it may continue independently. Its severed conduits reorganize, and its former edge becomes a new center.

Such fragments are the plateau’s offspring: valleys, marshes, ridges, and weather systems born together in a single enormous cutting.

Smaller propagules also drift from the latticed towers. These are hollow green bladders, nearly weightless, released during strong upward winds. They contain water, microbial films, and a folded disc of surface tissue. They have no means of steering. Many return to the parent plain and are absorbed. Others vanish over the escarpments. A bladder that lodges on a damp aerial root adheres, flattens, and begins to spread. For years it may remain a mere hanging leaf. Given time, it can become a platform capable of bearing pools.

There are deaths, though few corpses.

A district that loses its conduits pales from green to ivory. Its pores cease opening. Neighboring tissues form a seal beneath it, and digestive fluid rises through the dead plate. The surface subsides as its inner fibers liquefy. For a while a thin roof remains, brittle and resonant, covering a warm hollow. Rain eventually breaks it. The fragments are drawn down and vanish.

Only pieces torn wholly away escape this reclamation. They descend into the clouds like wreckage: curled plates, snapped spars, empty cisterns. Whether anything lives below to receive them is not evident from the plateau. From above, they simply disappear.

The inhabitant of this country therefore changes according to the distance from which it is regarded.

At close range there are responsive cells, microbial films, repair bodies, pores, valves, and grafting fronts. Farther back there are plates that feed or abandon one another, flowers that regulate heat, towers that harvest weather, and lineages contesting the shape of watersheds. From the horizon there is only the plateau, behaving with a coherence none of its parts possesses alone.

It has no animal awareness hidden in its depths. It does not watch the weather or intend its architecture. But neither is it passive. Sensation is spread through pressure, chemistry, hydration, and light. Memory persists as successful curves, favored branch angles, old grafts, and tissues predisposed to split where previous structures survived by splitting.

Its thoughts, if the word can be tolerated, are ridges.

Its reflexes are spillways.

Its immune responses are cliffs closing green over a wound.

And its inhabitants are forever becoming its anatomy: brief local lives arising from the common fabric, performing some act of capture, repair, exchange, or support, then fusing back into the country until nothing remains that can be pointed to and called the creature apart from the place.
