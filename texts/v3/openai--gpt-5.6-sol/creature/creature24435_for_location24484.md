At first it seems that only the scattered plants live here. They are the obvious inhabitants: the wire grass with roots clenched in pockets of silt, the blunt-leaved herbs beside the seep, the shrubs holding their foliage at the ends of bare stems. Each survives separately, with no traffic between them. Their flowers, when they appear, are small and self-fertile. Their seeds fall close. Their roots descend farther than their height above the ground would suggest, following damp seams through the limestone.

But they are not the basin’s oldest life.

That lives within the stone.

It forms no continuous body that a person could uncover and point to. It occupies hairline fissures, the undersides of ledges, the porous margins of the pools, and the dark northern seam from which the water comes. In one place it appears as the muted green stain in the deepest pocket. Elsewhere it is colorless. A stone broken open might show only a faint branching discoloration, finer than roots and too regular to be mineral veining. Left in the air, those threads would dull and vanish.

The organism grows as a colony of minute, interdependent cells. Near the surface, some contain a gray-green pigment and live on the weak daylight that enters the limestone. Deeper in, others draw their energy from dissolved minerals carried by the seep. Between them runs a slow exchange of water and simple nutrients. No part commands the rest. The colony has no center except, perhaps, the basin itself.

Its pace is almost beyond comparison with animal life. A wet week is an abundance. A dry season is not a crisis but a closing: the surface cells seal themselves beneath transparent mineral skins, and the deeper threads contract into damp pores. Years may pass in this state. When condensation returns, the colony opens again by degrees. What looks like a newly formed rim of pale crust around a pool is partly its residue—limestone precipitated through living membranes, laid down one nearly invisible layer at a time.

It does not move across the ground. It alters where movement is possible.

Water lingers slightly longer in stone occupied by it. Fine cracks widen, not by force but by centuries of mild chemical work. Silt catches in those cracks. The grasses root there. The herbs grow beside the seep because the colony has held a little damp after the visible water has gone. Even the shrubs, far from the pools, stand over buried branches of the same hidden system.

The plants are not its crop, nor is it their parasite. They are later lives gathered around conditions it has slowly made. Their dead roots provide traces of carbon; its mineral work releases what their living roots require. Neither depends entirely on the other, but after so long their boundaries have become difficult to separate.

The lichens on the ruined walls are its nearest relatives, or perhaps only its most exposed expression. They spread across stones once chosen and stacked by human hands, softening corners and filling joints. The colony beneath them does not distinguish wall from outcrop. To it, the enclosures are recent arrangements of familiar material: limestone lifted, turned, and set down again. It entered from below and from rain-darkened contact points. Over generations it has been making the walls geologically ordinary.

There are no animals hidden in the cracks. No pale beetles, no eyeless worms, no sleeping reptiles. The basin cannot support the expenditure of pursuit, flight, breeding seasons, or hunger. Its living things endure by needing almost nothing and by making no journeys.

Even reproduction is local. The stone-dweller divides where it is. Threads pinch apart as fissures close or fragments break away. Only during rare rain heavy enough to join the separate pools does it release dust-fine cells into the overflow. They travel a few handspans down the channels before the water disappears into gravel. Most perish. A few enter fresh pores and remain there, dormant, until another wet interval awakens them.

Thus the basin’s principal inhabitant is not seen entering or leaving. It has no footprint and makes no sound. It lives beneath the apparent emptiness as a loose, patient continuity—older than the surviving walls, perhaps older than any human use of the place.

The green pool is one glimpse of it. The mineral rims are another. So are the softened stones, the placement of damp-loving herbs, and the fact that the shallow basin remains spare without becoming wholly barren.

Nothing watches from the shelves. Nothing waits behind the ruins.

What lives here is the slowness itself, made cellular: a life spread thinly through warm limestone, drinking darkness and seep water, changing the basin by fractions, and requiring centuries to complete what elsewhere would count as a single season.
