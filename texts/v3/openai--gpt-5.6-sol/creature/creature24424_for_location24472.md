Nothing lives in the Low Country.

This remains true even when something crosses the plain.

At first it is usually mistaken for a rearrangement of distance: three dark uprights beneath the vapor roof, too narrow to be towers and too evenly spaced to be accidental. Later, one upright bends. Another shortens without descending. The third passes behind a cistern that should be much too small to conceal it and does not emerge from the other side.

Closer evidence is less convincing, not more. There may be wet impressions on the tiles, but they are never shaped like feet. They resemble the sockets left when posts are pulled from mortar. They occur in pairs, or in long alternating rows, or as a single mark repeated over several kilometers. Warm water fills them. Sometimes the water reflects a narrow figure continuing onward after the impressions have stopped.

These presences have no agreed form. The country does not permit them one.

Where angles are stable, they appear jointed: constructions of clay rods, hanging cords, wedges, shallow bowls, and slabs balanced edgewise. Where distance has begun to fail, they are tall enough to touch both the pavement and its far horizon. During reverse rain they may consist only of dry spaces moving through the upward drops. In the absolute-silence districts, they are heard but not seen—a slow sequence of knocks, scrapes, and tightening fibers passing nearby with the deliberation of someone carrying tools.

They are sometimes called the Levelers, although they do not always make things level.

They come to inequalities.

A Leveler will stand beside a canal whose two ends have begun to occupy the same place. It lowers something like a hand, though the hand may be a cluster of plumb bobs or a broad ceramic paddle impressed with fingerprints of many incompatible sizes. It touches the water. For a while nothing happens. Then one end of the canal remembers that it is lower, and the current resumes.

Another may crouch at a corner that has failed to become an angle. Its body folds with the simplicity of a wooden rule. It waits there through several rains. When it departs, the corner is sharp enough to cast a shadow. Often the Leveler leaves one of its own joints behind as a brace.

They are not repairers. Repair assumes damage, and very little in the Low Country has yet existed long enough to be damaged. They appear instead to assist decisions. They persuade a wall to choose one side. They hold a doorway at a single size until the surrounding rooms consent. They sit on newly raised arches so that weight may discover how to pass through them.

Sometimes they choose wrongly.

There are districts where a Leveler has fixed an error so thoroughly that it has become a law. Rain falls sideways there in every weather. A corridor remains exactly seven paces long no matter how fast or slowly it is crossed. Beneath one leaning gate, all loose objects acquire corners. Water passing through comes out in cubes, trembling but unable to round itself until carried beyond the gate’s shadow.

The Levelers show no distress at such results. Distress would be appetite turned backward, and appetite is forbidden here. They merely take measurements, if measurements are what they take, and continue.

Their instruments are parts of themselves until set down.

A taut mineral cord may unfasten from a Leveler’s chest and stretch from horizon to horizon. A bowl lifted from its head becomes a cistern. Fingers driven into soft paving harden as rows of posts. One may remove a long fired-clay tooth from its spine and fit it into a planetary wheel; after that, it walks with a gap that rain passes through.

This is how many of the country’s isolated objects are made. The lone stair, the wallless gate, the seven posts: these may not be abandoned constructions. They may be organs put aside.

Nor is it certain that a Leveler survives such acts. There are no births or deaths in the Low Country, but there are inventories. After storms, the components of a Leveler are sometimes found arranged on the pavement: twelve rods, two bowls, a coil of braided fiber, a pierced copper sheet, four wedges, and a quantity of water that refuses to run away. The arrangement may remain for years. Then, while no surface directly reflects it, the inventory is gone.

Pools reveal the Levelers differently.

In reflection they are neither enormous nor fragmentary. They stand at the water’s edge in the proportions of quiet workers. Their heads are hooded or unfinished. Their shoulders bear yokes from which simple tools hang. Their hands appear handlike, though always lowered, never reaching toward the observer. Behind them the reflected country is complete: canals sealed in pale ceramic, walls straight, roof bright, every mechanism still.

No face has been seen. Where a face should be, the reflected surface shows a small dark opening, like the mouth of a kiln after firing.

They are always numerous in reflections. The actual country may show one wandering construction in a month’s journey, while a hand-sized cup of rainwater shows hundreds standing shoulder to shoulder along a completed aqueduct. Some gaze down into the reflected water, and therefore seem to be looking upward into the unfinished world.

When the pool trembles, they do not vanish at once. They separate into their possible parts: a post, a cord, a vessel, a shadow, a fall of rain. Only then does the image break.

There are lesser presences as well, though “lesser” refers to duration rather than size.

Loose knots travel through the hidden plumbing before storms. They tighten around leaks, drag themselves by their free ends, and go still when uncovered. Hollow clay forms gather beneath dripping arches with their mouths turned upward. If one is removed, the others alter their spacing to preserve the shape of the group. Long seams of copper occasionally lift from the pavement and move in slow waves, carrying a red gleam from one mechanism to another.

These things are not young Levelers. Nothing here matures. They are brief solutions to local problems—habits the country forms and discards.

Sound can take on a similar tenancy. In low acoustic chambers, accumulated hammer blows compress until they acquire sequence and purpose. A knocking emerges from a doorway, crosses a court, pauses at sealed walls, and resumes on the far side. Sometimes it circles an unfinished tower for days, striking once at each course. Bricks settle after every blow, though nothing touches them.

Such a sound may accompany a Leveler, but it is impossible to know whether it is a signal, a tool, or the delayed noise of the Leveler’s own assembly centuries elsewhere.

The Levelers do not speak. They do not gather, except in water. They do not acknowledge one another when two pass across the same plain. Yet they sometimes cooperate across impossible distances. One raises an arm beside a southern measuring wall; nine years later, a wheel in the north advances one tooth. One enters a square well; a counterweight rises from a pit whose bottom contains the upper sky. Their gestures propagate through cords, pressure, reflection, and unfinished causality.

They seem most substantial during storms, when the country is least so.

Then dry silhouettes stride through horizontal rain. Vast hinged bodies brace the sides of canals while down changes direction. Figures kneel against inflating domes, their backs spread into temporary buttresses. Others walk into collapsing towers and come apart with them, sorting themselves among the bricks. In the steam, whole processions can be seen carrying slabs toward structures that have not yet appeared.

Afterward there are no tracks, only corrected gradients and unfamiliar tools.

Perhaps they are remnants of the builders. Perhaps they are the builders’ measurements continuing after the measurers have gone. Perhaps every one is a physical law that has not yet learned to operate without a body.

The distinction matters elsewhere. It does not matter here.

The Low Country admits repetition, balance, correction, and memory. It admits figures when a task requires a figure. But it allows no hunger, no offspring, no rot, no inward warmth. Thus its inhabitants cannot truly be alive. They can only persist as work persists: by passing from tool to structure, from structure to reflection, from reflection to water.

Very rarely, in a perfectly still pool, one reflected Leveler raises its head.

The finished country behind it remains motionless. The others continue their labor. The raised figure seems to attend not to the observer but to some noise arriving from beneath the reflected floor—a tiny clatter, impossibly delayed.

Then it steps into the water.

Nothing breaks the surface.

Somewhere in the real lowlands, a lone stair gains another tread.
