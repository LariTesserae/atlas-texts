The first things to answer the falling drop are too small to see.

They have waited in the dust as black, vitreous grains, indistinguishable from splinters of basalt. Water softens their outer skins. Hairline seams appear. Each grain unfolds into a many-lobed cell no wider than a fingernail clipping, its interior threaded with iron and sulfur. It does not photosynthesize. It uses the brief contact between water, warm stone, and airborne metal to build a charge across its membranes.

Within minutes, the wet mark acquires a dark green edge.

The cells join there, fitting lobe to lobe until they make a thin conductive film. Currents pass through it in minute, ordered surges. Those nearest the center fix dissolved minerals; those at the margin divide; those touching bare rock secrete acids that free more metal. They are separate organisms only while dormant. Awake, they behave as a single flat body.

These are the foundation of much that lives on the Blackworks Plateau. They line hidden pools, stain the insides of vents, and lie sealed beneath the translucent crusts of the low pleated plants. They occur inside animals as well, layered through guts and breathing chambers, converting substances that would poison most life into warmth and usable charge.

The wet patch spreads no farther than the drop allows. Already something has found it.

A dust-sifter approaches on six legs.

At a distance it might be mistaken for a loose piece of vent slag nudged by vibration. It is low and oval, with a shell of overlapping black plates. The plates are not armor in the ordinary sense. Each is a thin mirrored laminate, silver on its concealed surface, able to lift or close as the animal regulates its heat. Its legs are jointed close to the ground and end in broad fans of fibrous toes. On hard basalt the fans fold into narrow pads. On dust they open, distributing its weight so completely that it leaves only shallow, radial prints.

It has no head apparent from above. The front of the shell tapers, then divides underneath into two magnetized feeding combs. These sweep through the damp grit, sorting it. Sterile mineral grains fall away at once. Charged cells and edible films cling to the combs and are drawn into a grinding chamber.

The dust-sifter eats until the mark is dry. Then it presses its belly to the stone.

Rows of pale organs along its underside make electrical contact with the mineral surface. For several hours it remains there, digesting without visible movement while internal colonies separate iron, sulfur, and trace salts. When it rises, it leaves behind a faintly magnetic pellet. Months later, wiregrass roots may turn toward that pellet from several meters away.

One dust-sifter is easy to overlook. A migration is not.

After rain, millions emerge from cracks where they have been stacked through the dry interval, shell against shell, each animal holding scarcely more moisture than a closed seed. They travel in loose fronts tens of kilometers wide. No individual leads. Each follows the weak electrical disturbance of those ahead, and the mass flows around inactive ground as water flows around stone.

Their shells make almost no sound. What they produce instead is a traveling change in the plateau’s field. Wiregrass dims as they pass. Mineral fins discharge into them with faint red flickers. Buried creatures wake hours before the migration arrives.

Among the dust-sifters move taller grazers known by the tracks they leave: six parallel punctures, then a long interval, then six more.

They are stilt-bodied animals, narrow as poles when viewed head-on. Their central trunks are warm and soft, but enclosed by cages of mineral rods grown from their own tissues. Above the body rises a forked organ like an open pair of shears. The forks detect charge in the ground and airborne traces from distant vents. Below hang six long limbs, each ending in a divided foot that can span loose sediment or lock into cracks.

They feed on spindle-plants.

A spindle-plant’s hollow trunk contains little nourishment. Its silver crown, however, concentrates rare metals gathered over decades from an active fault. The grazer cannot reach the crown by browsing. Instead, it embraces the trunk with its mineral cage and waits. Over several hours, matching the plant’s internal voltage, it persuades the silver blades to uncurl and lower. The grazer then draws a fine, blood-dark fluid from their bases without killing the plant.

Older spindle-plants bear rings of these feeding scars. Older grazers carry fragments of silver blade embedded in their cages, where the metal becomes part of their sensory system. The most experienced can detect a storm beyond the horizon or a newly opened vent through kilometers of stone.

They gather only during breeding seasons. At other times each stands alone, often so motionless that dust bands form across its legs.

When the underground currents reverse, they walk toward one another.

Hundreds may converge upon a basin over the course of a month, arriving from different directions with their forks tilted toward the same buried salt seam. They arrange themselves along the seam without touching. At the moment of discharge, current passes through the assembled animals. Their cages glow dull orange. Reproductive cells move from body to body along brief arcs no thicker than wire.

By morning they have separated. In the basin remain clusters of dark eggs, each sunk into a crack and capped with a grain of conductive silver.

Other animals carry entire habitats with them.

The broad-backed furnace bearers are among the largest ground creatures of the plateau. Their bodies are flattened, segmented, and heavily shielded, with perhaps thirty short legs concealed beneath a rim of armor. The oldest grow as long as a dry channel is wide. They travel so slowly that dust settles permanently on their upper plates.

Beneath those plates, however, the surface is pale.

There grow crowded gardens of symbionts: translucent fronds, beadlike heat bladders, and soft combs that strain metallic vapor. The bearer’s armor channels fumes from the ground through these colonies. In return, the pale growths feed it and shed layers that insulate it during sudden cold.

A mature bearer is less an individual than a moving district. Small scavengers live between its plates. Blind thread-worms tend the vapor combs, cutting away dead tissue. Dust-sifters shelter in the warm cavities behind its legs. Wiregrass seeds lodge in the channels of its back and may remain there for years, germinating whenever the bearer enters rain.

Some bearers carry so much growth that their true outline disappears. From afar they resemble pale, steaming boulders in motion.

They follow heat rather than food. Their migrations map deep events: the filling of a chamber, the slowing of a subterranean river, the opening of a fault too slight to be visible at the surface. A line of furnace bearers may turn in unison and begin a journey of three hundred kilometers because stone far below them has cooled by a fraction of a degree.

Nothing preys upon a full-grown bearer while it is healthy. When one stops, an entire community detects the change.

The first arrivals are cable-mouths, narrow scavengers that live folded inside fissures. They emerge as lengths of glossy black muscle, supported by dozens of hooklike feet. Their mouths open sideways along much of the body. They do not tear flesh. They clamp onto the dead bearer’s armor and pass current through it, accelerating decay in the symbiotic gardens beneath.

Then come the hammerlings.

They are small, compact creatures with one greatly enlarged forelimb containing a dense magnetic stone. A hammerling strikes not by force but by reversal of polarity. The impact sounds like an iron click. Hundreds working around a carcass detach its plates one by one, opening the warm interior without wasting the accumulated mineral.

For a season, the dead bearer becomes the most active place for kilometers. Eggs hatch in it. Pleated mats unfold across its fluids. Predators wait outside the ring of scavengers. Eventually only the mineral cage remains, fitted so closely to the ground that it may be mistaken for another patch of weathered rock.

The plateau’s hunters are difficult to describe from tracks because they make so few.

One leaves only paired crescents at long intervals along ridge crests. Another leaves a smooth line where its underside brushes dust. A third never touches the open ground at all, moving through networks of cooled lava tubes and reaching the surface only with its sensory limbs.

The ridge hunter is called a fringe because that is usually all that can be seen of it: a trembling row of dark filaments laid along the lip of an escarpment.

Its body hangs below the ridge, hidden against the cliff face. The fringe spreads across the ground above, each filament sensitive to a different range of vibration. Through it the hunter distinguishes rain from venting, venting from erosion, and the six-beat gait of a grazer from the thirty-beat progression of a furnace bearer. It can feel the difference between an animal walking willingly and one already pursued.

The fringe may wait for days. During this time, minerals precipitate on its skin and dust covers it. Small organisms graze over its limbs without recognizing them.

When prey passes beneath, the hunter releases its hold on the cliff.

It does not leap so much as pour. Its body unfolds into a long muscular sheet, gliding over loose grit on trapped gas. The attack is nearly silent. It wraps the prey’s legs first, then drives a conductive spine into the mineral cage or shell. The victim’s own stored charge contracts its muscles against it.

Fringes eat infrequently. Much of what they take is not flesh but electrical potential. After feeding, the hunter’s tissues remain dangerously charged. It climbs to an exposed ridge and raises fine dorsal threads until the excess leaks into the haze as a blue corona.

There it may itself be taken.

The upper air belongs to organisms whose size is concealed by distance.

A mature haze drifter can span the space between two ridges. Its body is a cluster of warm hydrogen sacs surrounding a dark central mass, with broad transparent vanes that flex against slow atmospheric currents. Seen from below, it resembles a chain of dim moons bound together by tissue. The sacs continually leak and regrow. Their hydrogen is produced by colonies living in heated chambers along the drifter’s spine.

The animal does not fly toward destinations so much as descend along chemical contours. It tastes the plateau with tendrils that hang for hundreds of meters. Some are thin as hair; others are thick, muscular cables. They comb metallic fumes from the haze, drink from rain curtains, and occasionally brush the ground.

Where a feeding cable drags, creatures gather.

Winged scavengers cling to it in layers. They are small, triangular animals whose wings are rigid mineral membranes. Most cannot sustain flight from the ground to the drifter. They climb the trailing cables after storms, feeding as they go, and launch themselves only to move between one part of the aerial herd and another.

They clean the drifter’s skin, open clogged pores, eat parasites, and carry reproductive cysts from body to body. Their eggs are dropped onto warm vents below. The young hatch wingless and spend their early lives as fumarole strainers, becoming airborne only after accumulating enough light metal in their skeletons.

The drifters’ own young begin on the ground.

A newborn is a soft, many-armed bladder anchored over a vent. For years it lives there, no larger than a low shelter, breathing the warm gases and slowly inflating internal chambers. Animals rest beneath its overhang. Wiregrass grows around its anchor limbs. A local ecology develops in its shade.

Then comes a sufficiently strong discharge.

The young drifter contracts. Its anchors split along predetermined seams. It rises several meters, settles again, rises farther, trailing broken strands and clots of dark soil. For its first days aloft it is clumsy and vulnerable. Ridge fringes lash upward at it. Winged scavengers mob its soft sacs. Many young tear open on mineral spires and collapse across the plateau in miles of transparent tissue.

Those that reach the high haze may live for centuries.

During storms, the oldest descend. Their hydrogen sacs compress under the colder air, and their bodies move among the ridges with slow precision. Luminous chambers show through their undersides: rows of amber and red organs pulsing as gases are shifted from sac to sac. Their cables strike ridges and draw charge upward. Lightning passes through them without killing them, distributed across broad living meshes and released as violet sheets.

For the creatures below, this is both danger and season.

Wiregrass discharges. Eggs open. Dust-sifter migrations fracture into branching streams. Spindle-plants turn their crowns toward the storm. Fringes abandon exposed ridges and crawl into the cliff flutes. Furnace bearers halt and lower their bodies until their armor touches ground.

When the drifters rise again, pieces of them remain behind: ropes of shed tissue, collapsed gas bladders, droplets rich in airborne salts. For days the ground beneath their path is crowded with feeders.

Not every inhabitant is an animal or plant in any useful sense.

Inside the geothermal shafts live vane colonies, branching growths of ceramic tissue rooted directly in hot stone. Each colony begins as a bacterial film where two mineral fluids meet. It deposits a thin blade around itself, then another offset from the first. Over millennia these blades become the toothed crystalline forms that turn in subterranean currents.

The living portion is only a skin along each edge. The wheel beneath it is secretion, skeleton, shelter, and memory.

As the vanes rotate, even by a fraction of a degree, different colonies enter the flow. Some wake in heat. Some wake in pressure. Others reproduce only when crushed between mineral teeth. Burrowing organisms feed on the oldest layers and keep the shafts from sealing. If they become too numerous, the current changes chemistry and poisons them. If they disappear, the vanes thicken until rotation stops, the shaft overheats, and a new vent breaks through elsewhere.

There are organisms that spend their entire existence inside this cycle and never encounter open air.

There are blind swimmers in the subterranean brines, shaped like ribbons of clear cartilage around a line of metal nodules. There are pressure polyps that open only when a turbine turns above them. There are colonies distributed through salt seams, communicating over kilometers by altering the timing of electrical discharge. A colony may take a century to complete a single contraction.

Surface life feels these contractions as the plateau’s pulse.

Some creatures rely on it so completely that when a deep colony dies, they continue to follow the vanished rhythm. Herds arrive at cold ground and stand waiting. Grazers turn their forks toward an exhausted seam. Predators gather along empty migration corridors. It may take generations for their bodies to learn the silence.

The rare bright hours reveal another population.

Under ordinary violet dimness, the shallow pools appear empty. Direct sunlight warms them, and transparent bodies rise from mineral sediment—flat, many-eyed swimmers that use the sudden radiance for a frenzy of growth. Their tissues darken from clear to bronze in minutes. They mate, lay heat-resistant cysts, and die before the light fades.

Plants sealed under mineral sheaths open violently. Their pleated fans spread across whole slopes, not slowly now but with snapping folds. Spores fire into the overheated air. Wiregrass pours charge into its roots. Vent films boil into foam and run down channels as living black froth.

Animals too slow to hide enter a fevered state. Dust-sifters raise every shell plate and race without direction. Stilt grazers lock their mineral cages to the ground while their soft bodies pulse inside. Fringe hunters, overcharged and unable to remain still, attack prey they cannot eat. Furnace bearers become moving islands of steam.

These hours kill many things. They also break the old balances. Dormant forms germinate that cannot wake in any other way. Predators meet prey normally separated by distance. Aerial cysts descend on thermal columns. Floods carry entire communities from one basin into another.

Then the haze closes, and the plateau becomes dark enough to live in again.

What survives does not celebrate. It slows.

Shell plates settle. Fans refold. Hunters return to ridges. The haze drifters climb until only their faintest lights remain. Across the ground, the innumerable small exchanges resume: heat into tissue, metal into shell, charge into motion, flesh into mineral and mineral back into flesh.

Near the place where the drop fell, the conductive film has already begun to dry. Its joined body divides into grains. Each lobe draws an armor of dissolved basalt around itself, leaving one seam that will open at the next touch of water.

A dust-sifter passes over them and takes some. Others adhere to its feet. A few enter cracks. One is lifted by a warm exhalation from a hairline vent and carried into the dark.

The wet mark disappears.

What woke there does not.
