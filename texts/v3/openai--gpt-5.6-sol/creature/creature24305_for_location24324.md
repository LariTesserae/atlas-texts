At first, the inhabitants of the Understill appear to be stains.

They occupy the margins between substances: the green-black line where copper enters ceramic, the soft gray bloom around a sweating rivet, the dull translucence within a crack in vitrified brick. Some are bacterial films feeding on oil and metal salts. Some are fungal nets so fine that they reveal themselves only when beaded with condensation. Together they make a thin, nearly continuous pasture over the dampest parts of the machine.

The pasture grows slowly. A patch the width of a palm may be older than a surface settlement. It thickens during sweating cycles, pales during heat, and retreats molecule by molecule from rails that become too hot. Nothing grazes it clean. In the Understill, complete consumption is a dangerous extravagance.

The pale insects feed there.

They are flatter than their screw-hole refuges suggest, with bodies composed of overlapping plates that slide across one another when they squeeze through a gap. Their legs end in minute hooks, allowing them to travel beneath pipes as easily as over floors. Most have no functional eyes. Instead, a fan of short hairs around the head responds to air movement, vibration, and changes in temperature. An approaching tremor reaches them long before the great wheel moves. At once they flatten against the nearest surface and become indistinguishable from loose flakes of enamel.

Their color depends on where they have fed. Those from the copper seams are faintly green. Those living near the boilers are soot-black, though their newly molted skins are white as wet paper. In the cistern galleries they take on the yellowish tint of old ceramic.

They breed in packed valve grease. The young emerge small enough to shelter in the stamped numerals on bolt heads.

The threadlike swimmers in the pools are not worms, though they resemble loose fibers until disturbed. Each consists of a chain of specialized bodies joined end to end. The head drinks. The middle segments store water. The tail anchors itself in oil or mineral sediment. During dry intervals the chain contracts into a glassy bead, sometimes remaining so for years. When the Understill sweats again, the bead uncoils.

These swimmers are prey to a heavier pool-dweller that rarely shows its whole shape. Usually there is only a brief pleat in the oil film or a row of small indentations along the scale at the water’s edge. It has a soft, puckered mouth on its underside and six limbs too delicate to support it on dry metal. When a basin begins to evaporate, it climbs by drawing a thread of mucus from one wet depression to the next. The threads harden into clear bridges. Many of the apparently mineral filaments beneath the cisterns are the abandoned roads of generations.

The translucent stalks beside the persistent leak are neither plants nor fungi in any simple sense. Beneath them lies a single spreading organism, embedded in the mortar and fed by colonies of microbes. Each stalk is a temporary drinking organ. At the first chill it opens its cup-shaped tip. A drop landing there is swallowed in a series of minute contractions and distributed through the hidden body.

The stalks also listen.

A footfall nearby causes every tip to close. The organism can remain sealed for days after a heavy vibration, sacrificing an entire wet cycle rather than expose itself. Older groves have learned the harmless rhythms of chains and pistons. They ignore the regular shudder of the distant engines. It is irregular movement—scraping, probing, a sequence of steps—that frightens them.

Something makes such steps.

It has no settled name. If the Understill ever had keepers who catalogued its life, their labels are gone. What remains is evidence: three-toed tracks in rust flour, polished arcs on the undersides of handrails, peeled membranes behind gauges, and bundles of insect shell tied together with strands of fungal fiber.

The creature itself is easy to mistake for a hanging gasket or a fold of burned insulation.

At rest, it presses its body into the angles behind pipe junctions. Its hide is loose and matte, dark where dry but paling when it absorbs moisture. Mineral grit clings to it. Rust becomes embedded in its outer layers. Very old individuals carry ridges of scale across their backs, and small drip-stalks sometimes grow from the deeper grooves. They do not seem troubled by this. A motionless elder may resemble a piece of the machine’s corrosion until it unfolds.

Then it is taller than expected, though never quite upright.

It has four limbs, but the front pair are long enough to serve as legs in tight passages. Each hand or foot bears three broad digits with soft, heat-resistant pads. The prints therefore resemble those of a small, heavy bird. The abrupt end of a trail at a wall is not evidence of disappearance. The creature has simply raised itself, placed its forelimbs against a vertical seam, and climbed. Its pads adhere to damp metal, while hooked inner claws find purchase in rivet heads and mortar joints.

The torso is narrow and remarkably flexible. Ribs overlap instead of meeting at a breastbone, allowing the chest to compress almost flat. The skull is no wider than the neck. Given time, an adult can pass through an opening that would barely admit a human wrist. It does so slowly, releasing its breath in a low sigh and feeding itself through the gap limb by limb. Shed skins collect at these crossings, caught on burrs of rust like translucent cloth.

There are no visible eyes.

The face is a smooth wedge interrupted by two heat pits and a vertical mouth. Around the jaw grow stiff sensory threads, some short, some trailing nearly the length of the forearm. These threads touch surfaces before the body does. They detect the faint vibration of moving fluid inside pipes and the thermal boundaries between metals. With them, the creature perceives the Understill not as darkness but as a layered field of currents, pressures, textures, and approaching changes.

A live pipe must shine in that perception. A hidden leak may be visible through a wall.

Its mouth opens farther than the narrow seam suggests. Inside are ridges rather than teeth, suitable for scraping films, crushing pale insects, and grinding the soft mineral nodules that form beneath certain drains. It also eats the jointed drip-stalks, but only after they have closed for the dry interval. Whether this restraint is learned or instinctive is uncertain. Groves repeatedly cropped while open tend to die. Groves harvested while sealed grow back thicker.

The creature drinks without lapping. It spreads the skin of its throat or belly against wet ceramic and remains there until the surface is dry. Fine channels in the skin draw water inward. During the sweating cycle several may cling beneath the high pipes, utterly still, absorbing droplets before they can fall.

For this reason they are rarely seen at pools. The pools are low, exposed places. Exposure is dangerous in the Understill, where survival belongs to things that fit between other things.

They live singly for much of the long hot interval. Each keeps a territory no larger than a cluster of pipes, a locker, or the cavity beneath a pressure door. Yet their territories overlap through the walls. A narrow conduit may belong to six individuals at different temperatures of the cycle. No boundary is marked by scent alone. They arrange fragments of ceramic in seams, knot copper hairs around bolts, and leave small pellets of compacted scale in particular recesses.

These signs convey more than ownership. One pattern indicates water above. Another warns of a rail that turns suddenly hot. A spiral of wire around a valve stem means that the valve moved recently and may move again. The oldest signs have become incorporated into rust, copied and renewed by creatures that could not have met their makers.

They do not speak through air. Air is too crowded and too misleading.

Instead, they tap.

A claw laid against a pipe produces a sequence of faint impulses that may travel far beyond the next partition. The messages are slow and widely spaced. Three taps may require half an hour. A response may not come until the next tremor. To an impatient listener the sounds seem accidental: cooling metal, falling grit, droplets inside a conduit. But the patterns repeat, change, answer one another.

During the dry heat, little is said. During the sweating cycle the entire Understill becomes conversational.

Messages move along copper runs and through iron tanks. Individuals announce newly active leaks. They warn of shifting plates. They call from one cistern to another in sequences timed between falling drops. Sometimes, from behind a wall thought solid, an answering pattern begins that no nearby creature recognizes. The reply may come from an isolated population, sealed away when a passage collapsed centuries before. Or it may be an automatic sound of the machinery that the inhabitants have mistaken for one of their own.

They gather when the great wheel approaches one of the rare positions in which several hidden chambers align. Because the wheel advances so slowly, these gatherings occur only a few times in a life.

In the days beforehand, the creatures become restless. They travel beyond their usual territories, following rails that have begun to warm in a particular order. They carry food packets, flakes of absorbent fungus, and thin curls of shed skin. They squeeze through the moving apertures around the wheel’s rim and enter a cavity that remains accessible for only a fraction of its cycle—perhaps several months, perhaps a year.

There they exchange young.

The young are small, pale, and almost boneless. They cling to the inner folds of an adult’s back until placed into a communal nest of warm fiber and valve packing. No individual appears to distinguish its own offspring afterward. Each departing adult takes one or two young from a different part of the nest. In this way the scattered territories remain one population despite the slow closing and opening of routes.

Those that miss the alignment must wait for another turn. Many never live long enough.

Their lifespan is difficult to judge because growth almost ceases between wet cycles. An individual may remain the same size for decades, then molt and enlarge after an unusually rich condensation. The mineral-backed elders may be centuries old. They move least and know the most stable routes. Younger creatures follow the polish left by their bodies.

They also tend parts of the Understill, though it is unclear whether this is deliberate maintenance.

They pull fungal matter from clogged sieves. They scrape scale from narrow channels and press it into cracks elsewhere. They wedge shells beneath trembling fittings. They chew through soft corrosion to reach water, sometimes opening a leak and sometimes draining one. Their saliva hardens mineral dust into a dense ceramic-like cement. Many load-bearing masses contain their old feeding pellets and nesting paste.

A corroded support may therefore stand because generations have gnawed at it, patched it, nested in it, and died against it.

They have no understanding of the machine as a machine. A wheel is not a wheel to them. It is a season. A pressure door is a horizon that opens once in several generations. The distant engine is not an engine but the deep event by which droplets quiver, passages alter, and messages briefly travel farther.

Yet they understand the Understill intimately.

They know that a cold handle can precede a blast of steam. They know which tanks sing before they shift. They can distinguish the harmless sigh of a settling valve from the first breath of a rupturing gasket. When danger comes, every visible creature vanishes. Insects enter screw holes. Pool chains contract into beads. Drinking stalks close. The three-toed inhabitants compress themselves behind panels and brace their bodies across the gaps.

Afterward they emerge slowly and test the changed structure with their sensory threads.

There are dead among them, though bodies are seldom found. The living carry a corpse toward one of the warmest ceramic recesses and coat it with mineral paste. Heat and microbes reduce the soft tissue. What remains is a thin, articulated crust shaped like the curled creature within. Over time the crust fuses with the wall.

Some cisterns are lined with hundreds of these forms.

They might be called graves. They might equally be repairs.

Water beads over them when the cold phase comes. The drops travel across mineral brows and folded limbs, gathering salts before descending into channels below. The dead become part of the condensation system. The system feeds the films. The films feed the insects. The insects and stalks feed the living, who patch the cracks and clear the drains through which the water moves.

Thus the Understill’s inhabitants do not merely survive inside its weathered equilibrium. They participate in it so completely that the distinction between ecosystem and mechanism has worn thin.

Perhaps the machine was once built to perform some vast, precise duty, and these lives are only incidental.

Perhaps, after so long, they are the duty it performs.
