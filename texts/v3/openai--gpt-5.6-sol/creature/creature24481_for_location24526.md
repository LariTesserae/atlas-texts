At first the inhabitants seem to be many species disciplined into one routine.

The roof-creatures wake warm beneath their fur. Their mineral masks have no expression that an outsider can read, but they recognize one another by heat, pressure, and the minute burr of wing joints. Packed along the ridges, they groom soot from the pale nap of their neighbors and lick condensation from rivet heads. They mate, quarrel over exact handspans of roof, and sometimes shove a weaker body into a gutter. Yet when the low note sounds, private irritations vanish. Every blunt head rises. Thousands launch as one body, circle the hall, and return—each to its own weathered hollow in the masonry.

None appears to command the others. The siren does not frighten them. It may not even be a signal in the ordinary sense. The note, the wingbeat, the pressure change in the ducts, and the impulse to rise seem to be different surfaces of the same event.

The translucent herd animals are more visibly docile. Through their hides, food can be watched passing into branching stomachs. Dark bones flex inside them like machinery viewed through furnace glass. They have six narrow legs, lidless lateral eyes, and blunt mouths suitable only for accepting what a pipe gives. While they queue they lean against one another, sharing warmth. Calves occasionally step out of line, but never far; another animal presses its flank against them until they are restored to place. Their sparks mark the paving for a few hours. By morning those tiny burns have faded into the common polish.

The herds do not belong to owners. They belong to routes.

A particular animal may spend years walking between one feed nozzle, one pressure door, and one sleeping bay. It grows heavier, sheds clear skins, bears young into a receiving cradle, and eventually enters a building from which only shell cylinders and protein pellets emerge. The animal ahead does not turn to watch. The one behind does not hesitate. This is not because they cannot feel fear. Their bodies possess every apparatus of alarm: glands, quickened hearts, membranes that flood dark under stress. Fear occurs, but only at its allotted stations. In certain tiled rooms the herd cries out together for precisely forty-seven breaths. Then the color drains from their tissues, and they proceed.

The smallest citizens know even less separation between life and civic function.

Black gutter-swimmers have mouths shaped like files and feet that taste ceramic. To them the city is an endless wet canyon, one body-width deep. They hatch where warm runoff enters the channels and spend their lives following mineral gradients. At crossings they touch antennae, exchanging salts and fragments of chemical memory. Each carries in its gut a grit of magnetic metal. When valves open beneath the street, entire populations turn at once, compelled along bends they have never traveled before. They clean the gutters because cleanliness is the trail they hunger for.

The red nest-things in the walls are almost all mouth and hardened fingertip. They cannot see, and no single one can make the sound heard in the lanes. Each clicks against brick at its own slow interval. Thousands together produce the continuous dry chatter. Where masonry begins to fracture, the rhythm alters. Other red things arrive bearing paste in abdominal sacs. They pack the flaw, press themselves into it, and die there. Their skins vitrify. Much of the city’s red mortar is ancestral.

The conduit-burrowers live in darkness threaded by current. They are milky, soft, and indefinitely long, though no one specimen spans more than the distance between two junction boxes. Mouth joined to tail, they make living cables. Electricity passes through their wet tissues in pulses that trigger feeding, reproduction, and memory. A break in the chain causes pain down the whole line. Nearby burrowers divide rapidly, pushing pale new lengths into the gap until the circuit closes. Their dead liquefy into conductive gel.

Thus the lamps are lit by colonies that experience illumination only as a distant easing of strain.

Under the rails, the coin-flat animals sleep in stacks. A single layer is thin enough to be mistaken for a stain, but the colonies descend deep between the sleepers, flesh laminated with mineral dust. The passage of a cart sends pressure through them. The upper layers tremble; the lower layers release oil; the rails settle a fraction of a hair. They are bearings, shock absorbers, and perhaps listeners. Patterns of traffic pass through their compressed bodies for centuries. If the city remembers in any centralized way, part of that memory may be stored there—as tremors, as thicknesses of flesh, as old routes impressed into generations that never move.

The cistern eels are less peaceful. Packed white over white, they bite constantly. Their skin is without pigment, their eyes reduced to silver knots, and their jaws are fine enough to draw blood from neighboring gills. But every wound releases a hormone that stills the surrounding mass. Violence spreads its own antidote. The eels churn water through themselves, filtering metal, salt, spores, shed cells, and the minute eggs of species that use them as moving nurseries. During rain, the cisterns swell and the uppermost eels briefly have room to straighten. Many appear disturbed by the freedom. They knot themselves again as soon as they can.

The armored feeders at the north wall may be the oldest individual creatures in the basin. Their plated bodies have rooted into sockets in the basalt, and layers of mineral paste have fused their lower limbs to the street. They resemble gates that have grown jaws. Warmth leaks from seams between their plates. Small animals enter them according to an interval measured somewhere beneath thought.

The feeders are not without appetite. Between deliveries, digestive sacs contract around emptiness. Their tongues flex. Their saliva runs into grooves. Still they do not snap at anything outside the appointed stream. Hunger here is not an instruction to seek. It is a pressure that closes a circuit when the proper body arrives.

Each feeder bears evidence of personality, if personality can survive such confinement. One grinds its left jaw before closing. Another emits a faint piping sound after swallowing. One has worn a hollow into the stone with a foreclaw that moves no more than a finger’s breadth. These variations persist for decades. They are not corrected because they do not alter the total.

Below everything live the reducers: blind masses in warm vaults, with many mouths opening and sealing in waves. They have no fixed outline. Gas bladders lift portions of them against the ceiling; weight tears those portions free; the fragments creep back and rejoin. Pipes enter their flesh and emerge elsewhere. They digest waste, corpses, old mortar, failed eggs, metal filings, wash water, and occasionally machinery. They distinguish poorly between organism and object. Given time, they reduce both to the same useful paste.

Workers sometimes appear among them—if “workers” is the right word for the narrow, jointed creatures that climb from service hatches carrying replacement valves in their thoracic cages. Their front limbs terminate in keys, clamps, brushes, or ceramic nozzles. Some are born with these implements. Others acquire them while soft, allowing the tools to root into their limbs. They move through ducts too small for human shoulders, polish bearings, remove clots of tissue from pipes, and feed worn components into grinding mouths in their own abdomens.

They do not repair the city from outside it. They are one of the ways the city heals.

No creature in the basin seems capable of imagining the whole. The winged animals know roofs and the circular flight. The herds know pressure at their flanks. The gutter-swimmers know gradients. The wall colonies know cracks. The reducers know what can be dissolved. Even the great feeders know only waiting, fullness, and the recurrent taste of the same prey.

Yet the whole behaves as if it possesses an exact and ancient self-knowledge.

That knowledge may reside in no animal. It may be distributed through them: in migration impulses, gut chemistries, electrical chains, quantities of eggs, the dimensions of mouths, the delay between a siren’s two notes. A million small hungers intersect to produce streets, schedules, power, sanitation, manufacture, birth, and disposal. What looks like obedience may simply be anatomy extended beyond the skin.

Or the animals may be organs, and the city the animal.

Its basalt rim is a shell. The pumping hall is a heart with too many chambers. Rails are loops of intestine. Factories are glands. The warm upwelling is food drawn through a buried mouth. Its inhabitants circulate as corpuscles, enzymes, symbionts, and thoughts too brief to recognize themselves. The basin breathes combustible vapor, drinks rain, builds its own tissues from its own dead, and maintains a single shape against the abrasion of time.

But even this is not quite enough. An animal changes as it lives. This place changes only in order to remain exact.

The beings around the central court are the most difficult to classify. Many species stand there: masked flyers folded on the ground, transparent herd animals, black gutter forms kept wet by dripping pipes, red clickers in portable clots of brick dust, tool-limbed workers, juvenile feeders not yet armored into place. Predator faces prey. Water-dweller faces dry-born. Creatures without eyes orient as precisely as those with many.

They are not worshippers. They exhibit no submission, ecstasy, or dread. Nor are they guards. Nothing tries to cross their ring.

Each stands until some internal limit is reached. Then it leaves, and another arrives by a different route. The replacement occupies the empty position before the gap can be perceived. Over lifetimes, every species passes through the court. Some remain for a minute; some for years. Molted plates and dropped scales are collected at once. Birth occasionally occurs in the ring, as does death, without changing its circumference.

All face the shallow hollow at the center.

Perhaps this is where the city keeps its image of itself. The still water shows no sky because the sky is irrelevant; no observer because an observer is foreign material. It reflects every lit factory and moving animal, including those hidden behind walls and beneath streets. It shows eggs inside molds, eels below opaque water, reducers flexing in their vaults, and the deep pipes descending toward the inexhaustible warmth. Nothing in the reflection is symbolic. It is an inventory without perspective.

The animals at the edge may be reading it, though most lack eyes.

They may read it through vibration in their hooves, electrical tension in their flesh, pressure against mineral masks, tiny changes in the temperature of the court. Each receives only the part that concerns its body. A wall colony feels the location of a future crack. A herd mother feels how many young must be born. A feeder tastes the hour of its next meal. A roof-creature knows which hollow will be vacant after rain.

Then they depart and perform that knowledge as appetite.

There may once have been architects here: beings who raised brick halls, laid rails, shaped brass gates, and bred flesh into tools. If so, the present inhabitants do not remember them as masters. They may remember them as ingredients. Their bones could have entered the reducers, their metals the skeletons of the herds, their nerve patterns the conduit colonies. Perhaps the city absorbed its makers so completely that command ceased to require commanders.

Perhaps that absorption is still occurring.

Anything entering the basin is first noticed by the roof-creatures, then tasted by gutters, measured by gates, echoed through the rail colonies, and represented in the unrippling court. The city does not attack the unfamiliar. It discovers what the unfamiliar can become.

A loose object becomes aggregate, feed, insulation, current, mortar, and eventually itself again.

A living stranger would be treated with the same patience.

Not killed, necessarily. Not preserved.

Given a route. Given a hunger. Given a place in the ring.
