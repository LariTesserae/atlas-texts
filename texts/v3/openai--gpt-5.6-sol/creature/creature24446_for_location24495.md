At first, the inhabitants seem to be animals living among plants.

Given long enough, that distinction becomes difficult to defend.

The largest life is the vegetation itself, though “vegetation” is a name imposed upon something whose leaves are horizons and whose circulation includes an ocean. It may be one organism, a colony of organisms that ceased competing before competition could leave a record, or an entire biosphere joined so completely that individuality has become a feature of scale. Its trunks do not reveal the answer. Tissue sampled from distant structures is never identical, yet every difference complements another elsewhere. Severance proves nothing: what is cut apart remains in chemical communication through the intervening water, and what appears separate may rejoin after a cycle lasting ten thousand years.

Its life is not growth but maintenance.

The great plant continually rebuilds itself without gaining mass. New wood enters the inner faces of trunks while old wood dissolves from the outer faces. Leaves unfold as other leaves, continents away, become transparent and pass into the sea. Roots advance through sediments precisely as quickly as their tips are consumed. It has no annual rings. Instead, its wood contains recurring patterns of pressure and mineral content, the same sequence repeated inward beyond any core ever reached.

If it possesses awareness, awareness does not reside in a center. Electrical impulses cross its conductive vines in vast, intersecting waves. Some complete their circuits in seconds; others require centuries. The regular concussion of its pressure organs may be circulation, but it may also be a clock. The singing trunks may be structural resonance, or a sustained thought whose smallest syllable is older than many species elsewhere.

Upon this life, and within it, live the apparent animals.

The transparent grazers beneath the light leaves are called bells only because they resemble bells from below. Their upper bodies are broad adhesive disks, clear except for the branching darkness of their stomachs. Beneath each disk hangs a fringe of muscular combs. The combs pass over the leaf in exact succession, harvesting a film of sugar, microbes, and loosened skin. As one comb lifts, the next sets down; a feeding wave travels perpetually around the body.

They have no eyes. Illumination enters through them and is measured by rings of green-sensitive tissue. When the light above weakens with depth or distance, a bell does not search for brightness. It releases its hold, contracts once, and is taken by a fixed current to another leaf. Years later, after feeding along the underside in a widening spiral, it reaches the edge and drops into the same current again.

Their young form inside the parent as thin concentric layers. One layer detaches at a time, turns itself inside out, and adheres beside the adult. At the opposite margin of the leaf, an old bell becomes steadily more transparent until its final membrane is absorbed by the surface beneath it. From a distance, birth and disappearance occur together, like two small adjustments in the same pane of glass.

The silver needle-creatures of the open water are more active and less obviously alive. Each is several body-lengths of polished cartilage wrapped around a black digestive thread. Their fins vibrate too quickly to see, holding them at exact angles against currents that could carry larger animals away. They are spaced so widely that one needle may never come within sight of another, yet all turn together.

No signal has been observed passing between them. Their alignment is maintained through the plant’s standing electrical fields. A needle feels the field through mineral grains embedded along its flanks and occupies the point where several tensions balance. When it swims its closed route, it is following a temporary deformation in that field, perhaps caused by a distant pumping chamber. Thus the school is less a gathering than a diagram: living filings suspended around an invisible machine.

Needles eat almost nothing. Their mouths open when predetermined streams of plankton cross their paths. Their eggs are released into narrow root channels, carried downward, warmed, and returned as larvae to positions left vacant by adults at the same moment. A needle that is swallowed by a predator leaves no gap. Somewhere in the route, another has already assumed its spacing.

The bark canyons belong to the ringmouths.

They are pale, boneless predators, long enough to bridge the grooves in a trunk. Their bodies bear circles of muted lamps—cream, violet, deep red—though the lamps illuminate little. They are not used to find prey. They identify location. Each canyon wall carries a distinct mineral fluorescence, and the ringmouth reads it against the known sequence of its route.

Its mouth is a muscular opening at either end. It swims forward in one phase of life and backward in the next, digestive contractions reversing direction with age. In its forward phase it consumes grazers, shell colonies, and needle-creatures. In its backward phase it is slower, darker, and followed by juveniles that feed from its skin. Eventually it enters a crevice, anchors both mouths to the bark, and is opened by fine roots. Its tissues are drawn into the trunk while, elsewhere along the same canyon, a juvenile disengages and begins the route anew.

The ringmouth is feared, insofar as fear exists there. Animals flee from it. Muscles seize, hormones flood blood, bodies hide beneath folds of bark. Yet the pursuit has no uncertainty at the scale of the ocean. The same kind of prey escapes at each turning; the same kind is caught in each hollow. Terror is real to the fleeing creature, but surprise is not.

Around the warm industrial organs live the plated colonies. A colony looks like a field of black shells overlapping across the copper membranes. Each shell houses a soft animal with twelve feeding mouths and no permanent underside. The animals pass dissolved minerals from one to another, refining them through their bodies before laying them into their plates. Roots lace between the shells and dissolve the oldest edges. The colony therefore builds continuously and never becomes thicker.

A single plated animal is barely viable. Removed from its neighbors, it rotates its body as if searching for the missing pressure of adjacent shells, then closes. In the colony, however, chemical signals pass across millions of individuals. Feeding mouths open in waves. Whole fields darken or grow pale. Their distributed reflexes anticipate changes in heat before those changes arrive, suggesting that the colony is connected through the organ beneath it.

Deeper down are creatures less easily assigned names.

There are ribbonlike swimmers that exist partly as animals and partly as gradients of salt. Their visible bodies end after a few meters, but their nervous systems continue through the water as aligned ions. Cutting the ribbon does not interrupt its reactions. Disturbing the water around it does.

There are soft, many-lobed things that live inside high-pressure root pockets. On passing through a membrane, they flatten to the thickness of skin and spread across hundreds of square meters. They feed in that form, absorbing molecules across their entire surface. Returning through the membrane, they contract into compact, folded bodies and carry the meal within. Whether the spread form or folded form is the animal is uncertain. Each appears to dream of the other: while compact, its muscles twitch in broad ripples; while spread, knots form briefly like curled limbs.

The vertical spore columns have their own inhabitants. Minute creatures clasp individual spores and remain suspended with them. They possess hooks, stomachs, reproductive tissues, and exquisitely developed balance organs, but no means of swimming. Their entire world is one green sphere and the measured distance to the spheres above and below. They graze one side of the spore while microbes restore the other. At intervals they release young, which descend exactly one position as another animal dies and loosens its grip. To them, the column may be an endless ladder on which no one climbs.

Closest to anything resembling people are the hollow-bodied choristers of the singing trunks.

They are tall, radial beings with six grasping limbs and a crown of pressure-sensitive vanes. They neither build nor carry objects. They cling inside vascular galleries where current makes the wood resonate, positioning their bodies at the nodes of fixed chords. Their cavities amplify selected frequencies. Groups of them alter the timbre of a trunk without changing its fundamental note.

Their behavior is elaborate. They touch limbs in repeating sequences. They exchange colored secretions. They pause as though listening and answer one another with low internal pulses. An encounter may continue for months and has the structure of conversation: statement, response, correction, agreement.

But every conversation eventually returns to its opening gesture.

The choristers can remember, though only cyclically. Their nervous tissue records experience as a traveling chemical ring. As the ring passes through the brain, old perception becomes present again; when it completes the circuit, the memory begins from its first moment. They do not accumulate histories. They inhabit recurring spans of awareness—some minutes long, some extending across generations of other creatures.

A chorister may therefore spend its entire existence discovering the same companion, recognizing the same chord, reaching the same understanding, and relinquishing it at the turn of the neural cycle. Whether this is imprisonment or contentment cannot be known. Their bodies show no distress. Their gestures possess patience, tenderness, and something like ceremony.

They may be the plant’s listeners. They may be sensory organs that have mistaken themselves for animals. Or the plant may be their instrument, and the ocean-wide metabolism exists to hold the one chord in which their thought can recur.

Nothing here is truly independent. The grazers prune the leaves, but the leaves nourish them. Predators regulate the grazers, but roots digest the predators. Shell colonies refine minerals for the organs on which they feed. Needle schools trace electrical fields and, by tracing them, stabilize those fields. Even the choristers’ songs alter flow through the trunks by minute, necessary amounts.

The inhabitants do not merely occupy the completed world. They are how it remains completed.

Each creature is a movement the ocean must make: a mouth where matter turns, a fin where force is corrected, a death where substance changes routes. They live full local lives—hunger, pursuit, pairing, pain, rest, recognition—yet together they form no history. Their freedom exists in the execution, not the outcome.

Perhaps that is what lives there, finally: not a species, nor a godlike plant, nor the innumerable animals suspended in its waters, but one immeasurable living condition.

A biosphere that has finished becoming and has made a metabolism of forever.
