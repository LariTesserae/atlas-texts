What lives in the Press is not best understood as a collection of separate animals. Separation is a temporary condition there, usually followed by death.

At first the hollow appears paved with black stone. Then one shield eases over another. A seam closes. A ledge takes a breath.

The most numerous inhabitants are the tiles, broad six-legged creatures whose bodies have surrendered almost every unnecessary dimension. Their limbs fit into grooves beneath them; their heads barely project; their mouths open downward against the rock. A settled tile is less like an animal resting than a seal pressed over a crack. It grazes the gray chemical films beneath its body and traps the faint exhalations on which those films feed. Its armor is grown through with whatever the mountain offers—iron grains, sulfur salts, basalt flour, needles of glass—so that age makes it heavier, harder, and increasingly geological.

Young tiles are soft, dark brown, and dangerously distinct from their surroundings. They hatch beneath adults and remain there, drinking secretions from the joints of the clustered pavement until their first armor mineralizes. Only then do they enter the slow circulation of the basin. They travel from fading vents toward active ones, though no individual seems to know where it is going. Each follows minute differences in heat and chemistry, and the multitude produces migrations with the broad, patient shape of weather.

Older tiles carry communities of their own. Mites graze their mineral crust. Thread-thin scavengers knot inside the warm recesses around their breathing pits. Shell-borers excavate galleries through dead layers of armor without immediately harming the living tissue below. Some tiles are so burdened with tenants that they seem furred, barnacled, or diseased. The arrangement is not always parasitic. Tiny grazers keep breathing channels clear; borers weaken armor before a molt; pale joint-worms eat fungal analogues and salt deposits that would otherwise lock the legs in place. Host and infestation cannot be cleanly distinguished.

Tiles molt rarely, and never all at once. The shield splits into plates that remain stacked over the tender body like loose roof slates. Neighbors immediately press close, sheltering the molting animal from grit and predators. In return they eat portions of the cast armor and incorporate its minerals into their own. Thus even growth is communal: one creature’s discarded surface becomes another’s skeleton.

Above this pavement wait the spires.

A spire has the narrow, severe form of something assembled from charred instruments. Three legs support it. Three remain folded beside a vertical body, their tips fitted with hooks and shearing edges. It has no useful sight. Instead, shallow pits along its limbs read the basin as a field of temperatures and interruptions. In so uniformly heated a place, warmth conceals; coolness betrays. A lifted tile exposes a momentary shadow of cooler stone. A newly molted body glows unevenly. A wounded animal loses heat through fluid at the crack in its shell.

The spire strikes at these absences.

Its violence is instantaneous, but its feeding is slow. It pins a tile, opens the softer underside with movements too small to see, and drinks until only the armored roof remains. Before it is finished, scavengers have entered the kill through its mouthparts. Mites climb its legs. Thread-creatures gather beneath its joints. The predator becomes a feeding ground around the prey it has taken. Even its solitary stance is an illusion.

Spire eggs are carried in the abandoned shells of their victims. The female places one in each emptied tile and plugs the opening with chewed mineral paste. Such shells are indistinguishable from living animals until the young spire emerges—sometimes months later, sometimes only when a gas-rise warms it enough to continue developing. A nursery may therefore lie hidden in the floor as a patch of apparent pavement, every dark hexagon containing a folded hunter.

Below them all are the makers of the hollow: the burrowers.

They are pale only because darkness has made color irrelevant. Long, ringed, and blunt at both ends, they work through porous basalt by wetting it with corrosive secretions, rasping away the softened grains, and passing the mountain through their bodies. What leaves them is denser than what entered: small glassy pellets, hot and smooth, packed into tunnel walls or pushed upward through vents in the living floor.

No burrower is often seen whole. One end may feed several handspans beneath the rock while the other lies threaded through a plug of shells. Some branch as they age. Portions separate, heal, and continue independently, so that an ancient individual may become a colony dispersed through an entire wall. The Press’s tunnels may belong to thousands of animals or to a few old bodies divided beyond counting.

They sense pressure with exquisite precision. Wind on the surface, the step of a bearer, the settling of a hundred tiles—each arrives below as a different geometry of strain. Before a gas-rise, the burrowers cease feeding and brace themselves across their galleries. Their stillness is the first silence, transmitted upward before the tiles lower their bodies.

The smallest inhabitants live at boundaries: shell against shell, flesh against stone, hot gas against cooler armor. Needle-mouthed mites harvest the microscopic heat-feeders embedded in the basalt. Coin-shaped crawlers graze the mites. Transparent ribbon animals lie flat beneath the coins and digest them from below. Salt-bodied things without permanent mouths drift through pores as dry cysts, awaken in the moisture of a wound, feed, reproduce, and harden again before the wound closes.

Some creatures complete their entire lives inside a single joint of a tile.

Others live only during upheaval. The pale threads that erupt during gas-rise spend most years packed into breathing chambers as tight coils. When the floor cracks and hotter gases pour upward, they flee toward any relative coolness: the undersides of shells, the interiors of carcasses, one another. Thousands twist together into white cords. These cords have no leader and no stable anatomy, yet they crawl as if they were animals in their own right. When the disturbance ends, the cords unravel, and their members disappear again into the pavement.

The shell-borers are more patient. Their larvae enter dead armor and may wait there through years of quiet, feeding so slowly that the shell seems empty. Heat wakes them. They emerge with wedge-shaped heads and jaws polished by glass. For a few frantic hours they mate, cut chambers into fresh carcasses, and seal their offspring inside. By the next day almost none remain exposed. Their adult existence is shorter than a single cooling of the stone.

The bearers are the nearest things in the Press to builders, though they have no image of what they make.

They are low, immensely strong, and blind even to heat. Their world is inclination, weight, and the taste of minerals through their feet. When a bearer encounters a flat object, a reflex passes through its six limbs: grip, lift, turn uphill. At a sharp thermal boundary it releases the object and searches for another. This produces rings around vents, walls along cool seams, arches where gradients cross, and chambers around places where hot and less-hot stone meet.

A bearer may carry a slab of basalt, a tile’s cast shield, the emptied shell of its own kind, or a plate cut from some animal not seen in the Press for centuries. The bearer does not differentiate. Suitability is flatness. Purpose is uphill.

Nevertheless, its structures are inhabited. Tiles roof themselves beneath the courses. Burrowers weaken the foundations. Spires stand at the narrow entrances. Mites gather in the protected dust. Generations of use refine what instinct began, until a meaningless pile acquires doors, chambers, ambush points, nurseries, and favored walls. Function enters after construction, as though the community were completing a thought the builder never had.

Bearers themselves are rarely attacked. Their armor is thick, but more importantly it is crowded with biting symbionts. A disturbed bearer opens grooves along its back, releasing a dark effusion of small defenders that swarm over whatever has touched it. In return, the defenders live inside its shell, feed on its waste, and are transported from structure to structure. Every bearer is therefore both mason and caravan.

Wind reveals the deepest nature of the inhabitants. When it descends into the hollow, no species responds alone. Tiles lock edges. Bearers lower themselves as anchors. Spires insert their rigid limbs into the gaps. Threads bind the joints. Small crawlers flood beneath the resulting mass. What had seemed predator, prey, parasite, and scavenger becomes one braided hide stretched over the basin.

From above, during such a storm, the Press might appear to contain a single organism: a black, many-plated body contracting against abrasion.

Perhaps that appearance is not misleading.

The life of the hollow has no central mind, but it possesses memory. Paths survive because shells accumulate along them. Safe crevices deepen because burrowers are carried there in the joints of sheltering animals. A vent’s history is written in concentric generations of armor. The old are not remembered as individuals; they remain as architecture, minerals, blocked tunnels, inherited parasites, and changes in the routes taken by the living floor. Behavior laid down over millennia becomes indistinguishable from intention.

Only the pale column is excluded.

Nothing colonizes it. Microbial films halt at the buried edge of its cool influence. Burrowers turn aside before touching its foundation. A tile pushed against it will labor until it escapes, even climbing over other animals rather than remain in contact. Spires find its thermal absence so overwhelming that they become disoriented nearby, lifting and lowering their striking limbs at prey that is not there.

When the column vibrates, the Press does not merely fall quiet. It attends.

Each creature receives the vibration according to its body: as pressure in a tile’s shield, a false horizon in a spire’s legs, a convulsion through a burrower’s tunnels, an irresistible tightening in the threads. Yet all cease together. For those moments the multitude’s scattered awareness is aligned upon the same untouchable thing.

Whether the column commands them, warns them, or simply interrupts the senses by which they know themselves cannot be determined. Once it warms, their separate hungers return. The tiles begin their nearly invisible migration. Spires resume waiting. Bearers climb with fragments in their jaws. Beneath them, the mountain continues its passage through the guts of the burrowers.

Thus the inhabitants of the Press are armored animals, chemical films, infestations, hunters, masons, and living seams—but also something larger that exists only through their compression. It has no face except the arrangement of the floor, no voice except the crack of a shell, and no body that can be separated from the hollow it inhabits.

It feeds on the mountain.

Over ages, it is slowly making the mountain fit around itself.
