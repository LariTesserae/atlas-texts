At first it appears that no one lives in the Low Green Works.

There are openings shaped like doors, but nothing passes through them. There are dwellings, but no smoke of meals, no bedding, no possessions accumulating in corners. The factory floors are attended, though never by hands. A jammed conveyor may remain jammed for half a day before a cluster of pale tendrils emerges from a service cleft, tastes the obstruction, and patiently unthreads it. By the following morning the tendrils have withdrawn, leaving only a smear of sealing wax.

The Works have inhabitants, but few of them are creatures in the ordinary sense. They are offices, trades, utilities, and instincts given vegetable bodies.

The road is one inhabitant. It is not the same organism as the drains that run beside it, though the two are grafted together at intervals and exchange water, salts, and warnings. The road feels pressure. It remembers habitual loads as bands of dense fibre and responds to an unfamiliar weight by stiffening ahead of it. Where nothing has crossed for many years, it slowly loosens and becomes soft enough for storage roots to pass beneath.

The halls are others. Each has its own metabolism and temperament. The press house is hot-running and acquisitive, forever drawing pulp, resin, and water toward itself. The drying loft is austere; it sheds shade leaves in wet weather and will poison any vine that blocks its ventilation. The compressor grove is a colony of perhaps forty piston trees, but its roots have fused so thoroughly that injury to one changes the rhythm of all. They breathe in sequence, correcting one another by pressure. Their long pauses are not rest. They are calculation.

No single organism governs the settlement. The municipal tower does not command in the way a ruler commands. Its daily note is more like a common condition to which the others have been bred to respond. It measures cooling air, moisture, stored pressure, and the faint contraction of the ground after sunset. When these agree, chambers in its hollow trunk open and release the dusk breath. Most days this occurs at nearly the same hour. In drought, it may sound early. During prolonged mist, it sometimes remains silent for two or three nights, and the Works pass those nights in a state of uneasy partial wakefulness.

There are older things beneath the municipal habits.

Every major structure descends into a buried anatomy much larger than its visible body. The tower is anchored by listening roots. The halls sit over bulbous organs filled with starch, dormant buds, spare membranes, and copies of structural joints not presently needed. The bridges have root fans at either end and almost nothing living beneath their middle spans, which may be why they retain old deformations so faithfully. They cannot correct their shapes without ceasing to be bridges.

Where these underground territories meet, the settlement is crowded beyond imagining. Roots lie layered like bundled cable. Some carry water, some pressure, some heat, some only chemical messages. Thick storage trunks descend among delicate sensory threads. Sealed cysts hold replacement parts for machines that have not failed in centuries. There are blind chambers in which new valves slowly harden around moulds grown from the remains of old ones.

The borders are marked by bitterness. A root entering another body’s territory encounters bands of resin whose taste conveys ownership, hazard, permitted depth, and sometimes an obsolete instruction. Most boundaries are respected. Others are tested repeatedly. A warehouse on the eastern court has spent eighty years extending one root beneath a cooling trench; each season the trench burns the tip away, and each season the warehouse sends another. This is as close as the Works comes to a feud.

Its inhabitants do not think quickly. A decision may appear as a slight thickening continued over decades. Memory is stored in scar, grain, mineral deposit, and repeated form. The bridge remembers the boiler because each generation of replacement tissue grew against the bent generation before it. The empty dwellings remember windows because light alters the chemistry of their walls. Municipal law survives because disobedient growth tastes wrong.

Whether any part of this amounts to consciousness is difficult to say. The Works distinguish damage from weather, waste from useful matter, trespass from contact. They anticipate rain. They ration heat. When one hall dies, neighboring structures alter their growth to preserve its function. Yet they also continue meaningless practices with perfect diligence. The window membranes are made and removed. A buried pipe is kept clear though both ends have been sealed. One loading gantry produces a fresh hook every ninth year, lets it harden, and then drops it into the court. Beneath the moss lies a mound of hundreds.

Smaller lives occupy the civic bodies as tenants.

Grey films line the warm channels, eating residues from sap and oil. Thread-fungi carry minerals between unrelated roots, though they are tolerated only within narrow limits. When their blue fruiting stars appear on an exposed wall, the wall isolates the patch with dry tissue. Maintenance growths arrive later to peel it away. Some fungi seem to know this and fruit only on inward-facing surfaces, beyond sight of the calibration blooms.

The white tower mollusks are the most numerous animals, although even at their greatest abundance they are easy to miss. Their bodies are nearly transparent, their shells chalky and cool. They graze at night along the moisture combs, removing the crust left by evaporation. At the first pulse from the municipal tower they draw into recesses, not from fear of the sound but because the unfolding combs would otherwise sweep them into the cisterns.

They breed in the seams of collector leaves. Their eggs resemble beads of cloudy dew and are occasionally mistaken for water by the tower, which routes them through its fine channels. Many young mollusks therefore begin life deep inside the settlement and emerge days later from a tap, a gauge flower, or the weeping joint of a pipe.

The shell spirals at the column bases are not their nests. The mollusks show no interest in empty shells.

Nor does the black bird appear to make them. There may be only one such bird, or a succession of nearly identical birds. It comes without calling, always alone, walking rather than hopping. Its legs are too long for its narrow body, and its feathers carry a green sheen like old glass. It eats the soft fungal films exposed when a drain opens, but it does not hunt the mollusks. When startled, it rises only as far as the nearest gantry and watches until the disturbance ends.

The Works dislike its weight in the moisture crowns and tighten their upper branches when it lands there. Along the gutters they permit it. The bird seems to have learned the distinction.

Something else makes use of the shells.

It is never directly seen. Now and then, after a still night, empty shells are found arranged in descending order of size around a warm vent or fitted into a crack that had been leaking air. Some spirals correspond to pressure changes in the underground system; others serve no evident function. Service tendrils usually leave them undisturbed. If one is removed, it may reappear elsewhere, though never in precisely the same arrangement.

The largest resident lives below all ordinary roots.

Its pale back—or perhaps only a hardened ridge above it—occasionally presses against the underside of the roads during long rain. No opening large enough for it has been found. It may be an animal, a mobile root organ, or something that predates the division between the two. It moves only when the deepest soil is saturated. The Works detect it well before it reaches the basin: compressor rhythms falter, buried vessels empty upward, and sensitive root tips pull into sheaths.

As it passes, the town yields.

Road fibres separate without tearing. Water arteries close. Pressure chambers soften and flatten. The movement beneath the surface is slow enough to follow on foot, if there were anyone to follow it: a broad rising of the ground, then a settling, with occasional tremors like a sleeper turning under blankets. It does not feed visibly. It leaves no tunnel. Yet in the months after a passage, growth is unusually vigorous along its route, and dormant structures sometimes wake.

One rain season it passed beneath the abandoned dwellings. For the next seven years, those houses produced door membranes as well as window membranes, though no inherited rule required them. On the eighth year they stopped.

The Low Green Works therefore contain no population that can be counted cleanly. A single hall may be one life above ground, forty lives in its machinery, hundreds in its grafts, and part of a larger life in the exchange beneath. A road can die in sections. A tower can survive its roots. Two buildings may become one during drought and separate again when water returns.

They do not have names. They have chemical signatures, pressures, intervals, and shapes of response. They recognize one another by what may pass between them.

Near dusk, this dispersed population draws briefly into agreement. The municipal note travels through trunks, resin collars, roof leaves, and the feet of the silent bird. The compressor trees finish their strokes. Warm organs close themselves behind insulating tissue. Mollusks retreat into mineral seams. Far below, the pale sleeper—if it hears—gives no sign.

Then every exposed surface attends to the air.

The inhabitants of the Works do not look upward. They have no faces to lift. But as the droplets gather, millions of collecting hairs incline toward them, and the whole settlement assumes the posture of thirst.
