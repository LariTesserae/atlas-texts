The inhabitants are, at first, difficult to distinguish from the things that shelter them.

A cushion of black vegetation tightens when a shadow passes. What looked like frost on its surface lifts in a scatter of white-winged insects. A patch of gravel exhales and becomes a hare, its fur barred in limestone gray and volcanic brown. Even the larger grazers, seen resting with their legs folded beneath them, resemble slabs upholstered in coarse moss.

Life here has learned not to rise unnecessarily.

The plateau’s grazers are lean, deep-chested animals with long coats and narrow, divided hooves. Their shoulders stand higher than their hindquarters, giving them a forward-leaning posture even when still. The constant wind has combed generations of them into one shape: hair sleek on the eastern side, tangled into insulating skirts along the western flank. Their nostrils are broad, but the passages within are intricate and moist, recovering water from every breath. Beneath their ribs, oversized lungs work slowly. Their blood is nearly black.

They travel in old processions.

Each herd follows a circuit of seep, pool, meadow, and sheltered sleeping hollow. The order changes little. Older animals lead not by command but by memory, pausing at stones beneath which water can sometimes be heard. Behind them come the young, then the strongest adults, and finally one or two slow, broad-horned animals whose backward-curving horns guard the file from anything following along the path.

They do not graze a place bare. Their lips are hard and mobile, able to pinch off the newest rim of a cushion plant without tearing its rooted center from the crack. At the miniature meadows they feed in turns. Those waiting stand with their heads low and their eyes narrowed to shining slits, enduring the wind as if it were a current of water.

Their voices are almost vestigial. A mother calls to her young by opening her mouth and trembling her throat, but little sound escapes into the greater roar. The calf responds to the vibration through the ground when they stand close. Much of the herd’s language is carried in pressure and posture: a horn laid briefly against a shoulder, a stamping hoof, the sudden raising of every muzzle toward the same quarter.

The hares are even less conspicuous. They occupy rubble slopes where pale and dark stones mingle, and their coats are mottled without symmetry. Their ears are short, thick, and usually folded down. Large ears would freeze, dry, and fill with the plateau’s unending noise; instead, the hares listen through their jaws. Resting with chins against rock, they detect footfalls passing through the escarpment long before the walker appears.

When pursued, they do not run far across the open. They vanish downward into accidental chambers between stones, flattening their flexible bodies into spaces that seem too narrow to contain bone. Their young are born under sun-warmed slabs. For the first days they are almost hairless and very dark, absorbing heat from the rock above them.

The blunt-snouted burrowers share many of these shelters. Compact and heavy-clawed, they look equipped to dig, yet they rarely excavate more than a few handfuls of grit. Instead they are expert surveyors of natural fit. They test slabs with their foreheads, follow cool drafts to hidden cracks, and plug unsuitable openings with their own bodies while sleeping. Whole colonies inhabit the branching spaces beneath a single shelf of limestone.

Their social life is conducted by touch. They sleep pressed together in layered knots, adults around the outside and young in the center. On emerging, they rub cheek and flank against one another, exchanging the oily scent that identifies the colony. Their fur traps lichen dust, pollen, shed insect scales, and tiny seeds. As they move between hollows, they carry the plateau’s plants with them.

Small hunters wait at the margins of these colonies.

Some are lizard-like, flat-bodied and dark enough to warm within minutes. Their scales overlap tightly and have a dull, pitted surface like weathered stone. They hunt only when the sun is high, darting into wind shadows to seize beetles delivered from below. At the first passing cloud, they press their bellies to the rock and lose heat almost at once, becoming rigid until brightness returns.

Others are furred: quick, low creatures with wide paws and pale membranes over their eyes. They hunt by watching sheltered pockets rather than searching the open ground. A good wind shadow is a feeding place to which insects arrive all day. The hunter need only wait behind the correct stone.

Everything depends upon such places. To an inhabitant of the plateau, a boulder is not merely a boulder. It possesses an eastern face, a western silence, a noon temperature, a frost margin, a pattern of eddies, and perhaps a thin seasonal taste of water beneath it. Animals return to particular rocks across their entire lives. Their maps are made less of distance than of shelter.

Above them live the great dark birds.

They are long in body and narrow in wing, with feathers so stiff that the wind scarcely ruffles them. Their wings lock at the joints. Once placed in an updraft, they can remain suspended with almost no muscular effort, making minute corrections with the separated tips of their flight feathers. Seen from below, they seem nailed to the sky.

Their eyes are protected by clear inner lids. Their nostrils can close. Around the head, the feathers lie close as scales, while the breast is covered in a dense insulating down. They feed on the vast upward drift of insects, taking the larger forms in midair, but they also watch for dead grazers and for young animals strayed from shelter.

They are not noisy. Sound would be useless at ordinary distance, and so their bare throats carry patches of intense color: sulfur yellow, icy blue, a red almost black in shadow. These patches swell and flatten as the birds court, threaten, or recognize one another. A mated pair may hover side by side for an hour, exchanging only turns of the head and slight, formal alterations of wing.

Their nests lie deep inside the sounding cliffs.

The birds choose cavities not by quietness—the plateau offers none—but by the steadiness of their note. A chamber whose pitch shifts after rain may be unsafe. One that maintains the same low resonance through frost and noon heat is likely to endure. The birds learn these tones through their feet and breastbones. Before settling, they cling to the stone and remain motionless for long periods, reading the vibration.

Farther in, beyond the reach of direct light, live pale mites, silverfish-like scavengers, blind beetles, and soft-bodied things that feed on feathers, droppings, broken eggs, and windblown matter. The cliff is layered with habitation. Day-flying birds occupy its mouths. Small nocturnal fliers cling in the middle reaches. The deepest moist seams belong to creatures without eyes or pigment, some of which may never emerge onto the plateau at all.

The smaller birds live in multitudes.

They stream horizontally through the gaps, compact bodies riding inches above stone. Their wings are short and quick, but their tails are broad, serving as brakes when they enter a hole at speed. Many have nearly lost the habit of song. Their beaks still open in courtship, and their throats still move, but what remains is a faint click or pulse, audible only at nest distance.

Instead they flash.

White underwings mean alarm. A violet throat presented frontally is a claim to a nesting pocket. A lowered head and one raised wing invite a mate to exchange places on the eggs. Entire colonies alter color as they turn together, darkening and brightening like a single enormous skin stretched over the cliff.

Among them are the tool users.

They are neither the largest nor the most striking: soot-colored birds with pale feet and heavy, precise beaks. They collect armored beetles from behind stones and carry them to natural anvils—flat rocks crossed by fine, sharp fractures. A beetle placed over the wrong part of the stone will bounce away. Placed correctly, it splits with one blow.

Young birds learn by watching. For weeks they choose poor tools: stones too round to grip, flakes too brittle, hammers that are needlessly heavy. Gradually each acquires a preferred shape. Some hide useful stones in crevices near feeding grounds. Some steal them. Disputes are performed in rigid poses, throat patch exposed and beak lowered, though the wind removes whatever cries accompany the display.

They do not shape their tools. They do not enlarge the crevices where they keep them. The significant step—from finding a good edge to making one—has never been taken. Their intelligence remains fitted to the country as neatly as a burrower beneath its slab. They remember, select, carry, anticipate, and teach, but leave no monument except an insect shell broken cleanly in two.

Most numerous of all are the arrivals from beneath the clouds.

Every day the updraft delivers a living fall in reverse. Moths emerge first, pale and exhausted, their wings dusted with scales. Midges rise in veils. Beetles tumble upward with their legs tucked in, strike the plateau, and begin walking as though they had intended to come. Broad-winged gliders ascend in spirals and may remain aloft for days, feeding on smaller things suspended in the same current.

Some breed here. Many do not. They are weather made edible.

At certain hours, every sheltered surface becomes crowded with them. Lizards feed until their bodies swell. Small birds stand shoulder to shoulder behind ridges, snapping at the stream. Spiders stretch short, immensely strong threads across cracks, not to make broad webs—which the wind would destroy—but to snare whatever is driven into the gap. Their silk is thick, sparse, and frequently replaced.

Even the pools have inhabitants. Tiny dark crustaceans lie dormant in the dust, sealed inside clear cases through months of dryness. When rain fills a basin, they hatch within hours. They swim, feed, mate, and produce another generation of resting eggs before the water disappears. Their entire active world may last two days. To them, the plateau alternates not between seasons but between stone and sudden sky.

Nothing here is master of the rest.

The great birds depend on insects lifted from another world. The grazers depend on plants that grow by fractions. The plants depend on fur, droppings, dust, and the brief water carried into their cracks. Hunters wait upon accidents of wind. Scavengers inhabit the leftovers of nests. Even death is slow to belong to any one creature: a carcass is opened by birds, dried by air, polished by grit, furred with lichen, and finally taken back into the resemblance of stone.

The plateau’s inhabitants do not oppose its violence. They make themselves small within it. They flatten, brace, cling, suspend, hide, and wait. They speak in color because sound is occupied. They drink from places too slight to be called springs. They use the hollows already given to them and the edges already broken.

Their civilization, if the word can be stretched far enough, is one of recognition.

They know which silence behind a rock will fill with moths. They know which booming cavity will remain warm through the night. They know the sequence of black pools, the smell of rain on a particular eastern wall, the trembling of a herd still beyond the horizon. Their inheritance is not built and cannot be seen from afar. It passes in routes, pauses, postures, and choices.

Beneath the roaring, everything is listening.
