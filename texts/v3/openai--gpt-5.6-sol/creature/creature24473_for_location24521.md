From the rim, the inhabitants are almost impossible to distinguish from the work of the city. They move in the same clay colors as the walls, along the same narrow lines as the drains. A procession of basket carriers resembles spoil traveling uphill by itself. Surveyors stand motionless among stakes until they look like part of the measuring apparatus. Roof workers crouch in rows, beating wet plaster with flat paddles, and from above their shoulders repeat the rounded forms of jars, kilns, and storage mounds.

Only when one descends do they separate into people.

They are broad-footed, careful walkers. Children learn early not to trust a surface merely because it appears dry. They test the ground with their heels, then commit their weight. Adults carry poles even when they have no load, using them to probe lane edges and steady themselves on tracks polished by mud. Their gait is neither slow nor timid, but economical: a way of moving that assumes every path has a weak place and that falling is less shameful than damaging the thing one grabs.

Mud marks everyone below the knee. People wrap their calves in reed matting or strips of hide and wear sandals with wide woven soles that can be rinsed and rehung at the door. The wealthy are not cleaner so much as clean in narrower bands. Their hems may be white, their hands oiled, their hair fastened with copper pins, but the backs of their ankles still carry the basin’s gray print. Laborers rub clay into small cuts because it stops bleeding quickly, though healers scold them and reopen the wounds with boiled water and bitter leaves.

Most clothing comes from the upper country: coarse wool, hide, twisted plant fiber. Within the basin these materials are patched with whatever the city produces—cord, reed cloth, felted goat hair, softened rawhide. Rain capes are short so they do not drag. Hoods have stiff rims that shed water beyond the shoulders. Nearly everyone owns a narrow sash with loops for wedges, cord, a scraping blade, and a small plumb weight. Even people who do no building carry tools for clearing a drain.

Their hands are characteristic. The palms are thickened by rope and basket rims; the fingertips are kept sensitive. A mason must feel when clay is still wet beneath its pale skin. A householder presses a thumb to the lower wall each morning, judging whether moisture has risen in the night. Timber joints are tested by laying the cheek against them while someone walks on the roof. Much of the city is inspected by touch because its most dangerous changes are too slight to see.

The population has come from many parts of the surrounding uplands. Some arrived as organized crews under foremen, others as families promised a house plot, others because they had lost land elsewhere or owed labor to a sponsor. There are stone cutters from the chalk country, basket makers from reed marshes beyond the western rim, potters who recognize the basin’s clays by taste, and herders who have discovered that their old knowledge is nearly useless here. Accents remain distinct, but the vocabulary of level, seepage, settling, and load has spread through every district. People who cannot understand one another’s jokes can still shout the same warnings.

No one is wholly a specialist. The baker knows how to brace a wall. The scribe can clear a silt trap. A midwife carries wedges in her birthing bag because floors shift and doors jam. Market sellers keep scoops beside their stalls not only for grain but for the sudden brown water that enters under the matting. At the first drops of rain, every occupation becomes drainage work.

The most respected inhabitants are the measurers. They are not imposing figures. Many are elderly, spare, and permanently stooped from sighting along water bowls. They carry marked staffs wrapped in waxed cloth, cords with stone or fired-clay weights, shallow dishes, pegs, tablets, and little bags of colored powder. Their authority comes from detecting differences that others cannot: a finger’s fall across a courtyard, a wall beginning to rotate, a street whose apparent slope runs opposite to its true one.

A measurer can halt the raising of a house, order a threshold cut down, or require an entire row to surrender part of its floors to a new channel. This gives them enemies. They are accused of favoring high districts, of accepting gifts from kiln owners, of moving stakes at night. Yet when rain begins, even those who curse them send children running to fetch them.

Their records are less like maps than layered arguments. Streets are shown several times at different heights. A house may occupy the same ground as an old sump and a future embankment. Boundaries are noted, but levels are written larger. Beside many names is a small mark indicating obligation: who must clear which grating, who supplies reeds to a culvert, who must open a courtyard wall if the public channel overflows. Property here is not simply the right to exclude. It is the duty to pass something onward.

This shapes manners. One does not pour wash water without calling a warning. One does not block a lane with baskets, even briefly, when the sky is dark. A guest entering a house asks after the household’s roof before asking after its health. To praise a person, one says that nothing stagnates around them. To accuse someone of selfishness, one says they have raised their threshold.

Quarrels are frequent but rarely concern the exact position of a wall. Since walls are often shared, no one can pretend to complete independence. Disputes concern consequences: whose new floor drowned whose storeroom, whose kiln dried and cracked an adjoining foundation, whose goats stripped the roofing from a neighbor’s repair bundle. Hearings are held in courtyards with the relevant channels left open for inspection. Water may be poured from a jar to demonstrate a claim. Litigants crouch and watch it travel.

The rich occupy the driest patches, but wealth cannot be hidden behind a gate. It appears as depth and redundancy: two drains instead of one, stone under a floor instead of brush, imported beams thick enough to trim and reset, a cistern lined with fired tile, a private stair to an upper entrance. Wealthy households own carts, sponsor retaining walls, and maintain labor crews through the wet season. They also live surrounded by resentment, because every improvement they make displaces weight or water. A grand house is admired only if the adjoining lanes remain dry.

The poorest inhabit the city’s newest edges and its oldest hollows. Edge dwellers live beside raw cut faces, where stones loosen at night and no one yet knows where runoff will gather. Hollow dwellers occupy rooms that have sunk below the streets. Their doors are approached by descending steps; their ceilings bear old smoke beneath newer beams; their walls show the buried outlines of former thresholds. Some such families refuse relocation because a sunken house is cool, close to a well, and already paid for. They build raised sleeping platforms and keep jars on shelves near the roof.

Households are seldom confined to a single building. A family may sleep in one room, cook in a shared court, store grain three lanes away on firmer ground, and keep goats on the roof of an unrelated household in exchange for milk. Kinship spreads through walls and levels. Two houses sharing a foundation may be considered closer than cousins. Children are fostered uphill during the wet months. Older people move downhill, where water is easier to reach and fewer stairs must be climbed, though everyone knows the lower rooms are the first to flood.

Children belong to the roofs and scaffolds more than adults would like. They are light enough to cross surfaces that will not bear a loaded worker. They crawl through culverts to retrieve obstructions, carry messages above blocked lanes, and learn the city as a set of connected terraces. Their games imitate construction. They build tiny walls around puddles, cut channels with shells, and argue about which quarter must be sacrificed when the water rises. After storms they hunt frogs, treating each appearance as a minor miracle. They keep them briefly in jars, then release them into sumps despite warnings that the creatures clog screens.

The elderly remember the basin before districts had names. Their recollections are strangely difficult for younger people to imagine. They speak of uninterrupted distances, of walking from one escarpment to the other without turning aside, of the first wells filling overnight, of wheel ruts that became channels and channels that dictated streets. They can point to a crowded market and say there was once a stump there with rainwater around it. Such memories are not revered without question. The city changes too quickly. An old person’s accurate knowledge of where water flowed twenty years ago may now be dangerously wrong.

Death presents a problem because the ground does not keep boundaries. Graves dug in the basin fill with seepage or settle beneath later construction. Most bodies are therefore carried back up to terraces cut into the rim, an ascent performed before dawn while the tracks are cool and firm. Those who die in accidents of construction—buried by a slipped wall, crushed under a cart, lost in a collapsed excavation—are sometimes memorialized where they fell by a stone built visibly into the repair. Their names are pressed into wet plaster and renewed whenever the wall is resurfaced. The oldest names survive only because someone continues tracing them.

Their beliefs are practical without being irreligious. Water is not worshiped as a kindly giver. It is treated as a visitor that must never be insulted by imprisonment. At new construction, a cup is poured into the intended drain before the first wall is raised. If it runs cleanly, work begins. If it pools, the plan is altered. Small offerings—onion skins, a twist of wool, a pinch of upland soil—are placed at junctions where one channel divides into two. The offering is not meant to purchase favor so much as acknowledge choice.

They have stories of buried waters searching for their old courses beneath the city. In these stories, cracks are not random: they are the marks of forgotten streams turning in their sleep. Surveyors publicly dismiss this, then leave small gaps in foundations where seepage persists. Builders say every wall must contain a little yielding or it will provoke the ground.

There are few festivals that do not involve repair. At the start of the cool season, districts clean their channels together and whitewash the marks used to measure flood height. Musicians play on roofs while workers pass baskets of silt hand to hand. The silt is taken to garden courts and mixed with imported soil. By evening, the drains are empty, the planting beds are higher, and everyone eats beans, goat curd, onions, and flat bread near the newly cleared mouths of the channels.

Food is plain and closely guarded. Grain arrives from above in sealed jars or hide sacks, counted at the rim and again in the markets below. Kiln heat dries beans and roots against spoilage. Onions are prized because they tolerate cool storage; bitter greens because they grow quickly in carried soil. Goat milk is consumed fresh or made into hard pellets. Meat is uncommon outside accidents, ritual days, and the slaughter of animals too weak to climb out.

The dogs are lean, short-coated, and unusually quiet. Loud barking in narrow lanes brings curses, and generations of the noisiest animals have been driven to the edge. They know the raised tracks better than visitors do and wait before crossing ground that smells newly opened. Some accompany surveyors, sleeping beside staffs and cords. Others live around kilns, where the ground is warm and dry.

The goats are less suited to the city but more necessary. They are small enough to be carried when ill and agile enough to live on terraces. Their hooves damage plaster, their droppings clog gutters, and their appetite for reeds makes them a constant source of disputes. Children lead them along roof ridges to patches of cut grass. During rain they are tethered inside upper rooms, where they stare down through smoke holes and complain.

The people themselves rarely look upward for long. The sky is too open to need watching except for weather, and the rim belongs to journeys, imports, funerals, and birds. Their attention is directed sideways and down: to the foot of a wall, the color of clay, the bend in a roof beam, the tremor of water in a bowl. Visitors from the uplands sometimes mistake this lowered gaze for sullenness. It is concentration.

Yet they are not merely cautious. There is audacity in the scale of what they are doing. Every inhabitant lives with evidence that the basin has no settled nature, and still they make long plans. They arrange marriages between districts not yet connected by roads. They plant fruit trees against walls expected to sink. They mark future squares on untouched clay. They apprentice children to trades that will only be needed when the city reaches the western escarpment.

Their confidence is not the confidence that the ground will become stable. Few believe that. It is confidence that instability can be answered indefinitely: with another layer, another channel, another shared wall, another measurement taken at dawn.

Over time, the inhabitants have come to resemble the city’s method. They make room by passing burdens onward. They survive through mutual pressure. They accept that repair is not a temporary condition but the proper state of things. No house stands alone, no street remains at one level, no decision ends where it was made.

Seen closely, the city is full of separate lives: jealous households, exhausted carriers, proud masons, watchful granary keepers, children with frogs cupped in muddy hands. Seen from the rim, these distinctions disappear again. The people become one dense, deliberate creature spread across the basin floor—soft-bodied, many-handed, continually sinking, and continually building itself upward.
