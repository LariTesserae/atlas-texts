At first, the inhabitants seem to be merely all the small things that make use of what the builders left behind.

There are socket-dwellers, soft as organs and without fixed adult shapes. In youth they are free-swimming flecks, each carrying a weak red light. They drift until they find an empty hinge cup, a cracked mortar joint, the hollow where a bronze pin dissolved long ago. There they pour themselves inward. Their bodies conform to whatever absence receives them. One becomes round, another long and folded, another thin as a sheet pressed between courses of brick. Only their feeding crowns remain exposed: little colorless fans that close whenever pressure changes. An old wall may contain thousands, so that the masonry itself appears to breathe.

The white grazers pass over these colonies without disturbing them. They have many joints but no clear division between limb and body. Either end may lead. When a passage narrows, they loosen themselves joint by joint and pull their length through like a chain being drawn from a drain. They feed on the velvet films covering tile and stone, leaving clean tracks whose curves persist for years. Their mouths contain minute ceramic grains, retained and used as teeth. As the grains wear smooth, they are swallowed and replaced.

Some grazers are older than the routes they follow. A blocked channel may leave one circling the same pillar for centuries, tracing a pale band into the film. If the pillar eventually falls, the animal continues across the new surface without hesitation. It does not preserve the old path. It preserves only the turning.

The vertical fish are more numerous but less conspicuous. They hang head-up or head-down according to the faintest current, held in place by fins that tremble too quickly to be seen. Their bodies are nearly transparent. The dark line of the gut, the pale hinge of the jaw, and a row of blue organs along the flank are all that distinguish them from the water. They have no lids and little that could be called an eye. The blue organs perceive one another.

Their signals are slow. A light begins near the tail and travels toward the head over several minutes. A neighboring fish may answer at once, or after an hour, or not at all. At times a message appears to cross an entire room, each animal passing the blue seam onward until it enters a doorway and is lost. At other times hundreds pulse without sequence, as though remembering different parts of the same event.

The bell-swimmers cluster beneath ceilings. They are delicate enough that the steady seep through a mortar crack can hold a whole colony in place. Each bell contains a white knot, and within that knot lives a smaller creature which never enters the water. The bell catches food; the knot digests it. Neither can survive alone. When a bell dies, its knot sometimes remains luminous on the stone for several days, pulsing after the surrounding flesh has become transparent and dispersed.

The copper-tube worms are almost never seen. Their works are everywhere. They occupy lever holes, tally cuts, the mouths of narrow pipes, even the porous bodies of old bricks. From dissolved metal they produce branching tubes finer than hair. These structures begin as shelters but may outlast many generations of worms, growing by inheritance into dense reddish webs. A slight change in pressure makes the webs ring against the stone.

The sound is too faint for any single swimmer to hear as sound. They feel it instead along their flanks, in their joints, through the thin floors of their sockets. A disturbance in one chamber can travel through the buried metalwork and emerge far away as a trembling in the walls. Thus a tile collapse announces itself beyond sealed doors. So does the passing of a ray. So, perhaps, does the movement of the plug.

The flat inscription-dwellers are among the cistern’s longest-lived animals. They begin as shapeless gray patches no larger than a fingernail. On finding incised stone, they spread into the grooves, following letters, borders, faces, and tally marks with exact fidelity. In time they cover the carving completely. The side exposed to the water becomes smooth and black; the hidden side takes on every cut and pit beneath it.

When one finally detaches, it carries the inscription on its underside in soft relief.

For a while, before the animal sags and loses the pattern, it is a living cast of words no inhabitant can read. It may move across a wall bearing the reversed name of a vanished builder. It may fold that name through a doorway, distort it around a pillar, and finally consume the organic residue caught in its strokes. The writing then relaxes back into flesh.

Armored scavengers follow such animals. They are small, deliberate creatures with many fine legs and no permanent shell. Each selects fragments from the ruin: glaze, shell, tube metal, mineral crust, occasionally a tiny carved chip. It cements these over its back in overlapping layers. Young scavengers wear bright, sharp debris. The oldest carry rounded mosaics thick enough to conceal the body entirely.

They test loose pieces by tapping them against the floor. Most of the cistern’s isolated knocks are theirs.

A scavenger may spend years dragging one blue ceramic flake from chamber to chamber before finding its balance unsatisfactory and abandoning it. Another may retain the same fragment until both shell and carrier are buried. Their armor is not camouflage; nothing here hunts by color. It is ballast, protection against pressure changes, and perhaps a form of bodily memory. A colony gathered in one place can contain samples from parts of the mountain now inaccessible even to water.

Above and among them move the shroud rays.

A ray is less like a fish than like a portion of darkness that has acquired thickness. Its upper surface absorbs nearly every living light. Its underside is marked with shallow folds and rows of sensory pits. The body has no rigid frame. It can narrow itself through a gap scarcely wider than a doorway, then unfold across half a chamber.

It feeds on almost nothing visible. Water passes through pleated chambers inside it, and the smallest suspended life fails to emerge. Yet feeding is only a fraction of what it does. Most of its time is spent resting or traveling a circuit so old that portions of the route have changed around it. Where a corridor has silted closed, a ray presses its edge into the sediment over successive passages until a new channel forms. Where a wall has fallen, it learns the altered opening. Its body remembers architecture as pressure and touch.

Each ray carries a population.

Bells adhere to its underside. Grazers travel its back. Tube-worm larvae hide in its sensory pits. Vertical fish gather in the slow water behind it. Even inscription-dwellers sometimes spread across its skin, fitting themselves to faint scar patterns in place of carved stone. When the ray crosses from one chamber to another, whole communities move with it. A room repopulates not by invasion but by the arrival of a floor that swims.

The largest rays bear pale circular scars near the center of the body. The scars match the Crown’s plug in curvature, though not in size. No ray could have made them by touching it as it is now. Perhaps the plug once opened farther. Perhaps the rays once passed through it when they were small and retained the mark as they grew. Perhaps the resemblance means nothing.

Beneath all these creatures lies the film.

It has no boundary. It coats warm tile, enters brick pores, lines worm tubes, and survives in the guts of fish. What appears in one place as colorless velvet appears elsewhere as black mineral varnish, translucent wax, or a faint skin over sediment. Its colonies exchange no visible tendrils, yet disturbances alter them across great distances. A chamber darkened by collapse may be followed, decades later, by a bloom in a remote stairwell. Compounds released from one wall are consumed many rooms away. The bodies of the dead disappear into it and return as bells, fish, tubes, armor, and blue light.

It may be many species. It may be one association that no longer has a meaningful outside.

Nothing in the cistern possesses a language in the builders’ sense. Nothing keeps plans or counts seasons. Yet the inhabitants respond together often enough to suggest something larger than coincidence.

Before a pressure reversal, socket-dwellers close their fans. Fish rotate in place. Bells contract toward the vaults. Grazers stop with several limbs lifted. Rays settle against the floor. The changes begin in different chambers at different times, sometimes long before the current itself arrives. Information may pass through copper tubes, through the bacterial film, through water, or through the accumulated touch of bodies. No creature knows the whole condition. The cistern knows it in pieces.

This is clearest in the Crown.

The assembly beneath the plug is not fixed. Individual animals arrive and leave. Fish die there and are eaten. Bells divide. Grazers cross the ceiling and descend again. Rays rest until film grows across their backs. Yet the gathering as a whole persists. When one layer thins, another thickens. Empty water beneath the pale disk is quickly occupied.

The colder seep may carry rare minerals. It may bear the chemical trace of an ocean inaccessible to the lower rooms. It may resemble the water in which some ancestral form once bred. But not all the gathered species feed on it, and some seem harmed by prolonged exposure. Still they come.

They arrange themselves by sensitivity.

Closest to the plug are thread-fish whose bodies are almost entirely sensory tissue. Beneath them hang the bells. The grazers grip the ceiling and one another in loose chains. Socket-dwellers fill every joint in the dome. Scavengers wedge themselves between the resting rays, weighted with pieces of distant chambers. The rays lie lowest, black body against pale floor, their edges touching.

When the plug rises, the response is immediate only in appearance.

The extinction of light begins before the colder water has crossed the room. Blue seams vanish, then red points, then the white knots of the bells. The darkness passes outward through the cistern faster than the entering current. Every animal capable of producing light suppresses it. Feeding crowns close. Fish stop straining. Grazers flatten themselves into grooves. Tube webs cease trembling.

The rays lift their edges.

For those minutes, the entire population assumes a single posture: closed, still, receptive. The cistern ceases to look inhabited because its inhabitants remove every sign by which they might be distinguished from masonry and water.

Something enters with the cold.

Perhaps only pressure. Perhaps a dissolved substance in quantities too small to nourish anything. Perhaps a vibration from the ocean above, transmitted through the lifted stone. Whatever it is, no single animal appears to receive it. The bells take one part, the worms another, the rays another. The film changes its chemistry. The armored scavengers feel the altered weight of the water through fragments of ancient tile.

Then the plug settles, and the whole separates again.

A fish kindles beneath an arch. A socket opens. A bell releases its white pulse. The rays unfold and resume their circuits. Creatures that seemed, for a few minutes, to be organs of one dark body return to grazing, filtering, scavenging, and waiting.

There is no monarch in the Crown Cistern. There is no hidden priest, no last builder, no pale intelligence crouched behind the plug.

What lives there is slower and less divisible.

It lives in borrowed cavities and inherited routes. It wears the mountain as shell, gut, nursery, and memory. Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, take centuries to cross a room. Its smallest gestures are performed by thousands of bodies that never know they are participating. It does not preserve the builders’ world, but neither does it merely erase it. It translates pillars into pathways, inscriptions into flesh, copper into nerves, and collapse into new habitat.

The handmade mountain is becoming its body.

The inhabitants are how it happens.
