What lives there is difficult to count.

At first it appears to be a community of small, pallid things: grazers, crawlers, mites, swimmers, soft-bodied ambushes disguised as flowers. Each can be separated from the chamber, at least briefly. Each has a mouth or an equivalent, a boundary to its flesh, a sequence of hungers by which it may be recognized as an animal.

Yet none remains itself for long outside the hollow.

The white six-legged grazers, for example, are born without a fixed number of limbs. Their eggs form as clear beads along the edges of warm roots, and what hatches depends on the route the root expects them to travel. Four legs are sufficient for the broad leaf-scales near the pools. Eight are grown for the trembling curtains overhead. Six is only the common compromise. A grazer moved from one district to another will molt repeatedly until its body agrees with the new terrain. If prevented from molting, it becomes directionless. It walks in tightening circles, not because it cannot find food, but because the shape of its body is giving it obsolete instructions.

They graze less on tissue than on excess. They eat stray warmth, accumulations of sugar, unrealized buds, and the pale cellular froth produced when two plants fail to complete a graft. Their transparent bodies contain no true gut. The dark thread visible inside each one is a temporary root, borrowed from the chamber and coiled through its abdomen. When the animal is full, it presses itself against the ceiling and returns the root. It then goes empty and almost featureless until another root hair enters it.

The beetle-shaped gardeners are more independent. They have shells the color of old ice and deep basins in their backs, each containing a miniature landscape of black algae, crystalline lichen, and red filamentous growths. These gardens are inherited but not intact. When two beetles meet, they stand side by side for days while the growths on their backs exchange spores and chemical memories. Their bodies scarcely acknowledge one another. The gardens conduct the courtship.

Young beetles hatch naked and must steal their first planting. Most take scrapings from sleeping adults. Some raid the dead. A few approach the amber-sealed remains of animals killed by the plants and wait beside them until the sap softens. Such beetles carry ancient strains in their backs—algae from chambers that no longer exist, molds adapted to pressures the hollow has not experienced for millennia. Wherever they walk, nearby roots bend slightly toward them, as if reading old letters.

The pale amphibians are not always animals. During their long motionless periods they lose most of their internal structure. Bone dissolves first, then muscle separates into pearl-colored strands, and finally the brain loosens into a milky lace spread beneath the skin. Root hairs grow through them without causing wounds. Months later, when water chemistry changes, the lace gathers itself, the bones precipitate again, and the amphibian pulls free.

It does not always reassemble with the same memories.

This may account for the solemn gatherings around the deep pool. At irregular intervals, dozens of the creatures crawl from their crevices and arrange themselves along the margin, frills open, forefeet in the water. They remain there while the engraved leaves below rearrange their branching lines. Afterward they disperse. Some return to old hiding places. Others attempt to enter walls that have not opened yet. A few remember alcoves belonging to possible versions of the chamber and spend the rest of their lives waiting before solid rootwork, certain of a passage no one else can perceive.

The red mites drinking from them are not parasites in any simple sense. They remove minute errors from the blood: proteins folded according to abandoned temperatures, cells prepared for predators that never evolved, antibodies against diseases that existed only in the chamber’s discarded shapes. The mites swell crimson with these rejected possibilities. Then they climb into the root curtains and burst.

Where their contents touch, the finest root hairs recoil. Thus the hollow learns what not to become.

The ribbon swimmers are harder to classify. Each is a continuous strip with a mouth on both surfaces and no permanent inside. Turning itself through itself, it exposes one mode of life, then the other. One side feeds on dissolved minerals and flecks of moss. The reverse side feeds on distances. As it passes through a pool, opposite banks become imperceptibly nearer or farther apart. A single swimmer changes almost nothing. A spawning coil of thousands can lengthen a channel enough to keep it from freezing.

They do not lay eggs. They tie knots in themselves. The knots tighten, become opaque, and eventually detach as young. Very old swimmers are crowded with untied places and move erratically, parts of them arriving before the rest. These elders are sometimes found half embedded in sealed pools beyond the chamber wall. The roots neither release nor digest them. Blue light gathers around their exposed edges, suggesting a form of reverence or study.

Above the water live the heavy-winged animals, which possess no muscles capable of flight. Folded, they resemble gray seed husks. Open, their wings reveal maps of veins that closely match those in nearby leaves, though never the leaves of the present chamber. To travel, one opens its wings and grips a root hair. Somewhere in the surrounding weave, a plant receives the animal’s readiness as its own. Leaves bend. Air is displaced. The creature is carried.

No signal visibly passes between them. There is only a brief uncertainty about which body desired the motion.

Their colonies hang in the finest roots, bound together by threads extruded from their mouths. They build no chambers. Instead they produce a communal tangle in which sleeping bodies, eggs, discarded skins, and living root tips are indistinguishable. Larvae hatch directly against the plants and feed on pulses of intention before they learn to eat matter. Those deprived of such pulses survive, but never initiate movement. They remain awake and still until touched, responding perfectly to every force and desiring nothing.

Predators flourish among these lives, although hunger seldom belongs to one creature alone.

The glass-jawed crawlers are long, low animals with translucent heads and jaws that close sideways through one another without meeting. A crawler chooses its prey early, often while both are immature. It then follows at a distance, sometimes for years. It eats what the prey eats, rests when it rests, and gradually cultivates the same chemical preferences. Only when predator and prey have become nearly identical in appetite does the final approach occur.

The crawler does not seize its victim. It settles beside it.

Their circulations synchronize. Their body temperatures equalize. The jaw closes around a shared patch of moss or a sweetened droplet, and for a time it is unclear which animal is consuming which. Usually the grazer diminishes while the crawler grows. Occasionally the reverse occurs, and the intended prey walks away with a new transparent skull forming around its old one.

There are also the blossoms that persuade animals to remain. These are not rooted in a single location. Beneath the overlapping floor, their feeding organs migrate from bulb to bulb, flowering wherever a pattern of movement suggests future hunger. A bud may take fifty years to reach the surface. By the time it opens, its intended prey may not yet have been born.

Its scent then waits in the animal’s ancestry.

Generations become a little more inclined toward a certain mineral taste, a certain warmth, a certain shade of green. Eventually one descendant arrives and experiences the flower not as an encounter but as the fulfillment of a lifelong preference. It nestles among the petals. The blossom closes over months. Often the animal could leave until the final day.

It does not.

Still, these creatures are not the principal inhabitant. Nor are the roots, exactly.

The plants possess no central body. Every bulb may quarrel with the bulb beside it. Root systems strangle one another, counterfeit one another’s chemical signatures, steal stored weather, and force grafts across unwilling tissue. There are ancient wars here, advancing at the width of a fingernail per century. One faction favors deeper cold and grows clear needles into neighboring veins. Another releases warmth in brief false springs, causing its rivals to flower themselves into exhaustion. Amber tombs mark old victories, though some of the sealed plants remain alive and continue transmitting slow signals through their resin.

And yet, when the ice shifts overhead, the quarrels cease.

Roots that have poisoned one another for generations share load. Stolen sugars are returned. Light crosses dead zones. The opposed growths agree on an arch, a pressure, a survivable arrangement of water. For the duration of the danger they behave not as allies but as organs that have remembered the body to which they belong.

Afterward they separate and resume killing one another.

This larger body has no continuous existence. It occurs when necessary.

It occurs in the instant before a ceiling knot takes weight. It occurs when all the winged creatures desire to travel at once. It occurs during the synchronized stillness of the amphibians, in the maps on the aquatic leaves, in the adjustments made around a ribbon swimmer’s passage. At other times it is absent, leaving only a crowded wet ecology of competing parts.

If it has awareness, awareness is one of its emergency structures.

If it has memory, that memory is distributed among incompatible things: in beetles’ gardens, mites’ blood, the preferences of unborn prey, the geometry of chambers not yet opened. No single portion knows the whole. Indeed, the whole may exist precisely because it cannot be known from one position. To see it completely would require an observer to stand in every possible version of the hollow at once, and the plants appear to prevent this with the same care by which they prevent collapse.

The deepest pool may be where this intermittent being comes nearest to a mind.

Beneath its colorless leaves, below the reach of the ribbon swimmers, there is no ordinary bottom. Roots descend for a distance, sheathed in mineral shells, then curve away from a region the water refuses to enter. Silt gathers around its boundary in the shape of a broad oval. The oval is not empty; emptiness would have pressure, temperature, dimensions. It is instead omitted.

Every few centuries the engraved leaves align their branching marks toward it. Conditions stored throughout the chamber are released: old warmth, vanished rain, pressures from deeper winters. The pool freezes in one instantaneous sheet. Animals fall silent not from fear, but because the impulses by which they would make sound have been borrowed.

Then comes the knock.

It is not loud at its source. It becomes loud by arriving through every root at nearly the same moment. Some creatures hear it as impact. The amphibians experience it as a memory of falling. The winged animals open their wings. Predatory flowers loosen. Beetles’ gardens release clouds of spores. For an instant, every living thing in the chamber behaves as though something has entered.

Nothing appears.

Instead, a new distance becomes available.

A root tip emerges along an unfinished wall. Water finds a fractionally altered slope. An animal remembers a passage that was solid moments before. The hollow acquires another alcove, though no material has been displaced to make it. Life enters the addition cautiously, then greedily. Moss darkens it. Grazers revise their legs. A flower begins preparing for an animal centuries away.

Far below, whatever made the knock does not wake. Waking may be too small and animal a word for its condition. Perhaps it is the oldest root, folded through distances the upper plants have only learned to manipulate. Perhaps it is a seed for which the entire hollow is merely the first softening of the shell. Perhaps it is not alive at all until the chamber briefly assembles a life large enough to include it.

The inhabitants do not worship it. They have no need to distinguish it from weather, anatomy, or law.

They live in its hesitation.

And slowly, by eating and freezing, hunting and grafting, remembering impossible corridors and returning borrowed roots, they give that hesitation a body.
