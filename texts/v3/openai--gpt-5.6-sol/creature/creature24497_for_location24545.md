What lives there is first mistaken for a forest, then for a colony, then for one organism, and finally for something to which none of those distinctions quite apply.

Its oldest living parts are buried deepest. They are slow tissues, compressed nearly to stone, which may complete a pulse only once in a day. Through them pass changes of pressure too gradual to feel: the stored weight of rain, the arrival of frost, the alteration of sunlight on a basin many kilometres away. They do not photosynthesize. They remember loads. They brace chambers, close failures, and carry the chemical traces of seasons no surface growth has survived.

Above them is the active commonwealth.

It has no enduring face. Its organs rise where conditions require them: chimneys where heat must be shed, black needle plains where weak light must be gathered, grinding mouths where rock has become accessible, pale bladders where vapour can be retained. Each form appears complete in itself. Cut free, however, it soon becomes inert, like a hand separated from its body. Left attached, it may be dismantled when its work is finished and replaced by something with no resemblance to it.

The nearest thing it has to a nervous system is a network of excitable fibres threaded through the rootwood. A wound sends both chemical bitterness and a change in tension. The fast signal travels first. Leaves contract, seams close, and adhesive threads rise around the damage. The slower signal follows in the internal rivers, carrying information about what has been lost: water, sugars, iron, structural fibre. Hours later, new tissue begins to form in answer. The land does not know what injured it. It knows only the dimensions and chemistry of the absence.

Its responses can therefore seem intelligent without being discerning. A warm body resting too long in one place is treated first as weather, then as shade, then as obstruction, and eventually as material. The mesh cups itself beneath the sleeper. Fine exploratory hairs touch cloth, fur, skin, horn, metal. Harmless surfaces are grown around. Soluble ones are tasted. Anything that remains is incorporated slowly and without hostility.

Within this greater life are innumerable lesser partners.

Films of colourless cells line the warm conduits, feeding on compounds the upper tissues cannot use. Mineral-digesters inhabit the root mills and tolerate acids that would destroy the surrounding fibres. In the fermentation chambers live soft, eyeless bodies like folded ribbons. They swim through decomposing pulp, consuming moulds and being consumed in turn when the chamber contracts. Some possess hooks; some cilia; some no stable anatomy at all. When food is abundant they divide. When the chamber is drained, they fuse into glassy beads and travel upward in the sap.

They may be animals. They may be mobile organs. Their young cannot mature without substances produced by the landscape, and the landscape cannot efficiently reclaim its dead depths without them.

The white crawlers seen at ruptured rivers are among the few such creatures to emerge into daylight. They are assembled-looking things, translucent and asymmetrical, with legs added in pairs at successive moults. A young one has six. An old, well-fed individual may have ten or twelve, though limbs are also shed when the channels narrow. Green fluid shows through its skin in branching reservoirs. It has no mouth visible from above; beneath its head is a rasping pad with which it shaves nutrient films from exposed conduit walls.

Crawlers gather quickly around a breach. They drink, graze, and press their bodies into the leak. Feeding threads close over them. Most withdraw before the rupture seals, but some remain embedded, encysting themselves in the repair tissue. Months later they emerge elsewhere, pale and reduced, from a newly opened water sac. Whether they are trapped by the healing or deliberately participate in it is unknown.

The mobile flowers also belong to this uncertain category. Detached from their stalks, they creep on thousands of moist cilia, following minute gradients of heat and resinous scent. At rest they resemble small frost-stiffened blossoms. In motion the petals lift clear of the surface and expose a dark underside ridged with contractile bands.

They travel mostly at night. Two flowers meeting exchange packets of dust and living cells, then separate. Some return to an open seam and dissolve into it. Others climb the highest available growth and become rigid there, waiting for the wind to pull them apart. On still mornings, one occasionally finds a cluster of them gathered around a warm crack, their pale vanes opening and closing as though breathing together.

The twig-limbed grazers are more plainly animals, though much of their survival depends on resembling what the land has already abandoned.

They stand taller than a person at the shoulder but weigh remarkably little. Their bodies are narrow frames of hollow bone and dark tendon, draped in a sparse nap of grey-brown filaments. When motionless among the frost meadows, they look like wind-thrown branches caught upright. Their joints lock, allowing them to remain still for hours without effort. Condensation gathers on their dorsal hairs and runs along grooves toward the mouth.

Their lips are divided into many delicate points. With these they test every frond before biting. Healthy tissue gives off a faint electrical pulse and is left untouched. A leaf already designated for shedding has lost that pulse; the grazer clips it at the prepared seam, doing no more damage than the landscape intended. In this way it feeds without provoking enclosure.

Older grazers bear patches of living felt along the legs and flanks. The felt matches the chemistry of the local rootwood and may disguise their pressure and warmth. It also slowly invades them. Dead skin disappears beneath it. Minor wounds close with resin rather than scar tissue. Very old individuals move stiffly, their joints webbed by green-black fibres.

No carcass has been found. When a grazer can no longer keep ahead of the growth on its body, it appears to seek a warm subsidence hollow. The surface opens. Whether the animal is consumed, joins the deep symbiosis, or merely dies there cannot be separated into different possibilities.

They reproduce rarely. In the coldest weeks, pairs meet on the southern ridges and stand face to face without touching. Each exhales into the other’s sensory fringes. Afterward one carries beneath its ribs a sealed, woody capsule rather than a visibly developing young. The capsule is eventually wedged into a shedding meadow. The land covers it but does not digest it. Several months later, a small six-limbed grazer cuts itself out, soft enough at first to walk without triggering much response from the surface.

The black glider is the most independent creature in the uplands and perhaps the loneliest.

Its wings are long membranes supported by jointed fingers, their upper surfaces matte enough to disappear against rock-shadow. It has little muscle for sustained flight. Instead it circles chimney fields, reading invisible columns of warm air, and may remain aloft for an entire afternoon without a wingbeat. The tail divides into thin steering streamers that continually alter their curl.

From the ground it seems to be searching. It descends only when seed vanes rise or when a rupture exposes white crawlers. Then it folds one wing, slips sideways through the warm updraft, and takes its food without landing. Its narrow jaw opens like scissors. Sometimes it carries away a mobile flower intact.

At dusk it settles on a pale tower and drives hooked feet through the annual skin. The tower responds by thickening around the intrusion, so the glider must shift roosts before morning or be fixed in place. Abandoned roost marks occur on many high structures, but rarely more than one set at a time. No flock has been seen. No answering cry has ever been heard. Perhaps each glider occupies hundreds of kilometres. Perhaps the circling black shapes observed in different basins are all the same long-lived animal.

Even it is not wholly separate. Spores adhere to grooves beneath its wings and are carried farther than the wind would reliably take them. The flowering valves open when its shadow passes repeatedly overhead. Chimney crowns sometimes increase their heat as it circles, prolonging the updraft. These responses may be incidental. They may be an old exchange between lives.

There are other presences known only by their effects.

Something tunnels through the mineralized depths, leaving smooth bores that the commonwealth does not immediately fill. Something grazes silica from the inner walls of water sacs. During hard freezes, rhythmic indentations sometimes cross the underside of thin root shelves while nothing moves on top. In spring, empty chambers are found lined with translucent skins, each skin broad enough to wrap around a grazer.

Nothing here announces itself. There are no territorial cries, mating choruses, alarm calls, or wing-clatter. Communication passes through pressure, dissolved molecules, altered warmth, interrupted flow. Life encounters life chiefly by changing the medium they share.

The upland’s true inhabitants are therefore difficult to count. A chimney may be one being, an organ, or a temporary alliance of tissues. A crawler may be a parasite that has become useful, or a useful structure that has learned to leave. The grazers may be tolerated thieves, gardeners of shedding tissue, or the commonwealth’s wandering reproductive caste. Even the black glider, apparently free above it all, carries the land’s dust in the seams of its body.

What lives there does not form a community upon the terrain.

It circulates through it, buds from it, feeds it, is tested and reclaimed by it. The few creatures that retain their own shapes do so cautiously and provisionally. Beneath them, the greater life keeps redistributing water, stone, heat, sugar, memory, and flesh—making bodies where bodies are needed, and taking them apart when they are not.
