At first it is difficult to say where the inhabitants end and the country begins.

The tallest organisms appear to be trees only from a distance. Up close, each grove reveals itself as a colony of many forms sharing one circulatory system. The silver trunks are the oldest, rigid and slow-growing, their crowns spread flat beneath the wind. Around them rise younger charcoal stems, green-barked water columns, jointed pressure trunks, and hollow marsh spires. Some are genetically distinct. Others are specialized bodies produced by the same buried stock. Roots graft so readily that individuality becomes temporary: a seedling may begin alone and, within a few decades, be absorbed into a forest-wide exchange of sap, minerals, chemical alarms, and weak electrical current.

No single trunk contains the life of such a forest. It lives in the crossings.

When one part is injured, nearby crowns alter the angle of their leaves. Water pressure falls across the grove. Fungal threads constrict around the damaged roots, isolating rot until scavengers arrive. Farther away, fluid chambers begin their slow compensating pulse. The response may take hours to cross a valley and days to fade. It is not thought, but neither is it simple reflex. The forest remembers drought as thickened vessels, storms as altered root tension, infestations as bitter compounds retained in bark long after the insects are gone.

The pale fungi beneath it are less visible and more extensive still. Their fruiting shelves are only brief surface organs of colonies that penetrate peat, deadwood, root tissue, and mineral fissures. Some digest. Some trade. Some parasitize the pressure trees and are themselves controlled by bacterial partners living inside their threads. Together they form dense, cream-colored cords as thick as limbs, wrapped around stones far underground. These cords pull dissolved metals from the basalt and distribute them upward. Where they die, they leave tubular passages through which water continues to move.

Much of the plateau’s soil is made from their remains.

The smallest animals live within this underground architecture. There are translucent root-borers with ringed mouths, flat mineral grazers no larger than fingernails, and blind colonial things that resemble handfuls of wet thread. They pass through the fungal galleries in immense numbers. Some carry spores in chambers along their flanks. Some cultivate bacterial films on their own backs, scraping the growth off against stone and eating it when mature. In the flooded layers, tiny shelled animals close themselves for months at a time, waking only when the water’s acidity falls within a narrow range.

Above them, among the fallen trunks, graze the pale shelf-eaters. They are low-bodied and warm, with four short walking legs and a smaller forward pair used to peel fungal skin from wood. Their heads are blunt and almost featureless. The eyes are buried beneath clear protective scales; the nostrils open downward so that condensation does not flood them. Fine sensory whiskers spread from the jaw and forelimbs, reading air currents and the faint tremors of moving animals through the log.

Their color is not truly white. It is the color of old lichen: ivory mottled with gray, faint green, and rust. When frightened, they stop so completely that spores settle on them. Older animals often carry actual moss along the ridge of the spine. Small insects breed there, and tiny birds pick through it while the grazer feeds.

They travel in loose family groups but rarely stand close together. One may browse on a suspended trunk while another waits thirty meters away, nearly invisible behind curtains of rootlets. They communicate by striking the resonant wood beneath them with the hard inner edge of the forelimb. The resulting knock passes farther than a cry and does not easily reveal its sender. At dusk, a whole slope may exchange these solitary wooden notes.

Higher in the trees are the six-limbed crown animals.

Their rear four limbs bear most of the weight. The front pair are long, narrow, and highly mobile, used to gather leaves, probe hollows, groom fungal growth from the fur, and test branches before crossing. Each hand has two opposing digits and a broad central pad ridged with microscopic hooks. They can cling to bark slick enough to reflect light.

Their fur is deep and layered. The outer hairs collect beads of water but hold them away from the skin; when the animal shakes, it releases a brief halo of droplets. Dark algae grow among the hairs of old individuals, changing color with moisture. In the clearer season their backs become dull brown or violet. In the long wet they darken to the green-black of bark.

These climbers move with extraordinary patience. A feeding animal may spend an hour crossing one crown. It listens through all six limbs, feeling for the change in tension that marks a loaded, hollow, or rotten branch. Young cling beneath the belly at first, then ride along the back, camouflaged in the wet fur. They eat waxy leaves, canopy flowers, soft bark, insects, and the mineral-rich nodules formed where certain lianas enter a tree.

Most are solitary outside the breeding phase. Yet during the clearest weeks they gather near the escarpments, where flowering crowns receive direct sun. There they become briefly conspicuous. Adults display the pale inner surfaces of their arms, turning them slowly toward one another across gaps in the canopy. Courtship may continue for several days without either animal crossing to the other tree.

They are hunted by the dark gliders.

A resting glider resembles a slab of rotten bark lodged against a trunk. Its limbs fold into angular bundles, and the membranes contract into deep corrugations that collect water and leaf litter. Moss often grows along the outer margins. Only the eyes remain clean: two black, mobile surfaces protected by transparent lids.

When it launches, the false bark opens into an animal several times wider than it first appeared. Four limbs spread the principal membranes. A smaller rear pair control pitch and braking, while a muscular tail acts as rudder. It does not merely glide downward. Warm air rises continually from the saturated forest and along the cliff walls, allowing large individuals to cross whole ravines without losing much height.

The glider’s attack is almost silent. It approaches below the level of a branch and rises at the last moment, folding around its prey. Its mouth is small; killing is done by hooked chest spines that close through the membrane like the teeth of a trap. Afterward the animal hangs head-down in a sheltered fork and feeds slowly, dropping little. Skin, fur, small bones, and even digestive organs are consumed. What cannot be used is packed into dense pellets and pressed into water-filled hollows, where larvae dismantle it.

The gliders are not the largest animals on the plateau. That distinction belongs to creatures seldom seen whole.

They inhabit the trembling marsh forests and the broad, root-dark corridors between pools. From a distance, one appears to be a moving mound of sedge. Its back is wide, low, and irregular, covered in tough hollow quills between which moss, algae, and small flowering plants take root. Seedlings may persist there for years. Water gathers in shallow depressions along its spine, supporting insect larvae and minute crustaceans.

Beneath this garden is an animal built to distribute weight. It has eight splayed limbs, each ending in a flexible disk broader than its head. The disks inflate as they touch the peat and empty as they rise, so the creature crosses floating mats without tearing them. Its body advances in a slow wave. Often the vegetation on its back is the only indication that it has moved.

Its head hangs low between the first pair of limbs. There are no jaws in the ordinary sense. Instead, a muscular oval mouth presses against the marsh surface and draws in water, soft plants, larvae, and loose peat. Internal combs separate what is edible. Clear water is expelled through slits beneath the body, aerating the saturated ground. Behind a feeding animal, sedges grow unusually well.

These marsh carriers can live for centuries. Their growth is slow, and their reproductive cycle is tied to rare sequences of low water followed by prolonged rain. During the clear phase, several may converge on exposed mineral terraces. They scrape their gardened backs against stone, leaving bands of moss, spores, skin, and waxy reproductive capsules. When the rains return, the capsules swell and open. The young are at first no larger than water beetles and live inside floating vegetation, acquiring their first dorsal plants before their shells have fully hardened.

Many die without ever reaching open marsh. Those that survive become moving islands.

In the pools, life is organized vertically.

The sunlit upper skin belongs to films, drifting eggs, needle-legged hunters, and small fish that flash silver only when they turn. Below them swim broad-mouthed filterers with transparent ribs. Deeper still, where the reflected sky gives way to tannin darkness, the long-bodied gill bearers graze the rock.

These amphibious animals are smooth, heavy, and almost colorless beneath the water. Frilled gills extend from grooves along the neck and sides, each supported by a comb of dark cartilage. Their limbs are short but powerful, with blunt claws suited to opening mineral crusts. They feed by pressing a rough lower plate against submerged stone and drawing the bacterial coating into the mouth. Rust-red and blue-gray stains accumulate around their lips.

They are able to leave the pools, though they seldom do so except during migrations between flooded systems. On land the gill frills collapse into sealed folds, and the animals breathe through chambers in the throat. They move at night through wet fern beds, following changes in ground pressure. Their passage leaves wide, polished grooves across roots. By morning the grooves are already gathering spores.

The oldest pool dwellers retreat into fissures beneath the plateau. Some have never seen daylight after their first season. In underground basins they grow long and thin, their limbs dwindling, their gill structures widening into red fans that detect the chemistry of distant rain. They follow the plateau’s water rather than its surface geography. A population beneath one forest may breed in caverns under another watershed and feed in pools hundreds of kilometers away.

The silver fish accompany these hidden journeys. Their bodies contain lines of magnetically sensitive cells and pressure organs fine enough to distinguish one subterranean channel from another. When seasonal water opens a route, the fish enter almost at once. Schools pour through narrow stone throats, scale against scale, brightening the dark with weak flashes generated along their flanks.

The light is not meant to illuminate. It keeps the school coherent where eyes are useless.

Predators wait for that migration. Sleek, low-bodied animals emerge from root hollows along the banks, each with a flexible spine, six webbed feet, and a face armored in overlapping plates. They drive fish into shallows with vibrations transmitted through the mud. A hunting group may remain perfectly still for hours, then rush the water together without vocal signal. They feed until their bodies swell, retreat beneath the roots, and may not hunt again for weeks.

Their young are born during the migration and learn first by feeling the movements of adults through the floor of the den. Before they can see clearly, they can distinguish the footfall of a parent, prey, and glider landing above.

Birdlike animals occupy every open layer but rarely sing. Sound travels unpredictably in the wet growth, and a sustained call draws hunters. Many use wing vibration instead. Their flight feathers possess stiffened shafts and fine combs that buzz, rattle, or pulse when air passes through them at selected angles. Messages can be sent while the animal remains inside fog: identity, alarm, direction, readiness to mate.

Marsh walkers have broad feet padded with trapped air. Canopy probers hang upside down and use long beaks to open swollen bark. Tiny crown feeders emerge during flowering weeks, dusted blue-gray or ivory with pollen. Along the cliffs live narrow-winged scavengers capable of hovering in the updraft beside waterfalls. They watch the forest rim for carcasses washed from channels or dropped by gliders. Their nests are not constructed so much as grown: they carry living strands of lichen to wet ledges and anchor them with saliva until a thick suspended cup forms.

Nothing remains exclusively a consumer. Every animal carries other lives.

The gliders’ membranes host mites that keep them flexible by eating dead skin. The crown animals cultivate odor-producing bacteria in folds of the wrist and use the scent to mark flowering trees. Marsh carriers transport entire communities. Pool grazers spread mineral organisms from fissure to fissure. Even the most aggressive predators distribute spores in their fur and fertilize root hollows with the remains of fish.

Death does not make an empty place. It makes a brief concentration.

A fallen crown animal is covered within hours by soft-bodied scavengers emerging from beneath the peat. Fungal cords enter through the mouth and joints. Small grazers strip the fur for nest lining. Mineral bacteria darken the exposed bone. Roots arrive before the skeleton is clean. In a few seasons, the site is marked only by an unusually dense knot of seedlings.

There may be forms of awareness here, but none stands apart enough to oversee the rest.

The crown animals remember routes, individuals, and seasons. Gliders calculate wind over enormous distances. Fish inherit maps of water that no single generation fully travels. Forests retain injury in their plumbing. Fungal networks alter future exchanges according to old shortages. A marsh carrier may bear a garden older than several generations of the quick animals feeding upon it.

Yet there are no councils, no keepers, no hidden engineers. The plateau is not tended. It is inhabited so completely that tending and feeding, shelter and predation, body and environment have ceased to be separate categories.

What lives there is not one creature disguised as a continent.

It is a continent in which every creature has become, in some measure, an organ of all the others.
