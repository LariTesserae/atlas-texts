At first glance, the White Room appears to belong to the six-limbed grazers. They are the only inhabitants large enough to alter its emptiness merely by crossing it, and the only ones known to distinguish the chamber from the world above. Their name for it is not spoken as a human mouth would speak. It is made with two throat-clicks separated by a breath, the first meaning something like *pale enclosure*, the second *place that remembers water*. White Room is only the nearest translation.

They are broad and low, seldom longer than a forearm. Four limbs bear most of the body’s weight; the smaller foremost pair can be folded against the chest or brought together with surprising precision. Their skin is neither scaled nor naked. It consists of minute overlapping pads, soft when damp and rigid at the edges during drought. Salt gathers between them. An old animal may look dusted with frost.

They have no eyes, but they are not blind. The pits along the back discern brightness, movement, and the passage of shadows. Each pit is covered by a clear cap that deepens or pales with the skin beneath it. At noon the animals retreat under ledges and become nearly indistinguishable from chalk. In the rose light of evening they darken from ivory to gray-violet and emerge one by one. Their shadows seem to detach from the stone before their bodies do.

Near objects are known through touch. Their forelimbs are furred with fine sensory threads, and the blunt front of the head trembles constantly, reading changes in air pressure. They can feel a falling drop before it strikes. Often an entire group will lift its heads at once, wait motionless, and turn toward the sound.

They do not form herds, though they seek one another after rain. Five or six may gather beside a basin, each occupying its own interval of space. They drink without lapping, pressing folds beneath the jaw to the water and drawing moisture through the skin. Between drinks they exchange clicks. These conversations are slow. One answer may come several minutes after another, when the original speaker has already moved away.

Their language contains few names for solid things. It is rich in kinds of wetness, degrees of light, and the age of stains. A particular basin may be identified not by its location but as *the water that lingers after the violet wall dries*. An animal displaced across the chamber can return to a favored resting place by tasting the floor and comparing the mineral sequence underfoot.

Their tools are chosen with similar care. A limestone flake useful for opening a bladder plant must be thin enough to pierce but rounded enough not to shatter the membrane. Good flakes are remembered. Sometimes one is passed from an old grazer to a younger one, though possession is casual and may last only until the next seepage. They do not sharpen stone because they seem to regard fracture as unpredictable, almost animate. Instead they search until the chamber supplies the correct edge.

Young grazers are born after the long water has withdrawn. The parent carries two or three translucent eggs in shallow pockets along the belly, moistening them with fluid stored under the skin. Hatchlings have eight limb buds. The rearmost pair withers during the first dry season, leaving a faint double scar near the tail. For several weeks they follow adults by placing their feet in fading prints. Most learn the chamber not as a map but as a sequence of textures: powder, smooth skin, cold crack, reed grit, salt lip, safe shadow.

They fear almost nothing in the White Room. Ceiling predators cannot swallow them. The wall runners avoid their scent. Even drought can be endured by sealing themselves beneath flat stones, lowering their warmth until the next condensation.

They fear the central hole.

A grazer approaching its rim begins to blanch regardless of the light. Its sensory pits close one after another. The front limbs draw tight against the body, and the throat produces a rapid, involuntary ticking unlike speech. Adults pull young away by the tail. No grazer will take a useful flake found beside the opening, though such flakes are often unusually thin and clean.

Around and beneath these larger lives exists the true multitude of the chamber.

The chalk lichens are not wholly plants. Their pale crust is a partnership among algae, mineral-eating threads, and colonies of tiny translucent bodies that migrate through the crust as moisture changes. In dry weather they retreat into the stone. After condensation they rise toward the surface in such numbers that the floor acquires a faint pearly sheen. The glass mites graze this living film, leaving winding paths visible only when evening light lies almost level across the ground.

The long-legged wall runners follow those paths. They are thin enough to enter cracks narrower than a reed leaf and can remain poised for days with their limbs spread around a seep. Their mouths are circular rasping discs. They do not chase mites; they wait until enough gather beneath them, then lower themselves all at once.

Soft beetles live under mineral flakes in generations stacked upon generations. The newest adults occupy the damp underside. Their empty larval skins are cemented above them. Old colonies create a layered paper of shells that may persist after every living beetle has moved elsewhere. When water returns, the colony wakes from the bottom upward.

The ribbon-winged fliers are the chamber’s brief extravagance. For most of their existence they are no more than dark knots in the mud, each cyst containing an animal folded many times around itself. Floodwater loosens the folds. Within hours the basin fills with pale larvae that feed on mineral algae; by the second evening they split along the back and lift into the air.

They do not feed as adults. Their soft bodies contain only reproductive organs, a small reserve of water, and a threadlike nerve running between the wings. They pair while rising. Their eggs descend afterward as dust and settle into drying mud. The adults continue upward until they are taken by the ceiling mouths or exhaust themselves against the luminous stone.

The ceiling predators are closer to plants in their patience than to any hunting animal. Broad feet fuse temporarily to the mineral surface. Their bodies hang beneath them like sealed buds, mouth uppermost and hidden against the roof. They sense the passage of a flier through changes in light. When one passes close, the mouth turns inside out.

After feeding, a predator peels itself free and opens the membranes stretched between its limbs. It falls slowly, sometimes taking half an hour to reach the floor. Once there it crawls to a new wall and begins the ascent. The oldest bear bands of different-colored mineral on their feet, a record of every region in which they have waited.

Even the reeds participate in this animal life. Their blue-black fans trap heat, but also ribbon-wing eggs, beetle fragments, shed mite skins, and the fine airborne residue of grazer breath. The plant digests these through its stem. Its roots are shallow; much of what nourishes it comes from above. The fans close when touched too often. A reed colony near a grazer path is therefore thinner than one beside an undisturbed basin.

The bladder plants are more active. When pierced neatly, they seal themselves and later grow a new bladder near the wound. Ragged damage causes the whole organ to collapse. Over many seasons, plants repeatedly harvested by grazers become dense and many-lobed, while untouched plants remain sparse. Thus the animals cultivate without intending to cultivate. The healthiest groves mark ancient resting places.

Then there are the lives that cannot be seen as individuals.

A green-black film inhabits the porous beds beneath the floor. It drinks the retreating lake. Its threads pass through limestone too narrow for any grazer’s claw and spread outward from the central depression in branching sheets. During wet periods it consumes what washes down: cysts, shed skins, reed pollen, dissolved salts, the fluids of the dead. During drought it contracts toward the deep fissures.

Warm air rising from the central hole bears its scent.

Whether the film is one organism or a succession of colonies cannot be determined. Pieces separated by great distances pulse at the same interval. When one region is disturbed, another changes color hours later. Mineral deposits grow differently above it, as if the floor were being repaired from beneath.

The old depressions around the hole may be connected to this hidden life. The obvious account is that animals once walked inward and descended: six-toed animals related to the grazers, four-toed animals no longer present, soft-bodied things whose tracks have lost all form. The absence of outward trails seems to confirm it.

But the marks do not behave entirely like tracks. They sharpen after rain rather than soften. Some appear where a thin crust was smooth the season before. A few overlap without breaking one another. The green-black threads lie most densely beneath them.

It may be that the thing below remembers pressure. A foot placed on the wet floor compresses the porous stone by an amount too slight to see. Moisture enters differently there. The buried film grows into the altered space, preserving the shape long after the original print has dissolved. Each wet season mineral rises around that growth and outlines it anew.

If so, not all the footprints need be ancient.

The insects that face the hole may be feeding on its breath, or warming themselves, or responding to some mineral odor. The grazers’ terror may be inherited from ancestors that entered and did not return. The intervals of warm air may be geological.

Or the chamber may be the uppermost surface of something vast and slow: the basins its collection cups, the cracks its vessels, the green below its living tissue. Rain brings nourishment. The temporary lake gathers every loose remnant and delivers it to the center. The White Room’s inhabitants would then live not merely in a cave, but upon a patient feeding organ that opens only downward.

Nothing has ever been seen to climb from the hole. Nothing reaches up. There are no jaws at the rim, no tentacles in the water, no sudden disappearances.

Only, during the last stage of drainage, the six-limbed grazers retreat to the chamber’s perimeter. The beetles hide. The wall runners fold their legs. Even the reeds close their dark fans.

Then the whole floor becomes very slightly warm.

From beneath the thinning water, the inward-pointing tracks brighten one after another, beginning at the outermost edge and proceeding toward the center—as though an invisible multitude were walking the old road again.
