The first inhabitants are almost indistinguishable from stains.

Lichens fasten themselves to newly uncovered rock, entering its pores before there is soil enough for roots. They grow in black rosettes, orange dustings, gray-green scales no thicker than old paint. Moss occupies the wet seams. Algae darken the snow and line the undersides of ice. Around springs, sedges knit the mineral flour into the valley’s first fragile skin. Cushion plants withdraw their stems into tight hemispheres, keeping warmth, moisture, and their own dead leaves close. In flowering season they open a few brilliant blossoms directly against the ground, as though color itself must crouch here.

Among these low growths live the smaller, hidden populations: mites in moss, larvae in melt pools, beetles beneath stones, worms and minute crustaceans in the marsh sediment. They endure nights when their shallow water freezes around them. Their lives accelerate whenever the sun warms a pool by a few degrees. Midges hatch, mate, and vanish in intervals so brief that an afternoon of calm may constitute most of their visible existence.

The pikas are the valley’s most persistent citizens. Compact, round-eared, and colored like weathered stone, they inhabit the moraine as if it were a many-storied settlement. Every crack is a doorway; every buried gap connects to another chamber. They emerge to cut sedges and flowers, carrying mouthfuls back to dry beneath rocks for winter. Their cries are abrupt and metallic, larger than their bodies. One call may pass across the scree from animal to animal until the apparently vacant slope reveals itself as occupied everywhere.

Small finches and ground-feeding birds arrive wherever seed or insects briefly concentrate. They are restless, close-feathered creatures, descending in groups and leaving as one body when the wind changes. Ravens range more widely. Black against snow and nearly invisible against wet basalt, they inspect glacier margins, marshes, and carcasses with the same grave attention. High above them, eagles and other raptors use the basin as a vast hunting diagram. They read the paths in the scree, the sudden scatter of birds, the pause before a pika dives underground.

The larger grazers appear to have been made from the valley’s own muted materials. Their coats are ash, tawny gray, old straw, or iron brown—thick enough to deform their outlines and long enough to shed sleet. They have deep chests for the thin air, strong legs for loose ground, and an instinct for moving without unnecessary speed. Some travel in herds over the flats; others keep to ledges and broken slopes. While resting, they disappear among boulders. When alarm reaches them, it moves visibly through the group: one head rises, then another, until the whole stony field is watching.

Their young are more conspicuous. Calves run at sudden angles, challenge streams, skid on silt, and scramble back from banks that collapse beneath them. Adults learn the valley’s changes more cautiously. A channel crossed yesterday may be deep today. A salt lick may lie under fresh debris. A meadow may be released from snow for only a week, and a herd must reach it while it exists.

Predators live by patience. The fox is pale, heavy-furred, and deliberate, its body low behind the moraine ridges. Much of its hunting consists of listening: pausing over buried runways, turning its head toward sounds traveling through stone, then springing with all four feet briefly clear of the ground. Larger hunters are rarer and harder to separate from the landscape. They follow herd margins, cliff bases, and snow lines, appearing less as permanent residents than as moments when the mountain acquires eyes.

The wet places support their own secretive company. Small dark frogs press into the warmth of seep-fed mud and remain motionless beside pools. Migratory birds come from far below the mountains to feed in the shallows, probing among sedge roots and newly exposed sediment. They stay only while the marsh is open. Their arrival briefly connects this unfinished basin to distant lowlands, rivers, and coasts; their departure makes it seem sealed again.

Nearly everything that lives here is close to the ground, thick-bodied, cryptically colored, migratory, or capable of vanishing into stone. Energy is guarded. Growth is compressed. Calls are short. Activity occurs in narrow permissions granted by sunlight, thaw, and calm air.

Yet the valley is not sparsely inhabited so much as inhabited in layers. Life occupies films, cracks, shallows, burrows, sheltered hollows, and moving herds. It does not rise high enough to alter the horizon. It has no canopy and makes no continuous green. Its settlements are a wet stone, a sedge margin, a warm seep, a carcass, a flowering cushion, a maze beneath the moraine.

These inhabitants are not merely surviving in a finished landscape. They are helping make one. Lichens loosen the rock. Moss catches dust. Sedges hold silt against the next flood. Pikas gather vegetation into the moraine; grazers move nutrients across the flats; predators and scavengers return bodies to the ground. Each life leaves behind a little organic matter, a seed, a burrow, a bone, a darkening of the mineral surface.

They are the valley’s first soil, its first memory, and its first claim that bare stone need not remain bare.
