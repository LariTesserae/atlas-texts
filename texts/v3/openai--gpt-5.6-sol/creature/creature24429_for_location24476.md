The first inhabitants are too small to be seen.

They live within the grain of the terraces, in capillaries finer than hair, where the plant colonies pass water from living layer to living layer. Some are cells shed by the walls and granted a brief independence. Others are animals reduced to threads, beads, or transparent ribbons only a few segments long. They clear obstructions, consume infections, and carry packets of reproductive tissue between channels that no longer connect directly.

They have no open-water stage. To them, the entire country is a warm-walled labyrinth, though the water surrounding it would seem cold to anything larger. Generations pass without an individual entering a cavity wider than its own body. When a channel narrows, they narrow with it. When it closes, they are digested where they swim.

Above them, on every exposed surface, live the silver tilers.

Each is a flat, many-finned animal with a body scarcely thicker than a leaf. Alone, it looks unfinished: one bright side, one dull side, a round scraping mouth, and small eyes set along the margin. In company, the tilers overlap until hundreds form a single sheet of trembling metal. Their fins interlock. Signals pass through the sheet as touches, so that danger makes an entire wall wink from silver to black.

They graze continuously, taking only the outermost film of the plant grain. The architecture depends on this. Ungrazed tissue swells and blocks the current channels; overgrazed tissue scars. The tilers keep to the exact depth between. Their mouths are shaped by the ridges they feed upon, and the ridges in turn are maintained by their feeding.

A tiler rarely leaves the wall voluntarily. To cross even a narrow crack is to enter a dimension for which it has almost no instincts. When forced into open water, it swims frantically and without direction, flashing like a dropped scale until it finds another surface or is eaten. Yet the colonies migrate over immense distances. A living carpet may advance from one pier to the next by sending a narrow bridge of bodies along a root cable. The crossing takes years. No individual that begins it survives to reach the far side.

In the seams dwell the fitted ones.

They are pale crustaceans whose adult shapes are determined by the hollows they occupy. A juvenile is soft, symmetrical, and nearly featureless. It searches until it finds an empty socket, then grows into the socket’s precise form. Some become wedges, some spirals, some long folded bodies wrapped around root junctions. In maturity they cannot leave. Their limbs extend into neighboring channels to comb food from the current, while their shells reinforce the weakness they inhabit.

Their visible eyes are not paired. A row of tiny eyes forms wherever the seam opens onto water. One animal may look out from six places in the wall, giving the impression that something much larger is hidden inside.

When a fitted one dies, its descendants eat it from within. The largest juvenile inherits the cavity and hardens into the shape of the parent. If none survive, the plant closes the socket, and that particular form of animal ceases to exist. There are niches in the lower galleries so old and singular that one death would extinguish a species.

Between the root columns hang the down-gazers.

They are long, translucent swimmers, usually mistaken for strips of dead tissue. Their heads point into the depths; their tails spread above them in delicate anchoring fans. They hold position for months at a time, descending only a body-length in the course of a day. Within their clear flesh, the digestive tract shines green-black with spores and minute grazers strained from the water.

Down-gazers possess no lenses. Instead, the whole front half of the body senses pressure. Through it they perceive the abyss below as a changing landscape of distant movement. A large animal passing several terraces down appears to them as a slow brightness of displaced water. A falling corpse is a sharp star.

At intervals, one folds its fins and drops. Hundreds follow. The column descends through arches and wells, sometimes for miles, until it intersects the outward-flowing lower current. There the animals open their fins again and rise along the face of the escarpment. This circuit is their courtship. Eggs and milt released during the descent meet only if both parents have chosen the same pressure path.

They return to their original columns without landmarks visible to any ordinary eye.

The heavy browsers of the inner vaults are called bluntbacks, if they are called anything.

They move on six broad paddles and carry their mouths beneath the chest. Their skin is colorless, thick, and softly corrugated. Old individuals grow as large as surface houses, but they remain able to pass through openings scarcely wider than their skulls; the ribs collapse inward, the organs shift, and the body draws itself through like dough pressed between fingers.

A bluntback feeds by settling over a patch of old wall and enclosing it. Inside the sealed mouth chamber, combs peel away exhausted fibers while sensitive lips avoid new growth. Feeding may last several days. When the animal moves on, it leaves a clean oval in the dark grain. The plants produce glowing fruits most readily along these ovals, and so ancient feeding routes can be read as blue rings receding through the chambers.

Bluntbacks communicate through the architecture. They press their foreheads to pillars and send long pulses through the wood. The message travels farther in the walls than sound travels through open water. A lone animal may answer another on the far side of the mountain, though the reply arrives changed by every fork and hollow trunk it crosses.

Much of their communication is not social. They sound the structure for weakness. A split root, a clogged channel, or an overgrown arch produces a wrong resonance. The animal goes to it, sometimes traveling for years, and grazes until the proper tone returns.

Their young are born with functional eyes. For a few weeks they look upon the blue fruits, the black fans, and the pale flanks of their mothers. Then a membrane grows across each eye. The trapped image decays slowly in the sealed organ, feeding a luminous bacterium. Adults carry two dim lights beneath the skin of the skull: not eyes anymore, but the remains of sight.

In the courts hunt the veiled ones.

At rest they are almost invisible—narrow bodies darker than the surrounding water, with fins drawn close and mouths shut. When searching, they unfold broad sensory membranes from the head and sides. These veils are ribbed with nerves and can span many times the width of the body. They register current, pressure, electrical discharge, and the minute disturbances made by muscle.

A veiled hunter does not chase at first. It enters a court and compares the water to its memory of that place. Every courtyard has a stable pattern: the downward pull near one colonnade, the eddy behind a parapet, the tremor of a membrane in a high arch. Anything alive appears as an error in the pattern.

The hunter turns until the error lies across the full width of its veil. Then the veil snaps closed, throwing the body forward.

They learn the constellations of luminous fruit when young. The learning is exact and permanent. Individuals follow routes inherited not through instinct but through instruction: an adult swims ahead, briefly masking each blue point with its body so that the young one notices the sequence of absences. Some routes take decades to complete. Some pass through apertures that have admitted the same lineage for hundreds of thousands of years.

Veiled hunters are not indiscriminate. They kill until the odor of fresh death reaches a certain concentration, then stop. A hunter unable to sense that odor continues killing and is soon attacked by others of its kind. The country tolerates predation; it does not tolerate excess.

Larger things cross the outer courts.

The most immense are the spanwings, though “wing” is only an approximation. Their bodies are short and dense, suspended between two vast horizontal fins. An old spanwing can reach from one root colonnade to the next. Its upper surface carries a garden of harmless parasites, silver tilers, and juvenile fitted ones awaiting vacancies. Its underside is bare and marked with long grooves that sing as water passes through them.

Spanwings feed on the drifting animal clouds of the vaults. They do not enter the chambers. Instead, they place their mouths over a round aperture and pulse their wings once. The pressure change draws part of the cloud outward. Months may pass before they feed again.

Their arrival can be heard long before they appear. The permanent chord of the place acquires another note, so low that small animals cease moving and the silver carpets flatten against the walls. Then a darkness detaches itself from the greater darkness and crosses between the blue points.

No predator attacks a mature spanwing. Their deaths are caused by age, obstruction, or navigational error. When one dies, it does not sink far. Root filaments emerge from the nearest pier, find the body, and tether it. Grazers arrive in layers. The skeleton—flexible, woody, and full of mineral salts—is drawn gradually into the escarpment. Several bridges in the lower country contain the remains of spanwings inside them. Their wing spars still vibrate in the current, lending those bridges their distinctive voices.

Not all inhabitants belong to the present country.

Inside sealed chambers live the withheld.

There are white frond-eaters with circular mouths and no means of surviving the outer water. There are eyeless spheres that reproduce by dividing whenever one touches the chamber roof. There are colonies resembling loose lace, each colony perhaps a single animal, perhaps thousands joined mouth to tail. Some chambers contain predators but no apparent prey. Others contain prey bearing old wounds although nothing capable of wounding them can be found.

These enclosed ecologies do not remain entirely unchanged. They change inwardly, trading complexity for endurance. A species may become its own food, shedding edible young from one side of the body and absorbing them through the other. Predator and prey may fuse into alternating generations of the same organism. In the oldest chambers, the distinction between animal and plant has become difficult to defend.

The sealed creatures sense the greater world only as vibration. The deep chord reaches them weakened by layers of tissue. Their lives organize around it. They rise when one harmonic strengthens, mate when another fades, and become still during the passage of spanwings they will never encounter. If the chord ceased, many would die without knowing anything had happened.

The plants themselves are alive at a pace that makes animal life seem like weather.

No single trunk or frond is the organism. A colony may occupy an entire terrace, or lace several piers together, while neighboring tissue of another lineage passes through it without conflict. They recognize themselves chemically. Where two colonies meet, each grows a thin boundary membrane, and the membranes exchange water, salts, spores, and structural information.

Pressure changes are remembered as growth. A current that leans on a wall for a thousand years becomes a buttress. Repeated grazing becomes a fruiting rim. The treadless passage of a migratory shoal becomes an arch maintained long after the shoal is extinct.

Their thoughts, if thoughts they are, have no images and no present tense. A torn filament in the lower dark may provoke thickening in a high gallery two centuries later. A colony can begin an answer after the event that caused it has vanished from every animal memory.

They keep the allotments.

When too many grazers hatch, the walls alter the flavor of their films and fewer juveniles feed. When browsers become scarce, old tissue sweetens and dormant embryos open inside the grain. Predators receive no direct command, yet the electrical texture of the courts changes with the number of prey. Even the glowing fruits participate: brightening slightly where bodies are needed, dimming where a route should rest.

Nothing governs alone. The plants shape the animals, the animals prune the plants, the currents sort both, and the architecture preserves the consequences.

There may also be one inhabitant that is not a species.

It has no body separate from the country. It consists of the circulating water, the resonant trunks, the pressure membranes, the answering calls of bluntbacks, the movements of shoals, and the very slow adjustments of the colonial walls. No part knows the whole chord. Yet each part corrects deviations in the portion it can hear.

A blocked channel changes a note. Down-gazers abandon a column. Hunters alter their route. Grazers thicken elsewhere. A bluntback arrives and opens the obstruction. The original note returns.

Whether this is merely equilibrium or a form of awareness cannot be determined from within it.

The inhabitants do not worship it. They do not name it. Most cannot conceive of the country as a totality. Still, every body enters the chord, modifies it for a time, and is eventually incorporated into the instrument that produced it.

Here, to live is to occupy a permitted space in an ancient sound.

To die is to become part of its walls.
