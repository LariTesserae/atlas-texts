Those who live in the Black Pith City do not, at first, seem to live there.

They resemble fittings.

A raised seam along a wall may be a sleeper. A row of dry knobs beside a doorway may be a household. What seems to be a bench may slowly tighten around the floor when the Whole gives one of its buried pulses. A watchman can be mistaken for a forked brace, an archivist for a blister in the Low Archive, a crowd for the roughened bark of a public chamber.

They do not stand apart from their city. They are rooted into it.

Some rise on narrow trunks and carry folded crowns of scale-leaves, but many have no upright body at all. They spread low beneath the pith, presenting only sensory ridges to the streets. Others hang from vaults in pale, desiccated clusters, their rootlets descending like strings until they touch a resonant surface. There are columnar inhabitants whose outer layers have hardened into permanent architecture. Chambers pass through them. Doors open in their sides. Their oldest branches serve as bridges, and their inner tissues remain awake around the traffic.

Whether these are citizens with buildings grown around them, or buildings that have become citizens, is not a distinction the city makes.

A resident is known less by shape than by response.

Strike the floor near one, and after a pause it answers. The reply may be a single contraction, a pressure traveling through the street, or a sequence of dry clicks made by seed coats drawn across one another. Close root tips exchange quicker messages by touch. They press, release, and press again, altering salt concentrations in the minute sealed films within their tissues. At longer distances, they use the city itself: trunks, walls, bridges, and vascular roads become the body of the conversation.

Their speech is slow only from outside.

A warning can cross the hollow in an instant of tension. A greeting may take an hour because courtesy requires the receiver’s whole root-crown to settle before it answers. Names are not sounds but characteristic patterns of pressure: a particular rising knock, a pause, then a soft shiver through three adjacent fibers. Old individuals have names so structurally elaborate that they can be spoken only by a district.

They possess no faces. Their attention shows elsewhere.

When curious, they extend fine pale root hairs toward a vibration. When alarmed, they withdraw oils from exposed tissue and close their scales. When many attend to the same event, the floor around them becomes taut. A public assembly can therefore be felt before it is reached: the street grows hard under the probing root, and every accidental tap returns too clearly.

They do not see. There are no eyes, even in the reduced botanical sense, though sealed buds retain patches of tissue that react uselessly to imagined brightness. These buds sometimes turn within their wrappings. They lean toward old chemical promises, following a direction that has no meaning in the hollow. Such growths are treated with tenderness. Some are pruned away before they exhaust themselves. Others are preserved in quiet chambers as listeners to the absent light.

The inhabitants know one another through weight, pitch, scent, and the architecture of strain.

Every living body has a tone. The young answer sharply because their tissues are tight and thin. Mature citizens sound layered, with overtones passing through many growth rings. The very old produce broken, spacious replies, as though several individuals were answering from different depths. Illness is heard as dullness, asymmetry, or an echo arriving where none should be. Grief is a prolonged loss of tension. Anger is a bitter volatile released into communal breath.

They have no blood and almost no free sap. What moisture remains in them is bound in dense gels, locked behind wax and pressure. Their active surfaces are leathery, resinous, or furred with absorbent hairs. A wound does not bleed. It exhales.

The wounded tissue gives off a brief mineral odor, then pulls tight. Nearby inhabitants send dry compounds through shared grafts: gums, salts, defensive bitterness, minute reserves of bound water. Scar tissue forms over years. During that time the injured one may be surrounded by braces and sounded daily. A severe cut can occupy the labor of several generations.

This makes bodily boundaries uncertain. Nearly every citizen is joined to an older root, as law requires, and the old roots join others. Food, warnings, memories, and sickness pass through these unions. Yet individuality persists. A graft may carry a hundred signals and still refuse one. A branch may belong structurally to one resident, chemically to another, and ceremonially to a third whose ancestor trained it into an arch.

Some inhabitants are singular trunks with a clear center of response. Some are colonies of root tips acting in concert. Some began as several seedlings and have fused so completely that no one can determine whether a council inhabits them or whether the fusion produced one mind. There are also dispersed citizens: a line of identical buds along an avenue, each apparently dormant until the whole line answers at once.

The Whole itself lies behind all of them.

They are not sure whether they are its children, its organs, its injuries, or merely opportunistic growths inside a chamber it abandoned. Their tissues can usually be distinguished from its ancient pith, but the distinction weakens with age. Every lawful building must be grafted to an old root. Every old root eventually enters the Whole. Thus the longer a citizen survives, the less convincingly it can be said to end.

The youngest begin in sealed cradles.

A seed is not planted in wet soil. It is set into a pocket of softened wax and powdered pith, with a measured grain of salt and a droplet so small it never appears as liquid. The cradle is closed immediately. Neighboring roots warm it by respiration. For years there may be no outward sign except a slight swelling and an altered scent.

Germination is a private fracture.

The seed coat splits in darkness. A root emerges first and is guided into a prepared channel by grooved fibers. Only when that root has taken hold is the shoot allowed to unfold. It does not rise toward light. It rises toward sound: caretakers tap from the direction of open space, and the new growth bends toward the repeated vibration.

Younglings are soft by the city’s standards. They are kept sheathed and rarely exposed to the drying avenues. They learn the district as sequences of impact. First the pulse of their cradle wall, then the tone of the nearest street, then the three-knock answer to fracture. Long before they can shape a signal of their own, they recognize the strain-pattern of the roots that support them.

Their childhood lasts decades.

During it, they are highly mobile, though nothing resembling walking occurs. A young shoot can withdraw from one guide channel and enter another. Root tips advance through prepared cords. Small bud-clusters may be carried in closed pods suspended from bridges, their grafts maintained by braided living fibers. This is the nearest the inhabitants come to travelers with bodies. With age, their commitments become physical. They thicken where they have remained. Their routes harden into limbs. Their choices become architecture.

An adult does not go to a workshop. It grows access to one.

A fiber-spinner occupies a frame of forked branches and extrudes strands through rows of hooked thorns. By contracting in sequence, it twists the strands into cord. A cutter grows hard, replaceable spines impregnated with salt. A surveyor spreads hair-roots across the floor and can distinguish a hollow beneath six layers of pith. A bridge-tender lives inside the bridge’s anchoring knots, sensing each change of tension and thickening the appropriate fibers.

The city’s “workers” are therefore often stationary bodies acting at a distance through tools grown from themselves. A root may operate a lever fifty paces from the trunk that feeds it. A valve keeper can inhabit an entire line of vents. Seed-rattle elevators are tended by citizens whose pods regulate the release of kernels one dry measure at a time.

Their technology is partly anatomy and partly agreement.

A mechanism succeeds because several beings consent to repeat small contractions in the proper order. One lifts the counterweight. Another hardens the latch. A third listens for the return signal through the floor. A machine may stop not because it breaks, but because one component has entered dormancy or withdrawn from the arrangement after a dispute.

Their disputes are rarely sudden.

Opposition appears first as delayed cooperation. A door opens a fraction too late. A conduit constricts. A public root ceases to transmit one district’s signals. Bitter compounds gather along a boundary. Because action is growth, a political position must often be maintained for years before it becomes visible. By the time a wall has thickened enough to obstruct an avenue, the argument that produced it may already have passed into another phase.

They are capable of violence, but it is difficult to separate violence from gardening.

A root can be strangled by a neighboring coil. A bud can be denied access to breath. Defensive resins can be sent across a graft in lethal concentration. A district can tune repeated impacts to fatigue a rival structure until it splits. The most feared act is not cutting but isolation: severing every communicative root while leaving the body alive.

Such punishments are rare. No inhabitant is easily removed, and every death alters the load borne by those around it.

Death begins as silence.

The dead do not fall at once. Their last tissues dry and harden. Connections remain under tension. For generations they may continue to serve as stairs, braces, sounding boards, storage pods, or walls. Their names fade only when their characteristic resonance is lost beneath repairs. An ancestor can remain physically central to a household for centuries, crossed daily by roots that remember how it once answered.

There is no decay in the animal sense. Without wet rot, without worms or familiar fungi, a body passes from citizen to material by almost imperceptible degrees. Resin crystallizes. Fibers separate. Salt works into the channels. Eventually the dead become so dry that a structural report may shatter them.

Then the fragments are gathered.

Nothing useful is discarded. Sound wood becomes pegs and mallets. Fibers become cord. Seed coats become wedges. Thin membranes are stretched across acoustic chambers. The finest dust is pressed into scars or laid in germination cradles. Thus the inhabitants remain among the works of the city, though no one claims that this preserves the self. A sounding plate made from an ancestor may reproduce one overtone of its former name, but only one.

Memory belongs more securely to the living.

Some keep it in growth rings, altering the density of each season’s tissue according to events. Some produce sequences of knots. Some train side roots into complex spatial patterns that can later be read by touch. The archivists of the Low Archive are broad, low organisms whose finest roots enter the stump’s seams. They spend lifetimes learning which pressures wake which notes.

An archivist scarcely moves. Its surface is dusty, often indistinguishable from the floor. Beneath it, thousands of root hairs maintain contact with different rings. When a question is placed before it, the archivist contracts in careful sequences, sounding the old wood from within. The answer may emerge days later as a low vibration through the plaza.

They are not always certain they have interpreted it correctly.

The deepest records predate their present forms of speech. Some may not be records at all, but scars, drought marks, or mechanical stresses mistaken for intention. Entire civic traditions rest on a resonance that could be an ancient crack.

Religion, if that is the word, gathers around such uncertainty.

No inhabitant worships light in any simple way, but many carry a bodily conviction that direction once existed. Some believe the vanished moisture was a first abundance from which all distinct beings separated. Others regard wetness as annihilation: a return to formlessness, swelling, rupture, and uncontrolled growth. The driest sects encase themselves in wax and speak only by vibration. Their chambers smell of salt and old resin.

Others listen for the Whole.

These listeners grow downward. They are narrow, pale, and nearly leafless, with thickened root crowns pressed into the oldest floor. Their lives are spent distinguishing ordinary settlement from the deep pulse that commands silence. Most never hear it more than once. Some do not hear it at all and die with their roots still waiting.

When the pulse comes, the listeners receive it before the towers flex. They close the mills, still the bridges, and pass the cessation signal outward. In the resulting silence, every resident becomes attentive at once.

For that moment, the city approaches a single organism.

Root to root, graft to graft, wall to tower, all tensions align around the pressure rising from below. Personal signals vanish inside it. Dormant seeds contract in their cradles. The dead answer through beams and sounding plates. Even unopened buds turn.

Afterward, separateness slowly returns.

Valves resume their whispering. Kernels pour. Disputes continue from the point at which they were suspended. The bridge-tenders retune their spans, and the archivists test the Low Archive for any ring newly awakened by the shock.

Most inhabitants assume the pulse means something.

Few agree on what.

Perhaps the Whole is speaking to them. Perhaps it does not know they are there. Perhaps their city is only the dry inner articulation of a thought too large and slow to recognize as thought.

Yet years after a pulse, the effects appear. A sealed scale curls back. A root abandons its old direction. A wall divides along a seam no surveyor had detected. The inhabitants gather around the change, touching it with fine exploratory hairs.

They listen into the opening.

Then, with the patience of beings for whom a doorway may be an answer and a century merely the time required to understand the question, they begin to grow toward it.
