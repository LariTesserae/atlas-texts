What lives there first appears to be the darkness itself.

It hangs from the roof in folded, breathing masses. It loosens in strips, pours between pillars, gathers again in distant vaults. Only when a gray band of fissure-light catches the movement does the darkness separate into thousands of small bodies: blunt muzzles, pleated ears, hooked feet, wings thin enough to show their bones. The bats occupy the buried country as weather occupies an open one. Colonies are its cloudbanks; commuting streams are its prevailing winds.

They are not one people or even one kind. Large fruit- and leaf-eaters crowd the brighter galleries nearest the surface, returning with pulp on their mouths and seeds in their droppings. Smaller hunters fill the interior. Some glean beetles from stone, hovering close enough that their wing tips nearly brush the walls. Others trawl above the pools or fly the long nave-like corridors, taking gnats in open air. Crack-dwellers flatten themselves into seams scarcely wider than a finger. Heavy-bodied species claim domes from which they can drop into flight. Every hollow in the roof has its proper tenants.

Their ordinary movements sustain much of what lives below. Guano falls in a continual fine rain, along with shed fur, fragments of wings, afterbirth, dead pups, the discarded husks of insects and occasional whole bodies. Nothing lies unused for long.

The guano fields seem still from a distance. Up close they are crowded landscapes. Cave crickets stand over them on high jointed legs, antennae searching ahead like paired threads. Beetles push through the black material in shining files. Mites cover fallen scraps so densely that the scraps appear to tremble. Pale, soft-bodied things graze the films on wet stone, and millipedes work beneath loose flakes where condensation keeps the dust dark. Larvae inhabit different depths according to moisture. Predators scarcely larger than grains of rice wait at the borders between them.

These small inhabitants possess the cavern in a more complete way than the larger animals do. They enter pores, cracks and buried channels inaccessible to hoof or wing. They live under every apparently empty acre. The bare floor is only the roof of another inhabited world.

Around the isolated waters, life takes on the color of what contains it. Shrimp are almost indistinguishable from reflections except for the dark threads of their digestive tracts. Translucent fish float over pale gravel with the reserved posture of leaves suspended in clear resin. Their eyes are diminished or hidden beneath skin; their heads are broad with organs that read pressure, vibration and dissolved traces. They do not need to search far. Food comes down with drips, washes through cracks, or falls struggling from the margins.

Salamanders lie along the shores in close ranks, moist bodies touching. Some retain feathery external gills throughout life. Others leave the water only to hunt among wet stones, returning before their skins begin to tighten. The pool frogs are less altered. They still have eyes, though their pupils are wide and their vision is suited to grayness. They sit half submerged among mossy basins, each mottled so precisely that a shore can erupt into motion without seeming to have held anything beforehand.

No creature in the pools behaves as though resources might fail next season. Neither do they behave wastefully. Their movements are economical because speed has little value in a world without sudden cold, drought or flood. Fish rise, feed and settle. Shrimp comb gravel. Salamanders advance by the width of a foot. Even pursuit has the calm appearance of ritual.

Under the fissures live the green-dependent animals.

The deer are small, narrow-bodied and deft. Their hooves are hard at the edges and spread slightly on rubble. Generations of low ceilings and broken ground have favored compact shoulders, supple necks and antlers reduced to short backward tines that do not snag on stone. Their coats are charcoal brown, not white, and their eyes remain large and glossy. Blindness would be no advantage to an animal that browses beneath dim daylight and must read the silhouette of a fox between pillars.

They move from one island of vegetation to another along inherited routes. At the brightest patches they eat moss, grass, fern tips and shrub leaves. Elsewhere they browse fungus, nose apart guano-rich soil for pale shoots, and lick walls glazed with salts. Fawns learn the country by following the hind immediately before them. A herd passing a narrow shelf places every foot in almost the same hollows, so the stone has become polished cups beneath generations of hooves.

Their lives are mapped as a sequence of reliable things: a mineral wall, a dripping ledge, a fern crack, a sleeping recess, a gap too narrow for pigs, a flat place from which approaching paws can be heard. They do not know the whole sunken country. No animal does. Each herd inhabits an archipelago of familiar chambers surrounded by darkness that is physically near but behaviorally remote.

The pigs know a different country. For them it consists of diggable basins joined by rough corridors. They are low, muscular and scarred along the snout. Their coats carry a permanent dusting of pale limestone over black bristles. They overturn the same dark fields their ancestors overturned, feeding on roots, beetle larvae, buried shellfish and whatever has fallen from the roof. Piglets trail their mothers through trenches already worn to belly depth in places. When alarmed, a sounder vanishes not into open distance but into a memorized confusion of blocks where larger pursuers cannot turn.

They are vigorous animals, loud and quarrelsome, yet even their disturbance has boundaries. They do not spread their rooted fields indefinitely. Beyond certain damp seams the soil is too thin; beyond certain pillars the forage no longer repays the effort. Every churned patch ends sharply against unworked dust.

Foxes patrol those edges.

They are long-footed and fine-eared, with coats that vary from soot-red near the openings to gray in the interior. Their whiskers are prominent, their tails carried low to keep from brushing projections. They hunt by sound more than sight. The smallest scrape of a cricket, the click of a displaced pebble, or the change in pitch when a deer steps from mud onto stone gives them a location. They know which echoes belong to prey and which are repetitions returning from far walls.

A fox’s territory may be a thin, crooked ribbon extending through many chambers while omitting most of the ground between. It sleeps in one fallen block, drinks at three pools, waits at two herd passes and visits a guano field when beetles hatch. Another fox lives close by according to distance but seldom crosses the same useful surfaces. Their ranges interlace without truly overlapping, like separate melodies sounding in the same reverberant room.

At the broad pools live the stone-bearers.

They resemble compact otters, though their limbs are adapted as much for climbing rubble as for swimming. They have blunt claws, strong palms and pale whiskers that fan forward when they submerge. Shellfish hide under gravel or cling inside flooded cracks. The animals roll stones aside, feel along the bottom, and surface with their prey held against the chest.

For hard shells they select pounding stones from the shore. The choice is deliberate: rounded enough to grip, dense enough not to fracture, neither so large that it cannot be raised nor so small that it wastes blows. One animal may carry a favored stone between feeding places for part of a day. It does not keep it forever. When feeding ends, the stone is left among stones. Another may find it later and choose it again without knowing it was chosen before.

Their cubs learn through interference. They snatch unsuitable shards, strike shells at poor angles, steal from adults and lose their tools in deep water. Nothing is taught formally, yet the skill persists. At some pools nearly every mature animal uses a stone. At others, equally rich in shellfish, they open prey with teeth or hammer it directly against a ledge. Customs exist, but no record of them does.

The crows of the light-bands are similarly local in their cleverness. They are black enough to disappear whenever they leave the diluted illumination, though their eyes remain visible as brief cold sparks. Their calls are harsh and conversational. They nest on inaccessible shelves using roots, fern stems, hair and bat wing membranes. A nest may be repaired for several seasons, but after abandonment it is dismantled by beetles, weathered by drips and stolen apart by other birds until no structure remains.

The crows probe cracks for larvae with twigs stripped of side growth. They wedge soft stems against rough stone and pull them through their beaks to taper the ends. Some drop shellfish from low shelves. Some follow pigs and inspect newly turned earth. They remember particular faces, feeding sites and caches, but memory dies with the bird or passes only imperfectly through imitation. Their intelligence leaves habits in the population, not monuments on the ground.

There are other visitors and half-residents at the margins: snakes that wait where frogs climb from water, rodents nesting in dry guano banks, small owls using the nearest chambers as daytime roosts, moths drawn inward until they become food. Some enter through fissures and never find a way out. A surface animal may live its full life below without becoming a cave species. Its descendants may do the same for generations, always retaining something unnecessary from the upper world: pigment, functional eyes, a yearly urge to breed that no longer corresponds to any detectable season.

None rules the country.

The bats are most numerous, but they depend on things too small for them to notice. The deer shape the plant islands, but cannot make those islands spread. Pigs remake patches of ground, yet the stone confines them. Foxes kill, but never enough to empty a route. Otters possess tools only while using them. Crows solve immediate problems and leave their solutions behind as ordinary sticks.

Even scavengers do not preside over death. Death is partitioned rapidly. A bat falling onto dry rubble may be opened by beetles before a fox finds it. A dead deer feeds foxes and crows first, then insects, fungi and bacterial films; bones are gnawed for minerals, dragged apart, pushed into cracks and covered by rooting pigs. In water, a fish is reduced among shrimp, salamanders and invisible organisms until nothing remains but dissolved substance. The rarity of bones is not evidence of peace. It is evidence of thoroughness.

The inhabitants are bound less by kinship than by recurrence. They inherit places through use. A newborn bat enters a flight stream already flowing. A fawn steps into a hoof-track older than its herd. A fox waits where foxes have always waited because deer continue to pass there. Frogs occupy the same indentations at the same pool margins. Crickets gather beneath ledges whose geometry has not changed since the collapse.

To an intruder, the multitude might at first look like a single immense society: fixed roads, crowded districts, regular migrations, specialized workers in roof, soil and water. But there is no agreement among them. The order has no overseer and no intended form. It arises because every creature repeatedly discovers the same advantages in an environment that never rearranges the question.

That is what truly lives beneath the limestone: not merely bats, deer, fish, insects, foxes and crows, but habits given bodies.

Each animal is brief. The pattern it enters is not. Individuals are born, learn a fraction of the dark, feed, breed and vanish into other mouths. Yet the colony remains the same weight upon the ceiling. The herd returns to the same gray light. The predator lowers itself behind the same block. A shrimp combs the same pale gravel where uncounted shrimp have combed before.

The country does not preserve its inhabitants as persons. It preserves their motions.
