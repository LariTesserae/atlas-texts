They are not a tall people, despite living so high.

Visitors from below often expect the plateau’s inhabitants to have been drawn upward by the thin air: long-limbed, hollow-chested, austere. Instead, most are compact and deliberate in their movements. They walk with knees slightly bent and one hand habitually free. This is partly caution—the streets are slick, bridges sway, and a familiar passage may end in open cloud after a night of frost—but it has become a manner carried even indoors. In conversation they brace themselves against tables. Waiting in line, they hook an elbow through a railing. Children learn three things almost together: how to walk, how to tie a retaining knot, and how to tell mist from smoke.

Their skin tends toward the gray-brown pallor of people who seldom receive direct sun, though clear weather can burn them in minutes. Old workers have fine dark stippling around the eyes and nostrils from iron dust, moss spores, lamp soot, and decades of wiping condensation from their faces with the backs of their gloves. Their hands are marked by cold cracks and small crescent scars from wire. Hair is usually kept short or braided tightly against machinery. Beards are wrapped in gauze in the workshops and threaded with ceramic beads in winter so that accumulated ice can be broken away without pulling the hair.

Nearly everyone wears layers of felt, waxed fiber, and loosely woven undercloth that allows sweat to escape before it becomes dangerous. Outer coats are cut asymmetrically, fastening high beneath one ear, with no open throat into which water can run. The shoulders carry replaceable pads where bridge cables and lifting straps rub. Hoods have narrow crowns stiffened with cane or copper so that dripping cloth does not touch the head. A person’s district and occupation can often be read from repairs: green resin used by gardeners, red thread from the foundries, pale fishbone stitching among cistern crews, black wire at the rim.

Nothing they wear is truly waterproof. They distrust anything that claims to exclude water completely. Sealed garments collect moisture inside; sealed rooms rot invisibly. Better, they say, to give dampness a path.

This principle governs more than clothing.

A plateau household is rarely a simple family occupying a set of rooms. It is a vertical arrangement of obligations. One household may consist of grandparents tending warm beds below the old street, two sisters maintaining lift brakes three levels above, unrelated apprentices in a former grain loft, a widower whose cistern wall adjoins theirs, and several children who sleep wherever the pipes are warmest. Membership is determined as much by shared drainage as by blood. Those whose roofs feed the same tank are expected to answer one another’s alarms, contribute labor to repairs, and divide shortages according to rules older than the walls themselves.

They say that people become kin in three ways: by birth, by burden, and by water.

Water is not quite owned. It is held, guided, owed, lost, contaminated, inherited, and sometimes stolen, but outright ownership is considered a childish claim. A cistern belongs first to the surfaces that fill it, then to the living things within it, then to those responsible for keeping it sound, and only after that to those who drink from it. Disputes can therefore become intricate. A household may accuse a factory uphill of changing the taste of its morning condensation; the factory may answer that a new roof orchard has redirected the fog; the gardeners may point to a bridge net installed two generations earlier. Cases are heard by moisture clerks who carry rolled plans showing not property lines but paths of seepage.

The best of these clerks can look at a wet wall and describe half a neighborhood.

They know which roofs are shedding properly, where an illegal warm pipe has been laid, whether a reservoir is overdrawn, and which pale bloom indicates ordinary mineral salts rather than the beginning of structural failure. Their profession combines law, hydrology, memory, and gossip. They press thin paper against stone to take patterns of damp, then store the impressions in heated archives. Some records consist of little more than branching stains labeled with dates and family names. To outsiders they resemble damaged maps. To the clerks they are genealogies.

The city’s engineers possess similar authority, though they reject any suggestion that they are priests. They are recognized by chalk cases strapped beneath their coats and by the small hammers they use to sound walls, axles, bridge pins, and cistern lids. They listen far more than they look. A clear note may mean a sound support or a dry tank; a dull note may indicate safety in one district and catastrophe in another. Apprentices spend years learning the difference.

The oldest engineers often become almost impossible to walk beside. They stop every few steps. They place a palm against masonry. They stare at hairline fractures while answering questions unrelated to them. At night they wake because a pump two buildings away has altered its rhythm.

They insist that the stone is not alive.

Then they speak of its thirst, fatigue, fever, and memory.

Those who work the cliff-lifts are more openly superstitious. Lift crews tie no decorative ribbons to cables, because anything fluttering may conceal a broken strand. Instead they place offerings inside the wheelhouses: a twist of grain, a carved wooden hoof, a lump of low-country clay. Each represents something that has risen safely. No corresponding offerings are made for descending cargo. Descent, in their view, requires attention rather than luck.

The brake tenders are among the city’s most respected workers. They sit in warm, windowless chambers beside enormous drums, reading changes in cable tension through the floor. Many are partially deaf, yet can distinguish a load of wet timber from caged livestock by the vibration transmitted through a cup of water. They work in pairs, one watching the gauges and one watching the other. This is because altitude sickness, fatigue, and certain gas leaks first reveal themselves as misplaced confidence. A brake tender who suddenly says there is nothing to worry about is relieved at once.

Rim crews are different again. They are leaner, heavily scarred, and regarded with a mixture of admiration and discomfort. They repair fog nets, inspect anchorages, clear ice, and descend over the escarpment to recover stalled freight. Their coats have bright inner linings—yellow, white, or blue—so that if they fall against the cliff they can open the coat and be seen. They carry speaking cones, rock nails, coils of line, and small sealed bells. The bells are not rung during ordinary work. A bell heard from below the edge means that the person attached to it cannot climb.

Rim workers do not say someone has fallen. They say the city has failed to hold them.

Names are written afterward on narrow lead strips and worked into replacement anchor plates. In this way the dead remain under tension, bearing a portion of every later load.

Death elsewhere is treated less dramatically. Ground suitable for burial is too shallow and too wet, and fuel for cremation too valuable. Bodies are usually given to the warm gardens after a period in the cold rooms, though the process is disguised by formal language. The dead are said to be “lowered into usefulness.” Bone is separated, cleaned, and incorporated into filter ceramics or orchard soil according to family preference. The seed-keepers accept small fragments of certain elders, grinding them into the plaster of vaults where rare varieties are stored. They maintain that calcium discourages mold. Families maintain that the dead recognize what must be protected. Neither explanation excludes the other.

The seed-keepers themselves occupy an uncertain place between agronomists, archivists, and monastics. Their houses are warm, dim, and astonishingly fragrant. They preserve not only seeds but graft wood, spores, dormant tubers, sourdough cultures, medicinal films, and soil from vanished gardens. They wear undyed coats to make contamination visible. Their hair is shaved, but not as a vow; mites are simply too dangerous in the vaults.

Every seed-keeper takes responsibility for one crop that no longer grows well on the plateau. These failures are tended with particular devotion. There are rooms for golden lowland wheat that produces only empty husks, for a red pear whose blossoms cannot be pollinated by any insect still living above the cliffs, and for broad beans said to have fed the first lift crews. The keepers plant a few each year, record their decline, and return what remains to storage. Preservation, to them, includes the memory of unsuitable things.

Children like the seed houses because the keepers know songs from isolated plateaus. Some songs have only three or four words in common with local speech. Others imitate machinery so precisely that old mechanics can identify obsolete pump designs from the rhythm.

Plateau speech is quiet but not especially solemn. People do not raise their voices in fog unless there is danger; sound can mislead when distance disappears. Instead, they speak close, often touching wrists or sleeves before beginning. Their language is full of words for partial visibility, structural sounds, degrees of wetness, and obligations that have not yet become debts. There are separate terms for water caught from a roof, drawn from stone, released by thaw, condensed on vegetation, or brought up from below. To call one by another name is either ignorance or fraud.

Their humor is dry, intricate, and frequently concerned with collapse. A common joke begins with an engineer, a moss tender, and a moisture clerk discovering the same crack. The engineer orders evacuation, the tender plants a fern in it, and the clerk declares it lies outside everyone’s jurisdiction. The ending changes according to which profession tells it.

There is little interest in heroism. The city distrusts acts that cannot be repeated safely. A person who makes a spectacular rescue may be praised and fined in the same afternoon: praised for the life saved, fined for the unsecured line or neglected inspection that made spectacle necessary. Greater honor goes to those whose maintenance prevents anything memorable from happening.

This produces a people attentive to small failures and suspicious of clean appearances. New walls are left with inspection gaps. Pipes remain visible. Clothing is patched in contrasting colors so that the history of strain can be read. Public accounts include losses as well as stores. A tool with no marks is assumed either untested or stolen.

Yet they are not without vanity. They polish brass valve wheels, train orchard branches into elaborate knots, and compete to grow blue fog-bells in improbable places. On clear days, people bring mirrors onto roofs to signal across the plateau. Districts answer one another in flashes. Courtships can be conducted this way, slowly and in full public view, though fog often interrupts at critical moments and provides plausible deniability.

Music favors pipes, low strings, struck stone, and instruments driven by dripping water. Large ensembles are rare because sound behaves unpredictably among the close walls. Instead, musicians position themselves through several rooms or along a stairwell. The audience hears only part of the composition from any one place. To hear the whole piece, one must move through it.

Many machines appear to participate.

A bellows in a metal shop supplies a sustained note. Lift cables hum when the wind crosses them. Water clocks release measured knocks beneath the floor. During festivals the regular industrial cycle is not stopped but tuned: loads are staggered, valves adjusted, and public fountains allowed to strike copper basins. The city performs by altering its work without ceasing it.

Food is dark, sour, dense, and served hot. Root stews, fungus loaves, bitter greens, small hard cheeses, preserved insects, and grain brought from below form the ordinary diet. Fresh fruit is precious but not always eaten. The first ripe fruits of a roof orchard are often passed from household to household for several days simply to be smelled. Eventually they are sliced thin enough that everyone connected to the cistern receives a piece.

Meat arrives rarely. Livestock raised from below are distressed by the ascent and seldom kept long. The city has no native tradition of herding, and many plateau children are uneasy around large animals. A cow seems to them an improbable volume of warmth. Goats are regarded as worse: intelligent, destructive, and insufficiently afraid of edges.

The few animals that thrive in the city are treated less as pets than as colleagues.

Cistern salamanders are counted during annual cleaning, each recognized by the pattern of red gills and the faint shapes of its organs. Children give them names, while reservoir officials insist on numbers. Both are entered in the records. If a salamander leaves its usual tank or rises repeatedly to the surface, the water is tested before anyone drinks.

Swifts are welcomed with a suspension of certain work. Fans are slowed, hot vents screened, and mesh collectors furled to prevent collisions. People stand in doorways watching the birds stitch the streets together. Because swifts spend most of their lives in flight, the plateau people say they belong not to the city but to the interval through which the city occasionally passes.

As for the six-toed fox, there are casts of tracks, tufts of coarse pale fur, and one blurred chemical photograph in the civic archive. No body has been found. Rim gardeners leave scraps for it in sheltered places, and the scraps disappear. Engineers attribute this to scavenging swifts, though swifts do not eat boiled roots and cannot lift iron-latched lids. The city emblem shows the fox looking backward while its front paws continue ahead. It signifies caution without retreat.

Not everyone stays.

Young people sometimes descend with freight crews, drawn by stories of rivers, deep soil, thunderstorms seen from beneath, and forests so tall that water falls from leaves in drops larger than coins. Those who return are changed by abundance. For months they leave taps open by mistake. They sleep poorly without the vibration of machinery. They complain that lowland rain is wasteful and that the horizon is too far away to trust.

Lowlanders who settle above change in other ways. At first they hurry, speak loudly, and close every window against damp. Within a few winters they begin collecting jars. They wake before condensation peaks. They can tell from the taste of a railing whether cloud has come from the industrial valleys or the forested slopes. Eventually they stop saying that the plateau has bad weather and begin describing weather as work arriving.

The most isolated inhabitants live near the abandoned margins.

They are fog-net tenders, lichen wardens, retired engineers, and people who prefer structures with fewer human voices. Some occupy rooms separated from the rest of a house by a missing corridor, reaching them by ladders or suspended walkways. They know the ruins intimately and claim that each has its own pressure behavior. A door that opens inward before rim ice. A stair rail that sings during distant rockfall. A cistern mouth that exhales warm air when the far cliffs are clear.

These people are responsible for many reports the city does not officially accept.

They speak of footsteps crossing floors that collapsed decades ago. They describe wet handprints appearing high on the exterior walls before storms. They say old buildings continue to collect more water than their surviving roofs could catch, and that certain sealed cisterns fill from below during cloudless nights.

There is also the matter of the lights.

On rare clear evenings, watchers see them on the far plateau where no settlement is listed: first one, then several, pale and steady above the black terraces. The civic observatory calls them mineral reflections, electrical discharge, or images displaced by layers of cold air. Lift workers say they are maintenance lamps. Seed-keepers possess songs that mention a sister city in that direction, though the songs may predate the last known bridge collapse.

Signals have been attempted. Mirrors by day, shuttered lamps by night, tension patterns sent along the remnants of old bridge cable. No reply has been formally recorded.

Nevertheless, some mornings a little extra water is found in the eastern cisterns.

It differs from the city’s usual condensation. It is clear, faintly warm, and carries spores belonging to no plant in the seed vaults. The moisture clerks label these findings as instrument error. Gardeners place a drop on trays of sterile soil. Now and then, something germinates: a colorless thread, a fan of black leaves, a moss that curls toward distant vibration.

Such growths are carried quietly to the seed-keepers.

Nothing living on the plateau is discarded merely because no one can say where it came from.
