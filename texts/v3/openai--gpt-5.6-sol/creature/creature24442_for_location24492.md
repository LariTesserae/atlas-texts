What lives there is easy to mistake for geology.

The smallest inhabitants wear the plateau on their bodies. Dust fills the seams of their armor; iron stains their undersides; lichens grow on the oldest among them until a resting animal becomes indistinguishable from a stone. The six-legged grazers advance in narrow files, each individual following traces of warmth and chemical absence left by the one ahead. They have blunt, sealing mouths rather than jaws. Pressed against rock, they soften the invisible skin of microbes and spores with a drop of solvent, then draw it in. Their passage is recorded as clean, dark strokes across the mineral crust.

They are slow only when considered alone. A file may outlast rivers. Generations hatch, feed, mate, and die without its leading edge ever stopping. Eggs are cemented into frost cracks, where geothermal warmth reaches them from below. The young emerge during rain and join the moving line; the dead are dismantled by pale followers with translucent bodies and soft, rasping limbs. Others come behind to consume the faint heat of decomposition. Thus even a procession has its own ecology: grazer, scavenger, heat-feeder, parasite, and the minute organisms carried in the grooves of their shells.

Many plateau animals have no fixed boundary between body and habitat. Burrowers swallow gravel and return it sorted. Lake creatures join one another into conductive sheets. Cloud drifters exchange internal gases until a flock behaves like a single buoyant organ. Colonies separate when conditions worsen and reassemble years later from individuals that may never have belonged to the same generation.

The drifters are nearly weightless: sacs of clear tissue stretched over branching conductive threads. Their skins gather charge from ice crystals and distant storms. By making one side of the body more positive than the other, they slide across electrical gradients in the clouds. They have little need for eyes. Instead, they sense pressure, charge, and the chemical signatures rising from warm basins.

From the ground they appear only as a dimming of the overcast. Yet the cloud layer can contain billions of them. Some filter airborne spores. Some hunt by discharging a brief field that stuns smaller floaters. Some trail filaments into the mist and gather beads of water along them. Heavy with condensation, they descend to the plateau, lay pebble-like eggs in seepages, and collapse into films that feed the pools. Others rise from those same pools decades later, inflated by gases made within symbiotic chambers.

The greatest surface animals are the carriers.

They stand on six or eight reed-thin legs, depending on age; younger limbs divide as the body grows heavier. Each foot spreads beneath the gravel into a temporary web, distributing weight so gradually that even saturated ground barely trembles. The torso hangs high above, ribbed with expandable bladders. From its underside descend curtains of black fiber, sometimes hundreds of meters long. Mist pearls on these fibers and runs upward as well as down, drawn by capillary channels into reservoirs along the animal’s flanks.

A carrier is less an individual than a moving watershed. Mosses root in the sheltered joints of its legs. Grazers climb its condensation curtains. Parasites live in its warm reservoirs; filter colonies line its throat; drifter eggs cling to its upper back where cloud and flesh meet. Whole populations are born, reproduce, and disappear during a single migration from a cold basin to a geothermal one.

The carrier does not govern them. It may not distinguish them from its own organs.

Its awareness is broad and unhurried. It senses groundwater through pressure in its feet and the distant movement of its kind through vibrations crossing the bedrock. A signal may take hours to pass along its body. The decision to turn may begin in the foremost limbs and reach the rearmost only after sunset. When deep conduits contract beneath the plateau, carriers freeze with their feet raised—not from alarm as a quick animal would know it, but because the interruption passes slowly through them, suspending motion until the ground becomes legible again.

They meet only rarely. Courtship occurs across great distances through patterned warmth sent into the soil. Two animals may answer one another for years while still beyond the horizon. When at last their routes converge, they exchange water, mineral slurries, symbiotic colonies, and clusters of eggs. Their young do not resemble them. They begin as soft, many-limbed things in warm mud, feeding on bacterial mats until their bodies lighten and their first condensation fibers emerge.

Not all the great animals are carriers. Across ore-rich country move low refinery-herds: massive, plated organisms whose bellies drag close to the ground. They crop mud, slate, mineral crust, and salt. Within them, nested chambers maintain sharply different chemistries—acidic, alkaline, oxygenless, furnace-hot. Metals precipitate in branching internal racks that are periodically shed. Their dung cools into black glass or porous metallic foam.

Over ages, their feeding has altered entire valleys. They follow the faint electrical signatures of buried ore, leaving sifted gravel behind. Calves remain near old slag fields, licking residual compounds from the glass. Adults travel alone or in widely spaced groups, communicating through low thermal pulses. Predators seldom attack them; their tissues are mineral-poisoned, and their body heat can cook anything trapped beneath the plates. Instead, specialized thieves pierce the softer membranes around the joints and steal droplets of refined metal needed to harden their eggs.

The lakes have their own societies, if society is not too deliberate a word.

In the salt-rimmed shallows, flat-bodied electrical animals bury themselves in black mud. Alone, each produces only a weak charge. During the breeding season they rise and link edge to edge, matching the conductive patterns in their skins. A pool becomes one trembling membrane. Nearby pools answer through waterlogged seams in the rock, and current travels across a watershed in blue, silent pulses.

The grid is courtship, nursery, and feeding apparatus at once. Charge draws dissolved metals toward the colonies. Eggs orient themselves along the field. Microbes bloom where the current alters the water chemistry. Predators enter and become disoriented, unable to distinguish their own nerve signals from the rhythm of the lake.

No individual understands the circuit. Break the chain and each creature writhes blindly in the mud; restore it and the whole lake acquires direction.

Below all of this is the greater population.

The plateau’s visible animals are only the exposed parts of a biosphere packed into pores and fractures. Soft miners push through cracks by inflating chambers along their bodies. Filterers spread membranes across underground trickles. Bacterial furnaces occupy mineral pockets, generating heat from reactions that proceed too slowly to sustain surface life. Pale thread-creatures connect these pockets, transporting salts and dismantling anything that dies above.

They are why carcasses vanish downward.

When a body falls, warmth and pressure summon them. The ground loosens beneath it. Fine threads rise into flesh, divide it along natural seams, and draw it underground in pieces small enough to pass between grains. Armor remains longest, but even that is softened by acids and carried away. The scavenging is so complete that death leaves less evidence than a footprint: a shallow hollow, briefly warm, with unusually clean gravel at its center.

Some deep organisms may extend for kilometers without possessing a central body. They line connected fractures as living conduits, contracting once every few months to move air and mineral vapor. Their contractions produce the plateau’s solitary note. Pools shiver; grains turn; surface animals flatten themselves to the stone. The vibration is not a warning, though life has learned to treat it as one. It is the respiration of something too dispersed to rise into view.

There are hunters here, but few chase.

One resembles a frost-darkened ledge until a grazer file crosses it. It warms a narrow patch of ground, drawing the cold-sensitive animals inward, then closes flexible stone-colored plates around them. Another waits beneath thin ice and reads footsteps as changes in water pressure. Cloud predators cast charged nets rather than pursuing prey. Even parasites may take years to complete an attack, growing unnoticed within a carrier’s reservoir until a wet season allows their young to escape.

Speed would be expensive and noise useless. Survival belongs to patience, insulation, chemical disguise, and the ability to wait without dying.

Accordingly, many inhabitants spend most of their existence dormant. Plants fold themselves into black knots. Eggs become indistinguishable from pebbles. Burrowers drain the liquid from their tissues and persist as mineral-crusted membranes. A dry basin may appear dead for half a century while holding millions of suspended lives a hand’s depth below the surface.

Then moisture returns.

Fans unfold from the soil. Armor cracks open. Drifters descend like sheets of dim glass. Lake animals join and kindle. Grazers reconstitute their ancient files. Carriers turn toward warmth, each bearing its hanging gardens and internal ponds. The empty horizon begins to move in every direction at once.

Nothing rules this life, and nothing designed its industry. There is no dominant creature in the ordinary sense. The closest thing to sovereignty is the cycle itself: stone passing through stomachs, water passing through bodies, charge passing through lakes, heat rising through colonies, and the dead being taken gently back beneath the ground.

The inhabitants do not build a world apart from nature.

They are the mills, roads, reservoirs, furnaces, wires, and weather of the world they inhabit.
