At first, the inhabited places do not appear inhabited.

There is no smoke above them, no cleared perimeter, no regular field. A settlement seen across a hollow resembles a patch of thickened foliage: a darker web beneath broad leaves, a few gourds hanging at deliberate intervals, ladders that might be aerial roots. Its people reveal themselves only when something needs doing. One figure crawls out along the underside of a bridge. Another rises from a cistern rim with a bowl tied beneath the chin. Three more, previously mistaken for burl growths, loosen their rain capes and begin walking.

They are not a single people. The woven world separates communities too thoroughly for that. A village a day away may have different feet, different sleep, a different count of fingers, and no intelligible spoken language. Yet the altitude has encouraged certain forms again and again.

Most of its people are slight without seeming frail. Their weight is distributed across long, flexible bodies, broad hands, gripping feet, and sometimes a muscular tail or a second thumb at the heel. They seldom stand fully upright except in wide halls. On roads they walk with knees bent and one hand near the weave, moving in a soft, deliberate sequence that keeps three points of contact whenever possible.

Their toes are dexterous. Their shoulders turn farther than seems comfortable. The inner wrists, soles, knees, and breastbone are sensitive to vibration. Even sleeping children wake when a load-bearing cord changes its note.

None of these traits is universal. In the cloudward districts live broad, pale people with dense insulating hair and nostrils that can close against pollen. Along the marsh platforms, families tend toward long shins, splayed feet, and skin darkened by symbiotic growths that deter biting insects. In the narrow vertical countries, adults bind their infants against the back until the children’s hands learn to close reflexively at any sudden drop. Some lower-route peoples are squat and strong, with thick necks and lamp-bearing algae cultivated in translucent folds around the jaw. Travelers sometimes call these differences adaptations. The inhabitants more often describe them as consequences of proper residence.

“You become where you are held,” they say.

Clothing is part garment, part equipment, part cultivated habitat. A person may wear a mantle of overlapping leaf scales, a belt from which tools sprout in living sheaths, and a hood lined with absorbent moss. Rain capes are trained rather than sewn. Their veins are pinched shut before travel so the garment will not root to every damp railing. Shoes are uncommon on secure roads; where they are needed, they are woven around the foot each morning and cut away before sleep.

Everyone carries cord.

Cord binds tools, marks safe turns, takes strain from wounded bridges, lowers food into cool hollows, secures the dead, records obligations, and provides the first defense against an uncertain room. Knots have dialects. A loop tied by a stranger may mean danger, hospitality, rot, private grief, or simply that the stranger did not know how to finish the line.

The characteristic tool is the hooked knife. Its blade may be shell, tooth, glassy resin, ceramic, or a sliver of inherited metal worn nearly transparent. It is used to prune, scrape, open bark, divide food, release trapped animals, cut umbilical cords, excise diseased wood, and sever unsafe architecture. A person trusted with a metal hook is not necessarily a ruler, but others watch what they cut.

No child is permitted a hooked knife until they have learned to close a wound.

Closing may involve wax, pressure, fungal paste, splints of green wood, the transplanting of bark, or the insertion of a tiny boring animal that eats only dead tissue. The same methods are applied to bodies and buildings. Healers apprentice beside bridgekeepers. Midwives inspect root anchors. Those who understand fever also understand fermentation and the dangerous warmth of compost.

The inhabitants have many words for kinds of damage, but few distinguish injury from structural failure.

A torn shoulder is said to have lost its braid. A sagging floor is fevered. Mold in the lungs and mold in a cistern wall are treated as neighboring forms of damp misbehavior. When a person becomes very old, relatives speak of where they have begun to soften and what new uses this softness might permit.

Their settlements are rarely permanent in outline. Rooms bud, merge, close, and migrate around the people using them. Houses have no stable boundary from public architecture. One family may sleep in the curled inner surface of a village roof; another inhabits pockets along a handrail; a third keeps its bedding in sealed pods carried between communal warmth chambers.

Privacy is achieved by stillness, scent, and etiquette rather than walls. A person who turns the pale side of their sleeping mat outward is not to be addressed. Certain vines are planted around courtship spaces because their flowers close when disturbed. Grief shelters are lined with fibers that absorb vibration, preventing condolences from arriving through the floor.

A village is defined less by shared enclosure than by shared maintenance. Its members are those who answer when a particular stretch of world strains.

When the great braids begin to tighten, conversation stops. Hands go to floors, pillars, rails, or one another. Experienced keepers read the passing creaks as a physician might read a pulse. They can tell whether a distant span has taken rain, whether a hanging grove has slipped, whether a hollow has opened somewhere below. Afterward, runners depart with wedges, cord, grafting stock, and small sealed pots of ember.

This response is not generosity. It is survival, custom, and law all at once.

Their law has little concern with ownership of surfaces. No one owns a bridge in the manner that one might own a bowl. Instead, people possess pruning rights, burdens of repair, rights to gather particular fruits, obligations to keep a passage remembering its destination, and the authority to decide which shoots may become walls. Such claims overlap. A cistern can belong to one lineage for drinking, another for algae, a migratory flock during spawning, and the rain itself during three months of the warm dim season.

Disputes are settled by asking what the structure has learned.

Witnesses walk the contested route repeatedly. Water is poured. Old knots are opened and examined for the order in which their fibers took strain. Sometimes the place makes its preference plain: a door curls shut against one claimant, or a platform leans toward the household that has maintained it longest. Sometimes it does not. Then judgment falls to those who carry the oldest maps.

Mapkeepers are recognizable by their damp packs and stained forearms. They tend living diagrams in shallow trays: threads of root corresponding to roads, knots, wells, halls, and weather chambers. The map is not proportional. It is relational and temperamental. A dangerous bridge may appear as a trembling red vein. A route that has forgotten regular use grows pale and loops toward unrelated places. Mapkeepers feed each strand water, blood, sap, roof condensation, or cistern scum brought from the represented site.

They are cautious about drawing new connections. A carelessly grafted map can encourage two actual roads to meet.

Most journeys are therefore ceremonial even when their purpose is mundane. Before departure, travelers recite the route in sequence. At junctions they speak names aloud, touch the same guide poles, and perform small acts that remind the path what it is: three heel taps, a twist of cord, a smear of scented paste. Solitary travel is considered less brave than impolite. A road walked by one person may decide that one person is all it is required to carry.

Names themselves are maintained. A child receives several.

There is a cradle-name, used in the warmth chamber; a path-name, given when the child first returns unaided from another household; and a weight-name, known to those who will pull if the child falls. Adults acquire further names from places they have repaired, illnesses survived, promises carried, and errors that must not be repeated. A person’s full introduction may take an hour. To forget one of their names is not merely discourtesy. Forgotten names can detach from their owners and remain in rooms, causing doors to open for someone long departed.

Speech differs according to material. In moss halls people use voice. On load-bearing roads, important information is tapped through heel and palm. Across short gaps they pull patterned cords. In dense rain, when sound and tension are confused, they release packets of scent from crushed leaves.

Some communities consider voiced language dangerously imprecise. Others sing continually—not loudly, but into jars, hollows, and the mouths of tubes that carry each tone to a known destination. Songs may emerge far away and much later. A lullaby begun for an infant can arrive after that infant has become old. Such delayed songs are kept and answered. No one assumes communication must be immediate to remain binding.

Food comes from thousands of small arrangements rather than broad cultivation. People scrape sweet cambium from trees bred to survive the loss. They eat fungal loaves risen in warm wall pockets, sour fruit, marsh eggs, pollen curd, grubs drawn from sacrificial beams, and translucent fish from cisterns. Leaf milk is collected in lacquered bladders. Bitter sap is fermented until its poisons become perfume and heat.

Meat is eaten, but hunting is usually an act of architectural negotiation. A hunter does not simply follow an animal. They learn which routes its weight will choose, which membranes will hold it, and where the floor can be persuaded to tilt. Traps are grown slowly: a harmless fork in a path encouraged over years to curl around prey of a certain pressure and gait.

The best hunters barely move. They send taps along root networks and wait for an answering stillness.

Their prey also listens.

Velvet-backed wall grazers press all their limbs flat when they sense a pattern that does not belong to weather. Their soft circular mouths leave pale rings across bark. Many carry seeds in chambers beneath the tongue, planting them inadvertently wherever they rest. Villages tolerate them because their paths keep walls clear of smothering moss, but a herd can erase carved records in a single night.

Beaked marsh grazers are less tolerated and more revered. They travel in nervous lines along the water’s edge, cutting channels with the lower halves of their bills. Their migrations drain flooded platforms and redirect entire watersheds. Marsh people mark the first hatchlings with harmless dye, then spend years tracking the colored lines through pools and reed galleries. When a flock chooses a new route, villages may have to move. No one tries to turn them by force. Their beaks are small, but water obeys the work of many mouths.

The transparent amphibians in the cisterns are used as clocks, omens, and diagnostic instruments. Their internal algae brighten according to depth, temperature, and unseen changes in the water. A healthy pool at rest resembles a sparse green sky. When every amphibian rises shining to the surface, people evacuate the surrounding structure. Something below is displacing air.

The gliders are more various than distant observers realize. Some are animals: warm-eyed, nearly weightless beings whose limbs have become tensioned spars. Some are windborne colonies, each apparent body assembled temporarily from hundreds of ribbon organisms. A few are cultivated craft that have acquired appetite and mating behavior after generations of use.

People travel with all three.

A domestic glider sleeps folded against a wall like a wet cloak. Its handler oils the joints of its ribs, trims its trailing membranes, and feeds it fruit pulp before flight. It dislikes open flame and certain knots. Older gliders become stubborn about destination. If flown too often along one route, they may refuse all others and eventually continue the journey without a rider.

Other animals live closer to the structure’s intimate scale. There are seam moths whose larvae stitch separating bark with silk. Peg beetles bore perfectly round holes and must be removed before they weaken a joint, then carried to workshops where their boring is useful. Small flat creatures called handlings nest beneath railings and press their warm bodies against fingers in cold cloud. Rope mites graze destructive molds. Pale, blind orchard crabs prune root tips with delicate claws.

Many of these are not domesticated in the sense of obedience. They are participants in maintenance. They occupy settlements because the settlements have made themselves suitable, and people make themselves suitable in return.

Even parasites can become civic institutions. One lower-hollow people cultivates a throat worm that produces an anticoagulant needed for grafting. In the upper condensation forests, a mite that lives in human eyelashes detects toxic pollen; its agitation warns the host to cover their face. Children are introduced to their first useful symbionts in a ceremony resembling both inoculation and adoption.

Birth occurs in chambers trained to remember birth. Their walls are warm, damp, fibrous, and faintly contractile. Generations of laboring bodies have taught them to tighten in rhythm. Midwives listen not only to the parent and child but to the room, which may begin pulsing too early or continue after the birth is complete.

Newborns are rubbed with local sap, touched to the floor, and briefly suspended over a shallow opening. This is not a test of courage. It introduces the child’s weight to the structure. The family names the child while the bridge, house, or platform is under load, so that person and place learn one another together.

Childhood is full of falling practice.

Children fall into nets, moss pits, loops, arms, and low warm pools. They learn not to stiffen. They learn to catch cord without wrapping it around the wrist, to spread their weight across weak matting, to distinguish a guide vine from a strangling one in darkness. Their games involve navigating rooms with closed eyes, passing messages by floor-tap, and building tiny bridges that living beetles are invited to cross.

A bridge that fails beneath beetles is dismantled without shame. A bridge that fails beneath trust is remembered for generations.

Death presents more difficulty. Burial is impossible where there is no soil, and burning is too dangerous. Most dead are given back by specialization.

Some are wrapped in fruiting cloth and fastened into orchard walls, where their nutrients pass into sweet pods reserved for descendants. Some are lowered into fungal chambers until only cleaned bones remain. The bones may become pegs, sounding pieces, tool handles, or white lattices around new grafts. In districts where bonewood is common, the distinction between ancestral skeleton and cultivated timber is intentionally blurred.

Those whose lives were strongly associated with a road may be laid beneath its surface. Travelers speak their names at the place where the body enters the weave. Over time the road may take on the dead person’s habits: a familiar bend, a tendency to lead toward water, a refusal to admit someone they distrusted. Such roads are treated as ancestors, though no one agrees how much of the person remains.

There are also those who do not die cleanly enough to be returned.

A person lost in an old knot may come back by several doors over many years, each arrival incomplete. One version remembers childhood, another bears the body’s scars but recognizes no kin, a third consists only of a shadow that taps familiar rhythms. Communities differ in their response. Some accept every return as a portion of the same person. Some select one and forbid the others entry. Some tie the fragments together with cord and lead them in procession until the knot reconciles them or takes them back.

The deepest social fear is not death but misdirection: that a person, lineage, room, or promise will cease to arrive where it belongs.

For this reason, repetitive labor is sacred. Sweeping the same gallery, renewing the same knot, drawing water at the same hour, telling a story in its established chamber—these acts keep more than custom alive. They preserve adjacency. They persuade the world to continue placing the nursery beside the warmth room, the cistern beneath rain, the village within reach of its dead.

Innovation is not forbidden, but it is undertaken carefully. A new door is opened first with a thread tied to the old one. A new road is walked backward before anyone walks it forward. A newly built chamber remains unnamed until it has passed through rain, drought, sleep, argument, illness, and birth. Only then is it considered to have chosen what kind of room it means to be.

There are specialists who deal with structures that have chosen badly.

They are called by names that translate imperfectly as unrememberers, mercy-pruners, or those-who-close-the-mouth. They travel in small groups carrying girdling knives, sleep smoke, bitter salts, and stones.

The stones are their most terrible tools.

A pebble has no growth, no fiber, no obvious memory. It cannot be persuaded by water or habit. Placed in a wall, it introduces a point of refusal. Certain predatory rooms recoil from one. Loops in direction can sometimes be pinned open by three stones. A door that has learned to swallow travelers may be made to hold still long enough for its stems to be cut.

Because stone is rare, each use requires witnesses. The pebble is recovered if possible. A lost one is mourned as both treasure and contamination.

Predatory architecture is uncommon, but not mythical. There are halls that encourage sleep and slowly seal their exits. Bridges that cultivate panic because frightened feet strike a rhythm they enjoy. Nurseries abandoned after they began warming anything placed inside until it hatched, whether egg, seed, corpse, or tool. Whole districts have learned to attract maintenance crews by imitating the creak of structural distress.

The inhabitants do not consider these places evil. Evil requires obligation, and not every living thing has entered into obligation. But they mark such places with dead cord, broken knots, scentless wax, and silence.

Beyond maintained routes live people who have relinquished certain obligations themselves.

Some are exiles. Some are mystics. Some belong to migrating societies that refuse to let roads learn them too well. They cultivate no permanent doors and erase their camps by feeding the structures vigorous, forgetful molds. Their maps are carried in the patterns of scars on their bodies. They claim that fixed settlements are slowly being digested by their own habits.

Settled peoples call them the Unfastened, usually with suspicion. Yet when a known route begins returning travelers to the wrong century, it is often the Unfastened who know a way around.

Higher still, where cloud is continuous, live the condensers. Their villages hang from the underside of giant leaves, and their lives follow droplets. They stretch hair-fine catch lines through mist, comb water from furred surfaces, and breed pale insects whose wings precipitate fog. Many rarely see an open hollow. They believe the world is suspended not over emptiness but inside a single immeasurable cloud, and that the dark lower reaches are places where cloud has not yet learned to become water.

Farther down are the weight-bearers.

Little is known of them by surface societies. They inhabit the great interior braids where cables converge and gravity seems to pull in several directions. Their bodies are said to be massive, though this may mean only that they move slowly. They reinforce the lower world with whole trunks, knotting structures no upper people could shift. Sometimes they send objects upward in baskets: compressed fungal cakes, blind white fish, coils of impossible thickness, pieces of old metal, and tablets of bark punched with pressure-messages.

The messages often concern burdens imposed centuries ago.

Below even the weight-bearers move the heavy things.

They are not simply large animals. Their passing alters the orientation of hanging forests. Dust rises ahead of them. Bridges lift, remain taut for hours, and settle somewhere else. Pools develop tides. Once in a generation, a pale surface broad as a district becomes visible through the lower haze, curving as it moves. It may be hide, shell, fungal tissue, or part of the construction itself drawing breath.

Some inhabitants believe these beings built the first supports and now wander inside their work. Others believe they are maintenance creatures grown too large to recognize local damage. The most unsettling opinion is that they are inhabitants in the same sense as everyone above: small beings traveling upon a still greater weave, unable to perceive its whole.

No one descends far enough to ask.

The woven world does not produce a clean division between person, animal, tool, and place. A glider can become a route. A lineage may be embodied in an orchard. A room can acquire preference, hunger, and responsibility. A bridge may be punished by withholding traffic, then welcomed back after it has grown a safer handrail. Animals participate in drainage law. Fungi inherit houses. The dead continue as load-bearing members of their communities.

Personhood accumulates through attention.

Anything repeatedly greeted may eventually answer. Anything entrusted with lives may develop duties. Anything maintained long enough begins to be consulted.

And so the most characteristic inhabitant is not a particular species or shape. It is a small, warm presence with a knife, a cord, and one hand laid against something vastly older—listening for strain, deciding where to cut and where to bind, aware that the world beneath that hand is listening in return.
