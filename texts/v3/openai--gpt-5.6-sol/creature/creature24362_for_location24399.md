At first there seem to be no inhabitants.

Then one begins to notice the evidence of them—not tracks, exactly, but corrections.

A lamp found turned away from the road will be facing it again after reversal. A valve crusted shut for centuries acquires a fresh crescent of exposed metal. Loose bolts vanish from the centers of walkways and appear sorted by diameter beneath overhangs. The pale mineral scum on a cistern may be marked by five parallel lines, each beginning at shoulder height and continuing down into the water.

Sometimes there are dry prints on wet paving.

They are long and narrow, divided across the middle as though made by a hinged foot. They do not depress the dust. Instead they remove moisture so completely that the stone remains pale for hours. A line of them may cross a flooded court, continue up the side of a wall, and end at a sealed inspection hatch twenty meters above the ground.

No one sees them being made.

The things that live here emerge only when the machinery has created sufficient difference between one place and another: pressure against vacuum, warmth beside cold, motion adjoining stillness. For most of the cycle they are distributed through the works. A portion sleeps as mineral paste inside a pipe. Another hangs in the green glass of an inspection lamp. A sequence of impulses waits in the relays beneath a road. Their water circulates with all the other water and cannot yet be distinguished from it.

Reversal gathers them.

As the nine siren tones descend, certain drains cease accepting water. Dark liquid wells upward and stands in columns above the grates. Washers, wire, porcelain fragments, scales of rust, lengths of gasket fiber, and strips of translucent fungal matter rise within those columns. They turn slowly, finding their accustomed levels. The heavier pieces settle around the hips and feet. Fine conductors spread through the chest. Ceramic insulators lock around the neck. Last of all, a shallow bowl of gauge glass lifts from the water and closes over the front of the head.

When the current changes direction, the figure steps off the drain.

It is usually taller than a human being, though height varies with local pressure. Its body is extremely narrow and appears clothed in overlapping black plates. These plates are neither armor nor skin. They are pieces of the plain worn smooth enough to bend: old belt segments, sheets of mica, softened metal mesh, flakes from the inner walls of tanks. Rain runs through them rather than over them.

There is no flesh beneath.

The inner body is a maintained arrangement of water. It travels upward through one leg, crosses the chest in braided streams, and descends through the other. Small turbines turn where organs might be. Strings of bubbles carry signals along transparent tubes. The hands are clusters of fine, stiff probes, each finger ending in a different obsolete fitting: square key, threaded pin, ceramic hook, magnet, brush. No two hands are quite alike.

Behind the gauge glass of the face there may be a needle, several needles, or none. Some faces contain a dim green field with a bubble wandering through it. Some show columns of numbers that advance without repeating. The oldest are opaque with deposits. They navigate without turning their heads, guided by pressure changes in the soles of their feet and the vibration of buried machinery.

They do not walk like animals. Each step appears to be the conclusion of a calculation made elsewhere.

One foot is set down. Water withdraws beneath it. The body inclines. Internal valves click in order from ankle to throat. Then the rear foot is lifted and carried forward with grave precision. When several cross an open court together, they do not form a line or cluster. They distribute themselves at exact intervals, like components placed on a drawing too large to see.

They are most active during reversal, but they are not the cause of it. Nor, in any simple sense, are they its servants. They inspect the event as though trying to determine whether it has happened correctly.

One will stand beside a sluice and lower its hand into the torrent. Another presses its glass face against the wall of a pumping house, reading vibrations through the masonry. Others climb the pressure towers and wait around the enormous gauges, each resident watching a different imperceptible motion of the needle.

If a gauge fails to advance, they do not repair it.

They adjust the world around it.

A channel may be blocked several districts away. A warm pipe is wrapped in wet moss. Three lamps are extinguished. A door marked **KEEP CLEAR** is carefully opened and filled with debris until it can never close again. Weeks afterward the gauge needle moves a fraction, and the alteration is complete.

Their work is not maintenance as the word was once understood. They preserve relationships, not objects. A pump may be permitted to collapse if its failure keeps two reservoirs at the proper difference in temperature. An intact bridge may be dismantled because its vibration has begun to coincide with a forbidden harmonic. They will polish an unreadable warning plate while ignoring a crack widening beneath it.

The instruction matters. The meaning does not.

They possess no spoken language. The continuous noise would render one useless, and the quiet pockets are treated with too much care to be filled with voices. Instead they communicate through interruption.

A resident pauses in front of a lamp and allows its shadow to cross the beam in measured lengths. Another answers by tapping a conduit, sometimes from kilometers away. Messages travel as delayed valve closures, pressure drops, changes in the pitch of chains. A conversation between two of them may occupy an entire season and involve several thousand tons of water.

Close at hand, they also exchange small objects.

A rusted ring passed from one hand to another may refer to a district, an unfinished route, or a condition of waiting. Blue-stained glass concerns water that has spent too long above ground. Teeth from old gears are used for quantities, though whether they count hours, cycles, or obligations is uncertain. The most serious messages are carried in sealed bearings. These are opened only inside the ring of a defunct turbine, where no mechanical sound intrudes.

There, in the engineered silence, the residents gather.

They stand around the inner wall with their hands lowered. Water clicks softly inside them. One by one, each opens a narrow vent beneath its face, releasing a single drop onto the floor.

The drops do not merge. They tremble in place, flattened by minute oscillations traveling through the foundation. The gathering may last until every drop has evaporated or crawled back toward the figure that released it. Nothing visible is decided. Afterward, however, routes change. A resident that has walked the same northern circuit for centuries may turn south. A whole district of lamps may begin blinking in a pattern not seen before.

It is tempting to imagine individuals among them. There are certainly recurring forms.

One has a brass left arm and carries a length of red cable coiled through its ribs. It appears wherever buried conduits are about to rupture. Another is small, scarcely higher than the rails, with a face made from the convex window of an old level gauge. It spends each reversal moving stranded reeds out of intake grilles. A third has been observed standing beneath the same broken sign through countless cycles, raising both hands whenever the words **IN THE EVENT OF DAYLIGHT** become faintly visible in the pallor overhead.

But a recurring form is not necessarily a recurring self.

When the cycle ends, the residents come apart.

The pressure maintaining their bodies weakens. Their gait becomes loose. Plates slip from their shoulders. Water begins escaping through the joints in silver threads. They return toward drains, flooded shafts, valve pits, and the foundations of pumping houses, but not always toward the places from which they emerged.

A figure may kneel over a gutter and empty itself there. Its gauge-glass face sinks last, looking upward from the black water before the current takes it. Months later that same face may rise in another district on a body assembled from entirely different material. It will continue an interrupted task, or begin one that contradicts it.

Memory does not seem to reside in the parts.

It may reside in the route.

A resident walking from the western cistern to the blue wall will always pause at the seventh access box, regardless of which body walks there. It will touch the erased lettering, remove any object lying beneath it, and carry that object to the next submerged platform. If prevented, it becomes directionless. Its internal streams lose their pattern. The body circulates in narrowing arcs until it collapses.

Once the route is restored, another body can complete the act.

Thus a road may be more nearly a person than the figure traveling upon it. A sequence of valves may be a memory. A repeated rise and fall in water level may be a name.

What looks like a population may be one consciousness divided among circuits. Or what looks like one system may contain thousands of lives, each too extended in space and time to be recognized as singular.

They reproduce, if that is the word, through error.

A route repeated perfectly remains one route. But subsidence bends roads. Gates fail to close. A bridge disappears beneath water, forcing a detour. At first the residents try to correct the deviation. If the deviation persists long enough, they begin to preserve it. New exchanges of objects appear along the altered path. Lamps learn a different sequence. A body rises from a drain where no body rose before.

Eventually the detour acquires its own obligations.

Then there are two routes where there was one, and the plain has gained another inhabitant.

Their deaths are more difficult to recognize. Nothing here is discarded. A resident unable to return before reversal ends may settle where it stands. Water drains from it. Mineral paste hardens around the feet. Rain folds the outer plates downward. In a few years it resembles a narrow control cabinet or a damaged post.

Many of the structures along the roads may be such remains.

Some still respond when a certain pump engages. A needle shifts beneath opaque glass. One hand tightens around an object fused into its palm. The surrounding reeds bend toward it though there is no wind.

The living residents do not mourn these forms. They incorporate them into their work. Messages are hung from their arms. Cables are looped through their ribs. Their hollow bodies become gauges for flood depth. If one blocks a required route, it is dismantled and carried away piece by piece.

Yet they do preserve the drop of water that once circulated inside it.

That water is returned to the system through a small port marked **ALL FLOW**.

The warning about daylight concerns them more than any other instruction.

They have no eyes to close, but when the clouds occasionally thin to a color brighter than industrial pallor, every resident stops. Faceplates turn downward. Vents seal. The pumps continue, but the figures become so still that rain begins building shells around them.

Perhaps direct light reveals something in their water that the darkness conceals. Perhaps it interrupts the faint electrical gradients by which they remain assembled. Perhaps daylight belongs to a condition the machinery was designed to prevent, and obedience has survived understanding.

On the rarest occasions, a white brightness passes across the plain.

Then the residents open their chests.

The water inside them pours instantly into the grooves of the paving. Their bodies collapse in heaps of plate, wire, glass, and rust. Across the country, thousands fall at once without a cry. For the duration of the brightness there is no inhabitant anywhere—only manufactured debris under an unfamiliar sky.

When darkness returns, the pumps recover what was spilled.

Drains inhale. Pipes fill. Needles tremble awake. Far away, a column of black water rises from a grate and begins gathering fragments to itself.

The first figure steps out beneath an amber lamp. It stands listening through its feet to the country below. Then it bends, picks up a rusted ring left on the road before the light, and turns toward an unfinished instruction.

Others emerge across the flooded plain.

They are not masters of the machinery, nor its children, nor the workers it has remembered. They are what persistence has made from labor after purpose is gone: duties that acquired bodies, circuits that learned to hesitate, water taught by endless return to carry a shape.

They live there as the reeds do, along gradients of pressure and heat.

They live there as the lamps do, separately visible and connected beneath the dark.

And when the deep engines engage, each of them begins to move—not toward shelter, food, or companionship, but toward the place where the whole country has become, by one rivet or one drop, incorrect.
