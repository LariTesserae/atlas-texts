At first they are easy to mistake for parts of the gardens.

A figure resting beneath a lintel may seem to be a bundle of folded fronds: soot-dark wrappings, a pale hand closed around a tether, strands of hair stirring like rootlets. They can remain motionless for a very long time. Only the regular opening of the breathing folds along the neck and ribs distinguishes them from the cultivated membranes behind them. Even these move slowly, widening to expose dark red fans, then sealing again.

They are people, though the water has revised nearly every familiar proportion.

Their bodies are slight and long through the limbs, with broad hands and feet suited to pushing from stone. Fingers spread farther than they appear capable of spreading. A web of tough, nearly transparent skin joins them almost to the final knuckle, yet leaves each fingertip free to read carvings, knots, heat, and vibration. Nails are thick and blunt. The soles and palms bear ridged pads that grip wet basalt without cutting against its young edges.

They are naturally buoyant. Fat lies in pale, soft layers beneath the skin, and the cavities around the lungs and digestive organs are threaded with oil-bearing tissues that keep the body from collapsing under pressure. Without ballast, a sleeping person may gradually rise from a bed and come to rest against the ceiling. Adults therefore wear drilled stones at the hips, sternum, and ankles. The choice of stone is personal. A person’s ballast is adjusted throughout life and is among the last things removed from the dead.

Their chests are deep but do not heave. Much of their breathing is done through pleated organs sheltered beneath the jaw, along the ribs, and under the arms. When exertion opens all these folds at once, the body seems briefly fringed. Skin also participates in the exchange, especially across the inner arms and flanks, and must be kept clean of choking sediment. Households groom one another with fine stone combs. Illness is often first noticed as a grayness in the breathing fans or a reluctance to open them fully.

Warm, poorly circulating water gives little oxygen. Haste is costly. Anger, panic, and laughter can leave someone faint. The inhabitants are not incapable of speed; when frightened, they cross a chamber with a single violent stroke. But afterward they must cling near a cool-water sluice, mouths open, while companions comb fresh water across their gills.

Their faces have the softened look of forms shaped without wind. Noses are low and sealable. Ears are small external ridges surrounding openings that can be closed against silt. Their eyes are large but not especially sharp. Most irises are so dark that pupil and color cannot be separated, though some are milky green, copper-brown, or reflective silver. Faces are read at close range. At a distance, posture and the position of the hands matter more.

Sight is only one strand of attention. They feel approaching movement in the fine hairs of the forearms and temples. They sense temperature with pits around the mouth and wrists. Through the jaw they hear impacts conducted by stone. A person leaning with their chin against a wall may be listening to work several streets away.

Hair persists, but is troublesome. It is usually braided flat against the skull, bound in nets, or grown into long cords weighted at their ends. Loose hair belongs to children, mourners, and certain ritual specialists. Among the old, those cords often become gardens in miniature. Luminous microbes settle in them; tiny grazers feed along the braids; carbonate beads accumulate where hair meets copper. Some elders cultivate this deliberately until their heads bear dim constellations.

Their speech is quiet and close. It consists of breathy tones, throat pulses, clicks made against the palate, and gestures performed against another person’s hand. Names have several forms: a spoken murmur, a sequence of finger pressures, and a particular knock that can be given to stone. The hand-name is considered the most intimate because it cannot be called across a room. It must be entrusted.

Much can be said without releasing breath. Fingers laid over the wrist can ask a question. A pressure at the base of the thumb can negate it. Family members hold long conversations while working side by side, their hands meeting only occasionally among the leaves.

They distrust sudden movement less as a moral fault than as a form of waste. Grace means arriving with enough momentum and no more. Children are taught to stop themselves without striking walls, to turn in open water using only wrists and ankles, and to judge whether an object will rise or sink before letting go of it. Their earliest games involve moving between handholds with their eyes covered. Older children race bubbles toward the False Shore, though adults disapprove because some follow too far.

A child is not considered safely born for several months. Newborns are soft, buoyant, and unable to manage their breathing folds in sediment-heavy water. They are carried inside living cradles: translucent pouches cultivated from garden membranes and fastened against an adult’s chest. Water is drawn through the pouch by ciliated surfaces. The infant sees its household as slow shadows beyond the membrane and first learns voices as vibrations through another body.

Children belong most strongly to a dwelling rather than to a pair of parents. A house may contain siblings, cousins, former partners, unrelated dependents, and elders whose own structures have shifted out of reach. Because houses can be detached and moved, ancestry is recorded not only by blood but by anchors. People describe themselves as descending from the eastern crack, the drowned arch, the third root-wall, or a warm spring that has since migrated.

The population is sparse partly because children are rare and slow to mature. Childhood lasts long. Bones require years to strengthen under pressure, and the intricate microbial cultures of the gut and skin must be inherited through food, grooming, and shared water. A person raised away from established households may survive but remain chronically ill. No inhabitant is entirely an individual organism. Each is accompanied by a carefully maintained community of luminous bacteria, digestive fungi, protective skin films, and mineral-fixing microbes passed down through generations.

Their wrappings preserve these living surfaces rather than conceal them. Cloth is woven from split fronds and softened stems, then inoculated with harmless growths that consume waste and discourage infection. Garments fit close at joints and loose over the breathing folds. Colors are rich when seen nearby—black violet, bruised red, green like oxidized copper—but most clothing is chosen by texture. Rank, craft, kinship, and mourning can all be recognized with the fingertips.

They possess little that can be owned without also being tended. A shell blade dulls. A root wall strays. A luminous bowl can starve. A rope frays from within. Property is therefore understood as a chain of care. To own something is to be the person answerable for its continued life or usefulness. Hoarding objects one cannot maintain is regarded as a childish form of cruelty.

Their crafts overlap. Gardeners understand masonry because roots move stone. Masons understand anatomy because walls and bodies both fail along seams. Healers cultivate fungi, regulate water flow, reset ballast, and listen to bone through the same resonant probes used to examine the mountain. There are specialists, but few professions remain separate for long.

The most respected are often the listeners.

They spend years learning the tones of pillars, foundations, root-bound arches, and sealed chambers. Many have callused jaws and patches of pale skin where they habitually press themselves to rock. During a sounding, they station themselves throughout the city and receive the struck note through different parts of the body. One listens with palms, another with teeth, another with the broad plate of the sternum. Their reports may disagree for days before being combined into a map of something no one can see: a new hollow opening below, a fissure pinching shut, a ridge bending under the settlement.

They do not imagine the mountain as inert. Nor do they quite call it alive. It belongs to a category that includes eggs, wounds, weather, unborn children, and slowly opening flowers: things whose present shape is only a stage in an intention not yet revealed.

Death is treated with similar uncertainty.

A body cannot be buried in the usual sense, and burning is unknown. Some dead are enclosed in porous stone cradles and anchored in ancestral gardens. Plants pass roots through the wrappings. The soft tissues feed a grove for many years, while bones are gradually coated in carbonate until they become pale structural forms. A rib may support a nursery membrane. A finger bone may be set into the handle of a family tool. Large pieces are rarely shaped, only incorporated.

Others are given to the upper water. Their ballast is removed, their limbs bound close, and a buoyant stem fastened through the funerary cloth. They ascend slowly until they disappear toward the False Shore. Whether they cross the density boundary or remain suspended beneath it is deliberately not investigated. Following a dead person upward is forbidden.

In reflection season, some claim to see these departed figures moving in the inverted city. They appear altered by distance and mineral haze: too tall, bent backward, or accompanied by structures not yet built below. Skeptics say they are weeds, sediment, or distortions of living observers. Believers reply that an unfinished world must cast unfinished ancestors.

The inhabitants do not agree on what waits above. For some, the reflected settlement is the city’s mature form, drawing the mountain toward itself. For others, it is a warning: every structure has an image that cannot bear weight. Builders tend to be cautious believers. They trust reflections enough to study them but not enough to anchor anything to them.

Their rituals are correspondingly practical. They retie bridges while singing. They exchange hand-names during repairs. Marriages, adoptions, and reconciliations are marked by taking responsibility for a shared wall or garden. A union is considered sound after it has survived its first structural shift. Separation is formalized by dividing the living things fairly, never by cutting them abruptly if they can be teased apart.

Conflict exists, though it rarely resembles an outburst. People feud over warm-water rights, garden succession, neglected roots, dangerous alterations, and whether a district should be moved or allowed to remain where it has always been. Because voices carry, public disputes are conducted almost silently. Opponents kneel at opposite sides of a stone table and present cords knotted with claims and replies. A mediator passes these between them, feeling each knot in turn.

The gravest crime is to create an unannounced hazard: to loosen an anchor, alter a current, move a guide-rope, or send a resonant warning falsely. In a city where every route depends on memory and touch, changing the world without telling others is close to attempted murder.

Yet they are not solemn at all times. Their humor favors patience. A joke may be arranged weeks in advance: a carved shell that gives misleading directions only when approached upside down, a harmless luminous growth encouraged to spell a rude hand-sign across a respected listener’s ballast, a house subtly reweighted so that every cup drifts toward one particular uncle. Laughter is released in small clicking pulses to conserve breath. In crowded rooms it passes from person to person until everyone is trembling soundlessly.

They keep few animals, but the tiny white grazers are sometimes tolerated as household scavengers. Children name them and build shell enclosures they immediately escape. The transparent crack-eels cannot be domesticated. When one appears, work stops. People gather at a respectful distance and watch it taste the water with its narrow head. Its emergence may mean nothing, but nothing in the young mountain is assumed to mean nothing.

The balloon-creatures above the streets are regarded almost as neighboring hermits. Individual ones are recognized by scars, filament length, and the organisms growing on their surfaces. They receive names, though no one knows whether they live long or merely look alike across generations. A district whose balloon-creature drifts away feels briefly abandoned.

No one knows why the gardens possess traps for absent pollinators or chambers shaped for animals too large to pass through any living street. The inhabitants maintain some of these structures anyway. They clean the broad gates. They keep open routes that serve no present traveler. They cultivate fruits they themselves cannot enter, opening them instead with stone wedges to gather the pulp.

This is not hope in the simple sense. It is an obligation to possibilities inherited from the plants.

Their oldest stories do not clearly distinguish between things that vanished and things that have not arrived. The enormous visitor may be an extinct beast, a future animal, an ancestor changed beyond recognition, or the mountain itself when it finally reaches the inverted city. Their language places all these in the same tense: the tense for what is absent but still exerts a shape.

So they leave room.

They leave room in gates, in genealogies, in plans for new districts. They leave slack in ropes and growing space around roots. They build houses that can be detached, laws that can survive the migration of a spring, and identities that do not depend on the ground remaining where it was.

A stranger might see them as frail: few in number, slow-moving, poorly armed, living among unfinished stone with shell knives and cords. But frailty is not how they understand themselves. Hardness belongs to basalt, and basalt splits. Survival belongs to what yields without coming loose.

They are patient without being passive. They wait in the manner of roots, exerting pressure continuously.

And while the mountain rises beneath them, they alter with it—retied, regrown, inherited, never quite completed.
