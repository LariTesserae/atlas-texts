Nothing here lives entirely in the present.

The beetles come closest. They are narrow, deliberate things, black enough to seem cut from the mountain’s fresh crust. After rain they emerge from hairline fissures in the basalt and walk toward the weeping cliffs. Each carries a single clear droplet in a shallow basin on its back. The droplet is larger than the beetle’s abdomen and never spills, even when the animal descends a vertical face. At the violet moss, the beetle lowers itself until the water touches the filaments. The moss closes over it like lips.

Whether the beetles drink, pollinate, or deliver something suspended in the droplets is unclear. They are occasionally found beneath the cooling roof, motionless but not dead, their legs folded with geometric precision. Removed from the shade, they resume walking from the exact point in their stride where they stopped.

The fish are less securely animals. They have no eggs, nests, or young. They enter with the rising water, already of several sizes, and vanish when the condensation falls. Their colorless bodies contain a single dark filament running from mouth to tail. When several swim together, these lines briefly join in reflection, forming diagrams like root systems or maps of underground channels. A fish lifted from the marsh becomes a drop of warm water in the hand. Sometimes the dark filament remains, writhing briefly across the palm before sinking through the skin without wound or pain.

The gliders belong partly to the clouds. Their wings are veined membranes stretched around an empty-looking center. No mouth, eye, or head can be found, though their bodies turn toward anyone who approaches. Hanging beneath the fern fronds, they resemble discarded translucent gloves. They feed without biting. A frond sheltered by them loses heat and gains water; by morning its mineral veins have grown into the same branching pattern as the glider’s wings.

They may be spores of some aerial organism rather than creatures in their own right. When they depart, they do not beat their wings. Condensation forms above them, and they fall upward from one new droplet to the next.

These small lives occupy the garden openly. The true inhabitants do not.

Their presence is first apparent in acts of maintenance.

A fern is cut before its mineralization can spread into the root. A clogged channel beneath the silicate crust clears overnight. A vine that has attached itself to one of the pylons is found gently detached and laid along a more useful crack. The severed surfaces are always sealed with the same transparent substance, neither glass nor resin. It remains flexible in heat and hardens in rain. Under magnification, it contains minute moving bubbles that arrange themselves against the direction of water flow.

No tool is left behind. Nothing is trampled except the stone, and even there the marks are equivocal: two impressions appearing together, followed by two more, as though the maker walked on paired limbs—or as though each step were taken twice at slightly different moments.

The inhabitants seem to enter by the stair of five steps.

The stair does not descend anywhere in ordinary space. Its lowest tread meets unbroken black rock. Yet just before dusk, moisture sometimes gathers on that final vertical face in the shape of a tall, narrow opening. One may place a hand against it and feel neither cavity nor surface, only a pressure like deep water. After the pylons sound, the wet outline is gone. Later, paired tracks appear at the top of the stair.

From these tracks and from distortions under the roof, a body can be inferred.

It is taller than a person when fully extended, but it seldom stands so. Its weight is carried on two long lower structures, each ending not in a foot but in a divided pad that presses the ground twice: once where the body is, and once where it is about to be. This produces the paired impressions. The second mark is usually fainter. On evenings when the marsh shows the unsupported doorway, it is the deeper of the two.

Above the legs is a narrow trunk with several folding limbs. Their number cannot be fixed. Shadows suggest four; disturbances in rain suggest six or eight. The limbs branch near their ends into fine manipulators suited to separating moss, opening mineral plates, and touching the hair-thin edges of cracks. They do not cut the fern trunks by force. They place their fingers around the base and persuade the living tissue and mineral tissue to experience different temperatures. The plant separates itself.

The surface of the body resembles the pylons: dull, pale, almost ceramic, yielding slightly under pressure. It has no permanent joints. A knee, wrist, or elbow forms wherever bending becomes necessary, then smooths away afterward. Water does not bead on this skin. It circles it at a small distance, describing the body more clearly than sight can.

There is no recognizable head.

At the upper end, the trunk divides into a crown of thin plates, overlapping like closed fern fronds. When the inhabitant attends to something, these plates unfold around an absence. Reflections bend toward that absence and do not return. One sees, in place of a face, a small section of the following morning.

This is why birds do not appear in the marsh. The inhabitants are not erasing them. They are looking upward through the water, and their manner of looking occupies the reflection first.

They do not speak, though the pylons may speak for them. The three dusk notes alter their posture. At the first, they become perceptible to condensation. At the second, they gain enough weight to leave tracks. At the third, when the marsh holds tomorrow’s image, they may cross fully into the basin.

Fully is a relative term. Even then, different portions of an inhabitant arrive at different rates. A hand may be present before the arm that carries it. The pressure of a body may bend reeds while its visible outline remains beneath the stair. Occasionally one is revealed under the suspended roof, where duration cannot continue to draw it forward. There it appears as a succession of thin, nested figures, each standing inside the next: positions from several moments compressed into one pale anatomy.

The roof is not a shelter for them. It is where they molt.

What they shed looks at first like translucent cloth. Left alone, it contracts into a hard silver-veined shell resembling a mineralized fern. Many of the dead-looking fronds around the basin may not be plants at all. Some possess too many ribs. Some shift their angle between rains. Once, after a particularly dense condensation, every rigid frond in the grove was found facing the stair.

The inhabitants collect the living crowns of tree-ferns and carry them into the marsh. They do not appear to eat them. Instead, they hold each crown just above the still surface during the third pylon note. The fern’s reflection opens before the fern itself does. By morning the crown is gone, and somewhere in the basin a new plant has emerged from bare stone, already rooted into a water-bearing seam.

They cultivate possibilities rather than specimens.

A plant that might survive tomorrow’s heat is moved there before the heat arrives. A fracture likely to widen is healed in advance. Water is taught a path it will need later. The copper reeds serve as articulated gauges, turning toward cool absences in the day ahead. Violet moss records the pressure of rains not yet fallen. The gliders carry these measurements to the cloud line. The black beetles return droplets containing the basin’s immediate past.

To the inhabitants, these may be one organism’s organs rather than separate species. The garden itself may be their body spread across stone, weather, and intervals of time. The pylons are its inner ears. The marsh is an eye that cannot see the present. The buried structures are roots. The suspended roof is a pause held open like a mouth.

In that case, the pale figures are not gardeners. They are the garden’s temporary hands.

There are hints that they once possessed a more continuous form. Under certain mineral skins lie impressions of broad, many-ribbed bodies, curled around channels now used by groundwater. The fossils cannot be old; the rock containing them is young enough to retain heat. Some appear only after rain and fade as the stone dries. Others are not fossils of what died, but molds awaiting what has yet to arrive.

The inhabitants show no hostility to visitors. They may not distinguish a visitor from a new variable in the basin. A person who remains through several cycles begins to be incorporated into its quiet adjustments. Reeds turn toward the person’s shadow. Moss grows where their hands rest. The marsh retains their reflection after they step away. Small faults in the rock heal around the pattern of their weight.

After enough nights, the future image in the water begins to differ.

At first the observer is merely standing elsewhere. Then their reflected clothing is wet when their actual clothing is dry. Then the reflection turns its head a moment before they do. Eventually, on a night when the narrow doorway appears over the marsh, the reflected observer is no longer alone.

One of the pale figures stands behind them, its crown unfolded around a piece of morning sky. Several delicate limbs rest near, though not quite upon, the observer’s shoulders. It seems less to seize than to measure: heat, moisture, age, the direction in which the body is likely to change.

By dawn there may be no figure and no doorway.

There may only be five wet steps leading out of the marsh, and beside the observer’s footprints, a second set pressed into the stone.

From then on, rain begins to curve toward them.
