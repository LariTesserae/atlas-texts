The people of the saddle are not numerous enough to become a crowd. Even on market days they remain recognizable as individuals: the woman who mends jar handles with wire, the brothers who cut roof slate, the old keeper of the eastern openings, the child sent three times because he forgets the stopper. Faces recur so reliably that absence is noticed before arrival.

They tend to be compact in build, strong through the legs and back rather than broad in the shoulders. From childhood they learn to lift without wasting motion, to walk on slanted paving, and to carry a full jar without letting water climb the neck. Their hands are pale with limestone dust, the creases darkened by soil, soot, grease, and crushed herbs. Older people often have one shoulder lower from decades of carrying water or fuel. Many have roughened fingertips from resetting saw teeth and testing mortar. The mountain wind leaves cheeks and lips chapped, but their skin is otherwise spared the harder sun of lower, drier country.

They dress in layers of goat wool, felted cloth, and repaired linen brought up by traders. Outer garments are short enough not to catch on steps or drag through channels. Sleeves are close at the wrist. Most colors come from what is available: cream, brown, soot-black, the green-gray of steeped juniper. Stronger colors are saved for narrow things—a woven cuff, a child’s cap, cords around keys—so that brightness appears in movement rather than at a distance. Shoes are repeatedly resoled. A good cloak may belong first to a parent, then be cut down for two children, then become door padding, jar wrapping, and finally patches for the knees of work trousers.

They are economical, but not joyless. Waste irritates them more than poverty does. A person who breaks a cup is not mocked for clumsiness so much as questioned about where each piece went. Children are taught to bring back bent nails, clean bones, worn cord, fragments of fired clay, and even handfuls of mortar that can be crushed and mixed again. They make games of sorting such things. A child may know the difference between five kinds of useless-looking shard and be unable to explain why a stranger would throw any of them away.

Water governs manners. No one drinks directly from a public vessel. No one washes upstream of another household’s opening. A jar left uncovered is understood as carelessness bordering on insult. During dry weeks, people remember who drew more than their turn, but they also remember illness, childbirth, visiting kin, and the needs of animals. The rules are exact because exceptions must be exact as well.

The keepers of water are not priests or rulers. They are usually older residents with good hearing, patience, and little inclination toward favoritism. They know the cistern levels by the change in echo when a pebble is dropped, and the hidden stream by its smell at each opening. Their authority lasts only while others trust their measurements. A keeper who lies would not be dramatically punished; people would simply stop sending jars during that keeper’s hours, and the loss of usefulness would be a severe disgrace.

Much of the town is organized through similar kinds of temporary authority. The person who understands a failing retaining wall directs the work until it is sound. The best baker controls the communal oven while bread is inside it. A herder decides when goats must be moved off a patch of turf. In the council court, age receives attention, but competence receives obedience. Those who speak longest are not necessarily the ones whose judgment is followed. Decisions often end not with a declaration but with someone rising, naming the necessary tools, and asking who will come at dawn.

Households are larger and less tidy in their composition than the closed houses suggest. Widowed siblings live together. Children sleep with cousins during winter, when fewer rooms are heated. A person whose family has gone down the western road may remain attached to their quarter through an oven share, a cistern share, and the obligation to sweep an empty court. Rooms pass between kin, neighbors, and temporary occupants without the building itself being considered sold. Ownership lies partly in use and partly in remembered labor: who roofed the room, who cleared its drain, whose grandmother lined the storage pit.

This makes the town patient with departure but wary of severance. People leave to marry, trade, herd in lower country, serve in another settlement, or seek work where timber and metal are easier to obtain. Their names remain attached to obligations for years. A vacant house is not spoken of as abandoned while someone still cleans its channel. Those who return may find their former sleeping room filled with lentils or slate, yet a place is made for them without ceremony. Those who do not return become gradually grammatical: first spoken of in the present, then as absent, then as belonging to the time of a particular repair.

Children learn the town through chores long before they understand its plan. They are sent to clear thyme roots from a drain, count oven tiles, carry messages between courts, watch a tethered goat, or sit beside a jar while condensation runs into it. They know which walls hold the morning warmth and which lanes deepen the wind. Their games imitate adult labor—building miniature cisterns, trading marked potsherds, holding councils over imaginary boundary disputes—but they also invent forbidden floods by pouring a cup of water through dust. This is usually stopped at once. The adults’ anger is real, though some remember having done the same.

Adolescents test themselves against distance. They climb to the cairns, descend farther into the ravine than errands require, or remain outside the gates until the last useful light. They are curious about places where houses have upper stories, where rivers run openly, where a person can burn wood without first considering how long it took to grow. Some find the saddle stifling. Others leave and discover that abundance can feel disorderly: too much sound, too many unknown faces, water running away unused.

Courtship is quiet because privacy is scarce. Two people may volunteer repeatedly for the same repair, walk together to check grazing tethers, or exchange objects that appear purely practical. A newly sharpened needle, a well-fitting jar lid, or a pair of dry shoe liners can carry more intention than ornament. Marriages join water rights and labor as much as families. Feasts are modest in quantity but prolonged by storytelling, riddles, and the careful serving of foods not often eaten together: fresh cheese, onions softened in fat, flatbread hot from the oven, preserved fruit cut into enough pieces for everyone present.

Their humor is dry and highly local. They joke about walls that have outlived their builders’ opinions, about goats who recognize boundary stones better than councilors, about houses so cold that lentils refuse to soften in them. People are imitated by the sounds of their work: a particular double tap of a mason, the cough of an oven tender, the dragging broom of someone who rises too early. Laughter seldom carries across the whole square, but it moves through workshops and kitchens in short bursts.

The dead remain nearby on the burial terraces. Bodies are wrapped rather than enclosed in timber, and stones from an opened grave are used again when the ground has settled. Grave markers are plain, often bearing little more than a name mark and a household sign. The dead are remembered less through monuments than through repairs: a stair cut by one, a roof pitch corrected by another, a pear tree trained by someone whose grandchildren now prune it. To praise a dead person, residents say that something of theirs still holds.

Their shrines are equally practical in appearance. A shallow niche may contain a lamp, a bowl for the first drops of rain, a smooth stone carried from another mountain, or a bronze fragment too small to reuse and too old to discard. People ask for ordinary continuities: that frost arrive after the last greens are lifted, that a child’s cough loosen, that a wall reveal its weakness before winter. Their sacred stories concern bargains, measures, delays, and warnings noticed in time. Miracles are regarded with some suspicion. A blessing that cannot be maintained is not considered much of a blessing.

There are specialists, though few can afford to be only one thing. The potter also tends onions. The midwife repairs felt. The person who marks property tiles may herd goats in late summer. One resident knows how to set simple fractures; another can judge clay by chewing a pinch of it; another remembers every household’s claim on the oldest cistern, though no one knows how the claim began. Skill is distributed deliberately. An expert who keeps knowledge private is thought dangerous. Children are made to watch, then assist, then repeat the task while the older worker remains close enough to intervene.

The goats are residents in a stricter sense than most outsiders understand. Each has a disposition and a known appetite. Some are trusted near garden walls; others must never be left facing a juniper cutting. Their leather-wrapped bells make a muted knock as they pass through the lanes. In winter they are brought into low stone shelters adjoining houses, and their warmth seeps through shared walls. Their milk, hair, dung, hide, and eventual meat enter nearly every part of domestic life. They are scolded by name and mourned without sentimentality.

The small birds at the water courts are tolerated because they drink little. Children identify them by how they land: the quick pair, the solitary gray one, the brown flock that waits on the roof edge. Lizards under warm slabs are left undisturbed unless a stone must be moved, in which case someone usually nudges the animal toward another shelter. Ravens are watched more carefully. Their arrival may mean nothing, but two circling low above the eastern quarter will draw faces into doorways.

What most distinguishes the inhabitants is not austerity but attention. They notice the altered note of a roof slab, the damp smell of a blocked channel, the slight outward belly of a wall, the first bitterness in stored grain. They live among slow processes and have made themselves sensitive to beginnings. Disaster is rare partly because small failures are not permitted to remain private for long.

This attention extends to one another. A household whose smoke does not rise is visited. A regular footstep missing from the morning lane is remarked upon. Pride exists, feuds persist, and old injuries may be carried for decades, but complete neglect is difficult in a place where every absence changes the sound.

They do not think of themselves as enduring heroically. Heroism suggests a crisis with an end. Their virtues are smaller and more repetitive: returning the stopper, sweeping the court, wrapping the bell, turning the cracked slab, teaching the child where the water goes. They inhabit the town in the same way mortar inhabits a wall—not as its most visible part, nor as something unchanging, but as what fills the irregular spaces and keeps separate stones bearing weight together.
