The first inhabitants are films.

They lie over the warm stone in layers too thin to look alive: green where a trace of light reaches, rust-colored around mineral seepage, pearly white in the permanent dark. Bacteria feed on what the cracks exhale. Algae wait through dry intervals as dull stains, then soften and spread when condensation returns. Fungal threads pass beneath both, entering silt, roots, shed skins, droppings, drowned wings. Together they make a living surface over the cavern’s mineral body.

Almost everything larger depends on this skin.

The smallest grazers are clear mites, springtails, and soft-bodied larvae whose organs show through their sides as dark grains. They browse in the hours after a drip begins, tracing wet margins that may be gone by morning. Some seal themselves inside grains of mud when a patch dries. Others retreat down fungal threads, following them as though they were roads. A fresh fruiting body can become crowded within minutes: first gnats and pale beetles, then mites clinging to their legs, then flat, shield-shaped insects arriving to drink the sweet collapse.

Many creatures are made for clinging. Their bodies are low and broad, their feet hooked or fringed, their colors borrowed from limestone, wet bark, old leaves, and clay. They do not leap unless forced. To lose contact with a surface is to enter open space, and open space is where hunters notice motion.

The cave crickets are among the boldest wanderers. Long-legged and clay-colored, they rest folded into mineral grooves until dampness thickens the air. Then they unfold and cross the chamber in careful stages, testing the stone with antennae longer than their bodies. They feed on fungus, leaf pulp, eggs, and anything newly dead. During floods they climb the hanging roots in dense, silent processions. When the water withdraws, they descend again, carrying spores in the joints of their legs.

Mud-building insects govern the edges of the pools. They are blind, soft-jawed things, each scarcely larger than a seed, yet their colonies outlast the generations that built them. They gather wet grains from the pool margins, mix them with saliva and fungal fiber, and raise chambers around pockets of warm air. Their newest towers are brown and fragile; the oldest ring faintly when struck by a drip. Inside are nurseries, mold gardens, cool shafts, refuse chambers, and blind queens swollen until they can no longer turn around.

They do not defend borders. A tower is abandoned when its seep fails and occupied again when moisture returns. Colonies merge, divide, and unknowingly inherit one another’s architecture. What appears to be a single settlement may contain the labor of thousands of generations and half a dozen related species.

The frogs live wherever water can be watched.

They are small enough to sit inside a hoofprint-sized basin, with narrow limbs and adhesive toes. Their backs resemble bronze, ash, bruised leaves, or wet stone. Their eyes are large but dark, adapted to outlines rather than color. They make almost no audible call. Instead, a courting frog lifts itself high, turns toward another, and pulses the skin beneath its throat. The flash is a muted blue, visible only nearby. A reply may come from a crack, a fern fold, or the opposite lip of the pool.

Their tadpoles develop quickly after floods. Some graze in temporary basins and must grow legs before the water vanishes. Others remain for months in deep rock cups, feeding on bacterial film and unfertilized eggs left by their mothers. When a basin becomes crowded, the largest tadpoles eat the smallest. Waste is rare here; kinship does not make hunger less exact.

The frogs’ silence does not protect them from everything. Pale lizards patrol above the pools, moving along vertical stone as easily as ground. Their toes spread into soft, pleated pads that grip damp limestone without scraping. When still, they are difficult to separate from the hanging roots: narrow head lowered, body aligned with a crack, tail dangling loose. They take insects, young frogs, and occasionally one another. Their tongues are short. Rather than striking from a distance, they creep close enough to seize prey with the whole mouth.

Their eggs are laid inside rotten root wood, where warmth and humidity remain steady. The young hatch with disproportionately long tails and translucent bellies. For their first weeks they remain in the upper tangles, feeding on mites and gnat larvae. Only after their skin thickens do they descend to the stone curtains.

Below the visible pools live the animals that belong to the water’s hidden continuity. Blind shrimp, colorless except for the food inside them, graze on calcite and drowned leaf fragments. Finger-length eels thread through cracks no wider than their skulls. Their eyes persist as dark flecks under the skin, useless but not yet entirely gone. They find their way by touch, taste, and tiny changes in pressure.

No one knows the full extent of their passages. An eel may vanish from one basin and appear weeks later across the chamber, fatter and scarred. Floods sometimes cast them into shallow cups where they cannot escape. There they coil beneath moss, slowing their bodies until the next overflow reconnects the water. Some survive long enough for condensation alone to refill the route.

The largest pool-dweller is the dark, broad-headed amphibian beneath the floating mats. Adults are the length of a person’s arm but seem larger because they are rarely seen outside water. Their limbs are short, their skin loose and furrowed, their mouths disproportionately wide. Algae grows over their backs. Small shrimp shelter in the folds around their legs, and insects sometimes lay eggs upon them.

They are not active rulers of the pools. Most of their lives are spent as submerged weight.

They feel footsteps through flooded stone. They feel a beetle struggling on the surface, a frog entering water, an eel disturbing silt beneath a shelf. Hunger gathers slowly in them. The strike, when it comes, is less a chase than a hole opening in the water. Afterward they settle again, digesting for days or weeks.

They also eat their own young. The larvae therefore remain in the narrowest flooded cracks until their heads are too broad to fit. At that age they emerge during heavy rain and disperse through temporary streams, each seeking a pool without an adult. Most find none.

The damp banks belong to the burrowers. They are warm-blooded, long-bodied, and nearly naked, with sealed ears and tiny eyes hidden beneath pale skin. Their incisors protrude before their lips, allowing them to cut roots and packed mud without filling their mouths. Colonies excavate branching tunnels around fungus-rich wood. They feed on grubs, softened roots, buried fruiting bodies, and swollen knots of mycelium that they harvest without destroying.

A colony smells of clay, milk, and bitter musk. At its center are the young, kept together in a chamber lined with shredded fern. Adults sleep in heaps to conserve moisture rather than warmth. One or two fertile females produce most of the offspring, but hierarchy is muted; tunnels collapse too often for rigid order. When floodwater enters, the colony moves upward in practiced confusion, carrying the smallest young by the loose skin of their backs.

Their digging reshapes the cavern more than the activity of any larger animal. Drips enter abandoned tunnels. Pools drain through collapsed nests. Fern roots follow old galleries. A burrower colony may spend years constructing a dry refuge only to create, after one rain, a new wetland for creatures it will never eat.

The bats occupy the high, hot pockets. They are small, dark-faced, and thin-winged, with fur the color of smoke. Unlike bats of the open air, they seldom fly through the cavern’s center. They follow walls and roots closely, slipping between stone curtains with folded turns. Their calls are faint and sharply directed, suitable for crowded surfaces. A loud hunter would hear too many echoes here and understand none of them.

At dusk some leave through the fissure to feed above the plain. Others never do. The cave-bound bats take moths, midges, and crickets stirred by condensation. Their wings are broad enough for hovering, and their hind feet can pluck insects directly from leaves. Mothers carry newborns for several days, then leave them in warm ceiling nurseries where hundreds cling together beneath a skin of moving vapor.

Around the roosts live specialists found nowhere else: eyeless fleas that feed only when a bat returns hot from flight, beetles that consume fallen dung, and small white moths whose wingless larvae graze oils from the roosting stone. Nothing produced by the bats remains merely theirs.

Even the dead continue as inhabitants of another kind. A drowned frog clouds with bacteria, collapses under beetles, threads over with fungus, and is gradually opened into the water. A fallen bat becomes a temporary country: hide for mites, marrow for larvae, bone for mineral crust. Within a season its shape is difficult to recognize. In time, calcite may preserve the hollow where it lay more faithfully than any flesh.

No creature possesses the entire chamber. Each knows a network of small certainties: three dependable drips, one safe root, a hunting ledge, a tunnel above flood level, the smell of a particular fungus after rain. Their maps overlap without agreeing. To the cricket, the cavern is a chain of sheltered grooves. To the eel, it is pressure moving through stone. To the bat, it is a folded field of echoes. To the great amphibian, it is the long patience between ripples.

If there are inheritors of the upright slabs and the fused ring, they cannot be distinguished from the rest. No hidden people tend them. No keeper patrols the level passage. But animals repeatedly shelter between the three stones; generations of frogs have left blue courtship stains on their lower faces, and burrowers have packed the dry basin with husks and gnawed roots. Whatever intention may once have existed there has been occupied completely.

The inhabitants have no single ruler, no fixed season, and almost no place that stays useful forever. They live by following changes too slight for an outsider to notice: a cooler breath through one crack, the first softening of a fungus knot, a root beginning to drip, the mineral taste that precedes rain.

When the cavern seems to exhale and all its concealed life adjusts at once, it is not obedience. It is recognition. Every body there, from the film on the stone to the sleeper under the pool, has felt the same small alteration and answered in its own way.
