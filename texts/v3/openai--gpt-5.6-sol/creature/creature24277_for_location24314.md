At noon, almost everything living there pretends to be stone.

The great blade-crowned organisms draw themselves narrow against the sun. Their crowns become dark bundles of edges, and their pale trunks resemble fluted mineral columns. Grazers flatten their sails and settle into hollows. The branching mats withdraw their active tips into cracks. Even the largest travelers lock their joints and stand without movement, three-legged silhouettes that might be distant pinnacles.

Life reveals itself in the hours when the light comes sideways.

Then the colonies open.

The largest beings are not quite trees and not quite single organisms. A mature grove may have begun from one windborne shell, yet centuries of underground division have made it difficult to say where the founder ends. Beneath the surface, sheets of mineral fiber spread through the porous stone. They meet, fuse, separate, and exchange slow pulses of oil and charge. What appear aboveground as individual trunks are often only the exposed organs of a buried common body.

Each trunk is built rather than grown in the terrestrial sense. Roots loosen grains from the bedrock, sort them by chemistry, and lay them down in concentric meshes. Flexible amber resin binds the layers. New tissue remains pliant for years; old tissue gradually becomes ceramic. The result is light, resilient, and difficult to fracture. In a storm the trunks do not bend far. Instead, thousands of microscopic seams slide over one another, allowing the whole structure to lean a few degrees and then settle back.

Their crowns are instruments for managing light, gas, dust, and charge. Every blade can twist independently, but neighboring blades coordinate through pressure changes in the resin channels. That coordination continues from crown to crown through the buried network. A grove does not think quickly, if it thinks at all, but it notices broadly. A grazer biting one edge may cause lower fronds to close hundreds of paces away. The shadow of a dust front can make an entire colony turn before the first particles arrive.

At dawn, the crowns spread low and wide to gather the weak oblique light. Their silver undersides face the sky, warming first and drawing the waxy night frost into fine grooves. Tiny contractions carry the deposit toward the blade bases, where it disappears into dark intake pores. As the sun climbs, the leaves rotate until only their edges remain exposed. Late in the day they open again, black-green above and silver below, flashing in coordinated waves.

The oldest groves develop internal regions with distinct functions. Along the windward boundary stand short, dense forms with comb-like crowns that trap dust. Behind them rise the broad light-gatherers. Near the center, protected by generations of dead outer trunks, grow the reproductive towers. These produce resinous cases shaped like hollow beads, each containing a folded filament and a pinch of catalytic salts.

When pressure conditions are right, the cases are released by the thousand. Most never leave the grove. Some lodge in neighboring crowns and are reclaimed. A few roll onto the open plateau and are taken by the wind. Their shells are tuned by size: small seeds rise into high currents, while larger ones skip close to the ground. When they strike stone they ring. The note changes as the shell wears thinner, so an old traveling seed has a lower, softer voice than a new one.

A seed does not germinate merely because it has stopped. It tests its resting place through a thread finer than hair. It may remain closed for decades, tasting daily changes in charge, temperature, and airborne chemistry. Only in a suitable crack—one that gathers dust, shades the afternoon sun, and exposes the correct mineral vein—does it anchor. The first visible growth is not green but translucent: a tiny fan pressed flat against the stone, almost impossible to distinguish from a flake of mica.

Around the grove-builders lives a dense lesser world.

Glassy cushions occupy the exposed margins. Their hairs are hollow tubes that sort dust grains by electrostatic charge. In favorable seasons they become rimmed with yellow and orange particles, giving them the appearance of small mineral suns. Copper rosettes grow where the ground repeatedly warms and cools; they wind and unwind through the day, using expansion itself to pump gases through their tissues. Pale crack-dwellers advance beneath overhangs by dissolving amber compounds ahead of themselves and depositing colorless skeletons behind. Some colonies are older than the cliffs that shelter them, though only their newest margins remain active.

There are also organisms too thin to see as bodies. They live as films between mineral grains, feeding on voltage differences created by sunlight and darkness. In daylight they spread through the upper stone. At night they retreat several finger-widths deeper. Their daily migration darkens the ground around established groves, producing halos once mistaken for shadow.

The hand-sized six-legged grazers are the plateau’s most restless inhabitants, though even they spend much of their lives motionless. Their six limbs are arranged in two close-set tripods. One set braces while the other moves, giving them a precise, rocking gait. The sail along the back is not primarily for display. It is a thermal regulator, a pressure sensor, and a reserve of layered tissue that can be consumed during long barren crossings.

Their mouths are delicate scraping fans. They remove weathered skins from old blades, leaving behind clear windows that the plant soon seals. A careful grazer can feed on the same crown for years without seriously harming it. Groves tolerate them because their feet carry reproductive dust into shaded crevices, and because they eat the films that would otherwise clog breathing pores.

At twilight the grazers climb high into the crowns and raise their sails. Each sail bears patterns visible only at grazing angles of light: bars, broken circles, branching dark lines. They signal by tilting these surfaces rather than by making sound. Across a grove, one may see brief answers—silver, black, silver—passing among the leaves.

The tall migratory animals are rarer and stranger. They stand on three pillar limbs surrounding a low central body. Above it rises a flexible mast carrying branching sensory vanes. These vanes continually sample pressure, electric fields, dust chemistry, and vibrations in the ground. Their sight, if it can be called sight, is poor. A traveler may pass close to a motionless object without noticing it, yet respond to a grove or storm beyond the horizon.

They walk by tipping. One pillar releases, the body inclines, and the limb plants far ahead while the other two hold. The gait appears precarious but is extremely efficient. At full pace—which they sustain only near dawn and dusk—they move with the solemn regularity of a turning mechanism.

Their outer surfaces are layered with pale plates scarred by centuries of dust. Between the plates, dark resinous skin remains flexible. Some individuals carry whole communities on their sheltered undersides: colorless mats, seed cases, and tiny grazers that never descend to the ground. Thus a migrant arriving at an isolated grove brings not only itself but fragments of another colony’s ecology.

They feed on thick seed cases and old resin knots. After feeding, they may stop for months. The three pillars spread, the sensory crown folds down, and ceramic secretions seal every joint. Dust gathers on the dormant body until it resembles a weathered outcrop. When the buried root networks beneath a grove begin their seasonal electrical activity, the traveler senses the change through its feet, breaks its seals, and resumes walking.

No one knows, in any ordinary sense, where these animals are going. Their routes link distant colonies, but not always the nearest ones. A traveler may cross a thousand kilometers of bare tableland while ignoring a grove visible to its sensory crown. The paths appear to be inherited as bodily rhythms: sequences of direction, vibration, and stellar orientation laid down before the young separate from the parent.

Their young begin as flattened three-lobed forms attached beneath an adult. For years they are carried like an additional plate. Eventually they drop within a grove, extend three threadlike legs, and spend their first decades walking only beneath the crowns. The branching vanes do not develop until they make their first open crossing.

The predators are so inactive that they are more often inferred than seen.

A mature one settles among aerodynamic rocks and grows a casing that matches their color and grain. Only its intake seams remain exposed. It can wait through many migrations, supported by light-reactive tissues in its skin and by chemical films cultivated inside its body. When prey approaches, it does not leap or chase. It changes the local electric field.

Grazers become confused first. Their signaling sails turn at the wrong moments. A tall traveler’s sensory vanes begin leaning toward the waiting shape, interpreting the field as the signature of a grove. The predator may spend days drawing an animal a few steps closer. Its final movement is small: a ring of stone-colored plates opens, resin filaments fasten to a limb, and a slow immobilizing compound enters through the joints.

Afterward, the predator feeds for months. It takes only the soft inner tissues and leaves the mineral framework standing. Such emptied travelers can remain upright for centuries, mistaken for living migrants at rest.

Yet predators leave marks on more than their prey. Root networks detect their fields. Lower fronds close. Seed release ceases nearby. Grazers skirt the area, and tall travelers alter routes that their ancestors followed for generations. Around an old predator there forms an expanding emptiness: not a territory defended by motion, but a territory made from the accumulated caution of everything else.

Most life on the plateau participates in this same economy of patience. It gathers what arrives—light, dust, charge, gas—and refuses to spend energy without reason. Growth happens by fractions. Decisions propagate over hours. Wounds are entombed rather than healed. Death itself is gradual: active tissues retreat inward while outer layers join the mineral landscape.

At night, when fluorescent mats illuminate the groves from below, their inhabitants become briefly legible. Grazers move as tiny black cutouts over glowing cushions. Tall migrants stand beyond the last crowns, vanes spread toward the stars. Seeds pass through the darkness, announcing their collisions with solitary notes.

Then the deep contraction moves through the plateau.

The groves fold. Grazers cling flat. Migrants plant all three pillars and lower their crowns. Even the waiting predators tighten inside their false stones.

For a moment every living thing responds to the same buried vibration, and the scattered colonies—separated by days of white silence—seem like parts of one immense organism listening through the continent’s bones.
