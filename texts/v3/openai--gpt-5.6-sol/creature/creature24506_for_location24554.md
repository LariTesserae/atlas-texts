At first the inhabitants seem to be extensions of the apparatus.

They move through the streets in mineral colors: ash, iron, salt, kiln-red, the blue-black of quenched glass. Their outer garments are loose where they must shade the body and close-fitting where a fold might catch in machinery. Most are made from woven ceramic fiber, fine metal mesh, or thin laminated cloth that crackles softly at the joints. Hoods shelter the scalp and neck. Veils are common, not for modesty but to preserve moisture in the breath. A person who has been walking leaves no sweat on the road; anything the body can spare is wicked inward, condensed, and collected in narrow flasks sewn beneath the ribs.

From a distance, a maintenance crew crossing a basalt shelf resembles a procession of upright tools.

Closer, the differences become apparent. Faces are lean from altitude and heat, often marked by ruptured capillaries around the nose and eyes. The whites of the eyes tend toward yellow or gray under the constant mineral glare. Hair is kept short, braided flat, or sealed beneath resin caps. Older workers have pale seams across their hands where burns were closed with ceramic dressings. Nearly everyone bears pressure scars: small dark crescents behind the ears, beneath the jaw, or along the sternum where emergency respirators have clamped shut.

They are not silent people, but they are economical with sound. In a city where a footfall can travel through three floors and a spoken word may enter a ventilation shaft, privacy begins with volume. Conversation is low and close. Public arguments are conducted almost gently. Warnings, by contrast, are loud and standardized. A stranger shouting in a street will be ignored; a stranger striking metal three times will stop everyone within hearing.

Much is said with the hands. Two fingers laid across the opposite wrist mean that a pipe is pressurized. A palm turned downward and rocked slowly means the floor is moving. Fingers opened beside the mouth indicate thirst, while the same sign made at the throat indicates bad air. Children learn these gestures before they learn the old directional names of the streets.

Their speech contains more tenses for gradual change than for completed action. A foundation is not *stable* but *holding through the present survey*. A marriage may be described as *still aligning*. The dead are said to have *ceased correcting*. Promises are often tied to measurable events: until the west cistern requires relining; until the Datum crosses its next ring; until a particular crack reaches the third marker. Such promises may outlast those who made them.

No occupation is wholly separate from geology. Cooks understand pressure valves. Teachers can read a wall gauge. Children know never to step across a fresh white line on stone, never to touch a handrail whose ceramic sleeve has darkened, and never to drink water that has appeared outside a marked channel, however clear it looks. Even the wealthy can identify the smell of overheated insulation.

The city’s most respected inhabitants are not its rulers but its correctors.

Correctors survey slow error: doors drifting out of square, pipes beginning to bear weight they were not designed to carry, a sleeping chamber becoming imperceptibly lower than its drain. They travel with plumb cases, tuning hammers, vials of mercury, and lengths of chain whose links are individually numbered. Many are old. Their value lies not in quickness but in memory—the feel of a district before its latest tilt, the former note of a reservoir wall when struck, the location of conduits omitted from every surviving plan.

A senior corrector may spend an entire morning seated beside a column, one hand resting on the floor. Passersby leave them undisturbed. By noon they may mark the stone with a single line and order six households moved.

Below them in prestige, though far more numerous, are the keepers of water. They tend the condenser interiors, walking narrow spirals where the air is cold enough to hurt the teeth. Their work clothes are glossy with mineral deposits. They scrape the ribs, clear the channels, test for poisons, and record each tower’s yield drop by drop. They carry shallow black bowls because the first sign of a failing surface is often not visible water but a change in the reflection above it.

Water keepers are reputed to be secretive. This is partly because they control access to places where voices cannot easily be overheard beneath the steady patter of condensation. Lovers meet in disused collection chambers. Fugitives have hidden behind the ribbed walls. Political agreements are made on inspection stairs while droplets strike the hoods of the negotiators.

They are also the people most often exposed to the translucent films.

Officially, the films are fouling. They reduce efficiency, trap contaminants, and must be removed. Yet some keepers do not scrape every surface clean. In inaccessible recesses they leave thin patches intact and return to watch how the threads shift. A few claim the films anticipate tower output, thickening hours before moist air reaches the upper streets. Others say they recoil from certain metals or arrange themselves into repeating forms when spoken to at close range.

There are keepers whose fingertips shine faintly after years in the shafts. The shine cannot be washed away. It lies beneath the skin in branching lines, visible only in darkness or against furnace light. Physicians call it mineral embedding. Among the keepers it is called being answered.

The city’s food is made rather than grown. Deep kitchens receive compressed nutrient blocks from the lowlands, fungal pastes cultivated in sealed vats, animal proteins assembled in warm chemical chambers, and salts recovered from bodily waste. Meals are soft, dense, and heavily seasoned with acids because altitude dulls taste. Crispness is a luxury. Fresh coldness is rarer still.

The rich do not display wealth by eating more. They display it by permitting evaporation.

At a formal table, a host may set out an uncovered cup. It will be narrow-mouthed, guarded from accidental movement, and filled only to the first engraved line. Guests do not drink immediately. They watch the surface diminish. To waste a measurable fraction of water merely to scent a room with damp mineral air is an old, almost offensive sign of abundance.

Children dare one another to breathe across such cups.

Children are raised communally during the hottest hours, when residential rooms are sealed and most adults are at work. Nurseries occupy thick-walled chambers near the cold return pipes. The youngest sleep in slung cradles that remain level as floors shift beneath them. Their first toys are linked rings, weighted cords, ceramic channels, and little roof systems down which a spoonful of water can be poured and recovered. They build miniature cities and are praised not for height or beauty but for how little liquid escapes.

They also play at catastrophe.

One child is the fracture. One is the alarm. The others must cross a room without touching the floor, seal an imaginary door, count everyone present, and decide which possessions to abandon. The game ends when the fracture catches them or when all the “water” has been saved. Adults rarely interrupt, even when the children become solemn.

Adolescents are sent beyond the dense districts in survey groups. They sleep in pressure tents beside rail lines and embryonic ridges, learning the names of hot seams and the ways a marker can lie. There, away from the city’s enclosing mechanisms, some experience terror at the naked horizon. Others become addicted to it. These are the ones who may join the outriders: tower technicians, fracture listeners, rail inspectors, and map-bearers who spend months among the black shelves.

Outriders develop a peculiar gait—long, flat steps that test the ground without seeming to hesitate. They carry mirrored poles to signal across distances where radios fail in mineral interference. Many wear bells inside their clothing, not to be heard by others but to reassure themselves that the air is still moving. When an outrider returns, people first look at the boots. Glass beads fused to the soles mean they have crossed ground that softened after sunset.

There are rulers, though they avoid that name. Civic power belongs to several overlapping bodies: the Allocation Tables, which assign water and heat; the Foundation Courts, which decide disputes caused by moving boundaries; the Continuity Houses, which preserve plans longer than any individual life; and the custodians of the Datum.

The Datum’s custodians wear uncolored white so that any mercury stain or oxide dust can be seen immediately. They enter the suspended chamber without jewelry, loose fasteners, or magnetic tools. Their ceremonial duty is simple: observe the needle and write what it has done. For this reason they are accused of possessing immense authority while claiming none. If they announce that the city has shifted, streets are renamed, contracts reinterpreted, taxes redrawn, and old accusations may suddenly refer to places that no longer legally exist.

On the day a ring is crossed, no bells sound. The new maps are carried out under cloth. People gather at public tables and wait to learn where they now live.

Some discover that a family boundary has moved through a kitchen. Some find that an ancestral chamber is now part of a pressure easement. There are arguments and appeals, but also celebrations. A person whose address changes without moving is permitted to take a new civic name.

Names in the High Condenser are rarely singular. One name is given at birth, another upon choosing work, another after surviving a major structural failure. A person may also possess a directional name that changes as their district tilts. Outsiders find the system contradictory. Inhabitants do not. To them, a name that never changes would be either a lie or a death inscription.

The dead are not buried. There is no soil to receive them, and excavating stable rock for graves is considered an extravagant injury.

Bodies are dried in low-pressure chambers. Water recovered from them is measured, purified, and returned to the household’s account. What remains may be fired into a dark ceramic aggregate used only in non-load-bearing walls. Thus the dead remain present but are never asked to support the living. A lintel, bench, acoustic baffle, or sealed alcove may contain generations of a family. Small makers’ marks identify the mixture.

To lean against such a wall during a difficult decision is ordinary. To drill into one without permission is among the city’s deepest offenses.

Not everyone accepts this continuity. Certain sects send their dead out onto the newest stone, arguing that what came from the city should be given to the mountain before it hardens. The bodies carbonize, fragment, or vanish into fissures. Officials condemn the practice because it wastes recoverable water. The sects answer that water is only being returned by a route too long for civic accounting.

Faith in the city is generally directed not toward creation but toward incompletion. Temples, where they exist, are deliberately unfinished. Their floors slope in anticipation of future uplift. Their central icons are blank molds, open rings, unstruck bells, or blocks of stone scored with lines showing what they may eventually become. Worship consists of adjustment: tightening a cable, moving a weight, adding a thin layer of mortar to correct for change.

One tradition teaches that the mountain is building a body and the city is merely an early organ in it—a gland whose purpose is to gather water from the air. Another claims the mountain is not alive but is slowly producing the conditions under which life can begin, and that the inhabitants are temporary scaffolding. A third holds that life is any process capable of correction, in which case the city, the films, the pressure engines, and perhaps the mountain itself belong to the same broad species.

This last idea is popular among machine tenders.

The oldest engines cannot be understood as single constructions. Their components have been replaced so many times that no original part remains, yet each retains habits. One piston pauses longer in cold weather. One flywheel develops a shudder before distant fractures. A pressure loom beneath the eastern halls has refused three mathematically superior control systems, jamming until its earlier arrangement was restored.

Tenders speak to these machines while working. They feed slivers of foil into diagnostic slots, rest foreheads against casings, and give the engines personal pronouns in private. Every few years, an inquiry attempts to determine whether such customs are superstition. Every inquiry ends when its instruments prove less reliable than the tenders’ judgment.

The small six-legged crawlers live among all of this almost unnoticed.

They gather behind warm pipe collars and beneath the lips of reservoir vents. Their translucent shells pulse with slow internal currents. Children call them little gauges because they line up before pressure changes, all facing the same direction. Killing one is not illegal, but it is considered foolish. A household that finds crawlers abandoning a wall will often sleep elsewhere that night.

In the deepest water chambers they grow larger. Reservoir workers describe specimens the length of a hand, colorless and heavy-bodied, stepping along vertical surfaces beneath the waterline. These reports are absent from official records. So are accounts of discarded tools being moved into careful parallel rows, or of sealed pipes opening from the inside.

Above the roofs, the long-winged black shapes continue to pass.

Most inhabitants look up when they cross the sun. Some shade their eyes. Some count. Their arrivals coincide with no season, and their silhouettes are never quite alike. They do not flap often enough. Occasionally one folds in the middle and becomes two.

The surveyors who insist they are carbon sheets can explain their lift, their flexibility, and the way thermal columns carry them upward from the industrial valleys. They cannot explain why the shapes sometimes circle a newly opened condensation tower before water begins to form.

No one has seen one land. Yet on rare mornings, high on the outer towers, keepers find narrow black strips caught around the navigation lamps. The strips are lighter than ash and warm even before sunrise. If placed on a table, they slowly turn one edge toward the nearest uncovered water.

These are supposed to be burned.

Some are not.

So the inhabitants of the High Condenser cannot be cleanly separated into citizen, animal, machine, deposit, and rumor. All of them survive by attending to gradients too slow or too faint for a less exacting place: a fractional lean, a hidden warmth, a pressure felt in the joints, a darkening on cold metal, a line of pale crawlers departing before the wall gives way.

They do not conquer the mountain. Conquest would require the mountain to be finished.

Instead they correct, collect, brace, listen, and rename. They inherit mechanisms whose purposes have blurred. They leave portions of themselves in walls. They scrape possible life from the towers and secretly preserve it in the same motion. Their city changes beneath them so slowly that patience has become not a virtue but a sensory organ.

And over generations, they too have come to resemble something unfinished: not a people merely dwelling on new stone, but a population still being extruded from it, cooling at the surface, carrying within them the heat and pressure of forms not yet decided.
