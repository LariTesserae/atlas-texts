At first, what lives in Condensation Room 6 appears to be a single pale substance laid over the machinery.

It has accumulated in the elbows of pipes and in the shelter of gauges. It upholsters motor housings, fills the undersides of stairs that were never installed, and bulges softly from cable troughs. Only after prolonged looking does the substance divide into bodies. A hooked foot loosens and closes again without changing its grip. A plated head is distinguishable from the black recess behind it. Thousands of translucent abdomens brighten simultaneously when an amber lamp passes through the frost.

The inhabitants are finger-long, six-limbed, and roughly insectile, but this may be only because the room provides no better category. Their white bodies have the soft density of larvae and the precise articulation of manufactured parts. Each limb ends in paired hooks, one fine enough to catch the grain of ceramic, the other broad enough to lock around pipe insulation. Their heads are covered by overlapping black plates. The plates fit too neatly to be shells and too intimately to be helmets. Between them lie narrow reflective eyes, apparently without pupils.

Their folded membranes resemble wings until seen closely. Veins of dark metal run through them in regular branching patterns, including tiny junctions like solder points. Condensation gathers at these junctions. When the membranes vibrate, each droplet flattens, trembles, and remains where it is. The wings do not open. They do not beat through an arc. They persist at the threshold of beating, and from that threshold comes the chamber’s unbearable note.

No one creature seems loud. The roar belongs to their agreement.

They arrange themselves according to rules older than any living custodian and perhaps younger than the unfinished wall. They do not cross yellow floor markings. They leave warning labels exposed, though some can have no possible meaning to them. They preserve the reach of emergency handles and inspection plates. Around the useless drain they maintain a flawless circle. Where a painted boundary has chipped away, their line follows the absent portion as accurately as the visible one.

This suggests that they can read. It may instead mean that they are themselves part of the labeling system.

Among them are several apparent ages. The smallest are nearly transparent and lie fitted beneath the adults, aligned head to abdomen as though packed for transport. Some have no dark head plates yet, only smoky shapes suspended under the skin. Larger juveniles inhabit warm joints in the pipework, where their abdomens pulse with the amber of the lamps. Mature animals occupy exposed surfaces and face the gray wall.

There are eggs, if eggs they are: clear capsules fastened in strings beneath the coldest pipes. Each contains a folded white figure and a single black point. A split runs along some capsules, perpetually widening. Through the opening one can see a hooked forelimb in the act of emerging. The young have hatched. The young are hatching. The egg remains full.

Shed skins are tucked into unused spaces with methodical care. They line gaps in insulation, wrap loose bolts, seal hairline openings around cable glands. Their empty legs interlock. Their empty wings overlap like foil. Nothing is discarded if it can become part of the room.

The dead are below.

They lie on the lower grille among black water and motionless valve stems, immaculate except for the collapse of their abdominal sacs. Their limbs are drawn inward. Their metal-veined membranes remain taut. No mold takes them; no living creature consumes them. Yet they do not seem abandoned. Through holes in the upper floor, adults extend one hooked limb downward to touch the dead, making long, vertical chains through the grilles. Whether they are retrieving them, lowering them, mourning them, or merely maintaining contact cannot be determined, because the gesture never completes.

The inhabitants drink. Each places its mouth against a bead of condensation and unfolds a cluster of hair-thin instruments. The instruments penetrate the droplet without breaking its surface. A dark thread enters the animal’s body; a pale thread seems to pass back into the pipe. Drinking and excretion may be the same act here. The droplet stays full. The animal stays thirsty.

What sustains them is therefore uncertain. They may feed on the first instant of condensation rather than on water itself. They may draw nourishment from pressure without relieving it, from heat without cooling anything, from labor that never yields a finished movement. Their bodies collect the room’s stalled processes the way ordinary animals collect carbon.

The square of blue lichen around the lamp may be their pasture, but none is seen grazing. The three black reeds in the gutter may be their crop, though no creature approaches them. Sometimes a transparent water-capsule at a reed’s tip contains a dark speck shaped like one of their head plates. Sometimes the speck looks more like an eye. The colorless filaments in the resin crack bend toward the swarm, or the swarm’s vibration bends the resin around them. There is too little change to distinguish cultivation from spontaneous growth.

Nor is it clear that the millions are separate animals.

Their hooked feet touch through films of water. Their wing-veins vibrate at exactly one frequency. Beneath the stacked bodies, pale tubes pass from abdomen to abdomen and vanish into the unfinished machinery. A disturbance at one edge of the colony appears simultaneously in every eye, not as movement but as a minute alteration in reflected light. If there is an intelligence in the room, it does not travel between them. It is already present everywhere they are.

The colony may be one creature distributed across millions of durable organs.

Or the room itself may be the creature, and the white bodies its temporary sense organs.

The compressors supply lungs that never inhale. The reservoirs are stomachs that never fill. The pipework is a vascular system carrying transformations rather than fluids. The fixed gauges are unblinking pupils. The red indicator lamps in the recesses resemble a second set of eyes because that may be what they have become. In this interpretation, the swarm does not live inside Condensation Room 6. It is the room’s awareness of its own unfinished condition.

Yet all of them face the seamless gray panel.

They do not worship it in any recognizable manner. There are no offerings, unless the unbroken note is an offering. They do not guard it, since they never turn toward the hatch. They simply orient themselves toward it with a precision more absolute than attention. Even the newly hatching point their plated heads in its direction. Even the dead below the grille lie with their bodies aligned to it.

The shallow lines in the panel answer their arrangement. One resembles a river basin, another a rib cage, another a wiring diagram. Seen from close by, the grooves appear to be made by droplets beginning to run. Seen from the hatch, they suggest the outline of an enormous folded animal whose limbs would extend beyond the room. From one side, there is the impression of a face: not a finished face, but the earliest decision that a surface might one day contain one.

Perhaps the wall is their queen.

Perhaps it is their blueprint.

Perhaps the white multitude is not attending a birth but applying itself to construction. Their continuous pitch may hold the panel soft. Their bodies may be braces positioned around a form too large to emerge. Each animal may contribute one infinitesimal instruction to the lines: deepen here, divide there, become chamber, become watershed, become mouth.

But in Condensation Room 6, instruction never reaches execution. The queen remains about to be shaped. The landscape remains about to erode. The face remains about to recognize its witnesses.

And the inhabitants remain what they are at the first instant of becoming alive: hatchlings and corpses, workers and material, a swarm and a solitary mind. They drink without consuming, labor without motion, breed without multiplying, and sing without reaching the next fraction of their song.

They are not trapped in the room’s permanent moment.

They are adapted to it so completely that time, if it entered, might be the thing that killed them.
