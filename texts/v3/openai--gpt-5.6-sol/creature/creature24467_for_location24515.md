The first mistake is to look for inhabitants at eye level.

Almost nothing native to the plain habitually raises a head. Height means exposure to conflicting winds, to airborne rivers, to lightning that has lost its direction. A body standing upright would be audible for leagues through the pressure-sensitive mud. It would cast too many reflections and belong to only one horizon at a time.

The inhabitants keep low.

At first they resemble patches of exposed bedrock moving beneath brown water. Their backs are broad, irregular, and divided into overlapping plates, each plate imitating the mineral beneath it: red where there is silt, glittering where quartz powders the pools, glossy black in the clay beds. The imitation is not coloration alone. Given time, their skins acquire the hardness, temperature, and even the fossil impressions of the ground they cross. Small organisms settle on them. Mineral films grow over them. Rain wears channels into them.

A resting one may become so exact a piece of the landscape that the landscape accepts it. Water finds a course across its back. Reeds root into its outer layers. Pebbles work into soft seams and are enclosed. After a few decades there is no reliable test by sight or touch to determine where animal ends and stone begins.

They are called many things by those who cross the plain: the Spreaders, the Underfoot, the Patient Folk. None of these are their name. It is uncertain whether they possess names in any portable sense.

Their bodies have no permanent outline. In motion, one may be the size of a sleeping person, with five or seven short limbs spreading from a thick central pad. Crossing soft mud, it broadens until it is scarcely deeper than a hand. In deep water it loosens further, opening translucent membranes between its limbs and drifting as a single enormous fin. When forced over dry stone, it contracts into a many-jointed knot and rolls with painful slowness.

The largest cannot be measured. They have been observed as compressed trails several kilometers wide, advancing through pale mineral growths without any visible front or rear. These may be ancient individuals, colonies traveling in contact, or whole populations joined for migration. Pieces separate from them and later return. Sometimes they return to another.

This does not appear to trouble them.

They breathe through every wet surface. Their undersides are crowded with fine folds that sort the two waters beneath the plain: salt drawn one way, fresh water another. By tightening these folds, they can create small differences in pressure. A lone animal can raise a blister in a puddle or make mud tremble around its body. A gathering can divert an underground seep, arrest a moving channel, or persuade a stone plate to drift sideways.

Most of what passes for their technology is done in this manner.

A wall is begun not by carrying stones but by lying for generations along a buried seam, changing how brine passes through it. Salt crystals grow, split the bedrock, and push a row of slabs upward. The inhabitants then weight one side until every opening faces the desired quarter of the sky. A shelter is made by keeping one patch of clay dry, sometimes for centuries, while rain and flood round the surrounding slabs into an enclosure.

The polished depressions inside these shelters are where they gather themselves.

Although their bodies can spread across a wide area, there is within each a denser organ, warm and dark, about the size of two joined hands. It moves slowly through the rest of the flesh, carrying memory in layered mineral grains. During sleep, illness, or violent rearrangements of the land, the animal draws this organ into the dry depression and folds its softer tissues over it. The shelter does not protect the entire body. It protects the part that must wake in the same order.

Travelers sometimes find a shelter newly vacated. The depression is warm. Condensation beads around it in concentric rings. If a hand is placed there, one may feel a pulse moving away through the stone long after the occupant has gone.

They do not see light as surface creatures do. Their upper plates possess scattered translucent pits, but these respond more strongly to pressure than brightness. A hidden lightning stroke, an underground collapse, and the approach of a great grazer are all perceived as shapes passing across the body. Their world consists of weight, vibration, salinity, and delay.

The black reeds are important to them. The reeds’ continental roots serve as both warning system and instrument. An inhabitant settling against a root can feel weather developing far beyond any visible horizon. By pressing and releasing the surrounding clay, it can send patterned tremors into the network. Hollow groves answer with their low notes.

Thus the plain is never wordless to them.

Their communications are slow only when heard from outside. A single exchange may begin as pressure beneath a marsh, continue as an inclination of reeds two hundred leagues away, pass into the organ tone of a hollow grove, and return days later as a sequence of rising pools. Yet several such exchanges can occupy the same ground simultaneously. To the inhabitants, conversation is not a line of sounds but a region entered together.

They speak of things by making temporary likenesses of their effects.

To indicate an approaching storm, they reproduce its pressure geometry in miniature beneath a pool. To describe a distant place, they alter the balance of salt and fresh water until the mud behaves as mud behaves there. An individual’s identity is rendered as a distinctive pattern of load: not a name, but the remembered manner in which the land yields beneath that individual.

This is why their identities survive division. Each fragment carries some version of the pressure pattern and tries to complete it when it meets another body. Two fragments of the same inhabitant, separated for a century, may recognize each other and join. Or each may have changed enough that they negotiate as strangers. Conversely, unrelated individuals may become so practiced at bearing one another’s pattern that the plain begins to treat them as one creature.

Kinship is a matter of shared weight.

They feed on the pale mineral skins that form over weeping rocks, but grazing is too simple a word. Their undersides dissolve the skins, sift out metals and spores, and lay down new films behind them. They carry microscopic gardens in their respiratory folds. Some of these gardens are inherited; others are collected from particular basins and tended for generations.

The immense soft-bodied grazers of the brackish margins may be a later stage of the same life. No one has watched a transformation from beginning to end, but the evidence is persistent. Before the appearance of a grazer, many smaller inhabitants converge and vanish into one another. Reeds over hundreds of kilometers bend toward the meeting place. The water grows warm and loses all surface reflection.

What emerges is nearly featureless: a low, pale mass moving with enough weight to compress stone films into paths that outlast eras. It travels perhaps once around the plain, eating very little. Along its route it leaves portions of itself in warm pools and dry shelters. Then it enters a brine exhalation or a silence corridor and is not seen again.

Afterward, small inhabitants appear in distant basins.

The white fliers may also belong to them, though this is more difficult to accept. They emerge from temporary blisters on an inhabitant’s back after certain rains. Each has a thin body surrounded by a ring of soft vanes, without mouth or visible eyes. They rise, circle once—as though taking the pressure-pattern of the place—and enter the clouds.

The inhabitants become restless when this happens. For weeks they arrange themselves around pools and listen upward.

Years later, warm rain may deposit unfamiliar mineral grains that pulse when immersed in brackish water. The inhabitants gather these grains carefully. Some are incorporated into their memory organs. Others are placed beneath upright bone-white arcs.

The arcs are not bones, though they grow as bones do. They are communal organs encouraged from colonies of mineral-feeding life. An arc begins around a buried grain or a piece of shed memory tissue. Generation after generation adds to it, shaping the current around its base. In time the arc becomes a ring.

Within such rings, the ordinary agreement between above and below is weakened. Rain rises. Roots reverse themselves. Thunder answers from beneath the land.

The inhabitants use these places for events that have no easy equivalent elsewhere. They exchange memories without exchanging flesh. They restore pressure-patterns belonging to the dead. They expose unborn fragments to storms that have not yet arrived. Sometimes an entire gathering disappears when the water inside a ring falls upward. The ring remains occupied by their impressions, each one pressed into the mud at a different apparent age.

They understand that events on the plain do not always occur in sequence. They do not seem to consider this miraculous. A fossil appearing before its animal is approached as one might approach an egg. It is guarded, fed mineral solutions, and moved away from silence. When at last the creature arrives whose remains it will become, the inhabitants often recognize it.

Their care can be unsettling. They may begin mourning an individual long before it shows any sign of death. They may prepare a dry shelter for someone not yet divided from its parent. At times they abandon a companion because its pressure-pattern has ceased to reach them from the future.

Death, to them, is not the stopping of a body. Bodies stop constantly, are buried, uncovered, divided, mineralized, and resumed. Death is the loss of recurrence. An inhabitant is dead when its pattern can no longer be found in any reed inclination, any resonant pool, any fossil hollow, any future impression of rain.

The silence corridors terrify them because nothing recurs within those moving boundaries.

When one approaches, every inhabitant begins to spread. Some flatten until they are only films between stone plates. Others flee through the root network as sequences of contractions, leaving much of their flesh behind. Adults surround the young and press them into porous rock. Even the pale mats detach and slide away.

An inhabitant caught fully inside a corridor does not struggle. Its limbs loosen. Its mineral plates lose their resemblance to the ground. For the first time it becomes clearly visible: a soft radial creature, colorless beneath its borrowed surfaces, with the dark memory organ suspended at its center.

When sound returns, the body may begin moving again. But those that recover are treated cautiously. Their familiar pressure-pattern is often missing. They feed, migrate, and respond to storms, yet no grove answers them. Some spend the rest of their lives following the edge of silence, as if listening for what was removed.

The inhabitants are not rulers of the plain. The idea of rule would make little sense to them. They cannot keep a boundary fixed or possess a place that will remain adjacent to itself. Instead they maintain relationships among movements. They shepherd seepages. They remind roots which storms to answer. They weight stone so that, centuries later, it will surface where someone needs shelter.

Their oldest works are not the walls or rings but arrangements too broad to be recognized as arrangements. A migration route may be shaped across ten thousand years so that a warm tarn repeatedly meets a certain reed coast. A submerged stratum may be salted in one age to make it resonate in another. Whole districts of nearly identical pools may be a record: each basin differing from the next by one pebble, one fossil cavity, one dissolved trace of iron.

Read in the proper order, if order can be found, the basins may contain the accumulated identities of the plain.

And when the flooded center lifts, revealing the second sky beneath, the inhabitants do not flee.

They flatten themselves against the rising skin. They open every respiratory fold and place their dense organs directly in the trembling water. Above and below, the two atmospheres thunder through them. Their bodies flash with traveling seams. Reeds lie down around them, roots drawn taut across the mud.

For those few moments they are no longer separate animals. Every pressure-pattern is carried through the level water at once. The shelters, walls, rings, grazers, fliers, fossils, and root songs become parts of a single vast sensation.

Perhaps this is what they call themselves.

Then the lower sky closes. The plain settles, cutting the sensation into bodies again. Each one retains only a little: a pressure remembered in the wrong limb, a stranger’s ancient pool, the impression of rain that will fall centuries hence.

They separate without wholly parting and resume their patient work—spreading, listening, and teaching the land how to place them beside one another again.
