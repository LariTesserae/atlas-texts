Those who live in the ward do not speak of themselves as inhabitants. They say they are **kept there**, though not in the sense of imprisonment. A gauge is kept. A promise is kept. Water is kept, briefly, before it remembers otherwise.

At first glance there seem to be very few people. One may cross three bridges and pass a dozen lit windows without meeting anyone. Then a face appears behind ribbed glass, or a figure rises from beside a canal where it had been sitting so still it resembled a bundled coat. The residents favor dark wool, waxed aprons, high boots, and close-fitting caps that keep moths from their hair. Their clothes are patched with rubberized cloth and strips of lead foil. Every garment carries the ward’s damp bloom at the seams.

They are narrow-bodied more often than not, with strong hands and an economical way of moving. The long-lived have pale, thickened nails and calluses along the sides of their fingers from testing vibration in pipes. Their skin is cool. Their voices rarely rise above conversation, and many speak with one palm resting on brick or iron, as if listening to a second exchange beneath the first.

No one is born to a fixed occupation. Work is assigned according to what a child first notices.

A child who wakes before the annual settling and complains that the floor is tilting may become a level-keeper. One who can distinguish a pipe-tap from a bird’s imitation is taught the message lines. Children who recover dropped objects from the clear settling pools are sent to the cistern crews, provided they return with the same number of memories they carried down. Those who hear old sounds before their release are placed with the wall listeners.

The smallest children wear bells made of wood. Iron bells resonate too readily, and might answer something below.

Residents do not measure age by birthdays. They count **descents**: the number of times new steps have been fitted to a familiar bridge during a person’s life. A woman of four descents is young. Someone who remembers twelve is consulted before walls are opened or pipes rerouted. The oldest people may have seen thirty descents, though by then their recollections become difficult to separate from delayed sounds stored in the masonry.

This is not considered senility. It is called becoming porous.

A porous elder may remember a workshop demolished before their birth. They may pause during supper and repeat, word for word, an argument held in a sealed pump room sixty years earlier. Families write such utterances on slate and compare them with maintenance records. Occasionally the elder speaks in their own childhood voice. More rarely they produce the pressure taps of rafter birds or the resonant note of a bell-throat. When this begins, a bed is moved close to a warm pipeline, and fungi are allowed to grow undisturbed around its joints.

The people eat canal eels, pale root vegetables raised in brick troughs, mushrooms cut from safe lines, and cakes of dark grain brought down from the upper streets. They do not eat bell-throats. They do not eat the translucent fruit of the grate-rooted trees, though they gather it in padded baskets and keep it in the coolest rooms. The rainwater inside each fruit is said to belong to a storm that has not yet occurred. Opening one may bring that storm prematurely—or merely release a mouthful of very old water. Neither possibility is thought worth testing casually.

Every household keeps several shallow dishes beneath its furniture. These are not placed to catch leaks. They reveal where a room’s water wishes to go. If the dishes begin filling in a line, the family shifts cupboards and beds away from it. If a dish fills from the bottom, the room is abandoned.

Their dwellings are sparsely furnished because possession is treated as a temporary arrangement with damp. Papers are copied on ceramic tablets. Portraits are etched into zinc. Beds hang slightly above the floor on chains. Cupboards have no backs, allowing the walls to be watched. At night, families leave one interior door unlatched so old sounds can pass through without striking it.

There are workers, although much of their labor consists of witnessing rather than operating. A valve tender may sit through an entire shift without touching the wheel under their care. A chain reader walks the round tower and marks which links have descended, noting scratches, mineral deposits, moth eggs, and changes in temperature. Crane attendants stand in the empty courtyard whenever the hook rises. They never walk beneath it. They look for strain in the cable and record the weight of what cannot be seen.

The droplet sorters are the most numerous. They work at long benches in the combing house, surrounded by steel teeth wet with distilled fog. Their fingers are deft and permanently wrinkled. They classify droplets not only by size but by reluctance, clarity, direction of movement, and the sound made when each touches glass. Some droplets roll uphill. Some cling to the shadow of a needle rather than the needle. Some contain minute noises: a snapped belt, a whispered name, rain against a roof no longer standing.

Such droplets are sealed in ampoules and stored separately.

Each ampoule is labeled with where the water appears to have come from and where it appears to be going. The second entry is usually blank.

The ward also shelters people who have no formal duties. There are bridge sleepers who make nests of discarded gasket cloth beneath the iron spans, sharing warmth with the bell-throats. There are coin divers, culvert mendicants, illegal mushroom sellers, and itinerant listeners who claim to locate unreleased voices in the walls. Some wear necklaces of numbered tags traded from drain crabs. The crabs object to theft but seem to accept barter, especially for polished washers or chips of green ceramic.

One old man lives in the electrical substation without ever touching the floor. He moves between suspended walkways of braided cable and sleeps in a porcelain cradle among the insulators. Blue arcs pass over his hands without burning him. He says each arc is a route being considered. His task, he claims, is to discourage electricity from remembering lightning.

No municipal record mentions him. Nonetheless, the substation fails whenever he leaves.

The animals are not pets, precisely, but many have entered domestic life. Water mice gather around kitchens and are fed crumbs on floating saucers. Householders watch the paths they take across flooded floors; a mouse that turns aside from an apparently sound board is trusted more than a carpenter’s hammer. Pale eels are kept in ceramic conduits beneath some homes. Their agitation warns of pressure changes. Families give them names that are never spoken near drains, lest the water carry the names elsewhere.

The pipe-tapping birds recognize particular residents. They greet maintenance workers with rapid, practical sequences and mock strangers by repeating their footsteps through distant walls. During courtship, a bird chooses two pipes that run to separate quarters and alternates between them until an unseen bird answers. Pairs may conduct an entire nesting season without meeting. Their eggs are mottled like oxidized copper, and the chicks are born with soft beaks which harden only after weeks of tapping.

Moths are treated as weather. No one tries to eradicate them. Their closed wings reproduce the color and texture of whatever surface they occupy, not by camouflage alone but by a subtler imitation: mortar lines continue across their bodies; rust flakes seem to extend over layered wings; painted numbers remain legible until a whole congregation takes flight and breaks the numeral apart.

The moth keepers maintain blank brick panels to observe them. Sometimes the insects assemble into patterns that do not match the wall beneath: a doorway, a length of vanished stair, the face of someone not yet seen in the ward. Most such images disperse before they can be copied. A few persist for days. These are covered with cloth and reported to the well attendants.

The fungi are closer to colleagues than wildlife. Experienced fitters address them before opening a line. The red undersides of their caps darken when pressure rises, but they also react to grief, electrical leakage, and the passage of broad submerged creatures beneath nearby foundations. A fungus that turns entirely inside out is grounds for immediate evacuation. This has happened only twice in reliable memory. In both cases, nothing visibly followed, though a new canal appeared months later.

The broad creatures in the deepest water have no agreed name. Each trade calls them something different: the pump crews say **cushions**, the canal children say **under-roofs**, and old ledgers refer to them as **municipal obstructions of a living character**. Their size cannot be judged from what rises against the grates. They may be many animals or one animal passing through the whole basin by channels too narrow for its apparent body.

Their backs are grey, soft-looking, and sometimes marked with regular rows of depressions. They have never been seen feeding. When one blocks a sluice, residents gather at a respectful distance and place warm metal on the bank: worn bearings, bent spoons, broken valve handles. The offerings are always gone when the creature moves.

No one knows whether it takes them.

Perhaps the water does.

Beneath all these lives—or among them, distributed through foundations and habits—is something the residents are reluctant to call living. The machinery has moods, preferences, and periods of apparent sleep, but so do old buildings. The basin breathes, but so does saturated stone under pressure. The works respond to the hidden creatures, to fungi, to annual settling, and occasionally to a person’s touch. None of this proves intention.

Still, apprentices are taught never to stand between a machine and the central well when the cap descends.

The well attendants are the nearest thing the ward has to a priesthood, though they deny this. There are always five of them, each responsible for one inward-pointing gauge and for the spaces between gauges. They wear undyed clothes and carry no metal. Their principal duty is to watch the needles without trying to interpret them.

When an attendant dies, the body is not buried outside the basin. It is washed in condensed water, wrapped in absorbent cloth, and seated for one night beside the capped well. By morning the cloth is usually dry—the only dry cloth in the ward—and the body is carried to an arch in the retaining wall. There it is placed behind new brickwork, facing inward.

Over generations, the ward’s walls have filled with such residents. Their names are stamped into the bricks in small letters. Water beads most heavily over these places, and bell-throats sometimes gather nearby.

The living say the dead are not trapped. They are helping the basin remember its shape.

During the simultaneous movement, when the cap lowers and every machine completes its fraction of work, all the ward’s inhabitants become briefly attentive to the same thing. People stop speaking. Birds hold their beaks against pipes. Water mice spread their toes on the canal film. Fungi tighten along the valves. The broad creatures rise beneath the pools. Even the moths open themselves toward the plaza.

For that instant, the ward is not a collection of species, workers, buildings, and devices. It is a single listening body arranged around a depth.

What it hears is never afterward agreed upon.

Some describe a second, lower ward operating far beneath the first. Some hear water entering stone. Some hear an immense chain gaining one link. Children often report a sound like rain beginning above a roof, although no rain falls.

Then everyone returns to their separate tasks.

The attendants mark the gauges. The birds resume their tapping. Crabs correct the circles around their grates. Families inspect their water dishes. Workers descend the newly lengthened steps, carrying lamps, tools, and blank record books.

And in the warm seams of the pipes, the ivory fungi slowly open.
