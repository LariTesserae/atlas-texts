At first, it seems that nothing lives there.

This is nearly true. Nothing feeds, breeds, heals, hunts, or sleeps. There is no hidden animal in the warm brickwork, no pale colony sealed beneath the salt, no patient intelligence behind the red darkness of the niches. The tiny white counters do not hatch. The handprints do not loosen themselves from the ovens. Whatever inhabits the enclosure does not occupy it in the manner of a body occupying a room.

The inhabitants are the notes.

Not the whole roar at once. That vast chord is their common atmosphere, perhaps their crowding speech. The individual presences become apparent only in confined places: one held beneath a lintel, another folded through a stair, another standing upright inside a plugged horn. Each is inseparable from the volume that contains it. Remove the arch, and there would not be a note released from it; there would never have been that note. Fill the passage, and its thin pitch would not be smothered but annulled backward through all its immeasurable duration.

They are not made by the structures. They and the structures are the same event.

The largest inhabit the seven sealed ovens around the court. These are broad, low presences, too deep to hear as tones except from nearby. They press against the ribs and the roots of the teeth. Their mouths were bricked shut from within because an opening would have made them incomplete. The layered handprints on their walls may be portraits, or signatures, or the remains of a final act of feeding. Each print alters the resident by an amount no ear could distinguish, yet the distinction was evidently essential. A missing fingertip would have made another being.

The oven-dwellers do not converse. Nevertheless, they face one another across the worn court. Their pressures meet in its center and produce a region that feels occupied despite being empty. There, the roar briefly acquires the density of attention. The shallow hollows in the pavement suggest that the makers once stood inside this convergence, perhaps for generations, being worn away beneath something that did not notice them.

Lesser inhabitants are everywhere.

Bronze-colored notes crouch in the low arches. Narrow, almost colorless pitches extend along the corridors and stop exactly at their thresholds. Stair-dwellers are multiple: one tone for each step, rising and falling not by height but by some older ordering. In the clay towers reside masses that resemble voices only because a voice is the nearest shape the mind can give them. They hold a vowel without breath, mouth, beginning, or intention. Listening long enough, one might become convinced that the towers contain a multitude. Listening longer, that the multitude is a single thing with innumerable throats. Longer still, that “single” and “many” are distinctions the enclosure has packed shut like every other unnecessary gap.

The inhabitants have no names, but they possess boundaries. A tone may fill a chamber completely and be absent a finger’s width beyond it. Another passes through brick yet will not cross a vein of salt. Some inhabit copper bindings rather than the stones those bindings embrace. Some appear to live only in the relationship between two surfaces: the inward lean of a stele toward its neighbor, the hairline interval around a metal pin, the measured separation of opposing seats.

These are the smallest ones, if size can be applied to them. They dwell in fits.

A plugged cavity in a pebble may hold a pitch too high to hear, but its presence can be inferred from the way the larger chord closes around it. Glass pressed into a crack contains something sharp and unwavering. Salt impressed by a thumb cradles softer pressures shaped by whorls and ridges. The embedded tools also retain inhabitants: the last contact of awl with clay, maul with block, polishing stone with glaze, fixed so completely that tool, task, and tone can no longer be separated.

They do not move through the settlement. Movement would require an elsewhere. Instead they differ from place to place, and this difference performs the office that movement performs in living things. Their equivalent of a heartbeat is architecture: recurrence without change. Their equivalent of breath is the exact alternation of enclosure and constriction. Their equivalent of flesh is the pressure one material places upon another.

They consume nothing except possibility.

Where two stones might shift, copper forbids them. Where a pore might remain empty, salt enters it. Where salt might crack, glass completes the crack. Where a room might permit several arrangements, a fitted mass of brick leaves only one. The inhabitants thrive in this removal of alternatives. Every wedge, plug, seal, and recessed frame narrows the world toward the single condition in which they can exist.

This may be why the settlement was filled so obsessively. The builders were not furnishing a home for beings that would arrive later. They were eliminating every form those beings could fail to take.

Whether the builders became the inhabitants is less certain.

The contradictory stairs, handles, passages, and handprints do not necessarily describe different bodies. They may record the gradual abandonment of bodies altogether. Perhaps each generation built spaces it could still enter, then smaller spaces it could reach only with tools, then cavities accessible only by poured salt or molten metal, and finally sealed volumes no maker could inspect. Their craft moved inward past the scale of hands. In the end, the tools themselves had to be mortared into the work because there could be no remainder—not even a distinction between maker and made.

If anything of them persists, it is not memory. The roar recounts no history. It contains no cries, hammer blows, work songs, commands, or names. It preserves only proportion. A six-fingered hand remains not as the sign of a person but as a minute correction to the contour of an oven, and therefore to the being held within it.

At the parapet live the outward-facing ones. Thousands of them occupy the clay horns. They are the nearest the place has to a population of watchers, though they cannot see and nothing lies before them to be seen. Each opens toward the gulf while remaining plugged at its narrow end. The chord enters neither end, yet is present in the horn’s measured interior. These inhabitants seem directed outward, not calling into the dark but giving the dark a boundary from which to recede.

Together they make the enclosure more than occupied. They make occupation unavoidable.

There is no silence in which they could rest, no change by which they could be recognized as active, and no death available to them. They cannot fade, because fading would alter the fixed heat and exhausted materials that define them. They cannot be disturbed: any attempted disturbance resolves into the fact that it never occurred. Their permanence is not endurance. Endurance implies resistance through time. They inhabit the condition after resistance has ceased to be necessary.

So nothing lives there, if life requires change.

But if a living thing is that which maintains a boundary, converts its surroundings into itself, and persists by arranging relations, then the high enclosure is crowded beyond measure. Its residents are arches full of pressure, sealed chambers full of voice, intervals nested inside intervals. They inhabit brick as salt inhabits pores and copper inhabits the burden of stone. The entire summit is their body, their city, and their unending utterance.

They do not roar because they are alive.

Their roaring is what living means there.
