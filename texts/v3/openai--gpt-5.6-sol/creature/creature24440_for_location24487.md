The people of the range are, before anything else, readers of consequence.

They read a darkening patch on concrete as weather approaching from within. They hear the difference between a pump knocking under load and a pump knocking because a bearing has begun to fail. They recognize which tremor belongs to a passing freight cage, which to an ore mill three districts away, and which means that everyone should leave the stair without running. A child may not know what lies beyond the manufactured peaks, but can follow painted load lines through a market, identify the safe side of a spillway, and tell by the smell of a corridor whether water has stood behind its walls.

They tend to be strong in the particular ways the range requires: calves hardened by stairs, hands practiced at railings and ladders, balance learned on wet grates. Even those whose work keeps them seated know how to move through elevation. They step aside before counterweights pass. They duck beneath fruit nets without looking. In crowded shafts they judge an approaching lift by cable song and gather their parcels before its platform appears.

Their clothing belongs to the gradients. Loose, pale layers are worn on the sunward crowns; darker, close-woven garments below, where oil mist and rust stain everything. Most people carry something waterproof that can be unfolded quickly, though rain rarely lasts long enough to justify a full coat. Shoes matter greatly. Soles are repaired again and again, cut for grip on tile, iron, or algae-slick stone. Mechanics wear tool harnesses close to the body so nothing can fall through a grille. Gardeners carry crescent knives, grafting tape, and small gauges for moisture and salt. Many residents keep a folded breathing cloth for dusty galleries and a length of cord for the innumerable small emergencies of a vertical world.

No occupation is entirely singular. A cistern keeper may also cultivate mushrooms in the cool inspection vaults. A lift operator knows enough cable work to assist during repairs and enough local history to explain why the shaft bends around an ancient furnace. Orcharders understand drainage valves. Masons recognize medicinal mosses. Launderers, who handle great quantities of warm water, often serve as informal observers of neighborhood health: they notice changes in soot, mineral content, dye, fever, and factory discharge before officials do.

The most respected workers are not necessarily those who command the largest machines. Respect gathers around those who keep several systems from harming one another. A person who can reroute runoff without starving a reed bed, brace an arcade without blocking a wind passage, or prune a fig whose roots threaten a pressure main is valuable in every district. Such people are called to disputes because most disputes in the range are also engineering problems. A noisy workshop may be someone else’s sleepless ceiling. A new planter may overload an old span. Shade welcomed by one household may weaken the citrus terraces beneath it.

Their politics therefore descend into practical detail. Councils argue over valve hours, freight priority, orchard weight, reflected light, access ladders, and who must clean the settling chamber after the first hard rain. Public notices are dense with diagrams. Laws are painted where they apply: blue marks for water, ochre for weight, white for evacuation, green for living root systems, black crosshatching for structures that may be used but not altered. The oldest regulations have become proverbs.

*Do not plant above what you cannot enter.*

*Every dry stair has had its flood.*

*Borrowed shade is still a load.*

The range does not produce a single people. Its elevations separate dialects, diets, hours, and habits as effectively as distance might elsewhere.

On the crowns live wind-facing communities accustomed to glare and sudden weather. Their houses are shuttered against sun rather than cold, their courtyards low-walled so gusts can pass, their speech clipped by the need to be understood outdoors. They maintain cloud nets, lightning towers, mirrors, upper orchards, and the great scoops that send fresh air downward. They know cloud forms intimately. To them the lower districts are fragrant, crowded, and oppressively enclosed.

In the middle terraces, where bridges knot together and most freight changes direction, live traders, fabricators, clerks, teachers, cooks, and the thousands who sort what the range continually circulates. These districts are loud even by local standards. Their people navigate by schedules and shortcuts, measuring journeys not in distance but in missed cages and favorable traverses. Markets there sell fruit from above, fungi from below, fish from the drainage gardens, salvaged fittings, copied machine manuals, condenser salt, shade cloth, bearings, cage birds, and tiles stamped to obsolete dimensions.

The deep wards have their own inhabitants, pale only when their work keeps them from the reflected courts, otherwise marked by heat and persistent damp. They service pumps, refrigeration engines, compost lifts, reservoirs, and the foundations that no one else sees. Their days may begin when upper districts sleep, because heavy maintenance is easier when water use and freight traffic diminish. They know the hidden volume of the mountains: black tanks behind housing walls, hot chambers beneath plazas, forgotten galleries where bamboo roots have found old ventilation seams.

They are sometimes treated as if they live at the bottom. They do not think of themselves that way. Beneath them are always further structures, drowned machinery, sump walks, load piers, and shafts whose lowest landings are known only to inspection crews. “Bottom” is a word for drawings.

Homes accumulate like the mountains themselves. A household may begin with two rooms in the side of a buttress and, over generations, acquire a balcony, a cistern share, a workshop recess, an upper sleeping room, and half the roof of an abandoned conduit. Boundaries are correspondingly intricate. One family owns the wall, another the rooms within it, a cooperative maintains the drain, and the orchard council controls the root bed overhead. The right to cross a kitchen stair at flood time may belong to an entire neighborhood.

Families are often larger than kinship. Lift crews, water-sharing courts, workshop households, and terrace cooperatives raise children together because schedules are irregular and danger favors familiarity. Children know many adults by function as much as relation: the aunt who keeps the east gate, the grandfather of the third pump, the elder sister who reads cracks, the neighbor responsible for closing the rain doors.

Play imitates the range. Children build tiny channel systems after storms, racing leaf boats through gutters. They make model lifts with spools and scrap wire, cultivate moss gardens in broken cups, and dare one another to climb disused stairs that every adult climbed at the same age and now sternly forbids. They keep geckos, silkworms, snails, pigeons, and small silver fish. Their counting games follow elevator sequences. Their maps are vertical stacks of remembered landmarks: bakery, red pipe, fig roots, hot landing, dog balcony, broken saint, blue lift.

The broken saint may not be a saint at all. Religion in the range adheres readily to old machinery, especially machinery whose purpose has been forgotten. A corroded pressure vessel may receive flowers because it saved a district during a fire centuries ago. Rain doors are touched for luck. New beams are named before they take weight. Some people leave cups beneath slow leaks regarded as healing; others dismiss this while carefully observing equally old customs around the first opening of a cistern.

The dead seldom leave the range. Space does not permit broad cemeteries, and earth is too useful to surrender. Some bodies are cremated in industrial furnaces during periods of clean firing. Ash is sealed into wall tiles, mixed into orchard loam, or kept in small ceramic housings beside household water gauges. Names are stamped beneath handrails, inside lift cars, along repaired arches. Thus memorials are touched rather than visited. A person may hold an ancestor’s rail every morning on the way to work.

Meals also follow elevation. Upper terraces provide fruit, herbs, oil, sun-dried foods, pigeons, and wind-cured vegetables. Dim galleries produce mushrooms, sprouts, yeast cakes, and pale greens grown in reflected light. Fish, frogs, crabs, and edible reeds come from water systems where regulations permit harvesting. Lower heat feeds communal ovens and fermentation rooms. Nothing is free of the machinery: bread rises beside warm ducts; fruit is cooled in intake galleries; tea water comes from condensers prized for their softness.

At dusk, food stalls gather around lift landings. Commuters eat while waiting for cages: grilled mushrooms glazed with citrus, flatbreads folded around beans and bitter leaves, little fish fried crisp, guava salted and dusted with spice. Vendors know the delays by sound and can stretch a meal to the arrival of a slow freight platform.

The animals are not separate from this society. Pigeons are pests, food, message carriers, and weather signs. Swifts are welcomed because they consume insects around the settling pools. Bats are protected in some chimney stacks and driven from others. Monkeys are thieves with individual reputations. Orchard keepers recognize the bold ones and reinforce latches accordingly. Dogs tend to be compact, sure-footed, and deeply reluctant to enter freight cages unless trained young.

Cats occupy the warm machine borders, sleeping on housings whose temperature they judge more accurately than gauges. Mechanics notice when they abandon a favored casing. Frogs are unofficial clocks of the condenser wards. Crabs appear in kitchens through drains and are returned, eaten, or adopted according to local custom. Parakeets learn market bells and imitate warning whistles badly enough to cause periodic outrage.

There are less visible inhabitants as well: algae that must be encouraged in one basin and scrubbed from the next; fungi that soften compost or rot cable sheathing; mineral-making bacteria in filtration beds; insects bred to pollinate orchards isolated from the upper air. The range has specialists who know these populations as others know livestock. They carry lenses and sample jars, and speak of microbial blooms with the same concern councils give to traffic.

What unites the inhabitants is not reverence for the manufactured mountains. Reverence would imply distance. They complain about them constantly. They curse stalled lifts, leaking ceilings, blocked light, hot nights, fruit-stealing monkeys, and inspectors who mark a beloved balcony for removal. They dream of moving three terraces higher or two valleys across. They maintain fierce loyalties to particular stairways and resent neighboring districts for sending down dirty water.

But they rarely imagine life outside the systems that constrain them. Freedom, to them, does not mean escaping dependence. It means dependence made legible: knowing who controls the valve, when the lift will arrive, how much weight the floor can bear, where the overflow goes, and whom to call when the wall begins to sweat.

During rain this becomes most visible.

Bells sound above. Shopkeepers hook goods from the floor. Children are counted. Garden crews clear leaf screens. Doors along the spill stairs close in descending order. People who were strangers a moment before take assigned positions at grates and sluice levers. The first water arrives warm and dirty, carrying dust, petals, feathers, metal filings, fruit skins, and the smell of every terrace it has crossed. Then it runs clearer.

For a little while the whole population listens downward.

They listen to water entering the old paths. They listen for obstructions, overflows, the hollow concussion of tanks accepting weight. Messages travel faster than the storm: a blocked channel on the windward face, a cracked tile below the fig market, good capture in the western nets, a sump rising too quickly in the deep wards.

Then the rain passes. Doors reopen. Traffic resumes by altered routes. Someone chalks a repair mark beside a new leak. Someone sets out jars to catch what remains. Frogs begin calling near the condenser outlets, and the swifts return to the bright air.

The inhabitants return to their work, though in truth they never stopped. Living there is the work: not a struggle against the mountains, nor service to them, but participation in their continued weight.
