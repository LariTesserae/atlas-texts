At first it seems that nothing lives there except the few reeds and stains.

The thing in the basin does not cross the surface. It has no trail, nest, droppings, shed skin, or regular place where it feeds. To look for it among the mineral plates is to look at the wrong scale. What lives there occupies the seepage, the buried pipes, and the unequal durations gathered around them. Its body is not hidden beneath the floor so much as distributed through everything that remains wet.

The nearest approximation is a colonial animal: something like a siphonophore assembled from water rather than flesh, with parts separated by yards of basalt and years of time. One portion may inhabit the clear beads along the wall, another the warm water in a standpipe, another the cream-colored collar around a buried pipe. These portions need not be active simultaneously. A droplet falling now may belong to the same gesture as a gauge movement six months later.

Where it presses close to the surface, the black water acquires a faint internal density. It does not cloud. Instead, reflections appear fractionally too deep, as though the pool contains more distance than its drilled socket should allow. Sometimes a colorless strand rises through it—thin as spun glass, jointed at irregular intervals—and rests against the underside of the salt skin. It withdraws if shadow falls across it, but not at once. The withdrawal may take several hours, leaving the salt membrane slightly domed and trembling.

The strands are not roots. They branch, reconnect, and occasionally form clear loops containing beads of metallic sediment. Under magnification, each loop contracts at its own rate. Some pulse several times a minute. Others have not visibly changed since the service booth was abandoned. Rust-red microbial stains cluster around the faster ones, feeding on heat or waste. The gray liverwort follows the slower branches. Neither colony crosses into the shadows of the plinths, where the buried portions of the inhabitant are closest to the surface.

Those circular shadows are not poisoned. They are occupied.

Beneath each plinth lies a dense organ of fused mineral and corroded apparatus. The concrete was poured around them, perhaps deliberately, though no plan would identify them as anything but pump foundations. One regulates pressure. One gathers electrical charge from differences in salinity. The third appears to do nothing for years at a time. When it does act, every loose piece of metal in the basin turns a few degrees, the green lamp darkens, and a transparent blister briefly swells from the severed shaft.

Inside that blister there is sometimes the suggestion of a head.

It is not consistent enough to be called one. The shape may show two dark points, or five, or none. It may resemble the blunt face of a larva, the socketed end of a cable, or a human profile reduced to a few pressure lines. It never breaks the membrane. After the buried valve gives its great inhalation, the blister collapses back into the shaft and leaves a ring of clear gel. The gel evaporates without diminishing, becoming thinner until it can no longer be distinguished from glare.

The inhabitant feeds on gradients. Warm water against cooler concrete, salt against fresh seepage, charged metal against mineral mud, one rate of time against another: these are its pasture. It does not consume reeds or microbes. Their scarcity is incidental. Where temperature and pressure become uniform, its filaments wither into brittle silica threads. Where differences persist, they thicken and sink into cracks.

This is why water never boils toward the center. The inhabitant takes the change just before boiling and carries it elsewhere. This is why open containers fill overnight. They are temporary organs, convenient hollows into which it extends itself. Water left in such a container tastes faintly of iron and old batteries. If poured out beyond the retaining walls, it creeps for a short distance against the slope before becoming ordinary runoff.

Its relation to the machinery is uncertain.

It may have grown into an installation built for another purpose, learning the pumps as a hermit crab learns a shell. It may be the installation’s intended product: a living regulator cultivated from geothermal bacteria, piezoelectric minerals, and some early industrial theory of self-correcting systems. Or the pumps and plinths may be prostheses added around something already found beneath the salt flats. The capped catwalk suggests not abandonment but observation. Its builders needed to reach a particular distance and no farther.

Whatever its origin, it now maintains the process that maintains it.

The continuous chord is partly its respiration. The turbine hum carries fluid through the deeper cavities. The pipe hammer is a slow muscular reflex transmitted through steel. The electrical whine arises where countless transparent filaments tighten against wet stone. Pressure releases are infrequent because each inhalation is immense. The blunt impact afterward is not an exhaust valve closing. It is the whole distributed body settling one layer lower.

Its sense of time is similarly anatomical. The irregular dates on the paper chart are not errors. They mark contractions in different parts of the same organism. Three days may pass in the wall seep while six years pass around the severed shaft. The bright bolts are enclosed in a region that has scarcely begun to corrode. The thick mineral collars belong to faster tissue. Rain caught in neighboring pools is divided between these local metabolisms, one pool aging through evaporation while the other remains in the first hour after the storm.

This makes the inhabitant difficult to injure. A blow delivered to one of its visible strands may not reach it until decades later. By then the hand that struck it is gone, while the bruise is only beginning to darken beneath the crust. Conversely, damage done long ago can arrive without warning. A plate of slag may buckle upward on a windless afternoon. A pipe may ring once, sharply. Clear fluid then beads from a newly opened crack, carrying flecks of material like pale blood cells made of mica.

It is not hostile. Hostility would require a quicker understanding of other creatures.

It notices weight, conductivity, retained heat, and repetition. A person standing still on the same plate each day eventually becomes part of its pattern. Condensation gathers more heavily on that person’s tools. The gauge lifts when they enter. Their reflection begins appearing in pools a moment before they lean over them. If they stop coming, the inhabitant continues to prepare for their arrival: wetting the handrail, filling a familiar container, turning loose metal toward the place where their boots used to rest.

Given enough visits, it may attempt an imitation.

A column of water rises in the service booth, supported by no vessel, roughly matching the visitor’s height. Mineral grains collect where joints might be. The top bends toward the control panel. One of the four switches moves, though not necessarily the switch the visitor used. This figure can take years to complete a single motion. By the time its arm has formed, the person it copies may be old.

The isolated reeds are perhaps earlier attempts at the same act. Each stands where something once paused: a survey rod, a cane, the leg of a tripod. Their waxy stems contain transparent fibers unlike plant vessels. They remain alive without spreading because spreading is not their purpose. They are markers in the inhabitant’s slow reconstruction of events.

It may eventually make animals in this way, but it has not yet understood movement. It understands only positions and returns.

For now it lies through the basin as a vast, patient wetness, partly organism and partly operating condition. Its oldest portions may extend far below the visible hollow, following pipes whose routes no surface plan records. Its youngest portions are still forming in the sharp black seams of the new floor. The recent slag is not ground laid over it. It is fresh tissue: armor extruded during some deep adjustment, cracked into plates so the water-body beneath can continue to breathe.

The hollow is nearly empty because its resident has no need to emerge. The droplets are its fingertips, the plinth organs its knuckles, the hidden installation its ribbed and resonant interior. The green lamp is not a signal that power remains.

It is the small, steady indication that the thing below is awake.
