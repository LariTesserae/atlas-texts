The people of the summit are not as withered as strangers imagine.

They are spare, certainly. Heavy bodies fare poorly where every basket must be carried upward and every room reached by stairs, ladders, or sloping roofs. Children develop strong hands before they grow tall. Adults have broad, gripping toes, corded calves, and a habit of placing each foot completely before shifting their weight. Even the elderly move with the slow certainty of creatures accustomed to a fatal edge.

Their skin is kept covered against sun and dust. They wear overlapping wraps of goat hide, thorn fiber, and cloth traded from below, dyed in lichen orange, soot black, resin brown, and the bruised purple of the cliffs. The garments are loose in shade and bound tightly at wrists and ankles when the wind rises. Many wear high collars over the mouth. These are not signs of secrecy. Breath is moisture, and no one lets it go carelessly into the air.

Their faces look younger in darkness than in daylight. Indoors, by the small resin lamps, one notices bright eyes, polished beads, painted eyelids, and hair dressed with oils. Outside, the glare reveals wind-lines cut deeply around nose and mouth. Children often have their heads shaved except for a braided strip beneath the crown, while adults bind their hair close with cords and bone pins. Loose hair catches thorns, seed hooks, soot, and the claws of nesting birds.

They smell of smoke and bitter plants. Instead of washing, they scrape the skin with smooth stone, rub it with fragrant ash, then work in a paste of oil, powdered lichen, and resin. The paste seals cracks and discourages insects. Every household has its own mixture. People can recognize kin in a dark passage by scent alone.

They speak loudly, but they are not a loud-hearted people. Their voices have been shaped by walls, wind, and distance. Names are short and hard-edged. Instructions are whistled, drummed, or struck on resonant stones. A message passed across the town may begin as three drumbeats on an upper terrace, be answered by a wooden goat bell, and end as a child shouting down a stairwell.

Conversation at arm’s length is different. It is full of touches: knuckles to the wrist, palm to shoulder, fingertips laid briefly beneath the chin. In the deepest lanes, where two people cannot pass without turning sideways, touch has developed into a second language. A hand against another’s back may mean *behind you*, *I carry heat*, *there is grief in the house*, or simply *do not step left*.

They do not organize themselves chiefly by blood. Blood matters, but rooms matter more.

A household is whoever sleeps around the same warm fissure and tends the plants rooted in its walls. A person may inherit a mother’s knife, a father’s name, and an aunt’s room, while owing daily loyalty to an elderly gardener who is no relation at all. Marriages often involve exchanges of sleeping ledges and roof rights rather than objects. When two people join households, the most serious question is not where they will lie, but which garden will receive their food scraps, ash, hair, bones, and dung.

Plants are counted among a household’s elders.

The oldest pillar succulents have personal names. Their scars record fires, famines, births, and renovations. Some predate the walls now embracing them. A family may live in three rooms built successively around the same immense plant, cutting windows to accommodate its growth and tying its upper arms to stone braces during storms. To prune such a plant without permission is not merely theft. It is an injury to the household itself.

Children learn plants before they learn genealogy. They are taught which pad may be eaten raw, which must be scorched, which bulb draws fever from the body, which sap seals leather, which resin burns clear, and which pale flower opens only once before dawn and must never be touched with bare fingers. They know where each root enters the masonry. They can tell from a slight slackness in a skin whether a plant is thirsty, diseased, or preparing to fruit.

Nothing green is ornamental alone. Yet beauty is everywhere. Thorns are trained into spirals. Lichens are encouraged to form bands around doorways. Night flowers are bred for scent, and households compete over blossoms that smell of pepper, hot wax, old fruit, or sleeping animals. Seed pods are selected for their voices. In the windy season the town becomes a vast dry instrument, each roof rattling in its inherited rhythm.

Their food is dense, sour, fibrous, and usually warm. Succulent flesh is sliced thin with stone blades. Pads are roasted under heated slabs. Seeds are ground with insects, blood, fat, or fruit pulp into dark cakes that keep for years. Goat meat is eaten rarely, because a living goat provides milk, blood, dung, hide scrapings, warmth, and new goats. Small amounts of blood are taken from the animals with practiced care, thickened in hot bowls, and mixed with bitter meal.

No one speaks of thirst casually.

A thirsty child is not scolded, but the whole household becomes attentive. Lips are examined. The tongue is touched. Food is changed. Work is moved into shade. Condensation medicine may be brought out in a sealed cup no bigger than a thumb joint. Its cloudy drops are applied to the gums or eyelids. To swallow them all at once would seem as reckless as burning a roof beam for momentary warmth.

Because moisture comes hidden inside living things, generosity is measured differently here. To offer someone fruit is intimate. To cut a fresh pad for a guest is extravagant. To give blood is a vow. The summit people have no custom of pouring libations for the dead; they would consider it incomprehensible to spill what life has gathered so painfully. Instead they release smoke, dust, feathers, and powdered pigment—substances the wind already knows how to carry.

The dead remain in the town for a time.

Bodies are dried in warm rooms near the deepest fissures, wrapped in fiber, and laid on the central stone during the dark hours. Relatives sit around them, not in silence, but repairing the deceased’s belongings. A sandal is resoled. A cracked mortar is rebound. A bone pin is sharpened. Stories are told only while hands are working, so that memory enters useful things.

Afterward, little is wasted. Hair becomes cord. Teeth and finger bones become beads, counters, or inlay. The rest is carried beyond the parapet to certain cliff shelves where the heavy-beaked scavengers wait. These birds are neither sacred nor tame, but they are tolerated as old partners. People recognize individuals by chips in their beaks and patterns of missing feathers. A bird that has fed on one’s dead is never driven from one’s roof.

The town has no rulers in the manner of the plains. Authority belongs temporarily to whoever understands the problem at hand. Garden disputes are judged by the keepers of roots. Building disputes go to those who know the hidden seams of the summit. Goat sickness brings the blood-tenders. During high winds, command passes to the rope masters, who can order any roof cleared and any bridge cut loose.

The closest thing to a permanent council is formed by the Rememberers of Weight. They keep no written archive. Their knowledge concerns what stands above what: which wall rests on an older chamber, which planted ledge can bear another person, which stair has hollowed beneath its plaster, which thorn trunk now supports a roof beam that appears to support the tree. They carry the town as a structure in memory. Before any room is enlarged, a Rememberer lies on the floor, presses an ear to the stone, and has people walk across nearby roofs. From the tremors and echoes, they decide what the mountain will permit.

Craft is esteemed more than possession. Everyone repairs, but some people become masters of the final use of things. They can look at a broken bowl and see a lamp reflector, then a seed tray, then a wall shim, then grit for plaster. A knife may diminish through six generations, from blade to scraper to awl to drill point, before its last glittering chip is pressed into a doorway mosaic. Objects are rarely said to be destroyed. They are said to have become smaller than their names.

Some inhabitants spend much of their lives on the cliffs.

These are the ledge-walkers: gatherers of eggs, catchers of swifts, collectors of salt crusts, rescuers of goats, and guides for the rare trade parties that climb from below. Their ropes are braided from many fibers and inspected strand by strand each morning. They descend barefoot, carrying loops around waist and thigh, moving over the ancient face of the mountain with such patience that from above they resemble dark insects.

Those who travel to the plains return altered. They speak more softly for a while, unused to nearby walls. They complain that lowland air lies heavily in the lungs. Rivers horrify them—not because they fear drowning, but because so much drinkable life moves past without being caught. Some cannot bear the sight of rain. Others stand beneath it weeping, then feel ashamed of both the tears and the water running uselessly from their clothes.

Lowlanders, when they reach the summit, often mistake its people for severe ascetics. In truth, they are fond of games, gossip, mimicry, and elaborate jokes involving echoes. They carve tiny animals from seed husks. They race beetles across warm slabs. Young people court one another by exchanging grafts from difficult plants or by secretly tuning the pods above a beloved’s sleeping place. On festival nights, drums begin in the lowest lanes and climb tier by tier until every roof is sounding. Dancers do not leap—the roofs are too crowded and the cliffs too near—but stamp, turn, and weave their shoulders while bells answer from the goat ledges.

The children are the boldest creatures in the town. They fit through gaps adults cannot, climb the thorn trees without seeming to touch them, and dare one another to lie belly-down at the parapet and watch swifts rise out of the haze. They know the warm paving intimately. They nap in hollows between roots, emerge dust-colored, and are often followed by lizards waiting for crumbs or flies.

The goats belong partly to them. Narrow as folded cloth and nearly impossible to startle, the animals walk outer ledges no human trusts. Each wears a bell whose note identifies its household. At dusk the town listens to the bells returning through the lower haze. A missing note is heard at once.

Birds are less owned than negotiated with. Seed-eaters are chased from new plantings and welcomed when insects hatch. Swifts nest under bridges and fill the evening with their shrieks. Scavengers are given bones but punished for stealing hide. Small pale moths pollinate the night gardens; children carry them gently from roof to roof in pierced clay boxes. Beetles live beneath the warm stones and are gathered for food, dye, shell, or divination, depending on species.

Even the fissures seem inhabited. Warmth emerges from them with faint sounds: sighs, ticks, occasional deep knocks that no hammer made. The summit people do not worship the mountain as a person, exactly. They regard it as something older than personhood, too reduced by age to desire much, yet not wholly insensible. They say it dreams downward. The heat is the remnant of that dreaming.

They avoid promises involving permanence. Nothing lasts; some things merely wear away with dignity. Their blessings translate roughly as: *May your bindings hold.* Their expression for great age means: *rounded past breaking.* Someone dependable is called a deep-set stone. Someone wasteful is said to have an open mouth to the wind.

Seen together, they are less a population occupying the summit than one element of its crowded ecology. Their houses shelter roots. Roots brace houses. Goats feed on cuttings and return dung. Birds clear bone. Beetles turn waste beneath the paving. Warm cracks dry food, bodies, plaster, and newborn animals. Human hands prune, bind, grind, carry, remember, and rebuild.

No part survives alone.

At evening, when the last sunlight catches the pillar plants and turns every thorn into a line of fire, the inhabitants gather on roofs and ledges. Their wrapped figures mingle with branches, ladders, smoke, tether ropes, wings, and stone. From a distance it is difficult to say where the people end and the town begins.

That distinction does not greatly concern them.
