The first inhabitant is usually mistaken for a vertical shadow.

It stands near a seam in the rock, narrow and gray, with its arms held close against its sides. Nothing distinguishes it from the cliff until it turns its head. Even then the movement is so slight that one may believe the stone has merely presented another surface.

Travelers call them the Still People. This is not their name for themselves. Their own designation, if they have one, is made of several low pulses in the throat, too deep to hear clearly through the roar. Felt with a hand against the speaker’s breastbone, the pulses resemble distant impacts: three close together, a pause of indeterminate length, then a fourth which may already have been sounding before the others began.

They are roughly human in shape, though “human” becomes less certain at close range. Their proportions vary subtly from shelf to shelf. Those found near the high troughs are long-legged and narrow, with knees that appear to bend a little too low. Those near the condensation basins are broad-backed, almost squat. A solitary figure seen at a distance may change from one type to the other as it is approached, without any visible transformation.

Their skin has the muted color of slate beneath frost. It is hairless except along the spine and forearms, where a coarse gray growth lies in one direction like the local moss. Some travelers insist this is vegetation rather than hair. Black lichen rings occasionally appear on their shoulders, and the Still People neither scrape them away nor show discomfort when the rings slowly widen.

Their eyes are clear and colorless. The pupil is difficult to locate. They do not blink often, and when they do, the lids close from both sides as well as above and below, puckering briefly like the mouth of a bladder. Their gaze seldom rests on a person’s face. They look instead at the rock beside the person, or into the space just above one shoulder, as though the visible body were only the nearest indication of where that person actually stood.

Their hands are warm only by comparison with the stone. Each has five fingers, but the branching creases in the palms continue past the wrist and can sometimes be seen dividing under the skin of the forearm. When a Still Person lays a hand against a cliff, those lines darken. It may remain in contact for hours. During this interval, figures on distant shelves sometimes raise their heads.

They wear no woven clothing. Some cover themselves in loose mantles that resemble hide until touched, when they prove to be dense layers of living moss attached directly to the skin. Others go bare, with frost gathered in the hollows of the collarbone and beneath the ribs. The frost does not melt. Young ones, if they are young, are often almost completely white with it.

No one has seen them build a stone ring. Nevertheless, they inhabit the rings.

A chamber that appears empty may disclose an occupant only after one has entered and sat down. There will be a breath from the far wall, distinct from the breathing beneath the mountain, and then a gray shoulder where no shoulder was before. The Still Person will be lying in one of the polished depressions, curled tightly enough that the body seems to occupy less space than its bones should require.

They sleep without closing their eyes. Their breathing is very slow—perhaps one breath in the time it takes frost to numb an exposed hand. Exhaled vapor gathers above their mouths in pale, compact shapes and does not disperse. Several chambers contain such suspended breaths despite having no visible occupant. The Still People treat these remnants with care. They pass beneath them bowed, and will prevent a traveler from touching one.

Whether they live alone or in groups depends on how a group is counted in that country. Rarely are more than two bodies seen together. Yet a person sitting among them may hear dozens of distinct throat-pulses passing through the stones. A Still Person may put one hand into a narrow crack and, after a pause, have its fingers clasped from within by another hand whose owner is visible several valleys away.

Their gatherings are arranged through adjacency rather than travel. On certain days—or conditions resembling days—many can be seen seated separately across the country, each at the edge of a different ravine, all facing inward toward a center that has no visible location. They make no gesture and produce no sound distinguishable from the roar. Afterward, new cairns are sometimes found beside the rivers.

The Still People do not walk as travelers do. They place each foot with deliberation, often testing the ground several inches above its visible surface before lowering their weight. At shadows that point upward, they turn sideways and pass as though through a narrow doorway. When descending, they may vanish below the lip of a shelf and shortly afterward appear higher on the same slope.

They never beckon. Beckoning is considered dangerous there, since the gesture may shorten the wrong distance. To invite another person closer, they touch the ground between them. To warn someone away, they touch their own sternum.

Their language is mostly contact.

Palm to stone means something different from palm to skin. Fingertips against the jaw concern direction; against the throat, memory; against the closed eyelid, weather. A warning is given by pressing a single knuckle into the listener’s ribs until the pressure can be felt in the bone. Gratitude resembles taking someone’s pulse, though the Still People themselves seem to have no pulse at the wrist.

They can speak aloud, but dislike doing so. Their voices are soft and dry, and every spoken word persists in the surrounding roar long after the mouth closes. For this reason they use words sparingly. A careless sentence may remain audible for years in some corridor of stone.

Names are especially hazardous. A name spoken once can continue calling its owner from several directions. The Still People therefore name one another with brief arrangements of taps, breaths, and intervals. The interval is the most important part. Two inhabitants may produce identical sounds and still be understood as different because one waits between them for the length of a rainfall, while the other waits for the duration of a wound healing.

They hear distinctions in the perpetual roar that outsiders cannot. To them it is not a single sound but an immense, stable record. They can point toward the noise of a stone struck before their birth. They can identify the thunder of a waterfall that hangs on the underside of a plateau no visible route reaches. They sometimes listen with their ears pressed not to the rock but to another person’s chest, searching among the heartbeats for echoes that do not belong to the body.

They appear to eat little. They press their mouths to lichen colonies, but the circles remain intact. They kneel beside the pale bladder-plants and touch their tongues to the suspended drop without puncturing the translucent skin. Afterward the drop is still present, though sometimes it hangs nearer the center.

At overhangs, they count condensation. If more than seven beads have gathered, a Still Person removes none of them. Instead it lays both hands against the underside of the rock until one bead appears elsewhere along the seam. Whether this prevents the shelter from opening onto another country, or merely moves the entrance, is unknown.

They drink from the narrow rivers by submerging their faces. The water does not ripple around them. They may remain face-down for an hour, breathing at the same slow rate as before.

The slate-colored browsing animals tolerate their presence. Occasionally one approaches a seated Still Person and fits its narrow head beneath the person’s arm. Both remain that way for days. The white moths gather on their backs, especially over the shoulder blades. No Still Person has ever been seen disturbing one.

The transparent larvae they watch with greater attention. An inhabitant may crouch beside a pool for weeks, following the continuous twitching with minute movements of the eyes. Some travelers believe the larvae are their children. Others believe the Still People consult them because they are the only creatures capable of true movement.

There are smaller Still People, though no births have been witnessed. The small ones are not proportioned like human children. Their arms and legs have nearly adult length, while the torso is slight and soft-looking, as if the body has not yet gathered enough stone around itself. They stay within the rings and sleep in depressions much older and larger than they are.

They are cared for communally, if care is the right word. An adult may enter a chamber alone and place food or water into an apparently empty hollow. Much later, on another shelf, a small figure will be seen with moisture on its lips. Adults do not carry them. In danger, they place a hand over the small one’s eyes, after which the child is no longer there.

Age is difficult to judge. Some have deeply folded faces and backs rounded like weathered cliffs. Others are smooth-skinned. Yet the same pattern of lichen, scars, and missing fingers recurs among both. A traveler may meet an old inhabitant on one shelf and, years later, encounter a younger version beside footprints the traveler has not yet made.

The Still People do not seem troubled by this. They recognize kinship in direction rather than descent. One may indicate a distant figure and communicate, through taps against the jaw, something interpretable as:

*That is the one from whom I am becoming.*

Another points to a frost-filled body hollow and conveys:

*That is where we continue to have been born.*

They have no graves. Bodies are sometimes found lying on open stone, especially near the motionless waterfalls. They are cold, rigid, and apparently without breath. No decay follows. If watched, the corpse remains. If left and later revisited, the shelf may contain only a polished depression and a circular lichen colony exactly the width of the body’s chest.

The Still People make no distinction between this and departure.

They are not hostile, but they do not rescue by force. If a traveler follows a stream toward its sound, an inhabitant may appear nearby and place a hand against its own sternum. If ignored, it will not pursue. Days later, the traveler may see the same figure standing impossibly close on a remote precipice, still holding the warning gesture.

Those who accept guidance are led without routes. The guide touches certain walls, waits at apparently meaningless patches of frost, and sometimes walks away from the intended destination. At the end, the traveler finds the stone ring or pass within arm’s reach. The guide is usually no longer present. A low pulse may remain in the rock.

Long residence among them changes a person. Speech grows burdensome. The roar separates into layers. One begins to recognize individual suspended falls by their thunder and to hear old footsteps continuing beneath new ones. The pulse slows. Frost lingers in the creases of the skin. Black rings appear where the shoulders meet the neck.

The Still People notice these changes but offer neither welcome nor alarm. They merely make room in the moss chambers.

Eventually, travelers who remain are seen less often in motion. They are glimpsed standing beside walls, then mistaken for shadows, then found on shelves no ordinary path connects. Their old footprints persist alongside newer tracks coming from elsewhere. Their names, if spoken, answer faintly from the roar.

Whether this is how the country acquires its inhabitants, or whether every traveler who enters has always been among them, is one of the distinctions the Still People do not make.
