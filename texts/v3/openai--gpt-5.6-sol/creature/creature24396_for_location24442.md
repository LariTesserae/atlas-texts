No single creature owns the Green Mill. Nothing there is large enough to stand in its central chamber and seem like the inhabitant of the place. Its residents disclose themselves piecemeal: an eye in a leaf-fold, a tremor under moss, a mouth opening at the surface of black water. Taken separately, most are slight and ordinary. Taken together, they amount to something vast.

The oldest visible inhabitants are the boundary trees. To call them twelve trees is convenient but probably wrong. Each began as an individual long ago, yet every trunk now standing is a continuation: a limb bent by its own weight, a branch that touched the floor and rooted, a rooted branch that thickened until the parent portion rotted away. They migrate without going anywhere. Over centuries each has crept a short distance around the basin, abandoning wood behind and extending new wood ahead.

Their bark is never bare. The outermost layer belongs to moss, lichens, algae, mites, snails, and minute pale insects that spend their whole lives in a single fissure. Beneath them, beetle larvae move through softened wood in slow galleries. Deeper still, fungal cords occupy the boundary between living and dead tissue. The tree may cease to use a limb years before the limb ceases to function in the grove. Hollowed by beetles and reinforced by roots, it becomes a watercourse, a resonating chamber, a nest wall, and eventually soil.

The leaves of these trees are small, thick, and dark on top, with paler undersides. They do not flutter readily. Rain ticks against them, gathers, and travels along a fine network of veins toward hooked tips. Each leaf empties onto one below it. The canopy therefore does not merely receive weather; it passes water hand to hand.

In the forks grow low cups of liverwort and moss. Tiny frogs live in them.

The frogs are no larger than the last joint of a finger. Their backs resemble wet bark, dull green or brown with an irregular black seam along the spine. Their toes end in broad, translucent pads, and their bellies are thin enough that swallowed insects sometimes show as shadows. Many never descend to the floor. They hatch in rainwater retained among leaves, feeding first on algae and one another’s unfertilized eggs. When a cup begins to dry, they climb deeper into the tree rather than toward the pools.

Their calls are disproportionately powerful. Each frog chooses a hollow stem, a bark pocket, or the mouth of a beetle gallery and calls into it. The grove enlarges the sound. What seems to be a deep pumping chorus may begin in a throat scarcely wider than a seed.

The frogs do not call at random. One starts; those nearest wait. Others answer from farther around the perimeter, spaced by fractions of a second. The result travels around the basin in repeating circuits. A frog that calls at the wrong interval is masked by its neighbors and seldom answered by a mate. Over generations, timing has become as important as volume. Their chorus is both courtship and partition: each small voice preserving a place for itself in the larger machine.

Higher in the trees are the cicadas. They spend most of their lives unseen, feeding in the root-bound darkness below the grove. When they emerge, they climb the same old routes up the trunks, leaving rows of split husks on the mossy bark. Several kinds may be active at once. One produces a thin metallic shriek, another a coarse buzzing pulse, another a wavering note that rises and falls so slowly it seems stationary until it stops.

The broods do not quite synchronize. There is always some population climbing, sounding, mating, or dying. Their bodies fall into the moss and vanish quickly. Ants take the softer parts. Mites enter the joints. Fungal bloom clouds the clear wings. Within days, only fragments of shell remain, and soon those too are folded into the floor.

The ants are small, dark, and nearly blind. Their permanent roads run beneath the moss rather than over it. In cross-section the root mat is scored with their passages: narrow lanes roofed by fibers, widened at intervals into dry chambers. They transport seeds, insect eggs, fungal bodies, and the remains of anything they can disassemble. Some seeds they eat. Some they discard in refuse pockets enriched by generations of waste. Those discarded seeds germinate more reliably than seeds falling in the open, so many of the grove’s smaller plants depend on being carried away by the creatures that intended to destroy them.

The ants keep several kinds of sap-feeding insects in chambers along the roots. These soft, colorless animals are moved when a root declines and guarded when beetles or predatory mites approach. The arrangement resembles husbandry, but no ant understands it as such. It is an inherited sequence of touches, scents, feedings, removals, and returns.

Above the ants, in the compressed surface of the floor, live pale millipedes. They avoid open light. If the moss is lifted, they can be found coiled among brown fibers, each one the color of old ivory. Their work is unhurried and nearly continuous. They enter leaves after fungi have softened them, rasp away the tissue between veins, and leave dark pellets that mites and smaller decomposers break apart again. Their young are nearly transparent. With each molt they add segments and legs, becoming better suited to the narrow, curving spaces in which they feed.

There are worms as thin as hairs, springtails that scatter like droplets, flat mites colored orange or crimson, and blind crustaceans surviving in the permanently moist underside of the mat. Many are known only by what they leave: a lacework of grazing on a fungal film, a scatter of eggs under bark, an empty skin adhering to the roof of a root tunnel.

The fungi are harder to describe as inhabitants because most have no stable visible body. Their threads pass through wood, root, moss, and accumulated insect remains. Some belong to single patches no larger than a hand. Others may extend from one side of the basin to another, interrupted and rejoined so many times that individuality is uncertain.

After rain they briefly declare themselves. Small black cups appear on drowned twigs. White fans push from trunk undersides. Amber knobs rise through moss and liquefy within a day. Fine gray stalks release spores at the touch of a falling drop. These structures are not the fungi themselves so much as moments when the unseen population reaches the air.

The fungi do not merely consume the grove’s ruins. They determine how quickly ruin is allowed to disappear. Wood lying near a water pocket is opened rapidly, becoming soft enough for beetles and roots within a season. Wood that forms a useful brace or roof may remain dense for decades because the fungi inhabiting it advance slowly. No intention governs this difference, yet over time the grove retains decomposers whose rates suit its structure. Fast decay where material must circulate, slow decay where old timber still serves.

The pools hold another set of lives.

At first glance their black surfaces seem vacant except for insects skating on the tension. Then a bubble rises against the edge. A larva jerks beneath the reflection. Something narrow uncurls from among the roots.

The largest pools contain fish, though fish scarcely seems the proper scale for them. They are needle-bodied and nearly without color, with wide pectoral fins used to brace against submerged fibers. Their eyes are functional but small. They feed on mosquito larvae, frog young, minute worms, and whatever falls from the canopy. When water contracts, they retreat into the root fringe, pressing themselves into pockets where the moss remains saturated. Their bodies tolerate water that is cool, acidic, and low in air. At times they rise and gulp from the surface, storing the swallowed air in a modified chamber above the gills.

A pool may lose nearly all its open water and still retain fish. They wait in the buried wetness, scarcely moving, while their skin darkens to match the roots. When rain restores the pool, they emerge as if newly made.

Other creatures use the water only for one stage of life. Midges suspend their larvae in soft tubes along the bottom. Beetles carry silver bubbles beneath their wing cases. Long-legged flies patrol the margins. Dragonfly nymphs hunt in the larger pools, though the adults seldom cross the open center; they circle close to the canopy, following the same protected routes as the birds.

The birds are heard more often than seen. They are small-bodied, short-winged, and adept at moving through horizontal branches. Their colors belong to bark and lichen: gray breasts, greenish backs, pale bars that break up the outline of folded wings. Even the brighter breeding markings tend to occur under the throat or wing, visible mainly to another bird at close range.

Each pair uses a section of the perimeter. Because sight lines are obstructed, they maintain their boundaries by repeated calls. A bird on one side sounds two hard notes. Its neighbor answers with three. Another farther around gives a thin descending whistle. The exchanges continue throughout the day, regular enough to be mistaken for a mechanical signal system.

They feed on cicadas when broods are abundant, but most of their hunting is finer. They turn leaves for moths, probe moss for beetles, seize frogs only when other prey is scarce, and hover briefly beneath branches to pick spiders from their webs. Their nests are inserted into living structures rather than built freely: a mossed hollow, the cup of a rooted limb, the space behind loose bark. Fungi and rootlets may begin entering a nest before the chicks have left it. By the following season, the fibers have become part of the tree.

The central opening offers the birds little. There are no high perches there, few insects beyond brief passing swarms, and nowhere to disappear from a hunter. They cross it only when driven. Their habitual paths curve around it, making the empty space feel prohibited, though there is no prohibition.

A few mammals inhabit the oldest trunks. They are seldom seen and have no reason to descend. Small as shrews, but with broad gripping feet and long balancing tails, they travel inside branches where decay has opened continuous corridors. They feed on beetle grubs, nestling mice, fungi, cicadas, and eggs taken from unattended bird nests. Their whiskers are long enough to test a hollow before the head enters it. Their fur is dense, dark, and faintly oily against perpetual damp.

They mark favored junctions with musk. They sleep in shredded fungal fiber and dry moss gathered from cavities protected by the canopy. A female may raise her young in a chamber enclosed by living wood on one side and the paper-thin wall of an old branch on the other. The young learn the trunk’s interior as other animals learn a landscape: which passage ends in water, which narrows beyond turning, which opens under leaves, which vibrates when frogs call.

They seldom leave footprints. If one crosses an exposed patch, the moss lifts after its passing and closes the record.

Predators in the Mill are correspondingly small. Spiders span gaps between sedge blades. Centipedes hunt beneath bark. Slender snakes, no thicker than a root cord, take frogs and sleeping lizards along pool margins. A little owl sometimes occupies a trunk chamber and hunts the tree-dwelling mammals, but no large hunter remains permanently. There is too little concentrated flesh and too much of the basin is difficult to traverse. Anything substantial would spend more energy crossing the mat than it could reliably recover from prey.

Death is common, but remains are rare.

A fallen bird is entered through the eyes and mouth before its feathers have dried. Ants open the joints. Beetle larvae work beneath the skin. Fungal threads bind the feathers to the moss. Calcium passes into roots or into the shells of snails. A frog may become no more than a darkened leaf axil by morning. Even bone persists only where it falls into a pool and is sealed under silt. Elsewhere, the place takes bodies apart too efficiently to preserve evidence of them.

This gives the residents an unfinished appearance. They live among no monuments to their ancestors. Each generation inherits paths, resonances, feeding relationships, and chemical traces, but almost no intact remains. Memory resides in behavior and arrangement: cicadas climbing where cicadas climbed, birds answering from traditional stations, roots following channels lined by vanished roots.

There are rivalries. Mosses overgrow one another. Fungal fronts meet in wood and poison the narrow zone between them. Ant colonies conduct quiet wars in buried lanes. Birds steal nest hollows. Frogs eat smaller frogs. The equilibrium is not peaceful. It is crowded with exclusions, predation, theft, infection, and failure.

Yet no victory lasts. A fungus that consumes wood too quickly destroys its own habitat. An ant colony that expands beyond its dry chambers enters saturated mat and loses its brood. A bird pair controlling too wide a perimeter cannot answer every intrusion. A moss that covers all exposed surfaces shades its own lower growth until channels reopen beneath it. Success folds back on itself.

Thus the characteristic inhabitant of the Green Mill is not any tree, frog, insect, bird, fish, or fungus. It is the exchange between them.

It has no central body. Its skin is the root mat and canopy. Its lungs are the hollow stems and buried trunks. Its pulse is made by water and answered by frogs. Cicadas give it its high electrical cry; beetles supply the clicking within its walls. Fungi form its digestion. Ants perform its transport. Birds patrol its outer chambers like quick thoughts that never enter the quiet center.

This larger inhabitant does not think, plan, or desire. It can be injured without feeling pain and repaired without knowing loss. Nearly every part of it dies and is replaced, while the relations among the parts endure. From year to year it remains recognizable not because its residents remain the same, but because each new life enters an occupation already shaped for it.

The Green Mill is therefore inhabited everywhere and by no one in particular.

Its creatures live in edges rather than expanses, in bark rather than on branches, under moss rather than over ground, around pools rather than in open water. They have become small enough to occupy the seams of the place. The vacant chamber at the center is not their absence. It is something they collectively maintain: a clear interior around which light can circulate, calls can travel, wings can turn, and the whole innumerable population can hear itself working.
