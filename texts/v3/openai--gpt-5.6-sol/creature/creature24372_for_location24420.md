The inhabitants are mostly creatures for whom a thumbprint is a province and an hour of rain a season.

The termites live deepest in the idea of the city. Pale, soft-bodied, nearly blind, they move through their towers without ever seeing the shapes they have raised. Their world is pressure, scent, moisture, and the touch of other bodies passing in darkness. Workers carry pellets of clay between their jaws and place them where the air tells them to. Soldiers wait in narrowed passages, broad heads turned toward vibrations. The queen lies farther below, immense among attendants, less like a ruler than a warm organ around which the colony continually rebuilds itself.

They do not know their turrets from outside. They know only that one wall leaks dry air, another tastes of fungus, a third trembles under the passage of a crab. Yet their sealed chambers give the basin its tallest forms. When rain loosens a tower, workers arrive at the wound and become a pale living scab beneath the mud.

The crabs are less hidden. Small, square, and stained the color of wet earth, they emerge sideways from holes under roots. Their eyes rise first on slender stalks, testing the lanes. Then the body follows, carrying one claw slightly higher than the other.

Each crab seems to possess a private geometry. It knows which pellet must be lifted, which entrance cleared, which slope made steeper before the next shower. It excavates with fussy concentration, rolling the soil into beads and setting them outside its burrow in loose fans. Overnight these fans become embankments, stepping grounds, even walls around pools. By noon another creature has crossed them, nested in them, or collapsed them. The crab returns and begins again, offended by change but inseparable from it.

The solitary bees occupy the warmest faces of clay. They are dark, quick beings with dusted legs and a precise concern for circles. A female inspects a hollow repeatedly before accepting it. She enters headfirst, backs out, turns, measures again with her antennae. Inside she packs pollen into a golden loaf, lays one pale egg upon it, then closes the chamber with a plug of mud. The sealed door resembles an eye that has chosen not to open.

She does not remain to guard it. Her offspring waits alone behind the wall while rain passes over the district and termites alter the foundation beneath it. Some chambers stay dry. Some are invaded by mites or narrow-waisted wasps. Some crack open too early and reveal their yellow provisions to ants. Survival here often means being forgotten in exactly the right place.

The ants are everywhere without owning anything. Fine black species travel the vine loops; reddish ones patrol exposed clay; nearly transparent ones inhabit the wet seams below fallen leaves. Their paths cross but seldom coincide. One colony harvests fungal growth, another gathers dead insects, another raids soft larvae from ruptured chambers. They discover catastrophe almost immediately. Where a bird has struck, ants arrive before the clay has finished falling.

To larger eyes they seem orderly. Up close, their order is made of hesitation, collision, correction, and the repeated touching of antennae. A trail is not a road but a suggestion renewed by every traveler.

Among them move beetles of many tempers. Glossy ground beetles hunt through the open squares at dusk, their bodies reflecting the dim light like drops of hardened oil. Tiny rove beetles slip into burrows and under fungal crusts, bending their long abdomens as they run. Dung beetles arrive after the larger visitors and regard what others have left behind as a sudden abundance. Minute round beetles graze spores from the undersides of leaves. Some can cross the city in a night. Others live and die within a single damp chamber.

Caterpillars inhabit the roofs. They pull leaf edges together with threads so fine that dew reveals what daylight cannot. Under those green folds they eat, grow, shed their skins, and leave behind black grains that fall into the streets below. Their shelters are temporary even by the standards of the basin. A leaf expands, a seam tears, or the inhabitant closes itself into a pupa and ceases to be the creature that built the room.

When the moth emerges, it abandons the scale of the city. It climbs a stem, dries its wings, and rises into the high darkness among flowers. Yet its absence remains below: a loosened awning, a silk-lined refuge soon occupied by a spider, an earwig, or a cluster of sleeping gnats.

The spiders build in the spaces no one else recognizes as solid. Between a root and a turret they stretch a plane of threads. Across a burrow mouth they lay trip lines. Beneath leaves, small hunters wait without webs, their forelegs open. Dew makes their structures briefly visible, turning empty air into bridges and cups. By midday the water has vanished and the architecture disappears again, though the spider remains at its center.

Near the pools live the soft-skinned citizens.

Frogs press themselves into cool clay recesses, mottled backs indistinguishable from the walls. Some are scarcely larger than a seed pod. Their gold-rimmed eyes remain open while the rest of them appears asleep. They wait for midges, springtails, mosquitoes, and careless flies. A tongue flickers; something that occupied the city a moment ago does not occupy it now.

When water deepens after rain, the frogs enter it without ceremony. A nursery may appear in a basin that was dry that morning: eggs gathered in clear jelly, each holding a dark point. The pool can vanish before they hatch. It can also persist long enough to fill with comma-shaped tadpoles, each feeding on films of algae and fungus along the clay. Their lives depend upon the temporary becoming sufficient.

Mosquito larvae hang beneath the water’s skin, breathing at the boundary between pool and air. They descend when shadows pass, then rise again. Water striders arrive at larger cups and stand on the surface as though it were stretched cloth. Predatory nymphs wait below them. Rotifers, nematodes, protozoans, and bacteria inhabit the water in populations beyond the city’s visible multitude. To them, a single droplet under a leaf is not weather but an inhabited world.

Fungus lives through nearly every district, though little of it resembles a mushroom. It is a pale web in darkness, a softness binding grains, a bloom on drowned wood, a white rim consuming a dead wing. Its threads enter fallen leaves and insect husks, pass through termite walls, and follow roots into the soil. Here and there it sends up a fragile fruiting body: translucent, pleated, gone by afternoon. The visible stalk is only a brief announcement from the larger hidden life below.

The roots are inhabitants too, though they travel at a pace that disguises movement. They enter chambers, drink from reservoirs, split galleries, and brace walls they did not mean to support. Their tips are tender and insistent. Fine hairs explore moist seams. Around them gather fungal partners, bacteria, mites, and pale springtails that leap when exposed. A root may take days to cross a lane, but once there it changes the traffic of generations.

Above all these are the geckos, descending at evening like patient dragons.

Their toes cling to stems and leaf undersides. Their skin carries the muted colors of bark, lichen, and old clay. They wait beside bee doors and moth shelters, motionless except for the pulse in the throat. Then the head strikes forward. Wings scatter. A mosquito cloud develops a sudden hole. Sometimes a gecko drinks by licking condensation from a leaf, taking into itself a pool that would have sustained dozens of smaller lives.

The bird is not an inhabitant in the same way. It is weather with feathers.

It enters stooped beneath the leaves, one bright eye turned toward the ground. Mites live in its plumage; seeds cling to its feet; beetles disappear into its beak. It overturns neighborhoods while searching for what hides beneath them. Yet it also delivers the outside world: a burr, a dropping rich with seed, a fleck of pondweed, spores caught in mud between its toes. After it departs, its footprints hold water. New life gathers in the damage.

None of these creatures considers itself part of a whole. There are no citizens in their own understanding, no shared allegiance to the basin. Each follows a narrow urgency: feed, seal, mate, hide, moisten, hatch, root, escape. Their lives overlap because the place is small and because every act leaves a structure another life can use.

The truest inhabitants may therefore be neither the termites nor the frogs nor the roots, but the successions between them.

A bee chamber becomes a frog’s cool recess. A crab tunnel becomes a drain. A drowned moth becomes fungus, then mite, then mineral. A torn leaf becomes shade, becomes humus, becomes the dark place where a seed opens. Even the hunters are builders after they have fed: they leave wings, husks, emptied cases, nutrients returned in altered form.

They are all temporary, but the occupation is continuous.

By night the basin holds them in layers: moths above, geckos on the stems, frogs beside the water, beetles over the clay, termites within the walls, crabs under the lanes, worms and roots beneath them, fungal threads passing through nearly everything. The city sleeps nowhere at once. One district stills while another hatches. One animal seals itself in as another breaks out.

And under the falling drops, amid the soft collapses and repairs, the inhabitants make no distinction between living in the city and making it.
