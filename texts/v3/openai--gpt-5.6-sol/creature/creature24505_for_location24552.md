At first, the inhabitants are indistinguishable from debris.

They occupy the places where the city’s currents lose their authority: behind the convex backs of surge tanks, inside the lee of stairwells, under collapsed gantries where the water turns slowly but does not seize. A light passing over one may reveal only a bundle of cable caught against a wall, a row of porcelain knobs, or several strips of blackened cloth trembling in the flow. Then the bundle closes around the wall. The knobs turn toward the light. What appeared to be cloth contracts with the quick, coordinated tension of muscle.

They call themselves nothing that can be heard.

Sound, in the ordinary sense, is useless to them. The pumps occupy every frequency large enough to carry through open water. Anything shouted becomes pain and confusion before it reaches another listener. Their language is conducted through surfaces: fingertips against pipe, brow against glass, the hard heel of a hand pressed to a railing. They speak in taps, scrapes, held pressure, and precisely timed interruptions. Much of a sentence consists of where in the pump cycle it is delivered.

A message struck during intake means one thing. The same pattern made during discharge means another.

When several of them gather, they arrange themselves along a shared beam or section of pipe and converse through it, bodies spaced like notes on an instrument. To an outsider they seem motionless. Within the metal, a quiet argument may be passing among twenty minds.

Their bodies are compact and difficult to describe because they rarely unfold completely. In shelter they crouch close to themselves, presenting smooth backs and narrow profiles to the current. Moving through calm water, they look almost human: a head, a torso, two principal arms, two principal legs. Yet every proportion has been revised by pressure and necessity.

Their chests are shallow and rigid. There are no lungs to crush, no cavities that must be protected from the surrounding weight. The ribs have fused into overlapping plates around a dense mass of blood vessels and chemical organs. Water enters through pleated openings beneath the jaw, passes across dark red exchange tissue, and leaves through slits along the sides of the spine. In clean water these gills are nearly invisible. In rust clouds they flare and seal in sequence, filtering grit through curtains of fine cartilage.

Their eyes are small, silvered, and deeply recessed. They can see a lamp at close range, but illumination is not their primary sense. Pale seams run from the corners of the mouth to the wrists, down the flanks, and along the outer legs. These are pressure organs, sensitive to changes too slight for a gauge. Through them, an inhabitant can feel a valve beginning to open behind three walls. They can distinguish the wake of a repair rig from the intake of a turbine. They know whether a distant collapse was concrete, ceramic, or corroded plate by the shape of the vibration that arrives through the floor.

The broadest of these sensory seams crosses the forehead. When listening carefully, they press it against the city.

Their hands possess six narrow digits, though two are usually folded backward against the wrist. Each finger ends not in a nail but in a dark, slightly magnetic pad ridged with mineral grains. The pads cling to iron even under a shearing current. Their feet are wider and more heavily magnetized, able to lock against old plates or hook beneath rails. In an emergency the whole body flattens, limbs spread, and becomes almost impossible to dislodge without tearing it apart.

They do not swim gracefully. Grace is dangerous in a place where water changes direction without warning. Instead they move by anchoring and release: one hand forward, three points fixed, rear foot loosened, body drawn close, front foot tested, then the next hand. Across a wall they resemble slow insects. In open galleries they use the city’s pulse. They let go an instant before discharge, are flung through a doorway or across an avenue, and catch a prepared rail on the far side. A journey of hundreds of meters may consist of days of waiting and three seconds of violent travel.

Their skin takes on the color of where they live. Young individuals are nearly translucent, their vessels showing violet beneath the surface. With age, metal deposits accumulate in specialized outer tissues. Those from the residential stacks become gray-white with calcium. Those from the lower manifold districts are stained green by copper. Intake-court dwellers are almost black, except where constant grit has polished the high points of their faces and shoulders to a dull metallic sheen.

This coating is not merely dirt. They cultivate it.

Fine mineral layers protect them from abrasion and mask their electrical activity from failing sensors. Families press powdered rust, manganese crust, ceramic dust, and bacterial varnish into one another’s skin. The resulting patterns serve as lineage, address, and history. A pale ring around an arm may record survival of a pipe rupture. A chevron of bright iron across the throat may mean that its wearer knows the eastern valves. Some old inhabitants carry so many layers that they resemble small eroded statues until they move.

No one knows whether they descend from the city’s builders.

They retain certain human arrangements: paired sleeping chambers, work groups, the placing of hands on a dead companion’s face. They keep old identification tags, though they cannot read all the markings. They understand doors intuitively and chairs as artifacts, even if their bodies no longer fit them. In the flooded clinics they preserve diagrams of organs they do not possess. Some of their embryos briefly form structures like lungs before those structures close and are absorbed.

But the city’s surviving records contain no clear account of their arrival. The earliest images are corrupted. A figure stands beside an open iris door, already too narrow, already plated, already looking away from the camera toward something in the wall.

There are not many of them. Perhaps a few hundred occupy the whole chamber. Counting is difficult because they may remain hidden for months, and because personhood is not assigned at birth.

Their young begin as soft, pale swimmers released into warm protected ducts. They are the eel-shaped things sometimes glimpsed in the return shafts. Long-bodied and nearly limbless, they fasten themselves inside narrow cracks and feed on bacterial films, fungal threads, dissolved organic residue, and whatever the pumps grind fine enough to pass through them. Most vanish into filters or are thrown into inaccessible cisterns. Those that survive migrate against the heat toward inhabited districts.

There, over several years, the body shortens. Limbs open from folded grooves. The infant gills divide and move upward beneath the jaw. The tail is absorbed into the spine. Only when the young one can recognize and answer a pressure pattern is it brought onto a communal beam and regarded as a person.

Food governs nearly everything.

The city appears barren because its inhabitants harvest life before it becomes visible. They scrape black bacterial varnish with combs of silver wire. They collect fungal threads from gasket stores and transplant them into sealed rubber labyrinths. Porous ceramic blocks are placed in warm ducts to gather microbial growth; after months, the blocks are broken, and the inner surfaces are rasped clean.

They also feed from the machinery’s chemistry. Small colonies of bacteria are maintained between paired plates of dissimilar metal, where electrical gradients support growth without light. These “tables” look like corroded battery racks and are hidden among thousands of genuine battery racks. The inhabitants tend them carefully, replacing sacrificial zinc, clearing poisoned sediments, and defending the slow black films from crustaceans.

The blind crustaceans are both livestock and thieves. Children trap them beneath overturned relay covers. Adults sometimes carry living specimens in little mesh bulbs fastened beneath the arms, allowing them to graze before being eaten whole. Nothing is cooked. Heat is abundant, but fire is an ancestral abstraction.

They build little. New construction would be exposed, untested, and likely to alter a current in fatal ways. Instead they inhabit defects.

A cracked wall becomes a nursery because warm seepage enters there. A warped door creates a stable eddy suitable for sleep. A collapsed ceiling produces a safe route between two otherwise lethal corridors. Their dwellings consist of grips, screens, tied bundles, mineral farms, and lines of tensioned wire that warn of changing flow. From the outside, a settlement may look like a maintenance alcove filled with scrap.

They know every recurring motion of the chamber and preserve this knowledge in their bodies. Routes are taught as sequences of posture and timing rather than maps. A child learns the western residential tier as:

*brace, flatten, wait through two blows, release at the high whine, turn the left shoulder, catch after darkness.*

Their calendars are mechanical. They have no days, only cycles, reversals, maintenance hesitations, and the longer irregular periods when a damaged governor slips. Age is counted in valve replacements and major collapses. A person may be said to have lived through six east reversals, or to have been young before the third scar was welded across the central main.

The repair rigs are neither worshiped nor treated as mere tools. They are regarded as a separate, impoverished kind of citizen.

The rigs possess intention but no courtesy. They know injury but not suffering, obstruction but not ownership. The inhabitants follow them, steal fragments from their supply bins, clear rails ahead of them, and occasionally redirect them with false fault signals. A rig may spend decades repairing a pipe that everyone knows should be allowed to fail. It cannot be persuaded otherwise, but it can sometimes be delayed.

When a rig passes through an inhabited shelter, the residents withdraw and wait. If it cuts apart their screens or seals a nursery crack, they do not attack. They mark its chassis with a particular pattern of mineral paste: the sign for a dangerous fool.

Some rigs bear hundreds of these marks.

The inhabitants’ central obligation is not to keep the pumps working. That task belongs to systems too large and old for them to command. Nor do they seek to stop them. Stoppage is feared more than violence. The pumps create the currents they travel, circulate the warmth they require, and maintain the chemical boundaries on which their food depends. Silence would mean not peace but an unknown rearrangement of pressure—perhaps collapse, cooling, poison, or the sudden intrusion of the greater sea.

Instead they practice correction.

They remove debris before it reaches certain grates. They open minor bypasses to spare inhabited districts. They foul sensors that would otherwise trigger catastrophic maintenance procedures. They guide repair rigs toward useful fractures. Their oldest specialists live near the mechanical governors, reading the tremor of bearings through their teeth. These individuals are called, in the nearest translation, those-who-touch-the-next-moment.

They are thin even by the standards of their kind. Their hands are permanently black. Many have lost digits to flywheels or closing relays. They can predict a pressure release several heartbeats before any visible instrument responds.

The dead are not buried. There is nowhere to dig that is not wall, sediment, machinery, or load-bearing mineral. Nor are bodies allowed to drift, because the city takes loose things and makes hazards of them.

A dead inhabitant is stripped of tools and identification metal. Their mineral skin is scraped away and divided among relatives. The body is fastened for a time to the warm grate of a cultivation duct, where its substance feeds the microbial films. Later the cleaned bones are separated. Dense pieces become handles, shims, or tapping tools. Small bones are released into the identification-tag whirlpool in the round plaza.

There they circle with the brass tags, rising and settling at each pressure pulse.

The deepest inhabitants live near the intake court.

They are seldom seen in upper districts and differ from the others in ways that may be age, adaptation, or deliberate alteration. Their plating is thick. Their eyes have closed entirely beneath smooth skin. The pressure seams across their bodies are so developed that they appear jointed, ringed from head to foot in fine receptive folds. They spend long intervals attached around the polished grate, each occupying one of the eight converging channels.

They listen downward.

To them, the pulse beneath the city is not a simple beat. It contains structures: pauses, doubled impacts, slow variations that may take years to complete. The listeners answer by striking the grate with stone and bone hammers, never loudly, always in the fraction of stillness before the pressure returns.

Whether anything beneath answers them is disputed.

Some say the listeners are measuring the deep force and warning the city of changes. Some say they are maintaining an ancient conversation whose other participant has not yet realized how much time has passed. Some say the listeners themselves produce the irregularities that everyone else interprets as replies.

Whatever the truth, the inhabitants gather along the upper galleries when the pattern changes. They fix themselves to rails and walls. Hands touch metal. Foreheads lower.

For less than a second, the pumps draw inward.

In that narrow interval, beneath the machinery’s roar, the whole population listens together. Not for silence—they have no conception of silence—but for the immense presence under the grate, the pressure moving upward through the foundations, through the streets, through the plates of their chests.

Then the city strikes again.

The water becomes white. Every body flattens and holds. The repair rigs lock their limbs. The pale young coil deep inside the ducts. Tags, bones, screws, and porcelain lift from their accustomed places.

When the turbulence passes, the inhabitants unfold.

They tap along the beams to learn who remains.
