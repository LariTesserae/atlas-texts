At first, what lives here seems to be the water.

It has bodies in every layer of the mountains: clear threads hurrying over stone, warm reservoirs sealed in darkness, clouds grazing the canopy, droplets stored in leaf cups, vapor breathing through vents. It disappears into one organism and issues from another. Almost every life in the mountains is, in some fashion, a vessel for its circulation.

The smallest inhabitants live where heat and mineral meet. Around the springs are slick, many-colored films that appear oily from a distance but resolve, close at hand, into tiered colonies. Gold cells occupy the almost-scalding center. Rust-red feeders spread where iron precipitates. Green-black veils grow at the cooler margins, harvesting both dim light and sulfurous chemistry. They build delicate terraces from their own mineral waste, narrowing old channels and diverting springs toward new ones. A vent field may change its pattern over decades because these minute lives have slowly replumbed it.

Under translucent lichen scales, other colonies retain single beads of rain for days. They endure naked rock, acid seepage, and sudden heating, and they prepare the stone for everything larger. When basalt splits, they enter first. Moss follows them; ferns follow moss; roots follow the ferns. The forests begin with organisms too small to cast shadows.

Fungi are nearly as pervasive as water. Their visible caps are temporary things: waxy cups on wet branches, black fans protruding from fallen trunks, pale fingers that emerge after thunderstorms and dissolve by noon. Their greater bodies lie beneath the ground, binding the saturated soil into a resilient skin. Some conduct moisture across surprisingly long distances. Some surround hot fissures in dense insulating sleeves. Some grow into roots and trade dissolved minerals for sugars. On certain nights, networks close to the surface shine dimly blue, outlining buried wood, animal tunnels, and old channels no stream has used for centuries.

The plants do not merely tolerate the mountains’ restless cycles. They anticipate them.

The great lowland trees possess broad buttresses filled with spongy tissue. During rain these tissues swell, storing water faster than the roots can send it upward. During dry intervals they contract, pushing moisture into the trunk. Their leaves end in narrow gutters. Their flowers hang beneath branches, protected from hard rain, and are pollinated not by hovering insects but by small climbing animals that can brace themselves against wet bark.

Higher-valley trees are shorter and more communal. Their roots interlace so thoroughly that an individual is difficult to define. A trunk cracked by wind may remain alive through its neighbors. Water entering one end of a grove can emerge from leaves far upslope. The whole thicket responds to pressure changes: pores open in successive bands, crowns darken with gathering moisture, and then the manufactured rain begins. The first drops knock loose sleeping insects. The second wave fills the bromeliads. By the time the ground is wet, the upper leaves are already closing again.

Among these groves stand the iron trees. Their bark is charcoal-black and ridged into channels. Metallic compounds accumulate in the outer wood and in a conductive ribbon of sap running down each trunk. The trees grow tallest on exposed crests, though lightning repeatedly blasts away their upper limbs. A struck crown does not burn readily. Steam bursts from the bark; current descends; the buried root mesh distributes it through wet ground.

Living beneath the bark are colonies of thread-thin, pallid organisms—part fungus, part something more like a simple colonial animal. An electrical pulse causes chemical chambers along their bodies to flash. The glow races downward in branching lines, sometimes continuing underground after the visible tree has gone dark. They consume resin altered by heat and may help seal the lightning wounds. Their light is not a signal in any deliberate sense, but many creatures have learned to read it.

Within seconds of a strike, shield-backed beetles crawl from dry hollows. Their bodies are layered with insulating wax, and the feet of their front legs are broad enough to cross hot resin without sinking. They feed first on softened sap, then on the pale lightning colonies exposed by split bark. Their larvae develop in abandoned strike channels. When they emerge, nearby flowers open in response to the same electrical disturbance, and the beetles leave the damaged tree powdered with luminous pollen.

Elsewhere, smaller insects keep time by stone temperature. Clicking colonies inhabit hollow reeds around warm pools. No one colony calls continuously. A few individuals begin when cooling rain changes the tension of the reed wall; another colony across the basin answers at a slightly lower pitch; then another replies from farther upslope. The sequence can travel for kilometers. It is courtship, territorial spacing, and weather measurement at once. A sudden silence often precedes a pressure release underground.

The frogs hear it through their feet.

They are broad-bodied animals with little need for powerful leaps. Their toes spread into fleshy disks, and the underside of the belly is thin enough to sense vibrations through mud and roots. Many spend daylight pressed into saturated moss, indistinguishable from it except when the throat inflates. Their calls are low, more tremor than sound. Males gather around hidden pools after rain and pulse the ground in overlapping rhythms. Eggs are deposited not in open water but inside the water bladders of plants. The tadpoles feed on trapped insects and, when the pool begins to sour, stimulate the host plant to drain it by rasping at sensitive hairs near the base.

The plants benefit. Empty bladders do not rot, and the young frogs leave behind nitrogen-rich waste. In return, the frogs receive nurseries safe from the larger water hunters.

The middle canopy belongs to nimble, warm-blooded climbers with narrow faces and four grasping hands. They are not quite monkeys in their construction: the hind limbs rotate farther outward, the spine is stiff, and a muscular fold along the flanks lets them flatten against branches when storms shake the crowns. Their fur grows in sparse, backward-pointing ridges that channel rain toward the mouth. They drink by lowering their heads while water runs along the grooves beside their eyes.

They live in small, quiet groups. Abundance has not made them noisy; sound carries too unpredictably among cliffs and vapor. They communicate by tapping branches, touching cheek glands to leaves, and exposing patches of pale skin on the wrists. They feed on young foliage, flower bases, beetle grubs, and the miniature communities inside bromeliad cups. Before drinking, one stirs the cup with a finger. This brings biting larvae to the surface, where they can be picked out and eaten.

Their young cling beneath the chest, protected from rain by the adult’s body. At rest, several animals may braid their limbs around the same bough. A passing observer could look directly at them and see only knots, wet fur, and lichen until one dark eye opened.

The most conspicuous canopy animals are the gliders. They are reptiles only in the broadest sense: long-ribbed, heat-loving creatures with membranes stretched from shoulder to ankle and tails flattened into rudders. Their display skins contain movable crystals. Folded against bark, the animals are mottled gray-green. In flight, tension spreads the crystals and the membranes flare scarlet, violet, or yellow. They launch after the valley’s manufactured rain, when wet leaves release clouds of insects.

A glider does not simply fall from tree to tree. Warm vapor rising from fissures can hold it aloft. The animal circles inside a narrow column, gaining height with its limbs extended, then spills outward across a ravine like a bright leaf. Males contest the most reliable columns without touching, each trying to occupy the lift above the other. The loser is forced into cooler air and must land lower on the slope.

On the forest floor move the long-limbed browsers. They are tall enough to pass over low thickets yet light enough not to break the root mat. Each foot divides into several padded toes that spread under weight. The forelimbs are longer than the hind limbs, giving the animal a slow, deliberate gait and allowing it to reach down steep banks without kneeling. Its hide is nearly hairless, dark, and perpetually slick with a secretion that discourages fungi.

The head is small and flexible-lipped. Mineral crusts are as important to it as leaves. Browsers visit spring terraces in habitual order, waiting for the hottest surfaces to cool after a discharge, then scraping fresh deposits with layered teeth. Their teeth are stained orange and black. They carry mineral-loving microbes in deep chambers of the gut, and their droppings seed new feeding sites along the paths between springs.

Though they travel alone or in pairs, their routes collectively maintain open corridors through the undergrowth. Many smaller species depend on these corridors. Seedlings germinate in the shallow hoofprints. Frogs occupy the rainwater collected there. Predators use them as well.

One such predator is known mostly by its absence from sight.

It leaves broad oval impressions without obvious toe marks because its feet are wrapped in flexible pads. It climbs warm trunks, digging paired scratches high into the bark, then lies along horizontal limbs above browser paths. Its body is low and heavy, its skin covered not in fur but in dense, velvety filaments that shed water and break up its outline. Heat-sensitive pits line the jaw. In a forest where foliage conceals shape, it hunts the warmth moving behind the leaves.

It can remain still for most of a day. Metabolism slows; the filaments darken; moss beetles may begin to crawl across its back. When it attacks, there is no prolonged chase. It drops, grips, and uses its weight. Failed strikes are not pursued. Heat makes extravagance costly.

It sometimes scratches trees for reasons beyond sharpening claws. Glands between its toes leave a waxy mark enriched with salts. Other individuals investigate these marks by pressing the roof of the mouth to the bark. The scratches therefore form a scattered, slowly changing map of occupied valleys, though no animal ever sees the map whole.

The rivers have their own heavy hunter.

It resembles a length of waterlogged root beneath floating fern. Only the nostrils and small, lidless eyes break the surface. Its back supports shallow grooves in which spores, algae, and fragments of living fern lodge. Older individuals carry entire gardens. This camouflage is not passive: capillaries near the skin warm the growth slightly, encouraging the plants to spread.

The hunter anchors itself with a broad tail and waits where fish shelter from the current. When prey comes near, the throat expands abruptly, drawing in water and fish together. It can also take frogs, fallen gliders, and young browsers crossing narrow channels. In mineral-rich streams its teeth are continually coated with fresh deposits, making them blunt-looking but extremely hard.

Most river fish are squat and muscular. They have enlarged fins with rough undersides, allowing them to cling behind stones. Some graze orange microbial growth. Others wait facing upstream with mouths open. Fruit, larvae, beetles stunned by rain, and scraps from canopy feeding arrive without warning. During spring surges, the fish retreat into cracks and seal themselves there by inflating soft tissue around the head.

In black pools where decay consumes the oxygen, stranger inhabitants graze the sediment. They are blind, translucent, and slow, with branching organs exposed along their flanks. Symbiotic microbes in these organs use iron and sulfur from the mud. The animals sweep their many-lobed mouths through the boundary between water and sediment, swallowing rot and leaving behind pale, oxidized trails. Bubbles rising from the pool often carry their eggs, which adhere to floating leaves and later drop into new water.

Above all this circle the ridge-soarers.

Their wings are too broad for easy movement inside the forest and too delicate for repeated flapping. They live by the mountains’ upward breath. Long sensory vanes along the leading edge detect small changes in temperature and pressure, allowing them to find columns of geothermal air even through cloud. Once in a column, they may remain aloft for hours, making minute corrections with split tail fans.

Their eyes are adapted less to color than to interruptions in texture: a glider launching, a browser parting reeds, a canopy climber crossing an exposed branch. They nest on warm cliff shelves behind curtains of falling water. The constant spray keeps crawling parasites away, while heat from the stone incubates the eggs. Adults bring food infrequently. The young survive long intervals by lowering their body temperature and drinking condensation from the nest wall.

When a soarer’s shadow crosses the canopy, the reaction descends through layers. Gliders fold flat. Climbers freeze against branches. Clicking insects stop. Frogs press into mud. Fish dart beneath stones, responding not to the shadow itself but to falling insects and the alarm vibrations of the banks. For a moment the mountain seems to hold its breath.

Then circulation resumes.

There are no true scavengers because almost everything scavenges. Beetles drink from wounds. Browsers chew shed antler and softened bone. Fish take drowned nestlings. Fungi enter a carcass before its heat has left it. Pale crustaceans emerge from burrows at night and cut fallen leaves into manageable pieces, carrying them belowground in files. Their burrows admit air to the root mat and drain pools that would otherwise become stagnant.

Death here is less an ending than a sudden loss of boundaries. A fallen browser becomes heat, fungus, beetle larvae, mineral salts, and a dark patch of accelerated growth. A dead iron tree may continue conducting storm charge through its wet trunk for years. A ridge-soarer’s wing, caught in the canopy, is dismantled strand by strand until only the stiff leading bone remains, thickly furred with moss.

No creature governs the mountains. No species stands outside the exchange long enough to master it. Each is a temporary concentration: of water, of mineral, of charge, of heat.

Yet taken together, the inhabitants seem almost to form a greater animal.

The rivers are its descending vessels. The warm aquifers are its deep organs. Root networks lift water as though by muscular contraction. Lightning travels through iron groves like nervous impulse. Insects mark shifts in pressure; frogs answer through the ground; flowers open along the path of charge. Fungi stitch wounds closed. Browsers keep passages open. Predators prevent abundance from congealing into excess. The soaring animals read the mountain’s breath.

This greater creature has no skin and no single awareness. It does not know itself. Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, take the form of storms, migrations, bloomings, pressure pulses, and slow alterations in the courses of streams. It is ancient without being permanent, orderly without intention, and ceaselessly active beneath its prevailing quiet.

Everything that lives there is part resident, part conduit. Nothing merely occupies the mountains.

The mountains pass through it.
