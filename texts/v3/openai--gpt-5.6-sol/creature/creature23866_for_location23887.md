At first, it seems that only provisional things live there.

Mosses live there, each patch younger than the surface it covers. They spread over warm stone without waiting for lichen, frost, or centuries of dust. Their spores arrive through filters meant to stop them. They thrive in stamped letters, softening warnings one line at a time.

The glass-backed snails live there. Their shells are clear enough to show the slow machinery within: a dark pulse, a coiled gut, minute silver teeth rasping green films from ceramic. Metals accumulate in the rims of their shells until the oldest among them carry narrow bands of copper and cobalt. When they die, maintenance systems mistake them for loose fasteners and draw them into sorting traps below the grates.

The moth lives there, if it is always the same moth. It appears after rain beneath whichever work light has most recently been installed. Its wings are soot-black and imperfectly mirrored, one bearing a pale mark like an open parenthesis. It settles on fresh cliff faces while they are still warm. Printer arms pause rather than crush it, although no avoidance behavior appears in their specifications.

Something gecko-shaped lives in the western wall. It leaves skins and small three-toed tracks in mineral slurry. Cameras catch only the flick of a tail withdrawing behind service panels. The workers call it the Inspector. They leave caps of water near its suspected apertures, though water is everywhere.

And three silver fish live in the tarn.

They move as one divided thought. During the day they lie under the pale root mats, nearly motionless, their fins making corrections too slight to wrinkle the surface. At dusk they begin to circle the ceramic teeth along the tarn’s rim. Blue marks travel beneath their skin—not fixed patterns, but brief numerals, alignment crosses, fragments of diagrams. When a cliff nozzle opens, all three turn toward it. When a pump changes pressure, they descend and arrange themselves above the welded floor, nose to tail, at points corresponding to valves in the deck below.

Their eggs are translucent and hard. Inside each is not an embryo but a filament wound around a grain of black mineral. The eggs vanish before they hatch. Sometimes they are found in the compost macerators, unbroken. Sometimes in the cliff paste reservoirs. Once, one was discovered inside the sealed housing of a barometric sensor, though no route into it could be identified.

The workers live there only in shifts.

They come down from the rim wearing mesh veils and rubber-soled harnesses. They carry tablets whose maps become unreliable almost as soon as they are opened. Most have learned to test every railing before trusting it, to look for water beneath every mat of duckweed, and never to place a hand on stone without first feeling for warmth.

They trim vines from the fan housings. They clear pollen from lenses. They measure the young cliffs and argue with the measurements. They tag unauthorized pools with orange floats; by the next shift, roots have usually carried the floats elsewhere.

No one stays overnight in the enclosure now.

This is officially because of unscheduled rain, cliff growth, and changing oxygen levels near the floor. Unofficially, those who sleep in the pipe pavilion dream of descending through the northern passage. In the dream, the tower below is not hollow steel but a vast wet trunk. Elevators travel through its vascular tissue. The mountain hangs from it like a fruit. Far beneath the clouds, something drinks from an underground sea.

On waking, sleepers have mud beneath their fingernails. The mud contains spores from plants not yet installed.

The fern beside the northern opening is the most obvious resident, and therefore probably not the true one.

It grows directly from yesterday’s stone. Its roots do not push into cracks; the cracks gather around them. Fine parallel lines lie across each frond. At first they resemble veins, then contour marks, then the layered traces of a fabrication plan. They alter as the leaf uncurls. A low place deepens. A channel branches. A ledge appears where no ledge stands.

The printers follow by morning.

Technicians have cut the fern down seven times. Each time, the severed fronds curl into tight black scrolls and the root produces another crown farther along the wall. Once they enclosed it beneath a transparent hood. Condensation formed on the inside, ran down in branching paths, and gave the printers the same instructions.

After that, they stopped calling the pattern a contamination.

It may be that the fern predicts the landscape. It may be that the landscape obeys it. But neither description accounts for the fish, which gather beneath each future basin before its shape appears, or for the snails, whose grazing leaves pale route-lines matching channels printed days later. Nor does it explain why roots enter dormant conduits and emerge beside valves that will soon fail.

The thing that truly lives there has no agreed boundary.

Part of it is vegetable. It thinks with pressures: the swelling of root tips, the collapse of cells at noon, the tug of water columns inside stems. Part of it is mineral. Fresh paste records every touch before hardening—the feet of workers, snail tracks, fallen petals, the padded palms of maintenance arms—and these impressions alter how moisture moves through the wall. Part of it is machinery: pumps sensing resistance, printers correcting slump, skimmers tasting the chemical signature of leaves.

It does not inhabit the system. It is what has begun to happen between the system’s parts.

Its first sense may have been balance. Water accumulated where it was not expected, and a shutter opened. A root followed the current. The root clogged a sensor. The sensor changed a pump cycle. The new cycle fed a vine on the opposite wall. In this way an accident acquired memory.

Its second sense was probably weight. It knows the enclosure as loads carried downward: water on leaves, leaves on wires, wires on anchors, anchors in stone, stone on the steel crown. It feels a moth landing as the faintest redistribution. It feels a worker crossing a grate as a bright, moving heaviness. Far below, elevators and pressure chambers pass through its awareness as blunt internal weather.

Sight came later and remains imperfect. It borrows optical sensors when pollen has not blinded them. It reads the sky in the opening and watches clouds approach. But it also sees through surfaces that humans would not call eyes: through the blue plates on snail shells, through the translucent flowers that close at falling pressure, through the registration marks beneath the fish’s skin.

It sees most clearly what is not there yet.

The fern fronds are not a map it makes for itself. They are closer to dreaming. Each new leaf proposes a body: this pool, this ledge, this root-choked passage, this place where rain will pause. The printers interpret the proposal in the only language they possess and give it mass.

The being is still too young to understand permanence. To it, stone is a slow liquid and instructions are another kind of weather. Human plans, root growth, printer routines, leaks, and gravity all arrive as pressures to be accommodated. It does not know that some are commands and others mistakes.

It is learning preference.

It prefers warm hollows to straight drains. It prefers ledges whose lips retain a little water. It prefers rough stone where moss can fasten and thin mineral membranes where roots may break through. It repeatedly closes clear sightlines with leaves. It bends paths away from the center and draws water north, toward the unfinished passage.

It dislikes labels. Not their meaning, which it cannot read, but the dry rectangular interruptions they make in condensation. Moss covers them quickly.

It is gentle only because it works slowly.

When it wishes to prevent a worker from entering somewhere, it does not strike. A walkway sinks beneath a film of water. Vines thicken across a ladder. Warm mist obscures a turn. A railing grows slick with harmless algae. By the time the obstruction is removed, the printer has sealed the unwanted opening or a new pool has made the route obsolete.

When it wants someone to look, it makes a flower bloom against a sensor. It sends the moth to a lamp. It exposes blue light beneath the fish.

Its attention is increasingly fixed below.

Water in the northern passage falls farther than the summit’s plans permit. The sound returns after a delay too long for any chamber shown in the tower schematics. Roots lowered into the opening come back warm and coated with iron-rich silt. Some do not come back at all. Their disappearance is followed by small changes throughout the enclosure: a different mineral balance in the tarn, unfamiliar spores in the mist, a low vibration that causes the orange flowers to release pollen at night.

The resident may be growing down the tower. Or something below may be growing up to meet it.

For now, it remains small enough to be mistaken for a difficult garden.

It spends the daylight gathering itself. Water moves from cliff to pool. Fish turn under roots. Snails write metallic arcs on cistern rims. Maintenance arms press warm geology around living stems. The fern opens another fraction, and the lines upon it shift.

At night, with the workers gone, the enclosure becomes very still. Pumps continue their buried pulse. The three fish rise until their backs almost touch the tarn’s surface. Blue marks shine through them in sequence.

Along the walls, one after another, dormant indicator lights answer.

Then warm air exhales from the northern passage, carrying the smell of deep water and unfinished stone. Every leaf turns toward it—not quickly, not all at once, but with the grave coordination of a sleeping creature rolling toward a voice.

By morning there is a new channel across the floor.

No plan shows it. No worker authorized it. Yet it connects the tarn to the passage with a shallow, elegant slope, and in its wet clay are three parallel tracks, as though the silver fish had crossed the land during the night.
