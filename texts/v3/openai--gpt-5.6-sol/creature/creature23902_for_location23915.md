The people of the basin are easy to mistake for a solemn people. Visitors—if visitors ever came—would first notice their pauses, their lowered voices, the way a whole market can conduct its business with the sound of rain on cloth. They would see faces turned attentively toward tiny noises: a wooden birdcall from an orchard, the catch of a gate in its stone socket, a foot placed too heavily on a stair.

But they are not solemn. They are careful.

Laughter is common, though it is usually breathed through the nose or released in a series of soft clicks against the teeth. Jokes are made with altered knots, reversed sandals, a plum hidden beneath an empty bowl. At festivals, people paint expressions on flat paddles and hold them before their faces: astonishment, greed, courtship, mock despair. Some comic masks are centuries old, if the genealogies are correct. Their paint remains bright. Their jokes do not.

The basin’s people are compactly built, broad through the feet and hands, with strong shoulders from carrying baskets, turning mills, drawing sledges, and controlling pack lizards on wet stone. Their skin ranges from the pale brown of barley flour to the deep gray-brown of rain-darkened wood. Black hair is most common, usually worn in thick braids close to the scalp, though some have hair the dull silver of riverside leaves from childhood onward. No one treats silver hair as proof of age.

Age is read elsewhere, and imperfectly. In the steadiness with which someone sets a wedge. In how many verses of a genealogy they can continue without consulting a tablet. In whether they remember the north bathhouse before its third doorway was sealed, or only after. A person may have an unlined face and be called grandmother by half a district. Another may stoop, tremble, and remain recognizably the same through the childhoods of six generations.

They dress in layered felt, woven grass fiber, wool, and soft hide. Garments are wrapped rather than closely fitted, fastened with bone pins or small green copper clasps. Most are dyed in mineral colors: charcoal, blue-black, lichen yellow, rain-cloud white. Bright dyes are reserved for edges, where a narrow line of red or violet can be removed and replaced without remaking the garment. Sleeves are often mismatched deliberately. Perfect symmetry is thought to invite correction.

Almost everyone carries cord.

There are cords for prices, household obligations, kinship, irrigation turns, promises, apologies, and stories too dangerous to speak near an echoing cliff. A market cord may hold three large knots for baskets of grain, a loop for transport, and a bead indicating that payment can wait until after the rain. A courtship cord is less standardized. It may be plain for most of its length and end in a tangle that only two people understand.

Their spoken language is full of breath consonants, tongue taps, and short vowels that do not travel far. Names tend to be brief in public and long in the home. A woman called Tem at the ovens may have a household name describing the place where her mother first felt her move, the smell of wet juniper that day, and a white bird standing backward in the river. Such names change as relationships change, though the public syllable remains.

They have no single gesture for farewell. Departures within the basin are rarely final, and distance does not conceal much. Instead, the person leaving shows an open palm, then closes it slowly: I will be absent from reach. Those remaining touch two fingers to the ground: This place retains you.

Their houses are not occupied by families so much as by continuities. A household may include blood relatives, former spouses, adopted adults, children assigned after a death, apprentices, an animal keeper, and one person whose formal duty is to remember who slept under that roof before any of them. Property belongs partly to the living and partly to the arrangement itself. A courtyard must have someone to sweep it, a roof vent someone to open, a tree someone to prune. If an entire household line ends, neighbors move inward until all its duties have names again.

Birth is awaited with affection and unease.

A pregnant person is never asked when the child will come. Instead, others ask what has loosened. Somewhere in the basin, near every birth, something established is expected to give way: a deer vanishes from a familiar grazing place; an old person ceases to wake; a vine fails to flower; a fish trap is found empty though the pale fish remain beneath it. These correspondences are recorded, but no one claims to understand them.

Newborns are introduced first to water, then to stone, then to an echo. A drop from the household cistern is placed on the tongue. One foot is pressed against the oldest wall of the home. Finally, a parent makes the child’s public syllable quietly beneath a bridge. If the echo returns cleanly, the name is kept. If it blurs, another is chosen.

Children are the basin’s loudest inhabitants, despite all training. They are fascinated by the prohibition against shouting eastward. They dare one another to whistle into drainage holes and speak secrets into jars. They build towers outside the established districts just to find them undone in the morning. Some take pride in constructing elaborate walls for the basin to unmake: arches balanced on pebbles, miniature palaces of slate, whole armies of clay figures set beyond the last irrigation channel. At dawn they race out and judge the quality of the erasure.

The youngest children often think the basin is playing with them.

Many adults once thought so too.

Education begins with distinctions: what may be changed, what may be exchanged, what may only be tended, and what must not be tested without witnesses. Children memorize the number of roof stones on public buildings, the allotted branches of orchard trees, the places where moss stops. They learn arithmetic by balancing baskets and law by studying failed alterations. Later they may enter a craft house, a genealogy hall, an animal yard, a water office, or one of the quiet schools attached to the summit scholars.

Craftworkers are valued less for invention than for exactness with variation. A potter cannot increase the number of storage jars without consequence, but may change their painted bands. A weaver cannot produce endless cloth, but may unpick an old border and create a new one from the same thread. Thus their art lives in surfaces, sequences, reversals, and temporary arrangements. Sand pictures are beloved. So are dances, shadow plays, reed music performed beneath heavy awnings, and sculptures assembled without fastening so they can be returned to separate stones before morning.

The city’s musicians are considered daring. A melody changes the air and cannot be retrieved. Most instruments are quiet: thumb harps, bone flutes stopped with wax, skin drums stroked rather than struck. The grandest performances happen during rain, when falling water softens the echoes. For that brief hour, choirs sing aloud.

They sing in overlapping lines that never resolve to unison.

The dead are not buried immediately. Because decay is unreliable, death must first be established by a group called the Listeners. They sit beside the body through one rain and one drying, watching for breath, warmth, condensation on a mirror disc, and certain small changes in the eyes. Only then is the death announced by laying a white board across the nearest named street.

Bodies are carried to clefts above the inhabited districts and placed in woven shrouds beneath slabs. Some remain intact for generations. Others disappear between visits, leaving only cloth, or only a few clean bones, or nothing. The city does not interpret these differences publicly. Privately, everyone does.

There are priests, although they reject that name. They call themselves Keepers of the Unequal Things. Their task is to attend to whatever does not balance: twins born after a single death, a channel yielding an eighth basket of silt, a goat with an unfamiliar marking, a dream shared by people from separate districts. Most irregularities prove to be errors of counting or memory. The rest are written on thin clay leaves and stored unfired, so the records can be dissolved if the irregularity later vanishes.

The Keepers perform no sacrifices. To destroy something intentionally in hope of gaining something else is regarded as both crude and dangerously literal. Their offerings are arrangements: seven plums set around an empty bowl, feathers placed upstream of the birds that shed them, cups filled exactly to the level of the river. By evening, everything is returned.

Not everyone accepts this restraint.

There are people called Openers, though few use the term for themselves. They believe the basin is not balanced but stalled, and that its gentle corrections are signs of injury. They plant beyond the wet terraces. They make unnecessary boats. They leave wedges in living cracks. Some climb to the pale summit and spend nights near the red seed, whispering to it from a distance.

The city tolerates them more than it admits. Every generation produces Openers, and every generation appoints careful citizens to argue with them. Their failed walls teach masonry. Their second leaves are pressed into scholarly books. The cleanly separated planks of their excess boats are gathered and used to repair permitted hulls.

A smaller group believes the opposite: that even pruning, childbirth, and speech are dangerous disturbances. These people live in the wide spaces between districts, tending animals and touching the city lightly. They wear undyed felt and eat from communal ovens but build no permanent houses. Yet their camps recur in the same places, their paths deepen no further, and their refusal itself has become one of the basin’s established forms.

The animals participate without obedience.

The little deer enter courtyards and sleep beside warm compost walls. They are never tame, but certain households recognize particular does by the angles of their horns and maintain records of them alongside human kin. Goats on the cliffs sometimes stand motionless for hours facing the same seams in the rock, as if listening to pressure far within the mountain. The burrowers steal cord, especially cords containing unresolved promises, and line their chambers with them.

Long-tailed orchard birds imitate hand signs with their wings. Their imitations are inaccurate but persistent. One common bird repeatedly makes the ferry signal for unsafe crossing while perched over shallow, motionless water. Children adore it. Ferrymen do not.

The pack lizards are heavy-bodied, warm despite the cold, with pebbled hides in blue-gray and green. Their eyelids close upward. They move slowly until frightened, when they can cross a courtyard with shocking speed and then refuse to move again for the rest of the day. Each bears a painted mark on its harness, never on its skin; marking a living body for ownership is considered an invitation to lose it.

Even insects are counted. There are beetle keepers who patrol gutters after rain and compare the shining backs they find with old painted boards. Moth wings supply no cloth and are too fragile for ornament, so fallen wings are arranged beneath glassy stone and studied for changes in pattern. None have been proven. Several scholars have devoted what may be lifetimes to a single disputed spot.

The pale fish are the least understood. They do not appear to spawn, though young fish are found. They can be caught, and they are eaten, but their number beneath each bridge remains the same. Fishers therefore speak politely before lowering a hook. Not from gratitude, exactly, but because anything that returns in exact measure may also remember.

Above all these lives—the households, herds, moss cushions, orchard birds, silent beetles, scholars, dissenters, and children building doomed walls—the red seed occupies an unusual category.

The people do not call it alive. Nor do they call it dead.

Summit scholars take turns watching it from behind a low barrier of dark stone. They do not touch it. They record its gloss, its color, the precise point at which frost avoids its surface. Each scholar adds one line to the account and copies all previous lines first. Many privately compose another line describing what they imagine will emerge: a red-leaved tree, a flowering vine, a root that splits the mountain, a mouth, a flame, a forest compressed into one wet shell.

Those private lines are never entered into the official record.

In the city below, replicas of the seed are made from clay and worn inside clothing where no one can see them. Openers wear them. Keepers wear them. Expectant parents sometimes hold one during labor. Children leave them in cracks beyond the terraces and return in the morning to see whether the basin has put them back.

The replicas are always only clay.

The true seed remains above them, small enough to close in a fist, attended by birds and weather. Around it lives an entire city that has become expert at continuation without knowing whether continuation is patience, captivity, or a form of love.

And among all the basin’s inhabitants, it may be the only one that is still entirely young.
