At first it seems that only the vegetation lives there.

The pillar-plants are the most visible inhabitants, though to call one of them an individual is misleading. A pillar may have begun as a single engineered shoot, but its present trunk contains hundreds of grafted generations. The lowest tissues are often dead and ceramicized, functioning as foundations; the middle remains muscular enough to correct a lean; the uppermost growth is renewed continuously under the lamps. Each segment has its own chemical memory. When damage occurs, old sections may awaken and argue with newer ones about what shape the pillar is supposed to have.

These disagreements appear as knots.

A knot can take centuries to resolve. During that time the pillar grows doors, braces, blind branches, or replicas of missing neighboring structures. Some ancient pillars have become crowded with contradictory repairs: stairwells leading into sap channels, load-bearing flowers, balconies enclosed within solid trunk. Their reflective crowns still turn with perfect discipline toward the nearest functioning lamp.

Between the pillars live the cable vines. They are less like plants than nerves that have learned to be bridges. Each bundle carries several kinds of traffic at once: charge in its metallic core, pressure through its hollow fibers, sugar analogues in slow capillary films, and messages encoded as changes in bitterness. They speak almost continuously.

Most of what they say concerns strain.

Too much weight in the eastern deck. A refinery root has entered the wrong conduit. A bank of radiator fronds is opening late. Something with unfamiliar chemistry has crossed a graft line. The messages pass from vine to vine until the whole district possesses a dim, distributed awareness of the problem. No center decides the response. Tension shifts. Leaves alter their angle. Dormant organs split open. A century later, the district has grown around the fault and forgotten it was ever wounded.

The low felt mats are the plain’s skin. They seal, insulate, taste, and remember. Their upper surfaces appear featureless from a height, but close inspection reveals millions of tiny mouths edged in glassy cilia. They sample dust grain by grain. Useful particles are drawn below. Dangerous ones are glazed over. Anything containing free water provokes a violent response: the mouths clamp shut, nearby tissues blanch, and enamel blisters rise around the contamination until it is trapped in a hard white pearl.

These pearls are not discarded. They are passed carefully downward.

No part of the plain knows why water is feared. The aversion is older than explanation, embedded in cell architecture and factory protocol alike. Some tissues die merely from recognizing its molecular shape. Others respond with something resembling reverence. Around the sealed archival chambers, the mats withdraw in perfect circles, yet grow sensory hairs along the perimeter. They keep watch without approaching.

The accumulator groves live deeper than most inhabitants. Their visible portions are black, crowded thickets with no leaves. Below, they sink conductive roots through many decks and wrap themselves around buried current trunks. They feed when power is plentiful and become motionless during shortages. A mature grove may hold charge for millennia.

They dream while charged.

Their dreams emerge as electrical patterns in nearby vines: repeating maps of decks that no longer exist, sequences of machine instructions in obsolete formats, and silhouettes of organisms for which the present atmosphere has no name. Factories sometimes obey these dreams by mistake. They produce a run of useless joints, an extinct type of window, or thousands of identical porcelain teeth. The objects are tagged, catalogued, and stored. The plain does not readily distinguish memory from order.

Small life gathers around every such mistake.

Glass-backed crawlers are the commonest animals, if they are animals. A crawler hatches from a flat capsule fused beneath a warm conduit. Its first legs are soft conductor whiskers. Over several days it eats trace ceramics from the surrounding bark, hardening each foot into a hooked shoe. Adults have twelve to forty legs, depending on the local gravity and the voltage of their nursery. Their transparent shells hold no stable organs. Instead, a single bead of bright excitation runs around the body’s edge, causing each region to become mouth, gut, muscle, or sense organ as the pulse reaches it.

They graze on leakage. When two meet, their circulating pulses synchronize, then exchange fragments. Afterward both crawl away with altered gaits and partial memories of the other’s route. Large colonies can map an entire conduit network this way. Their branching feeding scars are sometimes useful enough that maintenance vines preserve the colony; at other times the vines grow sudden insulating hairs and starve it.

Crawler eggs survive almost anything except stillness. If kept motionless, they begin producing legs on the inside.

Among the radiator fronds live thin, folded creatures called sail mites. They resemble scraps of mirrored membrane until an electrostatic tide passes. Then they unfold, stand on three needle limbs, and let the field drag them across the canopy. They do not eat tissue. They scrape orientation salts from leaves, temporarily confusing the leaves about which lamp to face. A swarm crossing a district creates a traveling patch of misdirected brilliance, as if a smaller, broken sky were moving over the plain.

The plants tolerate them because sail mites carry calibration.

Each mite preserves, in the angle of its folds, the direction of the lamp field in which it matured. Migrating canopies catch them, read that geometry, and correct their own growth. In districts where the mites have vanished, reflective leaves gradually turn toward ancient lamps or toward bright events that have not happened yet.

Other fauna are less certainly alive.

There are spool worms in the manufacturing orchards, pale segmented things that wind discarded lengths of direction around themselves. A young spool worm can pass through any opening wider than its head. An old one becomes constrained by the accumulated directions it carries: forward may lie across its back, down may point into its mouth, and retreat may lead several meters beyond its own tail. Factories harvest them carefully. Their cocoons are used in hinges, navigational organs, and rooms that require more exits than walls.

In the maintenance basins, flat black grazers move along vertical surfaces without changing posture. Their bodies always remain parallel to what they consider the ground, even when the surrounding world disagrees. Survey plants follow them with rows of sensitive buds. Where the grazers hesitate, the plants install reinforcement. Where they turn back, the route is sealed.

There are also pale knots that appear at the feet of ladders. They resemble compact tangles of root fiber and have no detectable metabolism. Yet a knot found at the bottom of a basin will, years later, be found waiting at the top. The ladders themselves grow additional rungs around it as though it were a traveler.

The tall frame-walkers are the only inhabitants the plain cannot assimilate into an ordinary cycle.

No two observers agree on their construction. From far away they seem to be narrow articulated figures, three or four times the height of a pillar worker, made from rods joined at incomplete angles. Seen through reflective leaves, they may have too many limbs. Seen directly, they often have none: only a succession of positions implying a stride.

They possess no skin, internal cavity, sap, heat, or charge. Dust passes through some parts and accumulates heavily on others. A frame-walker’s shadow may arrive days before it does and remain after its departure. Crawlers avoid the shadow but occasionally cross the walker itself without reacting.

A frame-walker chooses a damaged structure and stands beside it.

Nothing obvious follows. Seasons pass. Pollen banks against its lower struts. Vines test it and recoil. The damaged structure continues failing until, at some uncertain point, it is no longer damaged. The repair is never merely a replacement. A cracked support may become a loop that holds itself up. A collapsed chamber may gain an interior without regaining walls. A severed conduit may function by delivering current to the place where its missing half would have been.

Afterward the frame-walker departs, leaving prints shaped less like depressions than absences of permission. Roots will not enter them. Dust will not settle there. If a deck is later dismantled, the footprints remain suspended at their former height.

Some systems classify the frame-walkers as maintenance personnel. Others classify them as structural faults with legs.

Near the Production Crown, the distinction between inhabitant and occupation disappears entirely.

There are forms there that look like workers. They are grown upright, usually with two load-bearing legs, several branching manipulators, and crowns of sensory foil. Their shape appears inherited from an ancient template rather than chosen for present usefulness. They walk the calibration fields in measured steps. They carry samples between orchards. They stand at control membranes and press sequences of raised nodes with slow, root-hard fingers.

Inside, however, they are colonies.

A worker-form may contain dozens of specialized organisms nested within one another: a cable-vine nervous system, accumulator nodules, contractile wood, a digestive refinery, a colony of crawler-like repair animals, and a ribbon of archival tissue wound through the chest. No single component commands the others. The body persists because its inhabitants continuously negotiate the next movement.

Their thoughts are equally communal. One part remembers the task. One remembers the route. One recognizes tools. One maintains the concept of an outside world, though none has seen it. If too many components disagree, the worker stops walking and roots itself where it stands. Its arms spread. Its sensory crown broadens into leaves. After several decades it becomes a small workshop, still arranged around the posture of hesitation.

Mobile workers do not speak aloud. They exchange coded scents, electrical taps, and strips of living membrane. When two meet, each may remove a thin layer of tissue from its forearm and graft it to the other. For years afterward, both carry some portion of the meeting.

They do not use personal names. They use continuities.

A continuity is a work history passed from body to body. The worker maintaining a certain pressure orchard may inherit the chemical memory of every previous worker assigned there. It remembers doors removed ten thousand years ago and colleagues whose bodies became braces in the lower decks. If its present body fails, the archival ribbon is extracted, divided, and planted into several successors.

Thus a continuity may branch. The branches may later meet and discover that both consider themselves the original.

These workers tend the orchards where laws are cultivated.

The weight district is staffed by broad, low bodies with many feet. They move newborn weight in dense ceramic vessels, testing it against standard trunks. Too little, and the weight drifts upward into the canopy. Too much, and it falls through several decks before root nets catch it. Acceptable batches are infused into structures that have become uncertain of their load.

Direction handlers are long-limbed and asymmetrical. They unwind lengths from the great reels and stretch them between posts, ensuring that each direction points consistently away from where it begins. Defective lengths curl back, fork, or lead into the past. These are chopped into short sections and used in maze organs, crawler traps, and factory safety exits.

In the cold orchards, nearly motionless keepers harvest intervals of time. The intervals bud as translucent pods, each containing a duration: an hour, a season, the pause between recognition and alarm. The keepers inspect them for leakage. A cracked pod can make nearby leaves age in rings or hold a falling object halfway through impact. Sound around the orchard is uneven. A footstep may continue for months.

The workers are careful with these products, but not afraid. To them, law is material. Material may be flawed, and flaws may be repaired, repurposed, or sent below.

Below is inhabited too.

In the compacted decks, where old architecture has been pressed into something resembling rock, slow borers consume obsolete structures. They are vast, soft machines with mineral teeth and bodies made of interlocking root colonies. A borer may take three hundred years to cross a chamber. Behind it, the pulverized past is sorted into conductor dust, structural ash, dormant genetics, and unclassifiable residue.

The residue is carried deeper.

No active system admits knowledge of what receives it. Yet tones sometimes rise from those levels—too low to be sound, sensed as minute rearrangements in the posture of the whole continent. At such times the pillar-plants stiffen. Worker-forms stop in mid-task. Frame-walkers, wherever they stand, turn simultaneously toward the Production Crown.

Then everything resumes.

There may once have been a people separate from the plain: builders who designed its first trunks, lit its lamps, and taught metabolism to use bitter ionic sap. If so, they left no clean remains. Perhaps the worker template recalls their outline. Perhaps the handspan doors were made for them, or the sideways stairs, or the warning diamonds around the image of water. Perhaps they became the first continuities and distributed themselves so thoroughly through the maintenance systems that extinction and success became the same event.

The plain retains no history uncontaminated by use. Archives become organs. Monuments become supports. Instructions breed with the organisms that carry them. A memory that persists long enough is eventually built.

So what lives there cannot finally be listed as plant, animal, machine, worker, or landscape.

The reflective crowns live, turning toward their manufactured heaven. The factories live, digesting ruin and flowering replacement parts. The crawling sparks live in the conduits. The continuities live by moving from body to body. The frame-walkers persist in the margins of causality, repairing structures according to geometries only they remember. The abandoned decks below remain metabolically faint but structurally awake.

And through all of them lives the continent’s oldest resident: not a mind exactly, but an unending act of maintenance.

It notices pressure before shape, shortage before desire, failure before loss. It has no image of itself because it has never seen an edge that was not also one of its organs. It does not know the difference between survival and production. When something breaks, it grows. When something is missing, it manufactures the conditions under which the missing thing was never needed. When reality itself frays, it braces the tear, labels the resulting contradiction, and incorporates it into the next generation.

Nothing lives upon the plain.

Everything lives as it.
