At first, almost everything alive there resembles something that is not.

An animal resembles a patch in a wall. A feeding colony resembles corrosion. A nursery resembles a mineral vein. A sleeping herd looks like a scatter of fired plates left after construction. Even the city’s people, seen at a distance in their bright coverings, can be mistaken for shutters turning toward the sun.

This is not merely camouflage. In the basin, mineral likeness is the common shape of survival. Flesh must negotiate glare, heat, brine, and desiccation. Anything soft is hidden beneath shells, sealed inside water, or withdrawn into the stone. Life presents a hard face to the sky.

The most numerous inhabitants are too small to possess an individual outline. They live within porous rock and beneath crusts no thicker than a fingernail. Some form dark seams around hot-water outlets; some spread as opaline stains where rainwater meets brine. Their colonies partition themselves according to chemistry. One narrow band consumes iron compounds. Beside it, another uses the first colony’s by-products. A third survives only along the microscopic border between them.

Over centuries these films have altered the basin as profoundly as any architect. They seal leaks, open new pores with secreted acids, concentrate metals, and lay down glassy laminations. The oldest colonies have become geological structures: terraces, nodules, brittle chimneys, smooth folds like cooled wax. The distinction between living film and the mineral record of its former growth is often impossible to make. A colony may be active only a few hours each year, when rain lowers the salinity around it. The rest of the time it appears to be stone.

The wall-grazers feed upon this hidden abundance.

Each is a low oval of interlocking plates with a wet underside full of cilia, gripping pads, and rasping mouths. Their visible armor varies with district. On old silica walls they are milk-white and crazed with hairline fractures. Around copper works they develop blue-green margins. Those living near iron settlers are black, with a dull red bloom along the joints. They do not choose these colors; they acquire them from what they eat and from the materials incorporated into their shells.

When dry, they clamp down so tightly that even workers sometimes mark them as masonry defects. Rain releases them. Water enters channels between their plates, their bodies inflate, and thousands begin to travel. They graze in overlapping arcs, leaving clean tracks through salt and tarnish. Adults carry their young beneath the rear plates, where a few droplets can remain long after the wall has dried. During exceptional droughts, generations may live and die without moving more than the width of a hand.

The transparent pool animals are more visibly animate, though only while feeding. Their bodies are clear bells or folded sacs held open by internal struts. Rows of fans circulate water through them, sorting microorganisms, crystal dust, and organic fragments. Their pulses are not synchronized by sight or sound but by pressure. A whole pool may therefore begin beating as one body when a sluice opens, then stop at once when a predator enters the channel.

Their skeletons persist after death as nearly invisible frameworks. People collect these from drained basins and use them as fine filters, surgical scaffolds, and templates for growing certain crystals. In older districts, generations of discarded skeletons have fused into translucent reefs beneath the streets.

The drainage predators are among the few creatures whose bodies remain deliberately dark. They are long, ribbon-thin, and muscular, with sensory pits running from end to end. They map their surroundings through conductivity, tasting the city’s electrical field as other animals might see light. A working pump, a lightning discharge, a crowd of filter animals, and a human hand dipped into water all have distinct signatures.

They spend dry hours coiled in sump wells, where their metabolism slows almost to nothing. Flooding makes the streets continuous with their hunting grounds. Then they slip from grates and move through water shallower than their bodies are thick, flattening until they seem like shadows cast by no object. They do not bite. They wrap around prey and interrupt its internal electrical rhythms. Smaller animals simply cease moving and are carried away.

The basin walkers are less alien at first glance because they have legs and travel in herds. Yet their proportions belong to the city rather than to any open wilderness. Their bodies are lifted high above hot pavement on narrow, fluid-filled limbs. Reflective dorsal shields turn with the sun by tiny degrees. Beneath the head hangs a fringe of absorbent brushes, combs, and capillary tongues.

They drink walls rather than pools.

At dawn they pass beneath ledges, brushing up condensation and the life suspended in it. After rain they follow the retreating shade, harvesting films before the stone becomes hot enough to seal them. Their routes are old and precise. Herds use the same arcades, pressure yards, abandoned workshops, and underpasses for generations. Where construction obstructs one of these paths, the animals gather without noise and wait, sometimes for days, rather than cross exposed pavement.

They communicate through touch and vibration. A message travels down a herd as one animal taps the leg of the next. Alarm moves quickly; reassurance more slowly. Mothers identify young by the rhythm of pressure exchanged through the foot cups. During rest, dozens join limbs into loose lattices so that a tremor sensed by one is felt by all.

The aerial creatures are not truly balloons, though from below they appear to be. Their buoyancy comes from many chambers containing gases separated from geothermal vapor by symbiotic microbes. Their frames grow as branching conductive meshes, covered by membranes silver on the sunward side and dark beneath. Old individuals accumulate extra chambers rather than simply enlarging. They become archipelagos of buoyant lobes surrounding a central, knotted body.

Their tendrils trail through vapor, collecting droplets, microorganisms, and charge. Most of the time they drift alone. Before storms they assemble around towers and lightning masts, orienting themselves along the basin’s changing electric field. Charge passes among them through momentary contacts of their filaments. From the ground, these exchanges look like thoughts flashing through translucent organs.

They reproduce during the wettest weeks. Small chambers detach from adults and cling in clusters beneath tower ribs, feeding on condensate until they can lift themselves. Many are eaten by specialized threadlike hunters that live among the structural cables. Others are caught by city workers, tagged, and released near particular industrial towers, because their movements provide a sensitive forecast of atmospheric charge.

The people read all these lives as carefully as they read gauges.

Their bodies are not well suited to the basin’s exposed surface. Whether they came from elsewhere or whether the city has altered faster than they have is no longer a useful distinction. They are tall enough to wade the storm-flooded plazas, but vulnerable to heat and glare. Uncovered skin loses water rapidly. Eyes cannot tolerate prolonged reflection from salt pans. Their breathing organs must be protected from mineral aerosols that would slowly harden inside them.

Outdoors, therefore, a person is seldom seen directly.

The mirrored mantle is more than clothing. It is shade, condenser, breathing apparatus, water account, and public identity. Its outer scales shift between reflective and radiative states. Moisture exhaled into the mask is recovered in fine channels and returned to a drinking reservoir at the hip. The hem samples any water through which the wearer passes, warning of heat, acidity, or dangerous concentrations of dissolved metals.

Mantles differ subtly by profession. Channel keepers wear broad lower skirts resistant to current. Tower workers have narrow silhouettes and conductive threads that can be fastened to grounding rails. Refiners display ceramic badges made from the materials they are licensed to separate. Those who tend the deep microbial chambers wear matte mantles, because mirrored surfaces can disrupt organisms sensitive to stray light.

Faces are shown below ground, in the cooled rooms, but not casually above. Consequently, posture has become a public language. The inclination of the mask, the opening of shoulder panels, the placement of the hands against the mantle: these convey recognition, apology, rank, impatience, mourning. Two figures meeting in a bright empty street may appear motionless, yet conduct an entire exchange through slight alterations of reflected light.

They speak, but sparingly on the surface. Airborne speech is inefficient inside masks, and loud sound is associated with mechanical failure: cavitation, rupture, uncontrolled boiling, a bearing striking its housing. Raised voices trigger an instinctive unease. Most outdoor communication passes through short-range signals in the paving or through contact between sleeves. Children learn pressure alphabets before they learn to project their voices.

Below the city, the inhabitants are less spectral.

They live in chambers arranged around reservoirs rather than streets. Domestic rooms open onto shared cistern halls, where water ceilings throw moving light over glazed walls. Temperatures are graduated: cool sleeping vaults, warm communal baths, hot work galleries nearest the rising springs. People migrate through these zones over the course of a day as other societies might move between indoors and outdoors.

A household is defined by its water circuit. Those who share a sequence of condensation, washing, cooking, cultivation, and reclamation are considered kin whether or not they share ancestry. A child may be born into one circuit and later join another through apprenticeship or marriage. The joining is literal. At the ceremony, two domestic systems are connected, their flows sampled, balanced, and allowed to mingle for the first time.

No household owns water outright. It possesses turns, temperatures, permitted uses, and obligations of return. Wealth lies in access to clean early stages of a flow, but prestige belongs to those capable of making use of its later, more difficult states. A family that can recover something valuable from dense brine or contaminated wash is regarded as more accomplished than one that merely consumes purity.

Their food comes largely from the microbial chambers. Films are grown on stacked mineral plates and harvested as dark pastes, translucent gels, crisp salt wafers, and fermented masses with metallic or smoky flavors despite the absence of fire. Pool animals are eaten in some districts, especially after seasonal die-offs. Wall-grazers are protected almost everywhere, because removing them causes films to overgrow channels and changes the absorption of heat by buildings.

Meals are small and frequent. Every dish has a water cost, a salt burden, and a recovery plan. Cups are rarely filled. Liquid is served in sealed vessels with narrow valves, or bound into gels that release moisture slowly in the mouth. To spill a drink is not sinful, exactly, but it produces the same shock another culture might feel at seeing a valuable tool deliberately broken.

Children spend much of their early life near the cisterns. They learn to distinguish water by smell, surface tension, and the color of mineral residue left on a heated tile. They are taught never to trust transparency. A clear channel may be potable, sterile and poisonous, or saturated with compounds that crystallize only inside the body. By adolescence, most can identify the district of origin of a droplet from its taste—though formal etiquette discourages tasting unknown water.

Their games imitate separation. Children mix colored salts and compete to recover them. They build miniature sluices in shallow trays. They race harmless pool organisms through branching channels by changing pressure with their fingers. On rainy afternoons they are sometimes allowed above, tethered in groups beneath covered walks, to watch the plazas fill. To them a storm is not dreary weather but a sudden expansion of the world.

The oldest citizens often become keepers of flow records. The archive is partly written, but much of it is material. Narrow sealed ampoules preserve water from exceptional storms, failed wells, newly opened strata, epidemics, births, structural collapses, and treaty ceremonies. Shelves in the deepest halls hold thousands of these samples. Some contain only mineral signatures. Others preserve microbial communities in suspended states.

To remember an event is to retain something that passed through it.

The dead enter this archive differently. Their bodies are separated with the same care given to ores, buildings, and industrial brines, but the process is neither casual nor entirely practical. Families sit beside the sealed dissolution vessels through each stage. Recovered metals are made into small conductive elements and placed within the household’s water system. Carbon and other organic matter go to deep chambers whose microbial cultures may be centuries old. A fraction of purified water is added to the common cistern.

Thus the dead do not remain as bodies or monuments. They persist as circulation: a trace in a pipe fitting, a contribution to a living culture, an increase too small to measure in the reservoir from which descendants drink.

The people do not consider this disappearance. They consider burial the greater loss—a body isolated, its components mixed beyond retrieval, denied further use.

Still, not everyone is content with perfect return. In abandoned flooded stories, there are illicit memorials: unprocessed objects, sealed locks of tissue, old masks left intact, names scratched where salt will not quickly erase them. Some residents cultivate forbidden complexity in private basins, mixing organisms and minerals without immediate intention to separate them. These “tangles” are viewed with suspicion because they resemble waste, but also with fascination. New foods, medicines, and symbioses have emerged from them.

The city’s engineers likewise do not stand wholly apart from its ecology. Their oldest machines are inhabited. Microbial films regulate the chemistry inside conduits. Filter animals are encouraged at intake mouths. Wall-grazers clean condensation surfaces. Aerial creatures bleed charge from towers. Some valves contain living membranes that swell or contract more reliably than fabricated sensors.

Conversely, many animals carry manufactured parts. Herd keepers repair cracked basin-walker shields with ceramic patches. Aerial young are fitted with conductive rings. Drain predators in critical channels may be implanted with markers or gently redirected by patterned currents. After enough generations, engineered material is inherited as habitat: young grow around it, colonies coat it, bodies adapt to its shape.

No single intelligence directs this arrangement. The citizens manage it, but the animals alter it; the microbes sustain it; the water imposes terms on all of them. The towers sweat because people built them, because heat rises, because films keep their pores open, and because floating creatures draw charge from the vapor. A channel remains clear because sluices flush it, grazers scrape it, salts crystallize at designated edges, and workers recognize when not to interfere.

Seen briefly, the basin seems almost uninhabited.

Seen for an hour after rain, it becomes crowded.

Walls begin to move. Clear bodies pulse in every pool. Black ribbons test the flooded streets. Herds descend from shadowed bridges. Silver chambers gather among the towers. Masked figures emerge from pressure doors and stand ankle-deep in water, reading its temperature and taste through instruments in their clothing. Beneath them, in stone pores and buried halls, immeasurable colonies wake to the brief dilution.

And seen over centuries, the chief inhabitant is harder to name.

It may be the city, though the city has no single body.

It may be the water, though the water is always passing through.

It may be the process of separation itself: life and machinery continually taking the mixed substance of the world, sorting it into temporary forms, and releasing it to be mixed again.
