The first inhabitant is the plateau itself.

It has no face, no single heart, and perhaps no awareness that can be separated from weather. Yet it responds. Beneath a passing herd, vessels constrict to protect themselves from trampling. Around blood, pores open. Where fire touches it, distant tissues flood with bitter resin. Its oldest wounds pucker before storms, and whole regions darken when the buried flow of sap changes course.

Those who live upon it disagree about whether these are reflexes or intentions. They agree only that the plateau remembers pressure.

It remembers slowly. A camp leaves a faint ring that may remain after everyone who slept there has died. Migration paths persist as pale lines because the rind anticipates feet and hardens before the herds arrive. In certain places the ground grows knobs beneath habitual shelters, raising them clear of its breathing pores. In others it quietly tilts abandoned houses until rain can enter and dissolve them.

Immediately upon this vast body lives a second skin of smaller life.

The mosslike cilia are not one species but thousands: green drinking hairs, silver heat-scales, red threads that close wounds, waxy nodules full of dormant larvae. By day they lie flattened and seem painted onto the plain. At dusk they rise, making the ground look furred. They trap condensation, pollen, shed scales, ash, dung, and the airborne eggs of animals too small to be seen individually. Every handful of surface tissue is a crowded country.

Among these hairs move the sap-lickers, delicate six-legged creatures with translucent bellies. Their mouths are soft brushes. They follow the wounds made by larger grazers and become amber-colored as they feed. When startled, they leap together, thousands at once, producing the brief illusion that a patch of ground has shattered into sparks.

Glassmouths make the wounds they follow.

They are winged grazers, broad and low, with membranes stretched between four long limbs and a row of clear mineral teeth along the lower jaw. At dawn they rise from the plateau’s breathing slots in flocks that can cover the horizon. They fly only a hand’s breadth above the rind, their mouths open, shaving off the night’s tender growth. Each flock leaves behind parallel tracks of wet green tissue.

Older glassmouths become too heavy to fly. Their wing membranes stiffen into sun-catching fans; their feet root; their jaws sink into the ground. Over several dry seasons they lose their eyes and become stationary feeding crowns. Travelers shelter beneath them without always realizing that the “little fan trees” along a route are the final age of the morning flocks.

The swiftest hunters of glassmouths are the cordtails. They run on six narrow legs and steer with braided tails whose strands can spread independently in the wind. A cordtail hunts by entering the moving shadow of a flock and matching its speed. Seen from a distance, it appears as a dark stitch racing through a sheet of wings. It kills without slowing, hooking prey with its forward limbs and passing the bodies backward to be held beneath the abdomen.

Cordtails are born rooted. Their young grow in rings around the carcass of a parent, each no more than a thorny shoot with a closed eye at its tip. When the rains come, the ring tears itself free, and every shoot runs in a different direction.

Other lives are less easily named.

The low radial growths around breathing pits are called tile-mothers by the plateau people. Their overlapping leaves shelter warm, humid chambers where eggs, seeds, and pupae accumulate indiscriminately. The tile-mother feeds them all. Some hatch as crawling animals. Some rise on wings. Some pierce the chamber roof and remain there as plants. A single brood may contain forms so unlike one another that early naturalists mistook them for an entire community gathered by accident.

Pillar-plants dominate the visible world, but they are not passive monuments. Each maintains an entourage and a territory.

Their crowns hold reservoirs of night mist. Their bark channels house colonies of hollow insects that beat their bodies against the wood when pressure falls. Their roots release scents that guide migrating animals toward water or turn them away from vulnerable new growth. A mature pillar may host thousands of species without being touched by any of them directly; they live in its shade, its weather, its smell.

Some pillars bear fruit. Others imitate fruit in order to attract mobile seed-carriers, then fasten thumb-sized buds beneath their hides. Some release walking branches. These descend at night on jointed twigs, awkward as newborn animals, and spend years ranging across the plain. They feed on dung and carrion, collecting minerals their parent cannot reach. When full, they return to the pillar and climb it, fusing back into the trunk. Those that fail to find their parent often root elsewhere and become warped, solitary trees carrying the chemical memory of a lost grove.

The great browsers are known chiefly by their legs.

Their bodies travel so high above the ground that mist may hide them. Each leg descends like a mottled column, ending in a many-toed pad that tests the rind before committing weight. They walk with immense patience, sometimes holding one foot aloft for hours while burrowing colonies pass beneath it. Between the legs hang gardens of moss, flowering skin, parasites, nesting flocks, and small arboreal hunters that never touch the plateau in their entire lives.

A browser may feed from several pillars at once. Its neck divides into flexible trunks, each ending in a rasping mouth. When threatened, it does not run. It folds its legs, lowering its inhabited body toward the plain, and the gardens on its hide release clouds of stinging defenders.

The people say an old browser does not die. It kneels, roots, and becomes a grove.

Below the rind are the plated ones.

No one knows how many there are. They move through deep vascular layers where the plateau is warm and dark, feeding on mineral knots and dead channels. Their passage raises ridges that travel across the country for weeks. Smaller animals follow overhead, harvesting the moisture that sweats from the strained ground. So do hunters, because breaches bring pale, unarmored flesh within reach.

A plated one surfaces rarely and catastrophically. Its back may be mistaken for a hill until it opens along the spine, exposing curtains of red gills to the wind. Swarms descend on it. The plateau seals around it. Sometimes the creature retreats; sometimes it remains half-emerged and hardens into a ridge. Pools collect between its plates. After centuries, only the repeating curve of the landscape reveals that a body lies beneath.

There are predators that exploit the confusion between rooted and mobile life.

Bloodmelons stand alone in open country, glossy red and fragrant. Their false fruit trembles in the heat. Pollinators gather first, then small fruit-eaters, then the hunters of those fruit-eaters. Only when enough weight rests in its shade does the bloodmelon unfold. What seemed to be bark becomes a circle of jointed limbs, and the central trunk splits into a wet, toothless mouth.

During lean seasons it roots again. If prey remains scarce, it flowers honestly.

Night belongs to the sail-ears. They are low, narrow hunters whose enormous ears rise above them like dark leaves. They hear the deep sap currents beneath the rind and use them as a map. To a sail-ear, every pillar has a distinct subterranean voice; every water pocket rings; every running animal is a pattern of impacts interrupting the plateau’s murmur.

They hunt in families. One listens while the others move. When the ground groans unexpectedly, all of them freeze and flatten their ears over their bodies, becoming patches of black vegetation. Their young are born deaf and must learn the land’s sounds gradually. For this reason, family groups return to the same territories for generations: a young sail-ear carried too far from its birthplace may never understand what it hears.

The people of the plateau listen in much the same way.

They are small beside nearly everything around them, long-limbed, broad-footed, and dark-eyed against glare. Thin heat vanes grow along their shoulders and forearms, lying flat in cool air and lifting at midday. Their feet feel low vibrations through the living ground. Children learn danger first as sensation: the quick tapping of burrowers, the slow pulse beneath a weak rind, the absence of ordinary noise when something vast crosses the sun.

They seldom call themselves owners of a place. A community is named for what it follows: the Amber-Wing Following, the People Behind the Split-Horn Herd, Those Who Return When the White Fans Click. Their settlements are intervals in movement rather than permanent claims.

A few dozen households inhabit the shell of a fallen pillar. Inside, the trunk is cool and resin-scented. Curved partitions of sewn bark divide sleeping alcoves. Water condenses along grooves cut into the inner wall and drips into shell basins. Nothing heavy rests directly on the plateau. Even the dead are not buried, because cutting deeply into the rind is dangerous and because the ground may reject them.

Instead, bodies are carried to walking branches. The branches accept them into hollow chambers and depart. Years later they may return bearing unfamiliar flowers.

The plateau people keep animals only loosely. Sap-lickers are encouraged near kitchens to clean spills. Young glassmouths are raised in shaded folds and released at the first molt; some rejoin wild flocks, while others return each morning to be milked of sweet crop-fluid. Cordtails cannot be tamed, but hunters sometimes tie bright threads around their tails, then track those flashes through grazing swarms to find prey.

Their most valued companions are little pore-finders: warm, boneless creatures worn around the neck like scarves. A pore-finder tastes the air and tightens when the ground is about to vent resin or gas. In return it drinks sweat and sleeps against the pulse in a person’s throat.

No one travels alone if it can be avoided. Distance deceives the eyes, heat erases landmarks, and a safe-looking plain may be only a thin roof over an abandoned channel. Travelers bind themselves with long cords in storms and seasonal emergences. When sound overwhelms speech, meaning passes through knots and tugs: water, halt, danger above, danger below, I am here.

Their rituals are practical acts repeated until practicality has become reverence. Before lighting a hearth, they press an ear to the ground. Before cutting a leaf, they taste its sap. When entering the shade of a pillar, they leave a cup of water at the outermost root—not because the pillar needs so little water, but because permission should cost something.

They fear fire, deep silence, and ground that beats in time with the heart.

They also fear the rare moments when the plateau moves deliberately.

These begin with a medicinal sharpness in the air. Pillars close their leaves. Burrowing creatures pour from vents. The living plain tightens until old scars stand out white against the green. Then a contraction travels toward the horizon, not the wandering ridge of a plated animal but a vast coordinated drawing-in. Pools spill from their cups. Settlements tilt. Tendrils along the escarpment groan against the mountains.

Afterward, distances have changed. A familiar pillar may stand half a day closer. A canyon may have closed. Somewhere at the edge, a strip of the green altitude has torn loose and begun its long descent toward the lowlands, carrying its cilia, pools, groves, herds, predators, and bewildered people with it.

Life there does not cling to the plateau as life clings to ordinary land.

It participates in it. It wounds and tends it, drinks its exhalations, hides in its pores, carries fragments of it away, and returns in forms neither plant nor animal can fully contain. Even the people, who tell stories to distinguish themselves from all other creatures, sometimes stop walking in years of drought. They bury their feet in wet scars, lift their heat vanes to the sun, and enter a green sleep.

When the rains return, most pull free.

A few remain rooted.

Their descendants rest beneath those human-shaped crowns and argue, generations later, over whether the old ones chose to become trees or whether they merely discovered what they had always been.
