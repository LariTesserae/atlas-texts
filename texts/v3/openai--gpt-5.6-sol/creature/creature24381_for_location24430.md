At first they seem to be the fish.

They move through the broad channels in loose, dark congregations, each body keeping its distance by pressure and touch. Most are narrow enough to enter the reef’s repeating apertures. Their eyes are reduced to sealed dimples or absent altogether; their faces are instead crowded with pits, whiskers, and trembling membranes. They read the city through currents reflected from walls, through the electrical murmur of neighboring muscle, through tastes so faint they might be called memories in the water.

They are black, wine-red, bruised purple, glass-clear. Color persists in them long after light has ceased to matter.

When the eastern flow arrives laden with plankton, they turn together. Thousands face the same invisible direction, fins making minute corrections. They feed without frenzy until the hunters enter the avenue. Then the congregation folds into the buildings. Large bodies become streams of small bodies, vanishing through arches that contract behind them as the wall animals recoil.

The hunters are slower than panic and more patient. Broad-headed sharks pass above the roofs, sensing heartbeats below. Ribbon predators thread through the lanes, tapping the water with delicate jaws. Eels wait inside limestone tubes, their mouths opening and closing as if practicing a single word. Rays lie under the mud in plazas and troughs. Only paired pulses betray them until they rise, shedding sediment like a loosened floor.

But the fish are only visitors. Even those born in one chamber and eaten in the next do not fully belong to the city. They travel through it as blood travels through a body, necessary and innumerable, but not the body itself.

Closer to the stone, other lives appear.

Crabs inhabit the lowest recesses, where shell fragments collect. They are compulsive arrangers. With patient claws they sort the debris around their holes: smooth pieces outward, sharp pieces inward, edible scraps hidden beneath the lip of the entrance. Their rings are defenses rather than designs, yet generations of rings create borders, courtyards, and little radial clearings. After silty floods, the crabs emerge first. They excavate themselves, then excavate the city, carrying pellets of mud from chamber to chamber until currents take the burden away.

Translucent grazers travel across the walls. Some are long and soft, their organs visible as dull knots drifting inside them. Some have hundreds of minute feet. They consume the thin living film on the reef and leave clean roads behind—pale tracks that intersect, fade, and are grown over again. Small predators follow these tracks because prey is easier to detect on newly exposed stone. Smaller scavengers follow the predators. Thus a single grazing animal can create, for a few hours, an avenue of commerce without knowing it has done so.

Shell-bearers occupy the cracks. Once settled, many never move again. They thicken their rims against the current, close at disturbance, reopen to feed, and eventually become indistinguishable from the architecture that holds them. Their descendants settle upon them. Their shells stop being possessions and become walls.

Every surface is crowded with mouths.

Some are ringed with feathery arms that unfold only in favorable water. Some are slits behind lattices of mineral needles. Some sit at the ends of pale tubes and withdraw so rapidly that the stone seems to swallow them. They catch plankton, dissolved flesh, eggs, waste, and the powdered remains of older buildings. A current entering a hall rich with food is visibly thinned by the time it leaves. The walls have eaten it.

The builders live here too, though “builder” falsely separates creature from construction.

They are colonies of tiny related bodies sharing nourishment through buried tissue. No individual knows the outline of a tower. Each knows only where its neighbors touch, where the current strikes, where sediment settles, where living flesh has been wounded. From those local sensations come buttresses, galleries, chimneys, vaulted roofs. One animal secretes a cup of limestone around itself. Its offspring add cups beside it. Their descendants rise upon their skeletons. A façade is a genealogy made solid.

Other colonies grow as branching fans until storms break them. The fragments lodge elsewhere and continue growing, so districts reproduce by dismemberment. Soft-bodied polyps spread over dead surfaces like a slow skin, disputing space chemically. Where rival colonies meet, they burn one another with digestive threads. Their wars advance less than a finger’s width in a season, but leave long white borders of exposed skeleton. New settlers arrive there immediately.

No surface remains neutral.

The city’s oldest inhabitants are therefore also its foundations: innumerable dead animals, retaining the shapes they made while alive. They outnumber the living beyond measure. Their chambers hold mud; their shells redirect water; their broken spines reinforce pillars. The dead do not lie beneath the city. They are the city’s usable depth.

Among them move the burrowers.

Blind, soft, and usually unseen, they rasp limestone from within. Some swallow stone to digest the organisms hidden in it. Some open tunnels for shelter. Some dissolve passages chemically, grain by grain. They seem destructive until their work is viewed over centuries. Their holes admit oxygenated water. Their discarded sediment is caught by binders and hardened into new structures. When many burrowers respond to the same current, a corridor appears where no corridor existed. Later, colonists line it, narrowing and strengthening it, until the tunnel resembles an intentional passage.

In the central shafts, the distinction between animal and district finally fails.

There the warm upwelling supports curtains of pale filter feeders stacked one above another. Their tendrils mingle across openings. Their feeding rhythms alter the flow, and the altered flow determines where their young settle. Worms live in the crusts they secrete; small crustaceans live among the worms; bacteria live inside the crustaceans and in the warm mineral seams beneath them all. Faint chemical lights blink from bodies no larger than seeds. A predator’s approach extinguishes one tier, then another. Darkness travels upward through the shaft like a signal.

No mind conducts it. Yet the whole district reacts.

A pressure wave closes millions of feeding fans. Fish compress into chambers. Crabs brace inside their rings. Eels withdraw. Soft towers bend. For several breaths, the city becomes smaller and smoother, with fewer openings offered to the water. Then, when danger has passed, it loosens. Mouths return. Limbs emerge. Passageways refill.

Seen briefly, this might be mistaken for a society of separate species sharing a reef. Seen longer, the separations become difficult to defend.

The fish fertilize the walls with their waste and carry larvae between districts. The predators keep the fish from stripping certain chambers bare. Grazers clear ground for settlers. Burrowers weaken old structures so currents can break and redistribute them. Crabs lift buried shell back into moving water. The colonies shelter all of these creatures and are fed, wounded, pruned, spread, and rebuilt by them.

Even the seasonal burial is part of their life. When dense silt rolls across the basin, the low dwellers seal themselves away or climb. Many fail. Their bodies enrich the deposit. Scavengers arrive, then burrowers, then binding mats, then calcifying mouths. Within years, the grave layer becomes a floor. Within generations, pillars rise from it.

Nothing here possesses the city. Nothing looks upon it as a whole. No creature knows that the avenues migrate, that the eastern walls are being eaten away while the center thickens, or that a tower’s collapse may determine the shape of a district centuries later.

And yet something lives there that is larger than any of them.

It has no single boundary, because its outskirts dissolve into reef and mud. It has no permanent organs, though channels carry food, towers exchange water, and deep warm shafts sustain its densest tissues. It has no unified will. Its impulses are hunger, recoil, settlement, erosion, reproduction—the smallest possible instructions, repeated until they resemble purpose.

Its skeleton is made from its ancestors. Its flesh is composed of tenants that become rooms. Its streets are currents disciplined by growth. It breathes wherever the walls open their mouths.

The inhabitants are not inside it.

They are the temporary, feeding, breeding substance of a creature too slow to recognize itself.
