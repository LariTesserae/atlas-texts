The inhabitants of the basin are not machines, though machinery is the nearest language available for them. They have no distinction between organ and implement. A piston is a muscle that has forgotten flesh; a valve is a mouth persuaded to remain in one place. Their shells grow by oxidation, their joints bud from pressure fractures, and their blood is whatever water happens to be passing through them.

None possesses a body entirely its own.

The broad herd-creatures borrow rain through their flank grilles and return it as steam. Needle swarms exchange rings whenever they scatter. Predators wear prey until the prey begins acting as part of the predator. The aerial drifters trail their nerves over the ground. Vent-dwellers become organs of the ridges, and the ridges themselves labor like animals too large and slow to recognize. Anatomy passes continually from one being to another.

The most numerous inhabitants are the low plated grazers. They advance in dense fronts, their overlapping shells black-red with corrosion. Each is wide enough to cover a small room, but seldom rises higher than a person’s knee. Beneath its shell is a crowded forest of hydraulic legs: blunt, telescoping, almost boneless. These legs do not step so much as negotiate with the wet ground. One anchors while another fills; a third stiffens with brine; six more release. Their progress is a shuddering crawl accompanied by knocks, squeals, and bursts of vapor.

Their rotating mouths scour no ordinary food from the rock. They graze distinctions.

Where fresh rain lies over salt water, they drink the boundary. Where one stone is warmer than the stone beside it, they consume the difference. They feed on the tension between charged minerals, on the pressure trapped beneath ice, on the tiny imbalance between a pool and its reflection. Their passage leaves the terrain exhausted of contrast: equal in temperature, salinity, voltage, and depth. A fed herd is followed by a temporary peace in which nothing flows.

This peace is what attracts the ring-young.

These bright scavengers are scarcely creatures when still. A single one may consist of translucent needles, metal loops, shards of vitreous tissue, and several warm beads held in a loose electromagnetic agreement. They rush beneath the grazers and gather in the cooling wake of vents. Their chirring is courtship, navigation, warning, and possibly thought. No two calls can be separated.

When alarmed, each animal relinquishes its shape. Rings spring away in all directions, rolling across the flats or skipping over puddles. Needles sink upright into cracks. Glass splinters ride the runoff. Later the pieces reconvene, but not faithfully. One animal may return with another’s sensory cluster or with a mineral hoop that was never alive. Thus identity among them is temporary. They recognize one another not by body but by a recurring error in their motion—a favored wobble, a repeated skipped beat.

Their offspring may simply be reassemblies that discover they have no memory of belonging to anyone else.

Higher in the wet air drift the bladdered ones. They are broad, colorless membranes made visible by the dim things enclosed within them: blue discharges, coils of conductive fluid, chains of dark nodules that contract when thunder passes through old stone. They do not fly. They suspend themselves between pressures, inflating on one side and collapsing on the other. Storms pull them across the basin like slow transparent sails.

Their tendrils drag for kilometers. Some seek metallic veins. Others catch small animals and lift them into the membrane, where they remain alive among the electrical organs. A drifter may contain entire populations of captured rings, each turning in place and producing charge. Whether these are prey, parasites, or newly adopted tissues depends on how long they remain.

The crack-hunters are more definite.

Folded, one appears to be no more than a black seam filled with water. Its limbs lie nested inside one another, thinner than blades and under tremendous internal tension. It may wait through centuries of passing rain. When vibrations in ancient rock announce a herd, the hunter opens joint by joint. Each extension ends in a violent locking report. By the time its prey arrives, a dark scaffold stands over the fissure.

It kills through comprehension rather than force. Its clamps find the separations already latent in a grazer’s body: the shell boundaries, pressure couplings, soft gaskets, mineral sutures. It takes the animal apart in the order the animal once assembled itself.

For a time afterward, prey components remain fitted along the hunter’s frame. Bladders pulse in its torso. Scraping disks rotate at the tips of its limbs. Rows of stolen plates close over its vulnerable hinges. Then small composite beings begin to detach from it, bearing the hunter’s proportions in the prey’s materials. They creep back into the cracks and fold themselves into darkness. It may be reproduction. It may be digestion that has learned to escape.

Beneath all surface life move the round mouths.

No one sees their bodies. Their presence is known from the gradual leaning of water and the way tracks curve toward a point not yet sunken. They migrate under bedrock without tunneling through it. Stone becomes briefly permissible to them, softening overhead until whole shelves descend intact.

They do not bite. They receive.

What happens below lasts months or perhaps occurs before the collapse. Eventually the swallowed herd emerges elsewhere, polished down to silver-gray and subtly revised. Its animals may walk backward, vent inward, or feed by restoring gradients rather than consuming them. Some return joined flank to flank in wheels. Others carry pools in their shells whose reflections show the place of swallowing.

These altered ones are often followed by upright tracks that have no feet above them.

Tracks constitute a separate order of life. At first they are merely impressions, but age enters them differently than it enters stone. Grooves meet older grooves and exchange directions. Footprints rise into relief, retaining toe pads, joint scars, and drag marks of creatures long flattened into the basin floor. Once raised, they migrate toward low water.

A file of living tracks moves slowly, but nothing can turn it aside. It passes through herds, and for a moment each animal is forced into the gait of the vanished maker. Small ring-creatures collect inside the hollow undersides of these reliefs. Predators strike at them. New prints are made, and in time those prints also stand up.

In the central sheet-water live the tripods.

Their three legs are narrow above and widen near the ground into pads supported on compressed vapor. They never quite touch the basin. Rain can pass beneath each foot, though it comes out altered—warmer, older, sometimes falling in the wrong direction. The suspended bell between the legs is not carried like an organ. The rest of the tripod appears to have grown outward from it.

Up close, the bell is not smooth metal. It is made of minute nested mouths, each opening into the next. They contract in sequences too slow to watch. Between calls, smaller inhabitants crawl across the bell and disappear into its grain. Reflected extinct species gather around the legs. The crowded fauna allow the tripods room not by retreating, but by compressing themselves into thinner forms.

When a tripod sounds, it does not seem to communicate with another creature. The call addresses conditions.

Its deepest component enters teeth, fluid, shell cavities, and the oldest bedrock. The audible blast follows afterward, dragging chains of overtones across the flats. In response, some rule of the basin changes: gravity inclines, shadows multiply, reflections harden, or rain reveals that it has been occupying many directions all along.

The tripods may be lawmakers. They may be instruments through which the basin detects changes already underway. They may simply feed on the brief confusion produced when one physical law is exchanged for another. During their calls, tiny valves open along their legs and drink the trembling from every nearby body.

Among the ridges, individuality becomes harder to assign. Red valve-creatures lodge in vents and close their bodies around pulses of steam. Pale disks crawl over cracks, flatten themselves, and grow mineral rims until they become seals. Chain-bodied conductors stretch from pool to pool, coupling their shells to carry current. When a link burns out, its neighbors shorten themselves around the blackened remains.

These beings do not serve the ridge in any deliberate sense. The vent is warm, the leak is rich in dissolved metal, the current stimulates reproduction. Yet their appetites complete the ridge’s workings so precisely that removing them would cause the land to hemorrhage pressure and charge. The terrain is their habitat, employer, predator, and larger body.

Death only deepens that arrangement.

A dead grazer unfolds. Its broad plates separate into branching fans. Its hydraulic limbs become channels. Small creatures nest in the opened chambers, rain deposits metal around them, and the whole structure is pressed gradually into the flat. Much of the basin’s oldest “stone” is compressed anatomy: generations of shells and valves made indistinguishable from geology.

It is not inert. Storm pressure still produces microscopic contractions far below. Ancient feeding grilles open by fractions. Buried joints attempt forgotten movements. Sound travels especially well through these strata because the dead continue to pass vibrations from body to body.

This may be why extinct creatures remain nearby.

They appear chiefly in reflections: immense segmented migrants, delicate many-legged forms, floating sacks with internal lightning of unfamiliar colors. They cross pools too shallow to contain them. Living herds make room as the images pass. Needle swarms cling to their reflected backs. Crack-hunters unfold to strike at them and sometimes pull up pieces that exist only when seen indirectly.

A reflected animal can leave a wake, cast a shadow, and eventually die. Its carcass remains visible beneath the water until enough mineral gathers around the outline to give it weight. In this way an extinct species may slowly return—not resurrected whole, but precipitated from the basin’s memory of having contained it.

At the oldest margin live creatures whose bodies no longer agree with themselves. One side may have six limbs and the other a number that changes while counted. Their forward surfaces display tomorrow’s rain; their backs carry droplets from storms already absorbed into the ground. Some arrive before their own noise. Some bear wounds inflicted by predators that have not yet unfolded.

They are not distressed by this. Nothing in the basin expects a life to occur in order.

The newest offspring hatch abraded. The oldest hunters wait for prey not yet formed. A creature may spend centuries as a valve, an afternoon as part of a swarm, an age as bedrock, and then rise in reflection wearing the form it had before any of these. Species are habits the basin repeats. Bodies are temporary arrangements of water, metal, pressure, and remembered motion.

Taken together, the inhabitants may constitute one life, but not a harmonious one. They consume, crush, dismantle, conduct, seal, graze, and incorporate one another without plan. Their unity is the unity of weather or metabolism: countless local hungers producing a process too vast to possess intention.

Or the basin itself may be only the exposed surface of the true inhabitant.

Something turns beneath it without raising the ground. Water revises its sense of downward. The tripods call through strata made of the dead. Every grazer opens its vents, every drifter flashes, every chain tightens, and even the creatures that have not yet occurred become still.

Then rain rises from the flats in silver grains.

The populations move within it, grinding and pumping, assembling and coming apart. For a moment each appears less like an animal than a small mobile function of the land. Then the moment passes. The herds resume feeding. The rings seek one another. Black limbs unlock inside the cracks.

Whatever lives there continues making the place that makes it.
