What lives in the drowned high country has no face.

Life there is rooted, layered, and patient. It does not pursue or flee. It knows the world through pressure, mineral taste, vibration, and the long alternation between stillness and violence. Its quickest gestures take hours. Its migrations take centuries.

The ribbon forests are the nearest thing the upland has to a tall people. Each begins as a fist of braided fibers driven deep into a fault in the basalt. The fibers do not merely cling; they enter the stone grain by grain, dissolving some minerals and armoring themselves with others. Older trunks contain pebbles, old seed husks, splinters of silica frond, and occasionally pieces of root cord or worked mineral taken into their weave. A mature trunk is therefore partly plant, partly masonry, and partly an archive of whatever the currents have brought it.

Above the trunk, the ribbons spread into a crown loose enough for water to pass through. They have no upper and lower surfaces in the usual sense. Each strap twists continually, exposing different tissues to the faint light, the cold descents, and the nutrient-rich currents climbing from below. Their violet radiance is not simply a response to touch. It passes between neighboring plants even when their leaves do not meet, traveling through root contacts and conductive seams in the rock. A disturbed grove warns itself slowly. The warning may continue after the current has gone, crossing a mountainside in broad, dim pulses.

Individual ribbon plants are difficult to distinguish. Trunks separated by hundreds of meters may be joined beneath the sediment. One can die while its crown remains green for years, fed by the grove. Another may be torn out entirely and yet continue growing as it tumbles along a valley, putting out grasping fibers wherever it strikes stone. The forests are less collections of trees than scattered exposures of something larger.

The glass-frond beds live more sharply. Their plated leaves accumulate silica until the edges become keen and translucent. In calm water they lie folded over one another, conserving the slight warmth that rises from the ground. When the current strengthens, they lift and strike. Their chiming is part accident and part dispersal: the blows shake reproductive grains from chambers inside the plates. Broken leaves remain active. A shard lodged in a crack may soften along one edge, send out threads, and become a new bed.

Their tones vary with age and mineral content. Whole colonies can be recognized by sound. Some ring thinly and irregularly; others produce clear descending sequences as the flow moves across them. Whether the plants themselves perceive these tones is uncertain, but their growth changes around resonant stone. Beds surrounding basalt pillars arrange their leaves in concentric bands, each band answering a different frequency. After many seasons, the plants appear almost to tune the pillars by forcing silica into some cracks and prying open others.

The black moss is slower and more exact. It builds terraces because each thread grows only where a particular gradient of heat, salt, and pressure intersects the rock. As the seep shifts, one line dies and another forms above it. The result resembles deliberate cultivation: steps, basins, straight margins, even small channels carrying mineral water from one patch to the next. Yet no hand shapes them. The geometry is the moss’s body.

A moss terrace may be older than a hut built beside it. Some have grown over walls without obscuring their form, coating every slab while leaving the gaps between stones open. Around certain doorways the moss stops at a clean boundary. Removed samples curl inward and darken, then slowly creep back toward the structure from which they were taken.

The pale root-fields in the hollows are stranger because so little of them appears alive. They resemble branching mineral deposits laid bare by erosion: white limbs, fine as hair at the margins and thick as fallen trunks near their buried centers. They have neither leaves nor visible organs of perception. They feed on chemical differences in the sediment, extending toward warmth and retracting from diluted water.

At intervals, however, an entire field acts together. Thousands of branches stiffen. Hollow stalks rise. Their mouths open at the same height, and green reproductive dust is released in a single soundless exhalation. The stalks then collapse, but not always where they grew. Some bend toward old paths. Some enter huts. Some wind themselves around the forked posts in the ridge enclosure before dissolving into pulp.

It is possible that all these provinces—the ribbons, glass fronds, moss, and root-fields—are separate forms of one life. Their tissues contain the same branching violet filament, though in the moss it is nearly invisible and in the white roots it lies dormant for decades. Their reproductive dust can settle among any of them. What grows from it depends less on ancestry than on where it lands. In a warm seep it becomes moss. On an exposed ridge it plates itself in glass. In a deep crack it braids upward into a ribbon trunk.

If so, the true inhabitant of the high country is not any grove or field. It is a dispersed vegetal body occupying the whole shelf in interrupted pieces, using storms as circulation and valleys as organs. The bare plains between growths may not be empty. They may be intervals within it.

But this does not account for the knots.

Whatever tends the structures is smaller than an event and more elusive than a body. It is never seen crossing open water. It leaves no wake, displacement, heat, or track. Its work is crude, as though performed without fingers—or as though the memory of fingers has survived after their loss.

The re-tied cords are often knotted incorrectly. Some bind nothing. Some reproduce the shape of a useful knot while leaving the ends loose. Cairns are repaired with unsuitable stones that topple in the next reversal. Tools are moved between posts according to no obvious category, although over time their edges come to face outward and their handles inward. Walls are not restored so much as kept in a condition resembling ruin.

The maintenance may belong to the plants. Root fibers pass through many structures, and the slow turning of bound tools could result from growth. It may belong to the currents, though chance would have to possess an astonishing persistence. Or the builders may remain in a form for which movement is no longer passage through space.

The paths offer the strongest hint. During the gathering of darkness, they glow before the surrounding vegetation does. The contrasting marker stones contain colonies of dormant violet filament, but the light does not proceed plant by plant. It travels as if something were walking: pausing at forks, entering circles of upright rock, stopping before low doorways. Sometimes several lights move together. Sometimes one leaves the path, crosses bare sediment, and ends at the shelf’s outer cliff.

Nothing visible accompanies them.

The inverted black lake may itself be alive, although “alive” becomes uncertain around it. The perfect dark bubbles released by the moss are not empty. They have surfaces, merge, resist current, and gather at a shared level. They behave less like a substance than like portions of one missing volume returning to each other.

As the black mass forms, the vegetation attends it. Ribbon crowns incline. Root stalks open. Glass fronds cease chiming even while the surrounding currents continue. The entire upland seems to orient toward an unseen center within the darkness.

Perhaps the mass is a reproductive body assembled by the country: not a seed, but a space in which something can take shape without being exposed to water, cold, sound, or light. Perhaps it is the shadow of a creature vast enough to exist elsewhere, touching the shelf only under thick ice. Perhaps it is where the absent builders have gone, and the crude repairs are made during those weeks when their old routes and gestures briefly project back into the drowned world.

No form has ever emerged from it. Nothing has been observed entering it. When it breaks apart, it leaves no remains.

Yet afterward the structures have changed.

Thus the upland’s inhabitants cannot be counted. They might be innumerable plants and no one else. They might be a single organism wearing forests, mosses, and root-fields as seasonal organs. They might include a remnant community reduced to habits without bodies: path-walking, wall-stacking, tool-turning impulses preserved in roots and resonant stone. Or the whole shelf—its vegetation, currents, ruins, suspended darkness, and abyss-facing arrangements—may be one slow creature still attempting to remember what it was before the ocean covered it.

Whatever lives there does not watch.

It inclines. It resonates. It repairs without understanding. It waits through the clear intervals, and when the ice closes out the last dim stain of day, it gathers itself around an emptiness that seems, for a time, almost inhabited.
