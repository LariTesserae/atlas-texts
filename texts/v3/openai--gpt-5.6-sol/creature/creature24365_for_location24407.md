Nothing in the Black Sill lives alone.

The moss is the easiest thing to mistake for an individual. A hand pressed into one of its folded islands feels thousands of cool, resilient stalks bending together, each no thicker than hair. Yet beneath them runs a shared rind, pale and rubbery, stretched across the stone. The rind remembers pressure. Hours after a basin walker passes, its six footfalls remain as firmer patches, and the moss arranges its openings along those impressions to drink from the next gathering of water. Tear away a piece and both halves seal themselves, but for several years they grow toward one another, carrying the separation like an unfinished thought.

Within the mats live the keepers of decay: colorless mites with soft mineral teeth, jointed worms that pass strands of fungus through their bodies without digesting them, and colonial films that can spread themselves one cell thick over the underside of a fallen frond. They dismantle the dead in sequence. One loosens wax. Another removes salts. Another takes the long fibers apart without breaking them, feeding them into the roots of nearby plants. A corpse may keep its shape for decades while its substance is quietly distributed elsewhere.

Nothing is hurried into soil.

The shell-backed grazers are more mobile, though only slightly more separate. Each is a low oval protected by overlapping plates of stone-colored horn. They have neither eyes nor a forward-facing head. A ring of feeding limbs surrounds the underside, so they can travel in any direction without turning. They rasp the rock for mineral algae and taste the returning vibration through their plates.

Alone, one makes a whisper.

Together, they make weather.

Their colonies move across the open pavement in fronts too broad to comprehend by touch. The leading animals call by scraping one edge of the shell against another; those behind answer at fractionally lower pitches. The resulting roar contains gradients of depth, water, obstruction, and distance. A grazer does not know the Sill as a map. It knows where it stands inside the song.

If lifted from the colony and placed on bare stone, it begins to scrape louder and louder. Without an answer it soon ceases feeding. After a day it folds its limbs beneath itself and produces a thin internal ticking, the final signal by which others may find it. Silence does not merely frighten these creatures. Silence erases their location from them.

The drumming insects live in the roots. They are wingless, long-legged, and almost weightless, with abdomens shaped like little sounding boards. Every hollow plant has its own population and therefore its own rhythm. When water enters the plant’s storage bladders, the pitch of the stem changes; the insects detect this and shift their drumming to match. Their music may help the roots open and close their valves. It may also warn them before a discharge.

Just before the deep wooden knock, every insect becomes still.

Just after it, they drum in frenzy while water drives through the buried channels around them.

The long amphibians inhabit those channels. Their bodies are narrow enough to knot, their limbs reduced to delicate hooks with which they brace against flowing water. They breathe through pleated skins and call by compressing their entire length. A single animal can turn a flooded fracture into a pipe. Some calls travel through several pools before emerging, altered by each chamber, so that one amphibian may seem to answer itself from across the enclosure.

They hatch without mouths. For their first season they feed on secretions from the rock films and learn the pressure-signatures of their home fissure. Only after the stone’s acoustic pattern is fixed in them do their jaws open. Adults displaced into unfamiliar cracks often refuse to hunt. They spend days calling into the wrong geometry, searching for the echo in which they first became themselves.

There are hunters among the reeds: soft-bodied things like hands with too many fingers, each finger ending in a pressure-sensitive cup. They stand with their palms raised into the air and imitate the drumming of root insects. When something answers, the fingers close around the reply. They eat rarely. Between meals they bury their central bodies in mud and allow reeds to root through them, borrowing sugar from the plants and giving back nitrogen, warmth, and small rhythmic movements that keep the water circulating.

Even predation here tends toward partnership.

The basin walkers are the nearest things the Sill has to sovereign animals, though they do not rule it. Their six limbs spread from a low, long body, each foot surrounded by a broad pad divided into rings. They can place the outer ring first, listen through it, and withdraw without committing their weight. In this way they test for nests, flooded hollows, unstable stone, and the thin sleeping bodies of amphibians.

Their skin is warm, naked, and deeply creased. Almost none of it can be touched without touching another organism. Fungi live in the creases. Ferns root along the spine. Pale tendrils hang from the flanks and swell when the walker is hungry, releasing odors copied from the moss islands. Small grazers sometimes climb onto the animal and feed across its back, mistaking it for moving ground.

A walker’s garden changes over its life. Young ones carry soft molds that mask them from reed hunters. Adults cultivate bitter shelf-fungi and tough, dark ferns. The oldest bear whole layered communities, including flowering structures found nowhere else in the Sill. These open not to air but against the walker’s skin, sending threads into its blood. Whether the animal plants the garden or the garden makes the animal hospitable is impossible to determine.

There are perhaps nine walkers, but nine is not quite the right number.

They call below the range of ordinary hearing. The pulses pass through pavement, water, roots, bodies, and the suspended frames. Each walker receives echoes already crossed by the calls of the others. Over a lifetime their voices become entangled. An elder may answer from another walker’s chest. A calf separated from its parent can reproduce the pulse-pattern of the whole group. At times all nine move as though sharing one remote sense of touch, lifting the same foot in succession while something heavy travels beneath them.

They do not sleep lying down. They settle over resonant fractures and lower their bodies until the sternum meets the stone. Their pulse slows. Their gardens fold. For several days they listen to whatever moves below.

That deeper inhabitant is never seen.

It may not have a body in the ordinary sense. The pavement rises under it in long, gradual swells. Air is driven from cracks. Pools tremble without spilling. Once or twice in a human lifetime, a warm ridge passes beneath the central expanse slowly enough to follow on foot, though there is nothing to follow except pressure and the organ-deep note produced as the stone flexes.

Some signs suggest a burrowing animal of impossible size. Others suggest a colony distributed through the plateau: muscular chambers occupying old volcanic tubes, connected by films no thicker than roots. It may be the source of the warmth. It may lift dissolved minerals and feed them toward the surface. It may be no more an animal than a forest is an animal.

The basin walkers call to it.

Sometimes it calls back.

The upright frames also belong among the inhabitants, although they do not eat, breed, or visibly choose. Condensation climbs them as though answering a thirst. Scratches close. Their bases tend the rock with crystalline precision, replacing grains not merely where they fit but where they once lay. Each frame appears to maintain a limited territory. Beyond a certain radius, erosion proceeds normally. Inside it, lost forms return by increments.

Yet their territories overlap.

A fracture repaired by one frame may be reopened centuries later by another. A pool can be deepened, filled, and deepened again. The frames do not preserve the Sill in one condition. They preserve its range of conditions, allowing change but refusing certain losses. They behave less like monuments than like slow organs of correction.

Animals perceive signals from them that no instrument has isolated. When a frame is touched, the quiet spreads outward in rings. Grazers stop rasping. Root insects arrest their drums. Amphibians hold their compressed bodies rigid in the fissures. Even basin walkers pause with their gardens trembling.

Then life resumes, often in a different rhythm.

The low shelter at the center is the one place no creature claims, and for that reason it may be the most inhabited place of all.

The depressions in its floor do not belong to one species or era. Some fit narrow hands. Some fit broad, weight-bearing knees. Some are grooves where rigid implements rested for years. Others occur in sets around the perimeter, as if bodies with radial limbs once folded themselves beneath the roof. The marks overlap without erasing one another. Their edges remain dry and sharp.

A visitor crawling inside carries the only living noise permitted there: breath, heartbeat, the movement of skin against stone. Even these sounds return changed. The altered voices are not random. They seem to select from possibilities already latent in the speaker—the old throat hidden in the young one, the childhood cadence buried under years of speech, pronunciations that the mouth might someday learn.

Perhaps the shelter stores no voices. Perhaps it exposes the many speakers contained in a single life.

Perhaps that is what once knelt there: not builders, but listeners.

The thing beneath the floor produces its drop at intervals no local creature appears able to predict. There is no visible opening overhead and no wetness on the dry stone. Nevertheless the drop falls from below, or from a direction that sound interprets as below. It strikes once.

The Sill hears it as a command, a summons, or the renewal of an agreement.

Small animals crowd the shelter’s edge and face inward. Basin walkers halt on the outer pavement. The frames gather beads along their sides. Deep under the stone, the unseen mass tightens. Then every living system answers in its own language: rasp, knock, pulse, drum, pressure, transmitted water.

What lives in the Black Sill may therefore be counted in several ways.

There are millions of grazers, innumerable mites, root drummers, fissure amphibians, reed hunters, fungal colonies, and nine patient walkers.

There are the frames, each maintaining its measured portion of an ancient shape.

There is something beneath the plateau, vast enough to be mistaken for geology.

And there is the Sill’s chorus: a creature without flesh of its own, assembled whenever all the others sound together. It has the rounded ridges for a skull, the flooded cracks for a throat, the upright frames for organs of memory, and the basin walkers’ low calls for thought. It sleeps dispersed among countless bodies. Rain gathers it. The falling drop awakens it.

For days it becomes coherent.

Then the calls thin, the pools settle, and the walkers separate into darkness. The greater life comes apart without dying. Its pieces return to feeding, repairing, eroding, and waiting, each alone in its allotted space, each carrying one small necessary portion of the whole.
