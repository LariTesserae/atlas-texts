At first, the hollow seems to have only incidental inhabitants.

There are the nightmoths, thin enough to mistake for loose skins. In full light they are nearly invisible: two flattened wings, a soft hinge of a body, six legs finer than root hairs. Only when one of the wandering nights passes over them do they acquire color. Then faint veins appear in their wings, branching in patterns that resemble neither insect wings nor leaves but maps of places the hollow has not yet opened into.

They do not flee brightness. They feed upon it.

A moth settles at the edge of a dark patch and extends a glassy mouthpart into the illuminated air. Nothing visible is drawn in, but the air puckers. When the moth withdraws, a black point remains—perfectly round, apparently no larger than a pinprick, though looking directly into it gives the impression of tremendous depth. Some close within seconds. Others persist for days, drifting independently of wind and surface. The plants bend around these punctures. Roots avoid them. Once in a while, something on the far side appears to look back.

The moths sleep only in darkness, and their sleep is not passive. They fold themselves into the shapes of seeds. During long local nights they can be seen trembling with the effort of some internal metamorphosis, but when light returns they are unchanged. Perhaps the transformation takes longer than one lifetime. Perhaps each moth dreams itself into the seed from which an ancestor emerged.

The root crabs appear more purposeful.

They come out with the rain, squeezing through green pores in the floor as if the thick living membrane were water. Their legs unfold one joint at a time. Bark, amber scales, dried pith, and chips of pale wall tissue are fixed to their backs in irregular mosaics. Older crabs may carry miniature gardens: a cushion of moss, a curled fern no longer than a fingernail, one white flower opening and closing at the rhythm of the chamber’s pulse.

They are custodians, though not obedient ones. They trim dead fibers, scrape fungal films from the wet seams, sever roots that have entered the wrong vessels, and carry every clipping to a receptive fissure. Before offering the material, each crab turns it repeatedly in its claws, testing its weight or reading something written in its grain. Pieces judged unsuitable are not discarded. They are fitted to the shell.

Thus the oldest crabs become archives of injury. On their backs are fragments of every wound the hollow has survived: tissue scorched by alien light, root bark from before the chamber widened, callused membranes grown over vanished entrances, fibers marked by tools whose makers have long since returned to the dry world. Some bear scraps that cannot have come from this chamber at all—blue rind, black reeds, slivers of wood in which stars seem to move.

A crab disturbed in its work rises high on its legs and opens its mouthparts. It makes no threat. Instead it repeats, with extraordinary accuracy, the last sound made near the fragment it is carrying: a rainburst, a snapped root, a whispered name, sometimes a human cry centuries old. Then it folds itself and disappears into the floor.

Yet neither moths nor crabs are what truly lives there.

They are more like mobile functions of the place: appetite and repair given small bodies. They follow the drifting nights and brief waters because those are the conditions that permit them to become separate. When the hollow is dry and uniformly bright, they withdraw so completely that it is possible they cease to be animals at all.

The larger inhabitant is harder to separate from its surroundings.

Travelers call it many things: the Under-Grazer, the Root Whale, the Green Blindness, the Animal Behind the Wall. No name survives long without becoming misleading.

A flank is sometimes seen moving through a translucent membrane. It is smooth, colorless, and enormous, but not consistently curved. One witness sees the side of a long-bodied creature swimming through tissue. Another, standing only a few paces away, sees a pale human shoulder passing behind the wall. A third sees nothing except vessels pressed aside by a pressure that travels against the flow of sap.

Its eyes are more common than its body. They open in rows along the floor after heavy rain: dark, wet ellipses with no whites and no visible lids. They do not follow movement. Instead, different eyes open ahead of a traveler, so that attention seems to anticipate rather than track. Touching one produces no blink. The surface yields like the flesh of a fruit, and the hand comes away scented with childhood rain, old fear, or whatever memory the toucher most closely associates with being watched.

Occasionally an eye opens beneath a sleeping person and remains there until morning.

Those sleepers dream of standing above the sun-hot plain with their feet sunk deep into soil. In the dream, they can feel every stem for miles turning toward daylight. They feel rain strike the ground as a thought spread over immense skin. They feel the hidden chamber below as a small cool opening inside themselves—an emptiness that aches when occupied.

Afterward, they often insist that the eyes are not organs of the larger creature.

They are wounds through which the hollow looks inward.

During sap surges, the inhabitant enters the central space.

No outline precedes it. Hanging roots bend toward an invisible volume, but the volume has no fixed edge. Rain changes direction around it. Moths alight on surfaces no one can see and are carried in slow arcs through the air. A person caught in its passage feels neither impact nor wind. They feel, instead, a temporary loosening of their arrangement: the heart seeming to beat several paces away, one hand growing remote, the voice arriving from under the floor. For a moment the body is no longer a single place.

Those who remain still are usually unharmed.

Those who reach for it sometimes touch something warm and faintly furred. Others find wet bark, scales like overlapping leaves, or bare skin covered in gooseflesh. One traveler thrust both arms into the invisible body and withdrew them stained yellow with pollen. Months later, flowers began opening beneath her fingernails. Another felt a ribcage containing not lungs but a windy forest. A third touched his own back.

The creature never leaves prints. After its passage, however, footprints belonging to everyone present appear across the floor in routes they have not yet walked. Some lead to walls that later open. Some stop at fissures. Some circle a patch of brightness and vanish beneath it. Careful travelers study these tracks but do not always obey them. A footprint here is no more necessarily a prediction than a memory is necessarily of the past.

There may be no large animal.

What moves could be the hollow attempting, briefly, to occupy the vacancy it has preserved. The walls grow inward by instinct and withdraw by another instinct. Roots approach the center, hesitate, and turn aside. Leaves illuminate it but do not descend into it. Seeds crossing it hurry, falling upward or skipping the distance altogether. Everything botanical maintains the room’s emptiness with the tense precision of flesh held away from a wound.

During growth surges, that restraint weakens. For several minutes the place tries on a body.

It borrows eyes from the floor, skin from the walls, pulse from the vascular chambers, appetite from the moths, and articulated motion from the crabs. It gathers these traits around the central absence without ever completely joining them. The result is perceived as an animal because an animal is the nearest shape the mind has for something that is alive, bounded, and moving with intent.

But the intent is uncertain.

The inhabitant does not hunt. It does not graze, mate, nest, defend territory, or sleep. It seems to practice presence.

It passes again and again through the empty center as if learning how to be located. Each passage is a little more coherent. The bending roots describe a spine. The moths settle where a face might be. The local nights gather beneath it like a shadow. The slow chamber-pulse accelerates until, for three or four beats, it matches the heart of anyone standing nearby.

Then coherence fails. The borrowed body opens. Its parts return to the surfaces from which they came, and the hollow resumes its bright vacancy.

This may be why permanent dwellers are not tolerated. A shelter, a monument, even a ring of stones gives the center too definite a meaning. The chamber takes such things apart because it is preserving not mere space but possibility. It cannot allow the emptiness to become a campsite, a sanctuary, a grave, or a home. It is already reserved for an arrival that has perhaps been happening since before the hollow formed.

Visitors who stay too long begin to participate.

Their hair lifts toward the ceiling light. The patterns in their palms deepen into leaf-veins. Their shadows detach during the little nights and continue sleeping after they wake. They develop an awareness of water moving behind the walls. Eventually they find themselves stepping aside before roots twitch, answering words that have not yet returned as echoes, or waking with mud between their teeth and the taste of sunlight in their mouths.

The hollow does not transform them into plants in any simple sense. It makes them less certain where their living ends.

If they leave in time, the changes mostly recede. A few remain: an intolerance for roofs, dreams of rain falling upward, scars that green briefly each spring. Some can place a hand against an ordinary tree and hear, very far below, the rolling impact of the chamber’s pulse.

Those who do not leave are never found as bodies.

Sometimes, after many years, a new tall blade appears near the center. It is narrow, solitary, and unlike the low growth around it. It bends when spoken to. Along its edge are small irregular notches resembling teeth, fingernails, or the marks a person might cut to count days. It may persist for one rain cycle or several seasons. Eventually the floor draws it down, leaving the center clear again.

The crabs collect nothing from where it stood.

So the truest inhabitant may not be the chamber, the unseen creature, or any transformed visitor. It may be the absence itself: not emptiness as lack, but an unborn organism for which the hollow is shell, womb, and rehearsal.

The plants sustain it by refusing to fill it. The moths nibble openings through which it may sense other places. The crabs preserve the history of every failed emergence. The great half-creature is its repeated attempt to imagine a form. The chamber’s tangled distances are the first motions of limbs not yet committed to direction. Its confused causes are the thoughts of a being for whom before and after have not finished separating.

When all the leaves turn toward a brightness with no location, they are not seeking light.

They are attending.

And in the center, where there seems to be nothing, something continues—slowly, patiently, and with the full labor of the buried green world around it—to live before it has learned how to exist.
