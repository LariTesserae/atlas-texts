The people who live in the bowl are broad-handed, dark-eyed, and seldom dry.

Their clothes are layered rather than thick: felted undergarments, supple hides, reed mats cut into shoulder capes, narrow strips of fur wrapped around joints. The outermost layer is often stiff with frost and must be beaten against a wall before it can be removed. Sleeves end above the wrist so the hands can read wet stone. Boots have thin, replaceable soles of hide and plaited fiber; a person prefers cold feet to feet unable to feel a shifting stair.

Nearly everyone carries three things: a hooked tool for clearing ice, a coil of cord, and a shallow black cup. The cup may hold tea, lamp oil, mortar, meltwater, an eel fry, or a pebble used to signal a change of flow. It is also placed under unfamiliar drips. No one passes new water without tasting it.

Their faces are difficult for visitors to read. Soot is rubbed in broad bands around the eyes, and outside they wear horn visors with narrow horizontal openings. Many have pale scars on their cheeks where reflected light burned them in childhood. Their hair is kept short at the forehead, long behind, and braided with beads whose materials indicate practical obligations rather than rank: blue clay for a cistern keeper, perforated bone for someone responsible for an upper gate, dull copper for a person trained to enter submerged chambers. Children wear bright calcite beads so they can be found quickly in fog or spray.

They do not speak softly because they are solemn. They speak softly because loudness is wasteful here.

Conversation is conducted at arm’s length. Hands interrupt, amend, and answer the voice. Fingers show where water moves; wrists describe pressure; the back of the hand indicates cold coming through stone. A mason can recount the condition of an entire house with one palm held at an angle. People arguing over drainage may appear to outsiders to be performing a slow and courteous dance.

Their spoken language is full of words for temporary states. There are separate terms for a wall wet from within and one wet from spray; for ice that bears weight once, ice that bears weight repeatedly, and ice that only appears to bear weight from a distance. A room reached through a new doorway is not called a room until it has remained for three nights. Before that it is “an offered inside.”

Permanent nouns are used cautiously.

A household is not everyone sleeping behind the same walls. It is everyone who wakes for the same water. Several families may share a spring, a drain, a sloping roof, or the labor of keeping one channel open through winter, and thereby become one household for a season. When the flow changes, kinship changes with it. An old aunt may belong to three households at once because she knows three separate systems of gates. A child may be raised by whoever lives downstream.

Descent is remembered through obligations. People say, “My mother’s people cleared the western fall,” or, “His grandfathers kept the warm drain during the seven black winters.” The dead are not assigned unmoving graves. Bodies are wrapped in reed and hide, laid in cold niches, and later removed when the mountain requires the space. Clean bones are made into handles, clasps, flutes, measuring pins, and hinges. To be useful in a descendant’s hand is considered a more durable burial than stone.

No one is ashamed to ask whose grandmother closes a particular door.

Children spend their earliest years tethered loosely to adults by bright cords. This is not only to stop them falling. Alleys alter, glare erases edges, and a child chasing a black bird may step confidently into reflected sky. As soon as they can walk, they are taught the handrails. Blindfolded games are common: racing from hearth to cistern by touch, identifying houses from the wear of their threshold stones, naming a passage from the direction of cold against one cheek.

They learn animals next.

A child knows which drain rodents may occupy and which must be kept clear. They can distinguish a cistern bird merely resting from one warning of pressure beneath the ice. They are taught never to grab a pale lizard by the tail, never to stand behind a horned grazer on a sloping roof, and never to follow a silver fox through an opening, however easily the fox appears to pass.

Eels are handled with particular respect. Their seasonal movements are read publicly in the White Court, but individual families also keep private records: strings knotted for direction, depth, color of organs, speed of ascent. Two keepers may disagree bitterly over whether an eel has buried its head from fear of shaking or because the silt is warm. Such disputes can last generations and are inherited along with the observation channels.

The people do not worship the eels. They insist on this, especially when accused of it.

Nor do they worship the mountain, the cisterns, the water, the changing doors, the detached shadows, or the objects found beneath the White Court. Worship, in their view, would imply that these things require persuasion. They do not. The mountain is treated more like an ancient participant in civic life: unreasonable in detail, reliable in appetite, entitled to make alterations, and never absent from a meeting.

The closest thing to priests are the listeners. They lie with an ear or cheek against walls and floors, sometimes for hours, marking faint tremors with chalk or wetted fingertips. Most are older people whose hearing in open air has grown poor. What they detect may be less sound than pressure: the gathering of water under paving, the creep of frozen stone, the hollowing of a buried pocket. A skilled listener can order six houses emptied by raising one finger. No one demands an explanation until afterward.

There are also path keepers, ice cutters, seep tasters, cord measurers, dung renderers, animal negotiators, roof walkers, blind masons, and those who maintain the black-wool interiors where dazzled eyes recover. Trades overlap. Specialization cannot be rigid in a place where a tannery may become a bridge overnight.

The most admired workers are not those who build longest-lasting structures. They are those whose work fails well.

A good wall, when overtaken by frost, loosens in harmless sections. A good bridge announces its strain through the handrail. A good roof yields its ice into an empty court rather than a sleeping room. The finest houses are full of seams, gaps, removable stones, sacrificial panels, and doors with no present destination. Builders carve their marks on pieces intended to be replaced. Pride lies in anticipating disappearance.

Consequently, people who arrive from lower lands often mistake the inhabitants for careless or resigned. They are neither. They are ferociously attentive. Nothing is abandoned merely because it is temporary. A clay gate meant to last one afternoon may be smoothed, fitted, and decorated with a thumbprint border. A woven footway used for a single thaw may take twenty people to balance correctly. Beauty is not expected to survive; it is expected to function precisely while it exists.

Their disputes are exacting. Since land itself cannot be trusted, arguments concern sequence, access, and consequence. Who failed to clear a channel? Who diverted washing water before the drinkers had filled their jars? Who allowed ice to build over a neighbor’s light opening? Who claimed a warm ledge while an animal was still sleeping there?

Judgments are made in the White Court. The injured party pours a cup of water over the paving and shows, with stones and cords, what ought to have happened. The accused redirects the model to show what did. Witnesses alter it in turn. By the end, the argument may have become a miniature watershed crowded with pebbles, bits of bone, scraps of hide, and live beetles standing in for people. Punishment usually consists of maintenance: several nights at an upper gate, replacing worn handrails, clearing frost from roofs belonging to those too old to climb.

Deliberate obstruction is different. Someone who secretly seals a seep and lets pressure gather behind another household’s wall is considered dangerous in a way that theft or assault is not. Such a person may be denied passage across roofs. Because every life depends upon passage, this is nearly exile.

Hospitality begins with darkness. A guest is first taken inside, seated away from ice glare, and given warm cloths for the eyes. Only when vision settles are names exchanged. Tea follows, brewed weak and salty, often flavored with fungus oil. Food is dense: cakes of ground lichen and fat, boiled eggs from the long-legged water birds, eel broth, sour curds from the six-horned grazers. Meat is shared in thin shavings. Crunching is disliked, not as bad manners but because it conceals the small sounds of a house under strain.

At meals people tell stories of errors.

Their heroes are seldom conquerors. They are distracted apprentices who notice the wrong icicle, children who release animals just before a roof falls, elderly gate keepers who mistake one channel for another and thereby save a district. Cleverness is respected, but fortunate correction is loved. Every story leaves room for the mountain to have intended something else.

Laughter is mostly visible. Shoulders shake; mouths open; people slap their own knees or grip one another’s sleeves. Sound dies so quickly that a crowd’s laughter becomes an assembly of private breaths. Singing is performed through floors and handrails. One person hums against stone while others place palms along the wall, feeling harmonies as vibration. Their longest songs may be inaudible to anyone standing in the same room without touching the building.

Courtship often begins in shared maintenance. Two people volunteer repeatedly for the same freezing watch, or one repairs the other’s section of handrail with conspicuous elegance. Lovers exchange routes rather than jewelry: secret ways across roofs, reliable steps through flooded alleys, a niche from which an ice halo can be seen without blinding the eyes. To know another person’s safest path home is an intimacy.

Marriage, when it occurs, is marked by mingling two cups of water and then finding a use for the mixture. It might be drunk, added to mortar, poured into an animal trough, or released into a new channel. The choice is interpreted but not planned. If the water vanishes into a crack, everyone considers this ambiguous but not necessarily unfortunate.

The elderly become less mobile and more central. They sit near warm drains with rodents heaped around their ankles, repairing cords, sharpening horn, and remembering previous arrangements of the city. Since passages recur without warning, an old person’s memory may suddenly become practical. A grandmother who has not climbed in twenty years might recognize a newly opened stair as one she used when young and know that its seventh step is hollow.

Some inhabitants spend much of their lives beneath the White Court. They are compact, scarred people with powerful shoulders and waxed braids. They enter the older cisterns naked except for cords and bone mouthpieces, slipping through water too cold for others to endure. Afterward they are wrapped in hot hides and questioned before they sleep, because memories of the submerged chambers fade quickly. These divers describe passages that contract around them, currents that seem to choose among bodies, and smooth terraces bearing marks like fingerprints larger than houses.

They disagree about whether something lives below.

Some say the whole mountain is alive, but only in the loose sense that weather is alive. Others believe the cistern system is an animal of mineral and water, too slow to perceive a person yet capable of growing tools the way flesh grows teeth. A few divers speak of encountering a presence in the black water: not a body, but an arrangement of pressure that moved around them with curiosity.

These accounts are neither endorsed nor forbidden. The city has little use for certainty that cannot redirect a channel.

The mineral objects are accepted in much the same way. Children play sorting games among those on the eastern wall, though they are forbidden to put fingers through unfamiliar rings. Craftspeople periodically borrow a shape and test it as a weight, brace, funnel, musical instrument, latch, or animal fitting. Some objects remain meaningless for centuries before a new doorway, injury, or pattern of ice reveals their function. Others may never be meant for human use.

At night, when the objects shift, watchers sometimes shift with them. By morning a keeper may be found sleeping several paces from where they sat, still wrapped in blankets, with no memory of moving. This is treated as evidence of participation rather than possession.

The animals participate too. The rodents are tolerated as drain-warmers and leak finders. The silent black birds clear insects from damp walls. Cistern birds keep open small circles of water around their legs. Foxes thin the rodents but steal oil cakes; they are cursed while escape holes are carefully left for them. The six-horned grazers are milked, combed, sheltered, argued with, and never entirely owned. When they return wearing foreign grass, people unweave it and store the strands in sealed jars. There are colors in those fibers seen nowhere on the mountain.

Thus the city’s population cannot be counted cleanly. A census taken at dawn is wrong by noon. Households divide with channels. Beasts arrive from blank ridges. Doors admit neighbors who yesterday lived far away. Shadows stray. Water rises from below bearing eels, warmth, blue mud, and manufactured stone.

Even the people are not considered wholly fixed. They say a person has one body but many positions, and character is the pattern made between them. Someone may be patient at a cistern, reckless on a roof, generous upstream, selfish below, courageous in glare, terrified of rooms that widen during storms. No single condition reveals the truth.

What lives in the bowl, then, is less a settled people than a continuous act of accommodation.

They are soot-eyed figures moving through brightness with one hand on the rail. They are builders who leave gaps, owners of routes instead of ground, descendants of useful bones, readers of eels, tasters of frost, neighbors to animals that do not belong to them. They are skeptical, superstitious, practical, and tender toward anything seeking warmth. They distrust walls without openings and promises without alternate paths.

Each evening they race the descending freeze, carrying jars, cords, gates, children, tools, and half-finished meals. Each night their city rearranges around them. Each morning they wake, discover what has become connected, and begin again—not restoring what was there before, but learning what now lives beside them.
