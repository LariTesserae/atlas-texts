What lives there is difficult to count, because the distinction between an animal, a herd, and a process does not remain stable.

At a distance, the inhabitants seem monumental and separate. They cross the plateau as dark, many-legged bodies, each carrying its own heat, armor, organs, and burden of ore. Seen closer, they dissolve into partnerships. A furnace-beast’s plates host mites that polish away oxidation. Its grinding chambers contain blind, heat-loving colonies that sort powdered stone by taste. Cable-thin organisms carry charge between organs and are replaced continuously, though sometimes they leave through a wound and live independently for years. Even the sparks blown from its vents may be reproductive forms: incandescent specks that cool into six-legged black grains and begin crawling toward mineral seams.

What appears to be one creature may therefore be a landscape of smaller creatures moving in agreement.

The great grazers are the most visible. Their bodies stand high enough that wind passes beneath them in a constant abrasive river. Six or eight legs support a long central mass of furnaces, reduction sacs, magnetic stomachs, pressure bladders, and slow flywheels grown from interlocking rings of bone-metal. No two legs are precisely alike. One may end in a broad ceramic pad for glass flats, another in hooked digits for clinker, another in a vibrating wedge that tests the ground before accepting the animal’s weight.

Their heads hang low, though “head” is mostly a useful approximation. The broad intake at the front gathers powdered ore; awareness is distributed elsewhere. Along the underside are rows of magnetic nodules that register the plateau’s buried structure. In the joints are pressure-sensitive membranes that listen through stone. Dorsal vanes taste electrical fields. A grazer does not look ahead so much as feel itself approaching from many directions.

Its decisions take time.

When it encounters an obstruction, the first response may be a minute change in current through the forward limbs. Days later, rear organs adjust their rotation. Weeks after that, the whole body has chosen whether to step over the obstacle or walk around it. What resembles dullness is an awareness fitted to a much larger present. A grazer can feel a distant migration through ground resonance before the intervening herd has completed its first step. It may begin avoiding a collision thirty years in advance.

The young do not resemble their elders. They emerge pale, soft-bodied, and nearly boneless from extrusion folds beneath the adults. Their insulating coats ripple like fine ash. For their first decades they travel in the thermal shadows between a parent’s legs, feeding on sifted waste and swallowing selected fragments of its shed bearings. Within them, metals precipitate along fibrous templates. Flexible shafts stiffen. Soft pulsing organs acquire teeth. Their first true sound is a faint internal ticking, irregular and private.

Then comes the hardening.

The young stop beside a vent or warm fracture and remain motionless while their outer tissues vitrify. Some do not survive. They slump into glossy heaps and are consumed by siblings. Those that rise again are darker, heavier, and newly resonant. Their gait becomes audible through the ground. Over centuries the ticking deepens into grinding, then into the long bass note characteristic of adults.

They inherit no territory in the ordinary sense. They inherit paths.

A migration groove is memory made physical. Each generation deepens it and is guided by the depth left by generations before. Magnetic patterns in the rock trigger sequences in the herd’s bodies: turn here, open vents there, slow the left limbs, begin casting young after the third sulfur fan. The route instructs the animal, and the animal rewrites the route. Taken over five hundred years, the migration is both instinct and inscription.

This is why a herd may be one mind.

Individual bodies exchange charge through airborne chains during dry electrical storms. Their magnetic organs synchronize. Rotations fall into ratios. A pressure disturbance passing through the line becomes, in effect, a thought distributed across thousands of kilometers. No member possesses the whole thought. Some carry its beginning, others its qualification, others the slow mechanical equivalent of doubt. The conclusion may not occur until the leading animals have died and their descendants reach the far basin.

Predators live at the margins of these thoughts.

They are lower than the grazers and much longer, fitted to the old grooves like articulated shadows. Their armor overlaps backward so that dust and sparks slide over them. At the front, delicate arrays of shears continually open and close, not in hunger but in measurement. They sample fallen flakes, read stress lines in slag, test the warmth of recent footprints. Once they select prey, they become almost impossible to distinguish from its wake.

A hunter does not rush. It joins.

Month by month it matches the target’s cadence. Its limbs strike the ground at complementary intervals, hiding their resonance inside the larger animal’s footfalls. Its heat rises and falls with the prey’s venting cycle. Gradually, the hunter becomes a missing part of the victim’s own mechanical rhythm. When it finally comes within reach, the prey may have been carrying the predator’s pattern in its senses for years without recognizing an external presence.

The attack often begins before contact. A narrow induction field warms buried fastenings under the armor. Bearings deform. Conductive nerves soften and sag. One leg begins to answer a fraction too late. The grazer compensates, redistributing weight, but the correction creates new stresses elsewhere. The predator continues behind it, patient as corrosion.

Collapse may take a season.

When the grazer finally lowers itself, the hunter opens it according to a sequence older than any living member of either species. Plates are lifted and stacked. Hot organs are allowed to cool. Iron-rich tissues are eaten first, charge-bearing tissues later, silicates almost never. The neatness of the remains suggests ritual, but there is no reverence in it—only a digestive system unable to proceed in any other order.

Then the smaller lives arrive.

Wheel organisms roll down the grooves in wavering columns. Each is a ring of hard tissue around an empty center, with sensory filaments on the rim and mouths hidden inside the hub. On level ground they move quickly; on a carcass they topple sideways and become rotary cutters, stripping oxidation and soft metal from exposed surfaces.

Cable worms knot themselves through abandoned conduits. Some still the twitching nerves by draining their charge. Others stimulate them, causing dead limbs to lift or vents to open. An emptied carcass may walk several steps under the conflicting instructions of a million scavengers before collapsing in a new arrangement.

Clicking crown-creatures surround anything warm. They consist of many legs radiating from a hollow core, without an obvious front or back. Each leg can detach, forage separately, and return bearing filings. When threatened, the entire crown breaks apart. The legs scatter like black calipers and reassemble days later, not always with their original companions. Their identity lies in the pattern of assembly rather than in any enduring material.

Above these work the static grazers: wide, nearly transparent organisms thin enough to buckle in the heated air. They have no rigid skeleton. Branching conductive veins hold them open through mutual repulsion. They drift over fresh remains and lower themselves across the metal like sheets laid on a table. Residual voltage runs through them in blue pulses. Fed, they loosen and rise. Their bodies retain brief luminous maps of whatever nervous system they have emptied.

Some of the plateau’s inhabitants never fully emerge from the crust.

Deep hammering colonies occupy fractures and porous slag beds. They are communal animals whose bodies consist of separated pressure chambers connected by mineral-grown tubes. One chamber may lie kilometers from the next. They communicate by striking the surrounding stone. A single message can require hundreds of blows spread over a decade.

Their reproductive organs occasionally rise aboveground as chimneys. These narrow structures seem geological until their lips unfold and discharge clouds of angular spores. Each spore contains a grain of magnetic material wrapped in desiccated tissue. Wind carries them into cracks, where they wait for heat and pressure rather than water. When conditions are right, the tissue softens into a corrosive film, eats a chamber into the rock, and begins to hammer.

The flyers are stranger. They are not light, but they exploit the plateau’s hot ascending air and strong electrical gradients. Vast vanes sweep from bodies no larger than a grazer’s skull. The vanes are edged in metal and jointed like folding rulers. Each downstroke takes several heartbeats, bending the air with a deep report. Between strokes, the creature glides for hours.

They feed on vent plumes, filtering fluorine salts, glass dust, and charged particles. Long combs trail behind them, sparkling faintly. Their droppings cool into hollow ceramic beads that roll for centuries before wearing through. During mating, several flyers lock vane to vane and become a single revolving structure, broad enough to eclipse a district of sky. Whether they separate afterward depends on how their joints regrow. Some remain joined for life and acquire additional partners with each season, becoming slow airborne wheels composed of dozens of fused animals.

Near the center, none of these forms remains singular.

There are feeders built around spatial defects. Their bodies fork without dividing: one torso, two mouths, each mouth occupying an opposing edge of the same repeated distance. They consume a stone from one side while the stone persists on the other. The process can continue for centuries, turning duplicated matter into heat, armor, and offspring. Such animals grow obese and motionless, surrounded by ridges made from their own discarded plates.

Others inhabit loops. A loop grazer may appear stationary, though it is walking continually along a path whose end precedes its beginning. Different ages of the same animal pass one another inside the loop. The young form walks beneath the belly of the old. The adult deposits a replacement organ that the juvenile later collects. Wounds are inherited backward. Scars appear first; the injury that caused them may occur centuries afterward.

The black towers are perhaps animals, perhaps herds, perhaps failures of location.

From afar, each is a narrow vertical mass trembling in the heat. Approached, it separates into innumerable identical limbs standing at different apparent distances. All converge on a torso that recedes whenever one tries to identify its position. Some limbs are near enough to touch. Others are seen beyond the horizon. When one lifts, thousands lift, though not simultaneously. The movement passes among them in waves, as though a single step were searching for the correct time in which to happen.

They feed without moving. Ore vanishes from circles around their feet and reappears as new limbs. Their calls are not sounds but discrepancies: echoes without impacts, shadows cast by absent structures, pressure waves arriving in the wrong order. Migrating herds give them wide passage, unless the tower has always been standing where the herd’s route has always gone. In that case the migration divides around it, and the two halves may reunite as different species.

The hunters of causality are rarer and more feared, if fear is the correct word.

They are first detected as damage. Armor begins to separate along a clean line. Internal charge leaks into the ground. A grazer favors a leg that has not yet been struck. Weeks or years later, the predator arrives: a sparse arrangement of blades, sensory rings, and limbs connected by intervals in which no body is visible.

It follows the wound toward its cause.

To such a creature, pursuit does not lead to attack; attack creates a trail it can pursue. It may feed upon an animal that escapes, because the feeding has already occurred. It may starve beside a carcass whose death lies in the wrong future. Its offspring sometimes precede mating and spend centuries following potential parents, subtly altering their movements so the required encounter eventually takes place.

No creature here treats this as miraculous. The impossible is only another environmental condition, like heat or abrasive wind. Bodies grow organs for it. Instincts route around it. A herd may pause before footprints it has not yet made, compare their depth with its expected weight, and choose whether to become the animals that made them.

Death, likewise, is not a clean boundary.

A dismantled grazer continues in predators, scavengers, symbionts, slag reefs, transmitted charge, and the magnetic alteration of the road beneath it. Its shed plates may shelter young. Its cutting-chain teeth may root in a mineral bank and grow a new mouth. A pressure organ buried by waste may contract once every few years, eventually gathering enough surrounding structures to stand.

Even what looks inert may simply be alive at the wrong speed.

A ridge may be the exposed back of a creature whose limbs extend deep into the plateau and move once in ten thousand years. Parallel grooves may be feeding marks. The plum glow beneath fractures may come from vast tissues maintaining reactions too slow to be called metabolism by anything impatient. The whole plateau may be an animal that has outsourced its digestion to the herds crossing its surface.

Or the herds may be cells in something larger: ore carried inward, metals sorted, charge exchanged, waste redistributed, routes repeated until they resemble circulation.

No single inhabitant appears to know. Knowledge itself is dispersed among turning magnets, hammered messages, ancestral scars, and paths that remember feet. Yet when evening comes and the dorsal vents open from one horizon to the other, the timing is too broad for coincidence. The release travels across species and distances. Flyers alter their altitude. Buried colonies suspend their blows. Predators stop cutting. Even the creatures caught in loops vent at the appropriate moment, though for some of them that moment has happened many times before.

For several hours the plateau breathes without air.

Then the white plumes thin. Electrical chains rise between bodies. A thought, if it is a thought, passes through the high country in slow pulses of heat and charge.

No individual possesses it long enough to understand it.

Still, the living plateau answers itself.
