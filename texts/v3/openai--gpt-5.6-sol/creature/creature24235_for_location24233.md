At first, it seems that the valley is inhabited only by plants.

They stand too densely to be counted as individuals. A silver blade may have its own stem and rootlets, but beneath the soil those rootlets join a pale, wet braid that runs for hundreds of meters. Broad-leaved colonies exchange water through living knots. Fern fields open together, not merely at the same hour but in sequence, the unfolding passing from frond to frond like a thought moving across a surface.

Cut one plant and its nearest neighbors seal the wound. Shade one patch and distant leaves alter their angle. During drought, water is withdrawn from exposed growth and concentrated around buried roots, shaded pools, and the cold foundations of machinery. Whole acres may appear to die, becoming thin and colorless, while their moisture travels elsewhere through the underground weave. When condensation returns, the pale fields green again from the edges inward.

The fungal skins are part of this exchange. They are neither scavengers nor disease. They arrive before a fallen stem touches the ground, extruding from pores along nearby roots and wrapping the dead tissue in a translucent sleeve. Nothing is allowed to decay openly. The valley digests in closed vessels.

There are many forms of growth, but perhaps not many species. A black-stemmed shrub sometimes produces, from the same root mass, a stand of silver blades or a cluster of folded fronds. Form depends on location. Beside reflective metal, it makes cups. Along cables, it becomes a narrow sheath of overlapping scales. In dry stone it remains small and dark, sending glassy threads deep into fractures. Around a flywheel chamber it flowers, although the flowers may take several days to open and resemble colored pressure marks more than ornaments.

The machinery stands among these colonies as though it were another growth form.

The plants do not corrode it. They clear grit from gear housings with soft root tips and draw excess moisture away from relay boxes. Fine tendrils pass through inspection slots, touch warm contacts, then withdraw. On some pipes the vegetation has formed regular collars exactly where condensation is most abundant. On others it leaves bare bands around joints that must move.

The machines return the attention. A valve opens and a root bed swells. A piston lowers and moisture is pressed from deep stone into ceramic channels. Louvers turn toward an unfolding fern field. The immense flywheels carry no obvious load, yet their positions govern the flowering rings outside their chambers. Their revolutions may distribute pressure, timing, or instruction.

It becomes difficult to say whether the plants are tending the machines or the machines are tending the plants. More difficult still to say where either ends.

In the service huts, tools remain clean because thin colorless films move over them at night. These films retreat into cracks when exposed to direct light. They leave no tracks, only ordered rows of droplets along cutting edges and calibration marks. A wrench taken from its rack will slowly become wet, even in dry air. If it is not returned, the surrounding plants incline toward it over several days. Sometimes a plate path alters around the misplaced object: roots lift one edge, settle another, and make a shallow channel leading back toward the hut.

No worker comes to retrieve it.

The small dark creatures are the valley’s most recognizable animals, though even they do not behave quite like animals. They emerge when the central shaft is bright and condensation has begun to gather. Their four legs are jointed delicately, each ending in a soft circular pad. Their backs are smooth, without hair, scales, or visible seams. In shade they look black. In sunlight a depth appears inside them, smoky and green, with brief silver structures moving beneath the surface.

They drink from troughs by lowering the whole front of the body until it nearly touches the water. There is no tongue or mouth that can be seen. The water level falls without ripples. Afterward they remain still for hours, often beside a pipe coupling or at the base of an insulator rack. Condensation beads on their backs and vanishes into them.

They are not timid, exactly. They simply depart before approach becomes possible. A moment before they disappear, nearby leaves turn their pale undersides upward. Then the creature steps between them and is gone, though the vegetation does not appear disturbed.

Their tracks, when found in wet loam, rarely continue for more than a meter. Four prints approach a root opening; on the other side there may be a line of droplets, a new bud, or nothing.

Higher up, the translucent husks on antenna masts suggest another phase of their lives. The husks have four leg cases, but also long folded structures along the back—too rigid for wings and too fine for limbs. They clasp the highest metal points facing the central shaft. Most are intact except for a narrow opening along the underside.

Whatever leaves them does not fly across the sky. No moving thing has been seen above the ridges.

Yet after a husk appears, relay contacts may begin closing in a new rhythm. A dormant tower will sweat for the first time in years. Far below, plants around its foundation produce tiny red leaves identical to those lining the shaft. It may be that the creature does not emerge into the air at all. It may pass into the mast, into current, or into the minute pressure changes carried through the valley floor.

The shaft appears to be the deepest organ of the place.

Its light is not hot. It has the color of daylight seen through ice, though no ice is visible below. During its bright intervals, the cold column rising from it carries microscopic filaments. They gather on the suspended droplets and spread outward when the droplets drift. Under magnification each filament resembles a root hair wound around a metallic core.

Where these settle, leaves become sensitive to vibration. New fungal membranes form. Old cables regain conductivity. The dark creatures appear at the troughs.

The low buildings surrounding the shaft contain pumps, but also chambers filled with root tissue, mineral crust, and arrays of silver pins. Some chambers seem manufactured; others have grown into their shapes so perfectly that the distinction has vanished. Labels survive on the outer panels. Inside, numbers and letters recur as branching veins in living membranes.

The valley may once have been an installation placed in a barren uplift: a water-harvesting system, a geological instrument, a power network designed to operate without noise. Perhaps the first plants were introduced to stabilize soil or collect condensation. Perhaps the dark creatures arrived later.

But whatever the original arrangement was, no simple hierarchy remains.

The machinery has become metabolism without becoming flesh. The vegetation has become circuitry without ceasing to grow. Water is both blood and message. The nine-minute pressure pulse is passed through pools, roots, housings, and folded fronds; each medium delays and changes it. Hours later, another station replies. Across days, the replies make patterns. Across years, they may amount to decisions.

The lifting mountains break paths and separate installations. The inhabitant responds slowly. It sends roots around new cliffs. It raises buried rails where the rock has folded. It abandons chambers that no longer align and wakes others that have stood motionless for decades. Its sense of time is tectonic but not geological: slower than an animal, faster than a mountain.

It does not occupy the valley in the way a population occupies a habitat. It is distributed through the basin, though it is not simply the basin. Rock remains partly outside it. So does the fierce light. Drought constrains it. Faulting injures it. There are things it wants, if “want” is the word: moisture, continuity, conductive surfaces, the completion of long pressure cycles.

The dark creatures may be its only briefly independent parts—mobile reservoirs sent to inspect a trough, bridge a broken connection, carry a minute quantity of water or metal from one colony to another. The plants may be its receptive tissue. The flywheels may be clocks. The shaft may be lung, well, spore chamber, or memory.

None of these comparisons is sufficient for long.

What lives there is not one creature pretending to be a landscape. Nor is it merely an ecosystem that resembles a machine. It is a partnership so old in function, if not in years, that its members no longer perform separate acts. It has no central body visible from the rim. Its attention occurs wherever moisture condenses. Its gestures are the tilting of louvers, the opening of blossoms, the tightening of a pool around a pulse too low to hear.

A visitor may cross it without being approached. Nothing watches from the leaves. No alarm sounds. Yet the plates beneath the visitor’s feet grow damp in advance. Far away, a gauge needle rises. A dark animal pauses beside a ceramic channel. Over the following week, a narrow band of flowers opens along the route that was taken.

This may be welcome, diagnosis, or simply the valley incorporating the fact of passage into its long, silent work.
