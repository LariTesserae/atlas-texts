At first they are difficult to distinguish from what they have made.

A hand resting on a parapet lifts, and the parapet has an elbow. What seemed to be a bundle of roots under a bridge turns its head. A face appears between fern fronds, remains for a moment among the pale stems, then closes its eyes and becomes stone-colored again.

The people of the hollow are small, though not delicately made. Standing upright, most would reach from a grown person’s wrist to the end of the middle finger. They are broad through the ribs and pelvis, with short, strong limbs suited to climbing through their vertical city. Their feet are wide, the toes almost handlike. Generations of ladders, wet ledges, and narrow stairs have favored a grip that can be trusted without looking. Infants cling to clothing and hair long before they walk. The oldest citizens climb slowly but seldom fall.

Cold has shaped them more visibly than darkness. Their bodies hold heat close. Necks are short, ears lie flat, and a fine pelt grows along the spine, forearms, and outer thighs. It is not fur exactly. On children it resembles frost on fruit; in adults it darkens and thickens according to family, from chalk-blond to moss-brown and the blue-black of wet slate. Their bare skin is pale beneath it, though never bloodless. Cheeks, knuckles, and the rims of the eyes carry muted colors: lichen-yellow, berry-red, violet-gray.

Their faces are broad and still. The nose is low, with nostrils that can close against dust and mineral cold. The eyes are small and exceptionally clear, often green, amber, or almost colorless. In the hollow’s distributed radiance they need no widened pupils. Instead the pupil contracts to a dark point, giving their gaze an unsettling exactness. They notice the swelling of a water bead, the first loosening of a stair cord, the displacement of a grain of limestone under a door.

Their slowness is not sleepiness. It is attention.

They wear many thin layers: soft hide turned inward, felted moss fiber, woven grass, the shed down of roof birds. Garments wrap rather than fasten. Sleeves and leggings are bound close so they cannot catch on roots. Over these they hang short aprons crowded with loops and pockets, each containing some modest necessity—a chalk marker, a bronze hook, three lengths of cord, a shell cup, a twist of dried fungus, a smooth stone used for burnishing.

Nothing they wear swings freely. Pendants are tied down. Tools are secured at both ends. Even hair is braided against the skull or wound around small antler combs. Loose things are considered not merely inconvenient but immature: objects that have not yet learned where they belong.

Adornment is subtle and legible. A polished snail shell at the throat records a household’s right to a cistern. Green thread around one thumb marks a gardener. A collar stitched with white seed cases means the wearer has attended a birth within the last turning of the light shaft. The old often wear plain clothes, but their palms and fingertips are stained with mineral pigments, each color acquired from a lifetime of trades.

They have voices, though a visitor may live among them for days without hearing one.

Their mouths are made for quiet speech: soft consonants, long breaths, small clicks produced against the teeth. Names are rarely spoken. A person’s everyday name is a gesture performed near the chest or face—a bent finger, a turning wrist, two knuckles touched together. These signs are not arbitrary. They may refer to the ledge where someone was born, the shape of their first footprint, a habit of looking upward, or an ancestor’s craft. Since gestures change as bodies age, names change with them. An elder may bear a sign that requires both hands and includes the tremor in them.

Across greater distances they speak with cords. Every household keeps feathers, knots, beads, slivers of painted bark, and pierced seeds sorted in shallow wall niches. Messages are assembled rather than written. Their meaning depends on order, color, dampness, and orientation. A cord may announce that a stair is unsafe, ask for a bone-setter, propose an exchange of roof soil, or quietly tell a neighboring family that someone is dying and would like their doorway kept in view.

Children learn these arrangements before they learn spoken language. Their first games consist of sending nonsense along railings: water available where there is no cup, a birth in an empty storehouse, five kinds of mushroom arriving from a roof occupied by birds. Adults correct them by rearranging the messages into improbable but grammatically proper jokes.

Laughter is visible. The mouth opens, the shoulders quiver, and sometimes the hands flutter helplessly at the wrists. Almost no sound comes.

Their households are larger than their rooms. Kinship spreads through adjoining walls, shared roofs, borrowed steps, and the management of water. A child may sleep in one house, eat in another, and spend most of the day in the workshop beneath both. Parenthood is recognized, but less important than nearness. Those who answer when an infant grips the warning cord are considered its people.

Babies are born dark-haired and hot, an astonishing condition in the hollow. For their first months they are carried under clothing, passed from body to body so that no single adult becomes exhausted by giving warmth. Their cradle is usually a deep basket fixed beneath a stair, where the vibration of feet keeps them calm. A white knot appears above the nearest avenue, but no celebration happens all at once. Gifts arrive over many days: a spoon, a toe-ring, a packet of berry paste, a brick for the wall the child may someday continue.

They mature slowly. Childhood is measured not by height but by permissions. First the right to cross an unrailed bridge. Then the right to carry water without supervision. Then to sharpen a blade, tend a condensation cord, touch the public dispute objects, or sleep in a room whose outer wall opens over the lowest cisterns.

No single ceremony marks adulthood. One day everyone realizes a person has been entrusted with all these things.

Their occupations overlap as densely as their gardens. A mason also breeds beetles whose mouthparts polish lime. A water keeper may train willow roots through damaged walls. A cook is often a physician, because nourishment and medicine differ mainly in proportion. Bird tenders read the inaudible calls of roof flocks through their feet. They kneel with fingers resting on wicker beams, feeling courtship, alarm, hunger, and the approach of the tiny owl as patterns of trembling.

Their most respected craft is joining: stone to stone, roof to wall, household to household, old work to new. A joiner does not design a building whole. No one does. They study pressures already present and add the smallest piece that will let them settle more usefully. Their tools are wedges, rubbing stones, damp strings, and patience. A master may spend nine years correcting the angle of a single lintel by inserting flakes thinner than skin.

They distrust clean edges. A surface too straight has not yet entered into conversation with its surroundings.

Meals are frequent and small. They eat mushrooms, fern starch, bitter roots, bird eggs, pale grubs, snails, seed paste, algae, and berries divided with fine knives so that one fruit colors an entire household’s supper. Food is served cool or barely warm. Public ovens are used less for roasting than for drying, fermenting, and waking dormant cultures in clay jars. The strongest delicacy is a black paste made from lichen and mineral salt. It is touched to the tongue with a needle.

Water is never swallowed carelessly. Each sip has taken too long to gather. Children are taught to hold it in the mouth and breathe across it before drinking, acknowledging its cold. To spill water is not a crime, but everyone nearby immediately kneels and presses cords or moss to the wet stone. Waste is defined as anything allowed to go nowhere.

They do not bury their dead. There is too little unused ground, and no flame is permitted enough fuel for cremation. The dead are washed with condensation and wrapped in living moss. For a time they remain seated near their own doorway, secured gently among the beams, while neighbors continue to greet them in passing. Over weeks, beetles, fungi, and roots enter the wrappings. Bones are cleaned and separated. Some become stair pins, needle cases, tool handles, or the three pale steps needed to reach a high bed. The smallest bones are set into house walls.

This is not considered a transformation into objects. The people say the dead become load-bearing.

A few bones are carried to the black basin at the city’s center. Which ones are chosen is unclear even to those who choose them. They are laid beside the water, never in it, and left until a film of green begins to form. Then they disappear. Perhaps attendants remove them. Perhaps animals do. No one watches continuously, because watching continuously would be an attempt to hurry the answer.

The basin has keepers, but no priesthood. They are usually very old or very young: those least tempted to impose an explanation. Their duty is to clear nothing from the water, add nothing to it, and observe any change. Across the city’s remembered history, they have reported none. Each keeper nevertheless records their watch by scratching a tiny arc into a strip of chalk. The strips fill baskets in the roofless buildings until moisture softens them back into white paste.

The people do not worship stillness. They know too much about rot, settling, hunger, and birth to mistake stillness for life. What they revere is change small enough to include what came before.

They fear haste in the same way others fear fire. Their oldest warnings concern things that arrived too quickly: a red flood glimpsed by an ancestor; a root torn out whole; a stranger who tried to widen the entrance; a quarrel settled in a morning and regretted for six generations. When alarm comes, they do not run unless a body has already begun to fall. They brace doors, catch children, dampen cords, place palms against walls, and wait to discover which movement the city will accept.

This can look like passivity. It is not. Their courage is cumulative. Faced with danger, each person performs one small necessary act, trusting another to perform the next. A failing bridge may be saved by fifty hands, none of which knows the entire repair.

They seldom leave the hollow. Those who climb to the floodplain wear gray capes and move on all fours through sedge. Open sky disorients them. Distance seems wasteful; wind feels like a form of shouting. They gather fallen twigs, feathers, ore-stained pebbles, bark, and news of the seasons, then return with their eyes narrowed and their clothing full of seeds.

Some do not return.

The city keeps places for them—not empty rooms, since no room can remain empty, but small irregularities: a hook unused, a cup turned inward, a bridge cord bearing a knot whose meaning is “not yet.” These signs may persist for centuries, inherited by people who no longer know whom they await.

A hurried visitor would think the hollow uninhabited. A patient one would gradually understand that nearly every surface is under observation. Faces would emerge at windows. Hands would answer from bridges. A cup no bigger than an acorn cap would appear beside the stranger’s foot, filled with water that took a day to collect.

Only after the stranger had remained quiet for a very long time would someone approach: broad-footed, furred with cold, carrying no weapon larger than a needle.

They would place one palm against the limestone, one against their own chest, and wait for the newcomer to do the same.

It is their oldest greeting. It means, approximately:

**Here is the wall. Here is what leans upon it. Show us how much weight you bring.**
