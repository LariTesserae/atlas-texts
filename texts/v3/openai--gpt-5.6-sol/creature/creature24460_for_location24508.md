What lives there has learned not to rise above a surface.

The most visible inhabitants are the small, colorless clingers found beneath flooded shelves. Seen from above, one can be mistaken for a flaw in the stone: an oval lens of translucent mineral, flattened so closely that water passes over it without making a ripple. Its shell is neither wholly grown nor wholly secreted. The animal lays down a clear organic skin, and the water armors that skin with silica and pale carbonate. Older individuals become cloudier and more irregular, taking on the chemistry of whichever pool they occupy. A clinger from the basalt channels is smoke-gray; one from the white terraces is nearly invisible.

Beneath the shell are many short limbs, arranged less like legs than the bristles of a seal. Their number is difficult to count because some remain folded against the body and others divide near the tip. Each ends in a soft gripping disk ringed with microscopic hooks. The disks do not merely adhere. They test the rock continuously, tightening and loosening in sequence as pressure shifts around the animal.

A clinger exposed to air seems dead. Its limbs draw in, its underside seals, and the dark knot at its center contracts to the size of a seed. Submerge it, and after a long delay the feet unfold. It advances by fractions: release, reach, fasten, draw forward. It rarely crosses open stone. Instead it travels the undersides of ledges and through seams where the current is strong enough to bring food but not strong enough to tear it free.

It has no eyes. In that immense brightness, eyes would reveal little besides glare. Its shell contains patches sensitive to broad changes in illumination, enough to tell a covered crevice from an exposed one, but its true world is made of pressure and vibration. Every fall, drip, and hidden thread of water reaches it through the rock. The plateau’s roar, overwhelming to an outsider, is the clinger’s background stillness. It notices only irregularities: a cobble shifting half a grain’s width, another animal fastening nearby, the temporary obstruction of a pore.

When alarmed, it does not flee. All its limbs clamp down at once, and its body becomes part of the ledge.

There appears to be almost nothing for it to eat. The wet stone looks clean even under magnification. Yet along the boundary where pressurized water first meets air, there is a living film only a few cells thick. It has no green pigment and forms no visible skin. Its organisms use compounds carried out of the basalt—iron, sulfur, traces of hydrogen—and the chemical difference between deep water and exposed water. They grow slowly in grooves, pores, and the sheltered downstream faces of mineral grains. A whole terrace may support less living material than a handful of meadow soil.

The clinger grazes this film with a cluster of transparent scraping teeth. It leaves no obvious trail. By the time it has crossed the width of its own body, new precipitate has already blurred the marks.

The dark knot visible through its shell is not one organ but a compact gathering of them: gut, nerve ring, brood chamber, and a dense colony of symbiotic cells. These internal cells continue processing mineral-rich water when the animal cannot feed. They may keep it alive through intervals so long that hunger ceases to be a useful measure. A clinger can remain fixed beneath one shelf while a white rim thickens around its shell and nearly seals it into place.

Reproduction is equally unhurried. There is no mating season because there are no seasons to answer. Two animals that happen to meet exchange packets of cells through the water between their touching shells. Either may retain what it receives for years. The eggs—if eggs is the right word—are pressed into newly forming mineral crust and coated until they resemble clear grains.

Most never open. Some are buried beyond reach of oxygen; some are exposed by wear before they have finished forming. The few that hatch do so when water finds a new microscopic route through the crust. The young emerge almost shapeless, with six gripping buds and no hard covering. They crawl at once into a pore and remain there while the plateau builds their first shell around them.

Thus their generations may be separated not by years but by tiny rearrangements of drainage. A channel need move only the width of a hair to awaken a brood that has waited longer than the visible pool above it has existed.

They are sparse on the open shelves but abundant in certain hidden passages. Turn over the right loose plate—if any plate can be called loose here—and its underside may be stippled with dozens of pale shells, all aligned headfirst into the flow. They do not form colonies in any social sense. They neither cooperate nor contend. Their closeness merely records where the invisible food is richest. When one moves, its neighbors may answer with a brief tightening of their feet, a touch transmitted through the stone.

There may be other animals deeper in the plateau. Occasionally a narrow transparent thread is seen contracting inside a basalt pore. Sometimes an empty shell contains a segmented thing too small to have made the shell itself. Whether these are parasites, scavengers, juvenile forms, or separate creatures is difficult to establish. Nothing remains intact after death. Soft tissue is carried away; shells become nuclei for fresh mineral growth. Within a short time, the distinction between corpse and terrace disappears.

In this way, life participates in the plateau without visibly altering it. The films encourage precipitation. The clingers scrape some deposits away and seed others with their shells. Their abandoned coverings become pale scales inside the rims of pools. It is possible that many of the finest laminated shelves are partly biological, though no layer bears a clean signature. The organisms do not build structures so much as persuade mineral to continue doing what mineral already does.

The dark-winged creature above belongs less certainly to this place.

It passes too high for detail: a narrow body between long rigid wings, black only because everything behind it is white. It does not beat its wings while visible. It rides the smooth upward movement of air along the escarpments, holding its position for minutes before sliding beyond a ridge. Perhaps it crosses between warmer valleys. Perhaps it feeds on minute airborne cells lifted from the wet stone. Perhaps there is a ledge somewhere above the glare where it rests.

No nest has been found on the plateau, no feather in a pool, no dropping on the pale shelves. The clingers show no response to its shadow. To them, the brief dimming is only another harmless fluctuation in a world of refracted light.

The true residents remain below, pressed into the narrowest boundary the plateau offers: not quite in the stone, not quite in the water. They have no need for open ground, shelter, distance, or silence. They measure existence by grip and flow. Their bodies are shaped for a world in which nothing begins dramatically and nothing is allowed to fall far.

An individual may spend its entire life traveling the length of one submerged ledge. It may move less than a person’s handspan while mountains of water pass over it. When it dies, the feet release one by one. The current shifts the shell into a hollow, where it lodges among white mineral rims. Clear precipitate closes over it.

Soon there is only another rounded brightness under the water, fitted perfectly among all the others.
