At first it seems that nothing lives in the basin except by mistake.

The gnats are weather-blown. The fox comes once and does not return. Even the little blind crustaceans under the ledges appear less like inhabitants than particles carried upward from some deeper fissure. They are colorless, soft-shelled, scarcely larger than filings from the broken hammerstone. When exposed, they bend violently toward darkness and vanish into pores too narrow to admit a fingernail.

Yet they do not live independently.

The thing that inhabits the basin is difficult to recognize because it has no boundary corresponding to a body. It occupies the warm water, but is not the water. It lines the buried channels, but is not the stone. What can be touched of it is only the colorless slick found on certain pools: a membrane so thin that a finger passes through without tearing anything visible. Beneath that film descend transparent threads, branching through travertine and joining other threads in other cups. Some reach nearly to the spring. Others end in sealed pockets, abandoned and entombed in calcite.

Taken together, these threads form a single, slow colony spread beneath almost the entire basin.

It feeds where deep water brings sulfur, salts, and traces of metal into contact with air descending through the porous floor. Its living substance is clear. It makes no green pigment and needs no direct sun. Across the upper pools it presents only fine skins of cells, each skin renewed from below and thin enough to be mistaken for a peculiarity of reflection. In the deeper cracks it thickens into pale cords with the consistency of soaked tendon. No person has seen those cords intact. Exposed to open air, they slacken, cloud, and soon become indistinguishable from mineral paste.

The small crustaceans are its grazers, cleaners, and perhaps its offspring in a form capable of travel. They move through its passages, eating spoiled tissue and carrying living cells on their limbs and in the folds beneath their shells. A few cross from one branch of the colony to another whenever pressure opens a wet connection. Most never emerge into visible water. Their quick flicker under a ledge is the closest the basin comes to an obvious sign of animation.

The colony experiences the world as changes in flow.

A fallen drop is a brief pressure traveling across a pool. A footstep is a succession of deep compressions, alarming and enormous. A stone removed from a shelf alters the pull of water through a thousand minute pores. The colony has no organ that hears these events, but along its threads the chemistry changes. Cells tighten. Films grow more permeable in one place and less in another. Dissolved calcite is encouraged to settle along a damaged lip; a clogged passage is made faintly acidic until it clears.

This response is so slow that it cannot be watched. Hours pass before seepage shifts. Days pass before a cloudy line appears around an obstruction. Years pass before the correction becomes a rounded dam or a sealed crack. What looks like geological equilibrium is partly the colony’s metabolism expressed in stone.

Its oldest portions lie near the central pool.

There, beneath the level dark surface, the lining is not merely a film but a layered organ spread over the pool’s underside. Warm spring water passes through it before rising. It extracts almost nothing at once, but nothing ceases to pass through it; patience compensates for poverty. The faint breath above the pool is the waste heat of deep rock, though the colony uses the constancy of that heat as other creatures use shelter.

The measuring bowl at the edge has become involved in this body. Its notch concentrates a narrow, dependable current, and beneath the thread of overflow a translucent filament has grown down into the stone. Every disturbance of the bowl—every dipping hand, every removed measure, every refilling—once sent a sharp signal through the central tissues. The bowl may have been placed there to measure the spring. To the inhabitant below, it became something like a fingertip.

The workers who built the shelters never knew the extent of what they touched. They pried pale crust from the outer shelves, choosing deposits that formed in broad, useful sheets. Those sheets were not the colony itself but its cast-off architecture: mineral laid down around generations of living film. Harvesting wounded it only in the way that cutting a callus wounds the skin beneath. Water exposed the scored surfaces; clear tissue spread over them; deposition began again.

The workers may nevertheless have suspected something. They would have noticed that certain cuts healed more quickly than others. They would have found channels diverted from debris without any flood strong enough to move it. A wedge forgotten in a crack might be held fast the following season, not merely buried but enclosed with an unnerving neatness. Such observations could be explained by the spring, and probably were. The dark wedges were valuable. People did not leave tools to appease a pool if leverage could account for the same result.

Their cairns remain because the colony has no reason to destroy them. It grows most heavily where water and air meet, less beneath dry stones. The crossing, however, has been accepted piece by piece. Mineral has swollen around every slab. Water now moves through and beneath it as though the path had always been a natural rib of the floor. Given enough centuries, the cairns too may sink into pale collars and become low blunt pillars, their intention preserved only in their spacing.

The colony does not think in objects. It knows continuities: wet and dry, open and closed, warm and cool, flowing and still. A person standing among the pools is not perceived as a figure but as shadow, weight, warmth, salt, and a network of temporary dams. When the person leaves, these disruptions vanish in reverse. For a little while the place retains the dilute chemistry of sweat and skin. Then even that is sorted and incorporated.

It has no need to pursue, defend, or call. Injury arrives slowly and is closed slowly. Hunger is constant but slight. Reproduction may happen only when a crustacean enters an outlet crack carrying a few adhering cells toward another buried spring. Most such travelers die in cold stone. Perhaps, once in many thousands of years, one reaches warmth and founds another transparent web in another limestone hollow.

Whether the basin contains one individual or countless interjoined colonies cannot be said. Sections are sealed off and later reconnected. A branch may persist alone for centuries, feeding on a thread of sulfurous water, then join the greater network when calcite dissolves between them. No pulse passes through the whole. No center commands it. Even the broad tissue beneath the central pool can die without ending what lives along the walls.

Its timescale makes it appear immortal and inert. It is neither. Deep channels contain dead layers, gray and collapsed, over which living membranes have spread. Entire reaches have been mineralized from within. The rounded bosses are crowded with such remains: generation beneath generation, clear flesh becoming chalk, chalk becoming habitat for clear flesh again.

Thus the basin is not empty, nor exactly crowded with separate creatures. It is occupied by one life continually losing track of where one life ends.

The drops falling from the walls feed it. The blind swimmers tend it. The shelves are its discarded thickness. The channels are at once its dwelling and the shape of its activity. Even the bitter water in which passing animals refuse to drink has already crossed some portion of its body.

It lives without displaying itself. Its motion is the maintenance of a level. Its remains resemble the place that made them. And if it has anything comparable to memory, that memory is visible everywhere: not as thought, but as every sealed fracture, every altered course, every tool and stepping-stone patiently given a white edge and drawn into the enduring shape of the basin.
