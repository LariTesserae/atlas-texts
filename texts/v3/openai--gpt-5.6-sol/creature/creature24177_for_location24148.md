What lives here lives at an edge.

Not the great apparent edge between city and sea, but the smaller ones repeated everywhere: the hairline beneath a door, the damp margin of a channel, the shadow behind a conduit, the narrow shelf above the highest tide. Life occupies joints, faults, vents, cavities, undersides. It waits where two surfaces fail to meet cleanly.

The most numerous inhabitants are too small to distinguish. They form translucent skins inside drains and cisterns, pale scums under submerged grates, invisible colonies in sediment warmed by effluent. They consume what settles from the water and what leaks from the city. Almost everything larger is ultimately gathered around them, though no one looking across the basin would know they were there.

Then come the flies.

They do not distribute themselves evenly. They accumulate. On a shaded wall they may appear at first to be soot, until the soot changes shape. Around fish waste they make a dense, metallic agitation. In unused stairwells they hang dormant through the brightest hours, each insect separate and still, their combined presence turning the concrete granular. When a door opens, they rise together and reveal the clean silhouettes where their bodies had covered warning signs and old paint.

Mosquito larvae tremble in cisterns no deeper than a hand. Midges organize themselves over ponds in wavering pillars that bend without dispersing. Cockroaches inhabit a second, interior city: warm switch boxes, wall voids, cable sleeves, the dry spaces under refrigeration units. Silverfish move through abandoned records and the softened backing of insulation. They are the librarians of the place, reducing invoices, manuals, labels, and maps to lace.

The crabs are more visible and seem, for that reason, to possess the city.

Small ones live high on embankments, the color of chalk, ash, or uncured concrete. They vanish into bolt holes with a speed that makes the wall appear to swallow them. On the lower ground are rust-colored and violet crabs, mottled crabs, crabs furred with mud. The largest wait in the culverts where the tide presses inward. Their shells are scored from passage beneath gates and through broken reinforcement mesh. Some have lost claws to machinery or combat and carry the imbalance with no apparent shame.

After rain, they emerge in numbers sufficient to revise the meaning of a road. The road is no longer a route for vehicles but a broad exposed interval between one wet refuge and another. They cross it sideways, hesitating at vibrations. A truck approaching at low speed sends a wave of stillness through them. Then the whole surface resumes its many-directioned movement.

Their dead are quickly dismantled by the living. Their cast shells collect in corners, light enough to stir in the exhaust from ventilation fans. Workers sometimes find one standing in an elevator at dawn, or wedged inside a washbasin several floors above the ground, with no clear indication of how it arrived.

Birds inhabit the vertical city.

Pigeons hold the rooms left by people. They enter through missing louvers and broken panes, nesting in cupboards, on old light fixtures, in the bowls of sinks. Generations of them have softened certain floors beneath guano and feathers. Their descendants are darker than pigeons elsewhere, stained by dust and perhaps selected by the perpetual dimness. When startled, they beat through corridors in a panic of wings, striking walls and raising powder from the concrete.

Swifts belong to the ducts. Their nests cling inside ventilation shafts where machinery maintains a constant updraft. They emerge at morning in streams, circle the cooling towers, and vanish into the cloud-colored sky. At speed they are difficult to regard as animals. They seem like cuts opening and closing in the air.

Gulls occupy roofs, but not indiscriminately. Each colony has boundaries invisible to people and fiercely apparent to gulls. A rooftop may be crowded to its parapets while an identical roof nearby remains empty. Chicks crouch among cable trays and drainage stones, their spotted down matching the aggregate. Adults return from the tidal flats with fish, scraps, lengths of gut, pieces of food wrapping, and occasionally objects that are neither edible nor useful. Their nests contain wire, gloves, plastic seals, fragments of caution tape. The city enters their domestic lives as debris.

Terns prefer the widest, flattest roofs, where they can see threats coming. Sandpipers sleep in tight groups on pump-house ledges. Egrets patrol the shallows with the solemnity of inspectors. Ibis push their curved bills into sediment gathered beside leaking mains. Cormorants blacken the cranes. They face different directions according to wind and heat, wings extended, transforming each disused boom into a rack of dark emblems.

Pelicans wait at the outfalls. They know the cycling of the plants better than many workers do. Before warm water begins to discharge, they assemble without obvious signal, their large bodies aligned along barriers and floating booms. When the flow starts, fish rise stunned or oxygen-starved, and the pelicans feed with grave efficiency. Gulls attend them, stealing from their bills.

Raptors live above this abundance. Kites patrol the market roofs. Peregrines nest on cooling towers and transmission gantries, taking pigeons in bursts so fast that the event registers first as an explosion of feathers. Larger herons hunt rats in drainage lots. Owls use abandoned offices, sitting behind broken windows where their faces can be mistaken for pale masks left on desks.

At night, bats replace the swifts.

They pour from bridge cavities, expansion chambers, hollow piers, and the folded darkness beneath corrugated roofs. The first few circle alone. Then the sky acquires a texture, a streaming grain that thickens over the settling ponds. Their wings are almost inaudible from the ground. Insects disappear into their flight by the thousand. Some bats travel inland along canals; others turn toward the sea. A few never leave the basin, making repeated loops around lamps until dawn pales the clouds.

The rats live in routes.

They know pipe galleries that no longer appear on engineering plans. They cross beneath occupied buildings through spaces created by subsidence, climb cable bundles, swim flooded tunnels, and enter sealed districts through valves too small to seem consequential. Market rats grow heavy on freight and kitchen waste. Canal rats are leaner, wet-furred, and quick to dive. In the older blocks, colonies may occupy one floor while cats occupy another, the two populations separated by damaged stairwells, slick walls, or crab-filled courtyards.

The cats are high-dwelling animals. They watch from balconies and roof bridges, narrow-bodied from heat, their coats dulled by dust. They hunt pigeons, rats, geckos, and bats that fall exhausted. Some attach themselves loosely to maintenance crews and appear at the same meal break each day. Others live beyond handling, entering rooms only after people leave. Their eyes in a dark upper story are among the few signs that an apparently abandoned building is inhabited.

Dogs remain on open ground but avoid exposure when they can. They sleep beneath trailers with their noses toward the breeze and rise reluctantly when engines start. Many have the same long legs, short coats, folded ears, and indefinite brown coloring, as if the basin has been breeding a single practical dog for generations. They travel in small groups at dusk, searching loading areas and fish yards. They know which gates open automatically and which guards throw scraps. They give the largest crabs room.

Snakes take possession of the infrastructure intended to move water. Water snakes hunt fish and frogs where frogs can survive; rat snakes climb through service risers and roof spaces. They are found curled around warm motors, inside cable cabinets, beneath mats, among pigeon nests. Their shed skins hang from gratings or lie collapsed in dry sumps, perfect and empty. A worker may see only the last length of a snake disappearing into a drain and be unable to judge whether it was half a meter long or three.

Monitor lizards are less secretive. In rare sunlight they come onto concrete aprons and flatten themselves against the heat, black tongues testing air that already seems saturated with information. They raid nests, pry crabs from crevices, and enter waste yards through gaps opened by settlement. Large individuals become locally known. Workers recognize them by scars, missing toes, or peculiar bands on the tail. They receive unofficial names and are spoken of as if they hold positions in the facility.

The canals contain another population, visible mostly in fragments.

Mullet show their backs in shoals. Eels knot themselves under collapsed slabs. Tiny fish gather where condensation falls from pipes, feeding on whatever each drop dislodges. At high tide, small sharks pass the barred culverts and move through flood channels built too straight for them. Their fins divide reflections of gantries and lamps. Seals sometimes follow fish inland and rest on floating barriers, looking misplaced only until one remembers that the sea is under and around everything here.

There are also creatures that arrive by freight: geckos in shipping crates, beetles in grain, spiders beneath pallets, cockroaches sealed in appliances, exhausted birds resting on vessels from other coasts. Most die. Some find the city’s heat and cavities suitable and become another unnoticed population. Thus one warehouse may contain a fauna slightly different from that of the warehouse across the road, its inhabitants descended from a shipment unloaded decades before.

The people, too, are edge-dwellers.

They are seldom outside without purpose. They move between sealed rooms, enclosed walkways, vehicle cabs, control stations, and locker blocks where dry air pours from vents. Their skin is often filmed with condensation before sweat has time to form. Their clothes are light but cover wrists and ankles. Boots are high, doors self-closing, meals lidded. A handrail is tested before being trusted; a dark cavity is inspected before being reached into.

They learn the basin as a collection of timings. Which underpass floods first. When the crabs cross. When the midges hatch. Which roof belongs to nesting gulls in which month. When the tide backs water through a drain despite the pumps. Which corridor will be lined with resting swifts before dawn. Which warm cabinet is likely to contain a snake.

Maintenance crews know individual animals and recurring colonies. They know the one-eyed dog beneath the compressor shed, the monitor that sleeps near Gate Nine, the pair of kites nesting on the flare tower. They know where pigeons will rebuild no matter how often a nest is removed. They can identify a blocked drain by the behavior of crabs gathered around it. They read tracks in wet silt alongside pressure gauges and settlement markers.

Their work is to maintain distinctions: inside from outside, dry from flooded, clean from contaminated, machinery from nest. The distinctions never hold for long. A gull drops a crab shell into an intake. Rats pull insulation from a conduit. Barnacles narrow a gate. Midges coat a sensor. A cat gives birth in an electrical room. Mud crabs undermine the fill around a culvert. Each intrusion is corrected, logged, sealed, screened, or swept away. Each creates another edge elsewhere.

Children raised in the housing blocks do not initially understand that the city is considered barren. To them, a blank wall is where geckos hunt flies. A drainage ditch is where crabs molt. A floodlight is surrounded by bats. Empty ground is crossed by tracks too delicate to survive the next rain. They learn caution before disgust. They learn which animals can be handled, which can be eaten, which feign death, which carry disease, and which will defend a nest even against a person in a helmet.

Almost none of the inhabitants own the open spaces. Those remain hot, saline, exposed, and apparently vacant. Life gathers around their perimeter, watching from shade or waiting below the surface. The emptiness is real, but it is not absence. It is distance between shelters.

Seen closely, the city is a vast shared den whose occupants rarely meet on equal terms. People occupy its cooled chambers. Rats inherit its hidden routes. Birds claim its heights. Crabs patrol the wet foundations. Fish enter with the tide. Insects bloom wherever decay pauses long enough to be consumed. Bacteria live beneath all of them, turning every leakage and corpse toward another form of appetite.

Nothing grows here, but nearly everything feeds.
