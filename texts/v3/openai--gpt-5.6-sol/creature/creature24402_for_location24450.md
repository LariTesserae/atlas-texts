The people who live there are difficult to notice at first.

They wear the same values as the city: dark surfaces, little wasted heat, nothing loose. Their outer garments are close-cut and layered, made of dull fabrics that shed water without becoming stiff. Cuffs seal softly at wrist and ankle. Hoods fit beneath work helmets; collars rise over the mouth when the air sharpens. Most clothing is charcoal, mineral gray, or the deep blue-black of wet stone, but this restraint is crossed by precise color—an amber seam showing emergency access, a turquoise shoulder marking a water engineer, white tabs at the wrists of medical workers. Personal colors are often hidden inside coats, visible only in doorways and warm rooms.

Everything they carry is attached. Tools lock into shallow belts. Cups adhere to tables. Children’s gloves are clipped to sleeves. A resident stepping onto a bridge makes a sequence of small, habitual checks: collar, sole grip, tether point, the pale condition line along the rail. These gestures are not anxious. They are closer to punctuation.

They tend to be deliberate movers. A stranger might mistake that deliberation for solemnity, but it is simply the bodily grammar of ledges, wet floors, slanting lifts, and structures that may choose to flex. They place their weight before committing it. They do not lean casually against an unmarked wall. On stairs they leave room for anyone descending, because a descending person may be carrying water samples, a child, a repair case, or momentum that cannot be surrendered quickly.

Their balance is excellent, though they rarely boast of it. Very young children learn the city first through their hands. They trail fingers along guide ridges, distinguish warm rails from cold ones, and are taught which vibrations mean a freight passage, which mean a pressure adjustment, and which require them to sit down immediately. Games involve crossing painted patterns without touching the silver channels between them, or guessing the depth of a shaft from the delayed click of a dropped training bead. The beads are broad, bright, biodegradable, and recoverable. Adults still complain about them in the sumps.

No one is entirely unskilled in maintenance.

A cook can read a stress indicator. A teacher knows how to release a bridge coupling. A musician can clear a blocked condensation route without calling the water crews. Formal occupations exist—structural conciliators, reservoir keepers, inertia technicians, light-guide polishers, fracture surveyors, culture-room tenders—but the division between citizen and custodian is thin. Maintenance instructions are written plainly because everyone may need them. Public education includes first aid, fault behavior, water law, thermal discipline, and the etiquette of reporting a crack without dramatizing it.

Reports are precise. Residents do not say that a wall “looks wrong.” They say that the western seam has widened by half a nail, that runoff has changed direction, that a door has begun closing early, that a familiar vibration is arriving through the floor instead of the rail. The city’s systems notice more than any individual could, yet local attention is prized. A person who has walked the same passage for thirty years may detect a change before the instruments classify it.

This gives the inhabitants an intimate, unsentimental relationship with the mountain. They do not imagine it sleeping, angry, protective, or alive in a human way. Still, their language is full of bodily verbs. The stone settles, relieves itself, holds tension, opens, remembers a load. A chamber that has remained stable for generations is said to have accepted occupation. One that repeatedly sheds fragments is said to be refusing.

The mountain is never called conquered.

Those who work closest to the exposed rock carry thin articulated probes, patch cartridges, and sheets of temporary sensor film that cling to mineral faces like translucent skin. They spend long periods motionless, one glove pressed to a wall while a machine reads through their bones. Their work is less like masonry than negotiation. They identify where a load wishes to travel and make a safer route for it. The most respected of them are not those who preserve the most structures, but those who know when a structure should be detached and abandoned.

Abandonment is not considered failure. Rooms are emptied while they remain usable. Doors are removed, utilities drawn back, and a clean record of the chamber is kept. Sometimes the mountain closes such spaces gradually, narrowing their entrances over decades. Sometimes a black room remains beyond a barrier, intact but no longer trusted. Older residents remember meals held there, births, arguments, workshops, a particular view into a shaft that no longer exists. They may point toward solid stone and say, without much sadness, “We lived behind that.”

Their homes are compact and warmer than the public city, though never hot. Heat gathers in furniture, clothing, wall recesses, and sleeping enclosures rather than in open air. A household room may feel cold at the nose while a bench warms the spine and thighs. Beds close around sleepers in ventilated shells. Bathing uses little free water; warm vapor, cleansing films, and recovered condensation do most of the work. Steam is too valuable, and too structurally troublesome, to be permitted as a casual luxury.

Even so, they have luxuries.

A warm stone held between both palms is one. Dry socks are another. So is a room with no audible pump. Some households keep small sealed vessels of water taken from a memorable condensation event—a rare clear morning, an unusual storm, the opening of a new light aperture. The water itself is chemically ordinary and may eventually be returned to the system. Its significance lies in where and when it gathered.

Color is cherished in ways the public spaces only hint at. Inside dwellings, inner garments may be intensely red, yellow, violet, or green—the green especially vivid because it occurs nowhere outside the culture rooms. Children sometimes choose hues from archived images of lowland plants they have never seen. They know the names of forests, grasses, and flowers, but may confuse their scales. A leaf can be imagined as large as a blanket; a tree as soft as moss. To them, soil is an exotic material: dark, loose, biologically crowded, and alarmingly willing to get into everything.

Some residents work in the food strata, where the city becomes briefly humid, pale, and organic. They tend fungal lattices, cell beds, bacterial cultures, and enclosed vats whose living contents never enter the public water. These workers return with a faint smell on them—yeast, protein broth, warm mineral nutrient—which others identify immediately. It is one of the city’s few occupational smells. Meals are compact, hot, and strongly flavored. Crisp textures are prized because so much of the environment is smooth, wet, or yielding. Salt is common but carefully accounted for. Food that sheds crumbs is eaten over collection surfaces.

Communal meals occur in low chambers where people sit close enough to share body heat. Conversation there is not necessarily quiet. The city’s hush belongs to transit spaces and exposed shelves; behind insulated doors, its inhabitants laugh loudly, sing, interrupt, and argue. Their reputation for reserve is largely the impression produced by seeing them outdoors, hooded and single-file, each attending to footing.

Their humor is practical and often concerns systems behaving badly. They tell stories about cargo sent to the wrong level, maintenance swarms repairing sculpture, pressure doors trapping dignitaries between seals, or personal objects making long and humiliating journeys through the waterworks. An old joke claims that nothing dropped in the city is truly lost; it merely becomes the responsibility of someone forty floors below.

Waterworkers inherit many such objects. They maintain informal displays in lower stations: spoons, tool clips, toys, name tags, a ceremonial pin, one artificial tooth, countless bright training beads. Items are labeled by the reservoir in which they were found. Owners can reclaim them, though doing so invites a public annotation of the retrieval.

The population is not large enough for anonymity to be complete, but the city’s vertical organization prevents everyone from knowing everyone. People identify one another by shelf, route, or light well as much as by district. Someone may be described as living “below the third warm crossing” or “on the wall that shifts east.” Neighborliness follows fault planes. Those connected by the same evacuation routes drill together, keep emergency supplies for one another, and know which households include infants, people with limited mobility, or visitors unused to the terrain.

Families take many forms, partly because care must remain resilient when work carries people to distant levels or summit stations. Children often belong to a cluster of households linked by adjacent chambers. Meals, instruction, and night supervision pass among adults according to shift. Parenthood is private; child safety is communal. Any adult may correct an unsecured tether or stop a child from stepping across a warning line. The child is expected to listen first and complain later.

Older residents are conspicuous not because they move much more slowly, but because they move with fewer unnecessary corrections. They know where a stair will be slick before looking. Their hands find rails in darkness. Many resist relocation from high or awkward shelves, even when lifts make the journey inconvenient. Adapted rooms remain suspended level around them; seats unfold along routes; neighbors bring food and news. Their memories serve as a second survey archive. An elder may recall the sound a chamber made before a fracture opened half a century earlier, and technicians record the account alongside instrument data.

Bodies that cannot tolerate the cold or altitude live deeper, where pressure and temperature are steadier. This is not regarded as lesser residence. The deep districts contain clinics, schools, culture rooms, archives, and some of the busiest workshops. People born below may go days without seeing raw sky. Others spend most of their lives near the ridge openings and become restless under ceilings. The two temperaments tease each other: the deep-born are said to distrust weather; the upper-born, walls.

Visitors are obvious. They look outward instead of down. They stop in doorways. They overdress, sweat, then become cold. They grip rails too tightly and ask how deep the shafts are, a question residents often cannot answer because the useful measure is not depth but destination. Visitors listen fearfully when the mountain knocks. Residents listen diagnostically.

During a tremor, conversation stops at once.

People lower their centers of gravity and free one hand. Those carrying infants turn the child inward. Doors remain open or close according to their local instructions. The floor lights come on, and every person watches them without appearing to. When water moves sideways, no one tries to touch it. The strange redirection of gravity lasts only seconds, but the body remembers it: stomach, inner ear, teeth.

Afterward, the first question is not “Is it over?” but “What has changed?”

Surveyors go out. Route stewards inspect crossings. Households report broken seals and displaced objects. Children are counted by touch and name. Public channels fill with concise notices. Once immediate danger passes, people gather near shared walls and wait for maps to update. Tea or broth appears. Someone makes a joke too early; someone else objects; normal life begins through the argument.

They do not celebrate earthquakes, but neither do they treat stillness as the natural condition of the world. Stability, to them, is active and temporary. A safe home is not one that never moves. It is one whose movement is understood, accommodated, and given somewhere to go.

This conviction enters their ethics. They distrust rigid plans and permanent claims. Agreements include release conditions. Public offices rotate. Storage is distributed. Important records exist in several physical strata. A promise is valued not because it pretends circumstances will remain unchanged, but because it states how people will respond when they do not.

Their art often uses tension, reflection, and delayed motion. Thin metal pieces are hung where condensation gradually weights them until they bend and spill a line of water, then rise again. Clear laminates hold bubbles that migrate during pressure changes. Musicians play instruments designed for intimate rooms: ceramic reeds, tension strings coupled to benches, low percussion felt through the feet. Large concerts are rare. More often, neighboring balconies answer one another across a shaft, sound appearing from different heights without revealing the performers.

They are careful about loud noise. A percussive work may be beautiful, but it must not resemble a structural alarm.

Direct sunlight interrupts them all.

When a true beam enters the cleft, people step into it if their work allows. Hoods come back. Gloves open. Faces tilt, eyes closed at first against the brightness. Their skin shows a range of human tones made pale or sallow by diffuse light, marked at cheeks and noses by cold, sometimes patterned with the fine pressure scars left by masks and thermal seals. In direct sun they look briefly exposed, almost indecently visible. Small details emerge: silver hairs, mended collars, mineral dust in eyebrows, droplets on lashes.

Children try to catch the beam on mirrors. Adults tell them not to redirect it toward sensors and then help them do it safely.

No one worships the light, but appointments are delayed for it. Arguments pause. A person alone on a bridge may simply stand there until the brightness moves off them and continues down the wall. Some residents keep records of these encounters: date, level, duration, weather above. Others consider that sentimental and can nevertheless recall the last time sunlight reached their own doorway.

Death is handled with the same care as heat and water. The dead are not left in the mountain, whose young fractures make promises of permanence dishonest. Bodies are processed in sealed facilities, their useful elements returned to controlled cycles, while personal memorials remain small and distributed. A name may be cut into a replaceable ceramic strip near a favored crossing. A light guide may be adjusted to warm a particular bench for several minutes on an anniversary. If a memorial chamber becomes unstable, the memorial moves.

What persists is not the location but the act of keeping.

The city’s inhabitants are not grim, and they are not fearless. Fear is another local substance: recognized, contained, shared when necessary, never heated into spectacle. They understand the many ways their home might injure them. They also understand its reservoirs, redundancies, yielding bridges, attentive doors, and the competence of their neighbors.

They love the place without expecting it to love them back.

At night, figures pass through the blue-white path light, briefly multiplied in wet walls. A technician kneels beside a seam while gray repair grains gather around her glove. Two children race along a permitted corridor, their tethers clicking in pursuit. A reservoir keeper eats from a warm cup above black water. Someone returning from the summit carries ice feathered across one shoulder. Behind sealed doors, bright clothing hangs to dry, broth simmers under recovery lids, and old people tell stories about rooms the mountain has since taken away.

Far above, a bird may sleep on a vent rim. Far below, translucent creatures graze unseen films in a sump. Between them live the people: warm in small regions, watchful without constant alarm, making a society from drainage paths, fault lines, shared exits, and moments of sun.

They do not fill the fracture. They leave darkness between their homes. They leave ledges untouched and chambers unnamed. Their restraint is not emptiness but courtesy—a recognition that habitation does not require possession of every surface.

When the mountain shifts, they shift with it. When water arrives, they give it a route. When a wall opens, they listen before deciding whether to close it.

And when the clouds break, they stop wherever they are and turn their faces toward the narrow light.
