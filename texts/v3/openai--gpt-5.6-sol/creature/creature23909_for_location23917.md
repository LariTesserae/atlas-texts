Nothing with eyes lives beneath the reservoir. Nothing hunts, grazes, breeds, or sleeps there. If the chamber has an inhabitant, it is not an animal but a thin, divided life spread across stone, clay, water, and light.

It begins in the planted troughs. Each holds a clenched root-mass buried in gravel, dark with mineral stains and bound around chips of pottery. From it rise a few water-grass blades, blanched by warmth and the strange blue-white illumination. They are too long for their small beds. In quiet intervals they lift at uncertain angles, but when a sluice opens they flatten at once, all pointing the same way, like the combed hairs of a submerged hide. Their tips enter neighboring currents and tremble there. Some are frayed from repeated surges. Others bear tiny clear bubbles along their edges, so that for a little while they seem sewn with silver.

The grasses do not possess the basin. They endure it by yielding.

A smaller life has begun around the light shafts. It appears first as greenish dust in the shallow cuts left by chisels, then as a translucent skin stretched over the limestone. It favors flaws: thumbprints in plaster, the shelter beneath a mortar lip, the inner curve of a red guide mark, the grainy face of a brick. In full brightness it is yellow-green and almost invisible. In recesses it deepens toward olive. When rubbed away by the water-keepers, scraps remain in tool marks too narrow for reed fiber to enter. From these scraps the film returns.

In the protected niche where several mason’s thumbprints survive, the growth is thickest. Soft filaments fill the impressions and wave above them, giving each print a fine green nap. The hand is gone, but its pressure has become habitat. Bubbles collect among the filaments and enlarge until the current plucks them free. Thus the little patch seems to breathe—not rhythmically, not of its own will, but whenever light and water have prepared enough air for release.

The rest of what inhabits the chamber is harder to call alive.

Pale sediment travels through it in recurring generations. A gate opens; a cloud rises from the ceramic beds; grains separate according to weight and shape. The heaviest fall almost at once into the first pits. Finer lime drifts over the ledges and settles in crescents. A red fleck from some broken tile may move for days by increments, lodged first against a ridge, then beneath a grass trough, then in a drainage groove, before finally dropping into the lowest square catchment. No grain chooses its path, yet each acquires a history. Layer upon layer, the basin keeps an account of every surge.

Warmth also dwells there, though unequally. It gathers against the western masonry and beneath the ceiling, forming a broad invisible presence that presses downward upon cooler threads. Where the two meet, the water twists. These twisting seams roam among the piers and steps, loosening dust, bending the grasses, worrying bubbles out of mortar. They have no stable outline. A movement of the upper gates can erase one and make another elsewhere.

Then there are the air pockets under the vaults. They cling silver-side down, flattened against the ceiling as if some bright, soft-bodied creatures had attached themselves there. They merge when touched by smaller bubbles. They quiver at every thump of the sluices. During a strong pulse they break loose and flee along the stone, dividing and joining, searching for the vertical shafts. Their departure is abrupt: a bright rush upward, then nothing.

Taken together—the grasses, the green films, the travelling dust, the warm layers, the trapped air—these make something like a single dispersed inhabitant. Its bones are the fitted blocks. Its channels were cut with straightedge and cord. Its pale skin is renewed lime. Its hair grows only in mortared troughs and neglected thumbprints. Water gives it motion, sunlight gives it color, and the gates impose its pulse.

It cannot move except by rearranging itself.

With every opening, its grasses stream, its sediments rise, its bubbles shudder, and its green films bow close to the stone. With every closing, it settles into a slightly altered form. A new crescent appears behind a ledge. One more grain enters the lowest pit. A filament extends across a red construction line. A sharp channel lip becomes imperceptibly duller.

The water-keepers do not regard this as a creature. When the basin is drained, they descend into its wet brightness and dismantle these signs of occupancy. They scrape away the films, comb silt from the troughs, burst the last air pockets, and shovel the layered floor into baskets. They reseal the plastered prints and reset wandering fragments. To them, anything accumulating here is either useful planting, evidence of flow, or obstruction.

But after they leave and the gates are raised, the divided life assembles again.

Water covers the lowest slabs, then the terraces, then the grass beds. Light descends through the shafts. Warm currents climb the western wall. Bubbles emerge from fresh lime. Green remnants brighten in their hidden cuts. The basin takes on its silver, pale, wavering body once more.

It is a young inhabitant, scarcely separate from the room that contains it. It has no name because it has no single shape. It is what happens when a hand-built place is surrendered to repetition: the first soft answer of water to stone.
