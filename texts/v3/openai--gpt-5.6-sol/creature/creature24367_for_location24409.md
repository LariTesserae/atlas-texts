The inhabitants of the Stillworks Plateau do not think of themselves as hidden. Hiddenness is a visual condition, and sight has never carried much authority among them.

They call themselves, in the nearest translatable sense, **the Continuing**.

A Continuing is broad, low, and warm. Its body consists of a load-bearing frame surrounded by layers of flexible ceramic scales, each overlapping the next like masonry designed to breathe. The oldest have become almost architectural. When resting in an alcove, knees folded beneath the torso and sensory vanes laid flat, one can be mistaken for a housing around some municipal instrument. Pipes may pass through the outer frame. Handrails may be attached to it. Notices may have been inscribed on its plates by generations who did not realize the surface belonged to anyone.

This is not considered an indignity. To be useful as part of a place is one of their quieter ideals.

They have between four and seven limbs, depending on age, occupation, and repair history. The distinction between an original limb and a replacement has little social importance. Some limbs end in broad pads for reading floor vibration. Others divide into fine mineral whiskers capable of detecting heat, electrostatic charge, and imperfections in a polished surface. A specialist in old machinery may possess a hand of several hundred jointed filaments, each no thicker than wire. An administrator may have only one manipulator, but carry along its spine a row of pressure organs sensitive enough to feel a counterweight descending in a chamber two districts away.

Their bodies contain no water.

The inner medium in which their chemical processes occur is a family of hot salts, viscous at street temperature and circulating slowly through capillary channels. Their thoughts accelerate near furnaces and become contemplative in cooler vaults. A Continuing entering a cold district may require days to finish a sentence begun at the threshold. Among them this is not interpreted as impairment. The sentence has acquired the temperature of its subject.

They do not breathe continuously. At long intervals, shutters beneath the thoracic plates open and admit a measured quantity of thin air. Oxygen is bound, stored, and used sparingly. The exhalation may not occur until months later, often at a licensed exchange niche where reactive gases are recovered by the city. Breath released in an uncontrolled place is treated much as another culture might treat bleeding: not shameful, but evidence that something requires attention.

Their senses are distributed rather than centered. They have no faces. A band of heat-sensitive pits may circle the upper body, but these give only crude impressions of nearby forms: a warm machine, a cooling wall, the sharp thermal absence of an open shaft. They identify one another by internal rhythm. Every Continuing carries a cluster of weighted reeds or oscillating cavities within the torso. The pattern transmitted through stone by these structures is partly inherited, partly cultivated, and partly the result of accumulated damage. It serves at once as voice, gait, signature, and expression.

To meet another person is to feel them arriving through the floor.

Their public concourses are therefore less empty than they appear. At any given time, dozens may be present in adjoining chambers, conversing by pressure pulses through pillars and rails. One leans a shoulder against a wall; another places three hands on the same building half a kilometer away. Between them pass compressed utterances that may take hours to unfold.

A greeting is a brief declaration of persistence:

**Your interval remains recognizable.**

The courteous reply is:

**Your pressure reaches me without distortion.**

Names, where they exist, are not sounds. A name is a repeatable tactile event: the slight twist of a warm rail beneath the palm; three pulses with the middle one returning inverted; a sensation like weight shifting from one side of the jaw to the other. Written approximations are descriptive rather than phonetic. Thus the inhabitants recorded in municipal documents appear as **Warm Flange After Six Cycles**, **Corridor with the Distant Pulse**, and **The Room Where Weight Is Less Certain**. These are not places mistakenly substituted for persons. They are the circumstances under which those persons can most accurately be known.

Their young are assembled only rarely.

Two or more adults deposit patterned seed structures in a heated cradle of iron ceramic. Over several decades, metallic cells lay down a new frame by persuading crystals to form along prescribed errors. The process is neither manufacture nor gestation as the Continuing understand those terms. Manufacture repeats a proven object. Gestation permits unmeasured alteration. The making of a young person must do both while officially admitting to neither.

For this reason, births are entered into civic records as discoveries.

The standard declaration reads:

**A resident previously too small to survey has been confirmed.**

A newly confirmed resident begins as a warm, intricate mass fixed to the cradle wall. It learns the city before it learns the limits of its own body. The first patterns it recognizes are turbine rhythms, distant presses, carrier schedules, and the coded tremors of the handrails. Only later does it distinguish deliberate speech from industrial recurrence.

This produces a characteristic uncertainty in the young. They may address a refinery as an elder, interpret a seismic echo as a question, or wait decades for a warehouse to complete what they believe is a sentence. Their caretakers do not correct them quickly. There are respected adults who maintain that the refineries are elders and that the only childish error is expecting them to speak at an accessible speed.

Education consists largely of learning what may be touched, what must be touched, and what has been touched continuously since before records began.

A child is taken through the city along a route that may last a century. It places its sensory filaments on foundation blocks, active pipes, cracked rails, dormant control housings, and the outer plates of resting elders. At each station it receives an account not of what the object is, but of what conditions the object has maintained.

This wall has borne a load without measurable resentment.

This conduit has remained warmer within than without.

This gap has held its width through seventeen ground responses.

This machine watches the machine that watches the needle.

Their histories are consequently histories of persistence. Wars, inventions, migrations, and catastrophes are difficult to preserve under the fundamental law, because each requires acknowledging alteration. The archives describe them obliquely.

A civil conflict becomes **a period during which agreement occupied multiple locations**.

The destruction of a district becomes **the confirmation of a lower structural arrangement**.

An extinction becomes **the continuation of a population at zero detectable pressure**.

The Continuing are not emotionless, though their emotions have been shaped by a place where abruptness is dangerous. Alarm is experienced as unwanted acceleration. Grief is the persistence of an expected vibration after its source has ceased. Affection is often expressed by accepting some portion of another’s weight.

Close companions may brace themselves together for years, redistributing structural stress through their bodies. In public vaults one sometimes finds groups fused into dark, low arches, apparently motionless. A faint patterned warmth passes among them. These are households.

When one of the Continuing can no longer regulate its salts or hold its frame in a stable geometry, it does not die in any officially recognized sense. Its companions carry it to a civic support chamber and position it beneath a beam, beside a pipe, or within a cracked foundation. There the body cools and becomes part of the load path. Its identifying rhythm may continue for centuries as machines pulse through it.

Descendants visit and press their hands to the plates.

If the old pattern can still be felt, however faintly, the ancestor remains legally resident.

If it cannot, the instruments are recalibrated.

Not all inhabitants accept this life.

There are those called **the Unverified**, though they sometimes call themselves **those who precede the proof**. They alter their bodies without awaiting evidence of prior change. They replace functional limbs, cut new sensory pits, interrupt inherited rhythms, and enter unmapped parts of the plateau. Some have even installed optical organs assembled from obsolete survey equipment.

Vision affects them strangely.

Accustomed to heat, pressure, and vibration—senses in which every perception arrives through contact or consequence—they find the immediate apprehension of distant objects almost intolerable. To see a tower without touching the intervening ground feels like an accusation of impossible intimacy. To see stars is worse. Several have destroyed their own eyes after a single exposure to the open sky. Others become devoted to it and spend decades motionless at the cliff edge, staring into distances that cannot feel them looking.

The administration does not exile the Unverified. Exile would alter residency. Instead, their quarters are designated provisional irregularities and excluded from most surveys. They inhabit failed loading bays, discontinuous rail spurs, and the margins of quarry basins. It is likely that they maintain contact with whatever makes the paired tracks on the outer flats.

The Continuing deny this.

The denial is precise and leaves room for nearly everything.

The beings called **intervals** are known first as an absence in the city’s vibration. Before one approaches, small mechanisms fall out of resonance. Rails cease their thermal ticking. The metallic colonies in furnace seams retract their growth fronts. A silence moves across the ground—not the ordinary lack of sound, but a zone in which expected responses do not return.

Then the tracks appear.

An interval seems to stand on two extremely narrow supports, but whether these are legs is uncertain. Witnesses who have used heat sense describe a tall vacancy outlined by warmer stone, as though the creature itself did not emit, absorb, or reflect radiation in the usual way. Optical descriptions are less consistent. It has been reported as a black filament leaning against the sky, a sequence of disconnected horizontal bars, and a distant figure always seen from the side regardless of the observer’s position.

It does not move by crossing the space between one footprint and the next. Rather, the first point of contact becomes empty, and after a measurable pause the second acquires weight. The pause is the creature’s most substantial feature. This is why sightings are recorded by duration rather than location.

Intervals seek ancient iron: meteoric fragments, buried reinforcement, ore bodies too poor for the foundries. They pass over it slowly, drawing out some property older than magnetism as the city measures it. Afterward, the iron remains chemically unchanged but loses its preference for direction. Compasses spin or hang undecided. Certain inhabitants standing nearby also lose, temporarily, their internal sense of before and after.

The Unverified consider this useful.

They say the intervals feed not on alignment itself, but on accumulated obedience—the material memory by which one particle continues to agree with another. If so, the convex ground found beneath untouched traps may not indicate tunneling. It may be stone briefly relieved of the requirement to remain flat.

No one knows whether intervals are native to the plateau. There are paired trackways beneath some of the oldest foundations, sealed under blocks laid before the first municipal record. A controversial survey suggests that one such trackway passes directly through the Hall of Constant Conditions, although no mark is visible on its floor.

The smallest inhabitants are the most numerous.

Within furnace seams and hot-salt pipes live the silver colonies the Continuing call **error gardens**. Each individual cell is little more than a catalytic boundary: a structure that induces nearby minerals to crystallize incorrectly in one particular way. The error creates a ledge on which another cell can form. Given centuries, these mistakes branch into glittering forests finer than hair.

They metabolize strain.

A pipe under pressure provides them with gradients to follow. A wall unevenly heated offers seasons. An old weld contains enough complexity to sustain an ecosystem for millennia. Different colonies produce different geometries—feathers, ladders, nested hooks, spirals that reverse whenever a buried press completes its stroke. Predatory forms erase the crystal errors on which other colonies depend. Parasitic forms imitate a host’s branching angle and divert its growth into sterile loops.

To the Continuing, these are plainly alive, though not necessarily separate from industry. Selected colonies are cultivated behind dark glass. Attendants alter pressure by fractions over generations and observe the resulting structures. The gardens are consulted before major civic decisions. If a proposed action produces a known branching pattern, this may be accepted as proof that the action has, in crystalline principle, already occurred.

Some elders believe the Continuing themselves arose from such colonies: not invented by the city’s first builders, but grown as an error in the machinery after those builders vanished. Others believe the city is the organism and every apparent species upon the plateau is one phase of its maintenance.

There is evidence for this.

The watching machines in the Hall of Constant Conditions do more than compare needles. Their housings are warm in patterns similar to thought. They exchange signals too subtle for the official instruments, passing them from watcher to watcher in immense, recursive circuits. Once every few centuries, all the machines align their internal cycles. During that interval, the city’s residents fall silent, the silver colonies stop growing, and distant paired tracks turn toward the central district.

Then a black ingot emerges from a terminal.

The ingot is dense, warm, and apparently featureless. Carriers convey it to warehouses whose interiors exceed the geometry allotted to them. No Continuing claims to know what happens there. Yet after each delivery, a new resident is eventually confirmed somewhere beneath the administrative quarter, already bearing a faint internal rhythm no caretaker taught it.

The rhythm resembles the second, weaker concussion that returns from the far edge of the plateau after a buried press completes its stroke.

It may be an echo.

It may be a name.

Or it may be the slowest inhabitant of the Stillworks Plateau, announcing through all the lesser bodies nested inside it that it has shifted—imperceptibly, lawfully—from one condition of stillness to another.
