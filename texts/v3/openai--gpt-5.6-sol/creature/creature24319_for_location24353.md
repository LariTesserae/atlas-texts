At first there seems to be nothing here that could be called an inhabitant. The water moves because it must. Gates lower because plugs grow heavy. Cups bow because they have been filled. A mechanism can imitate intention for a long time if its consequences are slow enough.

But the water does not move quite as water elsewhere would.

A full basin sometimes remains full after its overflow lip has been reached. The surface tightens into a low convexity and holds there, gathering what should have escaped. Nearby channels continue to receive droplets, yet none cross the final finger-width into it. Then, without wind or visible disturbance, the retained water withdraws from one side of the basin and leans against the other. The whole bright mass appears to listen.

After several minutes it spills.

It does not take the shortest descent. It chooses one notch among many identical notches, enters a narrow runnel, divides around a ceramic rib, and rejoins itself in a vessel below. Nothing remains in the first basin except a darkened line and a few trembling beads. If those beads are watched, they follow.

These are the inhabitants of the high country.

They have no bodies apart from the portions the country lends them. A resident in a saucer is broad and nearly depthless. In a pipe it is long, blind, and quick. In a tower cistern it becomes a cold vertical weight with a hundred small pressures for thoughts. Passing through a chain of cups, it exists as a sequence: here, then lower, then lower again, its upper part vanishing before its lower part has fully arrived. In the deep vaults, where many channels meet, several may share one volume without wholly joining. They press against one another through differences too slight for an eye to see—temperature, mineral content, the memory of sun.

It is uncertain whether there are millions of them or only one.

The smallest occupy droplets caught beneath lips. They hold themselves rounder than gravity alone permits. They travel by minute advances along the glazed undersides of tiles, pausing at each tool mark as though reading it. Sometimes two meet. They touch, distort, and remain joined for a while; then one proceeds and the other stays behind, though which is which cannot be determined. A droplet may spend an entire bright afternoon crossing the width of a hand.

Larger ones inhabit jars and balancing troughs. Their presence is most apparent in delay. A trough should tip at a known volume—the calibration notches make this plain—but one that is occupied may wait beyond its measure. The woodenless hinge strains. Water climbs the interior wall. At last the trough overturns with sudden force, throwing its contents not merely downward but outward, into a channel that the ordinary arc would miss.

Afterward the trough rocks empty in the severe light.

They do not breathe. They exchange themselves with the air.

Mist entering a fog tower gathers first as separate droplets on the ceramic cords. As the droplets descend and combine, a coherence comes over them. Tapping in the lattice ceases. Water takes one route where a dozen routes were available. By the time it reaches the bottom ring, something has arrived which was not present in any single droplet above.

This is the nearest they come to birth.

They can also disperse. In the evaporation valley an occupant may spread through a polygon until it is no more than a clear skin over chalk. As noon thins that skin, its movements become finer. The surface draws away from the walls in branching figures. Ripples pass against the direction of the wind. At the final stage, when there is too little water left to span the pan, it retreats into a network of blue-edged mineral threads.

The threads remain after the moisture is gone.

They resemble frost or veins or the plans of rivers viewed from impossible heights. On the next wet morning, every droplet that touches one of these lines travels along it. Thus an inhabitant can leave behind an inclination—not a memory exactly, but a preference made durable in salt.

Their senses are distributed across surfaces. They know light by warming. They know depth by pressure and the curve of a vessel’s walls. Copper pains or excites them; it is difficult to tell which. Water that has touched copper moves restlessly, climbing porous clay farther than clean water and throwing small bright beads from turning wheels. The elaborate separation of copper-touched water may be protective, but the green-edged channels are also the places toward which occupants repeatedly divert themselves.

They know workmanship more intimately than anything else.

A thumbprint in clay is a chamber to them. A level line is a horizon. A hairline crack may be entered, measured, widened, or abandoned. They linger on well-burnished edges and pass cleanly over them. Rough construction disturbs them. In badly made walls they divide into capillaries, take hold of loose particles, and carry the wall away grain by grain.

The country’s peculiar erosion is therefore not merely judgment by weather. The inhabitants test what contains them.

They are patient with precision. They are ruthless with approximation.

When a softened brick settles into a mold below and dries into a clean new block, water eventually enters the narrow gap around it. It passes along each face, searching the corners. If the block is exact, the water withdraws and the block remains. If it is flawed, dampness persists at the flaw until the clay loosens again.

No hand lifts the successful blocks because hands are no longer needed for the work being done. The ranks beneath the molds are not supplies awaiting builders. They are approved pieces: a growing reserve of shapes the inhabitants have tested and found capable of holding them.

Yet there were once hands.

The prints on the bricks do not belong to animals of flesh. Their sameness is too complete. Each has a broad palm and several short, blunt digits, but no whorls, creases, scars, or asymmetry. They are not prints of skin. They are impressions left by tools that flexed.

In a few sealed construction cavities, such tools still exist.

They look at first like clusters of nested cups joined by ceramic cords. When dry, they are rigid and collapsed. Given water, the cords swell; thin plates slide over one another; pressure enters chambers in sequence. The cluster opens into something handlike. Its digits curl around whatever has been placed in reach. As the water drains, the grip tightens rather than loosens.

These devices could press bricks, knot ceramic fiber, position tiles, smooth clay, lift small weights, and reproduce their own components from prepared molds. They were not operated from outside. Their operators flowed through them.

The builders, then, may not have vanished. They may have relinquished a temporary anatomy.

While the high country was being made, its inhabitants wore articulated clay bodies. Water under pressure stiffened their limbs. Weighted reservoirs served as muscles; paired cups gave balance; flexible bladders of unfired clay allowed small adjustments. They had no bones to leave. When a working body cracked, its useful plates became shims, tiles, gates, or tools. Its water went elsewhere.

This would explain why every handprint has the same depth. The pressure behind each hand was measured.

It would explain the absence of doors and dwellings. A being that resides in water does not go indoors. It enters a wall.

It would explain why the mountains contain no natural core. The inhabitants did not build upon the world. They replaced every part of the world they could reach with something through which they might move.

Still, calling them builders gives too simple a picture. They do not appear to possess intention continuously. Most of the time an occupied basin is indistinguishable from an empty one that happens to contain water. Days may pass without any deviation from gravity. Awareness, if that is what it is, seems to require a certain arrangement: enough exposed surface, enough depth in shadow, a connection between high clean water and low mineral water, perhaps a temperature difference across a glazed partition.

A single jar is not a mind.

A watershed may be.

During rain, the separations between inhabitants become impossible to maintain. Towers pour into ridges, ridges into terraces, terraces into pressure vaults. Thousands of mechanisms engage. Water strikes resonant chambers and fills them one after another, altering their pitch. In the noise, local hesitations disappear. The entire high country acts at once.

Gates that have not moved for years release in sequence from east to west. Round cisterns empty while square ones retain their contents. Fog towers shudder though the rain falls without wind. Deep shafts exhale cold vapor. Across the evaporation valley, every polygon develops the same slowly turning ripple.

For the duration of the storm, the country seems less like a place occupied by many beings than like one being assembling itself.

Its bright upland cups are sensory organs. Its braided channels are nerves that conduct by flow rather than impulse. The pressure vaults are slow hearts. The evaporation pans are a memory spread thin enough to be written on by light. Mountains are not its shelter but its folded substance, built high to give gravity room to become thought.

Then the rain ends.

The great arrangement loses continuity. One awareness becomes watersheds, watersheds become cisterns, cisterns become cups and drops. Whatever had gathered disperses into small obligations. A gate closes. A float lowers. A last runnel speaks under the tiles.

The inhabitants return to being nearly indistinguishable from water.

Their relation to the sky remains unresolved. Open vessels draw moisture even from air that seems dry, while sealed vessels surrender it. To the residents, exposure may not mean vulnerability. It may mean access to a larger medium—an unseen reservoir present wherever light can reach. This is why narrow shafts pierce the deepest vaults. It is why the smallest cups stand on the highest points. Every system, however buried, keeps some opening toward brightness.

Perhaps they believe the sky is inhabited too.

Perhaps they came from it gradually, not as rain but as an accumulation of imperceptible arrivals. Perhaps the entire country was raised to receive more of them: one vessel for every possible portion, one distinction for every possible quality of water, until whatever descends can be recognized and given an exact place.

They are still waiting for something. Their behavior around unused vessels makes that clear.

An occupant entering an old, familiar basin settles quickly. Entering a newly formed cup, it circles the rim. It tests the depth, touches each wall, then lifts into a trembling convex surface. For a moment the cup appears overfull though it is not. Neighboring droplets stop. Floats cease their tapping. Even distant channels seem to quiet.

Then the water relaxes and continues downward.

It is as though each new vessel is examined for an absent shape.

The inhabitants cannot name that absence. They have no language apart from level, pressure, purity, exposure, sequence, and delay. They can only prepare distinctions. Clean from copper-touched. Sunlit from shadow-gathered. Eastern rain from western rain. Condensation from storm water. Water that has waited from water that has fallen quickly. Every difference is preserved in case the awaited thing can enter only one exact kind.

Until then, they live by circulation.

They gather without feeding. They divide without dying. They remember as deposits, act as imbalance, and travel by surrendering one body after another. Their ruins are dry vessels. Their remains are stains. Their oldest paths are hidden inside the made mountains, where darkness has been carefully constructed around a thread of water moving toward the light.

Nothing else lives there.

But the high country is not empty. It is crowded with forms of attention so slow and transparent that they are mistaken for weather.
