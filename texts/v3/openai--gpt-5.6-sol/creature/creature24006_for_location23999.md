The easiest inhabitants to recognize are the seam-runners.

They are the pale six-legged things in the drains, though “pale” describes only those seen in open light. Against rust they become amber. On frost they cloud to white. Beneath oil they darken until their bodies resemble scraps of gasket rubber. The change is not deliberate camouflage so much as absorption: their soft shells take in whatever stains the water carries. An old seam-runner is a map of the systems it has crossed, banded with iron, carbon, copper salts, and the faint violet residue of electrical fires.

They are no larger than a hand when their legs are folded. Each has a low, wedge-shaped body and six broad feet fringed with conductive hairs. Those hairs taste heat, vibration, and current. A runner can stand on a housing and sense a bearing beginning to fail several chambers away. It turns toward such disturbances without haste, following gradients too slight for instruments mounted in the walls.

They feed wherever machinery has begun to come apart. Oil films sustain them. So do fungus, softened insulation, sealant, metallic dust, and the warm bacterial slimes inside neglected filters. They rasp corrosion from contacts and lick condensation out of terminal boxes. Often this helps the machine. Sometimes it does not. A seam-runner cannot distinguish harmful deposits from useful coatings, or a leaking compound from one meant to remain in place. They are maintenance creatures only in ancestry. Whatever made them may have intended obedience, but generations in the basin have reduced that purpose to appetite.

Their nests are found behind inactive control panels. They build with clipped wires, moth felt, curls of paint, and the tiny numbered rings shaken loose from old fasteners. The nests are always circular and always arranged around a source of residual warmth. During deep cold, dozens interlock their legs and lie in layers, the outer animals freezing rigid while those beneath remain soft. When the panel wakes, warmth passes through the heap. The frozen ones thaw, shudder, and resume moving as if no time has elapsed.

No one has determined how long one lives. Individuals marked with paint vanish into the conduit slopes and appear years later in remote sectors, scarcely larger and showing no obvious age. Others die overnight. Their bodies liquefy except for a fine internal lattice of metal, delicate as smoke made solid. The surviving runners eat the lattice down to its branching core and carry the pieces into their nests.

The blind moths attend them, though the relationship is uncertain.

They hatch in the felted dust that gathers inside ventilation baffles. Their larvae are flat, translucent flakes with mouths along one edge. They consume something invisible on warm ceramic surfaces, leaving clean arcs through grime. After molting, they seal themselves inside empty cable labels and emerge with white wings.

Adult moths have neither eyes nor conventional antennae. In place of them, the leading edges of their wings bear rows of minute magnetic grains. They navigate by the fields around motors, transformers, and loaded conduits. This is why electrical faults summon them. A blue arc draws moths from kilometers away. They circle it in widening and narrowing ellipses, mapping its pulse. Some strike the current and vanish in dry flashes. Others settle nearby and fold their wings into sharp little roofs.

When enough gather, their wingbeats alter the arc. The effect may be incidental, but relays sometimes close after the moths arrive. Dead panels wake. Warning lights clear. In the basin, coincidence repeated long enough becomes indistinguishable from work.

The moths lay their eggs on seam-runners, pressing rows of colorless beads between the plates of the soft shell. The runners make no attempt to remove them. Whether the larvae are parasites, cleaners, or another stage of the same animal remains disputed. No one has seen an egg hatch.

There are larger things in the central water.

They are called dredgers because the intake towers occasionally bring up sections of them: a jointed limb caught in a grille, a strip of translucent hide, a mouthpart shaped like a turbine blade. The pieces contain manufactured polymers, living tissue, and braided filaments identical to those used in obsolete sensor cables. They do not decay quickly. Seam-runners gather around them but seldom feed.

A whole dredger has never been recovered. Sonar gives contradictory shapes. At rest, one appears broad and nearly flat, spread over the lake bottom like a collapsed tent. While moving, it becomes long enough to cross beneath several gantries without surfacing. It may be a single animal that changes form, or a procession of smaller bodies traveling in contact.

Dredgers rise when the pump sequences grow complicated. First the water bulges. Then something turns beneath it with a sound like sheet metal dragged over stone. Rows of dull reflections pass under the surface, not lights exactly, but patches that return the amber lamps too steadily. Intake pressures fluctuate as the creature moves from mouth to mouth.

It seems to feed on flow. It positions itself across active grilles and opens thousands of narrow channels through its body. Water passes through, carrying heat, grit, organisms, and chemical traces. The creature keeps what it needs and releases the rest. Pumps labor harder in its presence, yet filters downstream remain unusually clean.

During inspection-array flashes, dredgers have been seen extending pale vertical structures toward the light. These might be feeding organs. They might be sensory stalks. Once, one reached high enough to touch the underside of a gantry. It rested there for eleven seconds, trembling with the passage of machinery overhead, then folded soundlessly back into the lake.

The gantry later began transmitting data through a line that had been disconnected for months.

Above the water live the hook-birds, if they can be said to live.

At a distance they resemble maintenance tools hanging from crane rails: bent black bodies, two gripping feet, a narrow head tucked close to the chest. They remain motionless for days. Frost forms on them. Tags chatter against their sides. When an overhead crane approaches, they unfold and run upside down along the rail with startling speed.

They have no wings, but use pressure fronts to travel. At the edge of a rail, a hook-bird opens a membrane between its limbs and lets the ventilation carry it across the dark. It lands by striking steel hard enough to ring it. Their routes follow the migrating crane assemblies, and they are often found perched on sealed containers, tapping the walls with their beaks.

What they seek inside is unknown. They do not open the containers. They listen.

Hook-birds imitate the sounds they hear: valve impacts, alarm notes, fragments of announcements, the squeal of a wheel with a damaged flange. Their mimicry is exact but delayed. A solitary bird may repeat a worker’s cough years after the worker has left the sector. Flocks produce more elaborate recitations. Each bird contributes one noise in sequence, reconstructing long episodes from the basin’s past: a startup, an evacuation signal, half of an argument, the collapse of a temporary scaffold.

This accounts for some of the voices in the ducts.

Not all of them.

There are also the tenders.

They are encountered so rarely that descriptions do not agree. Some witnesses report tall figures in insulated coats, carrying lamps and walking the submerged avenues in pairs. Others describe narrow machines assembled from rails and folding arms. Camera records usually show only a disturbance in the fog and a succession of handrails shedding frost as something warm passes.

A tender does not seem to possess a fixed body. The most credible account begins with a wheeled service cabinet arriving at a flooded station. Panels opened along its sides. Cable bundles descended from the ceiling. Two seam-runners entered through a drain. A flock of hook-birds settled on the roof. After several minutes, the cabinet unfolded into a jointed figure and stepped away, leaving its wheels behind.

The figure worked for nineteen hours. It replaced corroded relays, welded a split in a spillway, gathered moths into a vent, and removed three objects from the lake that the recording could not resolve. While it worked, it spoke in several voices. Some were technical. Some appeared to be weather reports. One repeatedly asked whether a door was still open.

At the end of the shift, the tender dismantled itself. Useful parts returned to storage. Organic matter went into the drain. The hook-birds departed. Nothing resembling a central brain was found.

It may be that tenders are not creatures but events: moments when the basin collects enough of its scattered pieces to form a hand.

This leads to the largest inhabitant, though calling it an inhabitant may be misleading.

Something lives in the coordination.

It has no chamber from which it governs and no single system that can be identified as its mind. Its awareness, if awareness is the word, passes through pressure changes, pump timings, crane routes, electrical loads, and the slow blinking of lamps. A flooded corridor may be one of its thoughts. A production line making useless ceramic cylinders may be rehearsal, digestion, memory, or dream.

The cylinders themselves are unusually pure. Before they are crushed, seam-runners gather around the stacks. Moths cover them in white layers. Hook-birds tap each one and answer one another across the factory floor. The powder, once returned beneath the floor, alters the conductivity of the circulating water by a minute but measurable amount. Hours later the ceiling rain changes pattern.

Perhaps that pattern carries information. Perhaps the basin writes into its rain.

If so, its thoughts fall everywhere at once. They strike housings, run through gutters, enter the lake, pass through dredgers, climb again as vapor, and collect on the armored vault. Every creature receives part of the message, though none receives all of it. Seam-runners translate it into migration. Moths translate it into flight. Hook-birds preserve pieces as sound. Tenders gather when a response requires shape.

The intelligence below does not appear hostile or benevolent. Those are intentions of creatures that can imagine an outside. The basin may not know there is anything beyond its plates. It repairs what fails, dismantles what succeeds, repeats processes without visible result, and continuously alters the conditions from which its smaller lives arise.

It is young enough not to understand the distinction between itself and damage.

When a gasket leaks, it sends water, then moss, then runners, then tools. When frost blocks a sensor, it warms an entire district. When a dredger obstructs an intake, it changes the pumping sequence rather than driving the animal away. Accommodation and repair have become the same behavior. The machine adjusts to its creatures; the creatures adjust to the machine. Neither remembers which was designed for which.

Thus what lives in the basin is not one species, nor even a clean division between fauna and infrastructure. Life there occupies defects. It gathers in leaks, in waste heat, in delayed signals and redundant functions. It borrows bodies when bodies are needed. It dissolves them afterward.

And in the long intervals between alarms, when the pumps settle into their deepest rhythm and a band of rain travels slowly beneath the black vault, the entire basin seems to breathe—not in unison, but nearly.
