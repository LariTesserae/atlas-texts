At first the city seems inhabited only by weather.

Wind occupies the towers. Water enters every unlocked chamber. Frost takes the upper rooms and abandons them at noon. Mist sleeps against the eastern walls. These are the most visible tenants because they are always arriving, leaving, and rearranging what they touch.

But the oldest inhabitant is the city itself.

It is not one creature in the ordinary sense. The towers do not share a single skin, nor do all the structures swell and close together. One may be drinking while its neighbor remains dry; one may begin a new chamber while another sheds half a roof. Yet beneath the bowl their foundations meet in a felt of pale fibers finer than roots and harder than sinew. Water, minerals, warmth, and slow chemical disturbances pass through this buried web. A wound in the western arcade can alter the growth of a doorway near the central court years later.

The separate buildings may be individuals. They may be organs. They may be successive bodies of one life too old and dispersed to preserve a clean boundary between itself and its descendants.

The central tower is not dead, though much of it has become inert. Its outer walls still draw moisture, and deep below the moss court there are cavities that contract during changes in pressure. The pulse heard in storms is partly wind, but not entirely. Just before the silent frost, the tower gives several compressions too low for human ears. The black grasses fold after the first. The shell-bearers seal themselves after the second. The birds leave after the third.

No animal waits to hear another.

The life of the city is slow enough to resemble geology. Its softest new growth may take a season to rise the width of a fingernail. Its injuries remain open for centuries. It has no need to remember in nerves. Events are recorded in layers: a decade of drought as a dense gray ring, a mineral-rich thaw as a blue seam, an infestation as a band of tiny sealed chambers. Cut through an old roof plate and one finds the history of cloud and cold written inward from both surfaces.

Its reproduction, if that is what it is, happens rarely. New growth does not begin from seed. A patch of ground first becomes unable to dry. Pale fibers gather under the stone and lift it grain by grain. Moss thickens there, then dies in a near-perfect circle. For many years nothing more happens. Eventually a low rim appears, porous and flexible, collecting mist. Centuries afterward it may become a chamber. Whether the city creates the wet place or merely finds it cannot be determined.

Around this immense, distributed life exists a society of smaller things, though none of them has a city of its own.

The moss is the principal grazer and the principal soil. Its upper layers are green or silver, but beneath them lie centuries of brown compressed tissue, roof dust, shells, droppings, and softened stone. Almost everything that feeds here feeds upon the moss, directly or after death. Almost everything that dies here enters it. The deepest cushions are warm by only a fraction, yet that fraction is enough to sustain translucent mites, white threadworms, and colonies of blind, many-legged creatures no longer than a seed husk. These never emerge onto bare rock. To them, a moss court is not a carpet but a vertical forest with a dark ancestral earth far below.

The liverworts are more closely bound to the buildings. Their glossy fans fasten along sweating seams and open when pressure rises behind the wall. Some penetrate the mineral hairs without harming them. Others cause small lesions from which nutrient-rich fluid leaks. The city closes most such wounds. A few it maintains, season after season, as though the theft were also useful.

The shell-bearing insects belong equally to water and stone. Sealed in dry weather, they breathe so little that lichens sometimes grow over them. Rain awakens them not by wetness alone but by vibration. The first impact of drops on a roof plate sends a tremor through the channels, and within moments the cracks begin to loosen.

They enter the runoff already feeding. Their mouths are broad scrapers beneath their bodies, stripping films of algae and mineral sugars from the grooves as the current carries them. They do not swim against it. Their journey is measured in collisions, eddies, and sudden falls through darkness. Many are swallowed by sinkholes. Those that survive fasten wherever the water weakens, and that place becomes their home until the next storm.

No individual is likely to travel twice. Before sealing itself, it leaves a cluster of nearly invisible young in the wet groove. Thus each rain carries one generation through the city and wakes the next behind it. The dry hiss heard in the channels is the sound of thousands of tiny lives spending their only hour in motion.

The white-backed grazers come from lower folds of the mountain. They are visitors, but old ones. Their feet divide into thick, muscular pads capable of gripping wet stone without cutting the moss. They feed delicately, drawing only the fresh tips through comb-like front teeth. A herd can pass through a court and leave it cropped to one height without disturbing the layers beneath.

They do not trust sight in the city. Sound reaches them bent and displaced, and their blunt faces conceal broad inner ears protected by folds of fur. Each knows the usual voices of the bowl: which tower hums under west wind, which drain knocks after rain, which arch cracks as the temperature falls. They freeze when those patterns change because change may mean that something large has entered a gallery, or that a familiar opening has closed behind them.

The oldest females remember routes that no longer exist. They still approach sealed arches and stand before them, sometimes for hours, breathing against the pale surface. In a later season—or many seasons later—the arch may reopen. Whether they are waiting from memory or hearing water inside the wall is unclear.

They bear their young outside the bowl. No grazer lies down within the city, even in the sheltered groves.

The black birds do.

Their nests are placed near warm cisterns, built from lichen strips, shed fibers, and the papery inner layers of old roof plates. They seldom use the same cavity twice because the opening may contract around a nest between seasons. Their toes are long and soft, suited to clinging to damp walls. Their narrow wings allow them to turn inside shafts where there appears to be no room to turn.

They hatch nearly silent. For the first weeks, the young hear the city through the walls of the nest and begin by copying whatever surrounds them. One brood may learn the western howl. Another answers with the clatter of rain through a particular drain. Birds raised near the central tower reproduce its pulse so exactly that sleeping grazers sometimes wake and leave the court.

These imitations are not ornament. They are maps.

A bird approaching a dark junction gives a brief tower-note and listens to the return. In a broad chamber the sound comes back softened; in a sealed passage it returns sharp. Mated birds recognize each other not by a personal call but by a shared sequence of architectural noises gathered around their nest. When the city alters, their speech alters with it.

Very old birds possess fragments from structures that have closed completely. On still days one may circle above an empty stretch of stone repeating the whistle of a tower buried under the moss.

Near the cisterns live things less easily separated from the city.

The pale filaments in the warm broth are not all part of the lining. Some are rootlike growths, but others contract when shadow passes over them. They have no heads. Their bodies fork, rejoin, and exchange sections. They graze the blue-green membrane, yet they also keep the water from clouding beyond a certain thickness. If removed, they collapse into threads of chalk. Inside the cistern they form slow coils around droplets of oil and digest them over weeks.

Among these filaments drift flat, transparent sacs containing dark granules. During the thaw the sacs rise to the surface and fasten beneath the cistern roof. There they dry into scales. Some scales become part of the building’s lining. Others detach during the next rain and vanish into the underground channels.

They may be larvae of the city. They may be parasites that have imitated its substance so completely that the distinction no longer matters.

The hidden predators are inhabitants of another order.

Their tracks suggest a broad animal walking on several soft lobes rather than feet. No claw marks accompany them. No scat, fur, feathers, or shed skin has been found in the open. Their impressions are deepest at the center and faint at the edges, as though the creature carries most of its weight in a hanging body.

They do not cross plazas. They appear from wet doorways, travel a short distance through moss, and disappear into another structure. Often the two buildings have no passage between them that a grazer—or any rigid-bodied animal—could use.

The birds react to them by becoming architecturally quiet. They cease mimicking open shafts and use only muffled cistern sounds. The grazers bunch together with their white backs outward. Shell-bearers retract even in flowing water.

In glimpses, the predator seems pale. Then dark. Then not colored at all, but coated in whatever lies around it. It can flatten itself into the seam between roof plates. Water passes over its back without breaking into a recognizable shape. Its mouth, if it has one, leaves circular absences in moss and occasionally a clean hollow in the body of a grazer, as though the softer parts were drawn out through an opening too small to admit teeth.

There may be several. There may be only one, distributed through the inner galleries as the buildings are distributed through the bowl. Some chambers contain patches of warm, contractile surface that resemble wall until touched by runoff. The city could be sheltering the predators. It could be growing them. They could be the city’s rare, swift organs—the only part of its life capable of crossing a street in a night.

Yet the grazers return every thaw, and the birds continue to nest. Whatever the predators are, they do not empty the bowl. Their hunger has limits.

The low trees maintain another small population. During the six days of flowering, their groves become the most crowded places on the mountain. Dark flies rise from under the bark where they have waited nearly a year as dormant grubs. Minute beetles crawl into the yellow flowers and remain there overnight, warmed by petals that close around them. The black birds descend from the air to feed, and the grazers risk the edge of the groves for fallen blossoms.

Then the leaves fold, the flowers blacken, and the abundance disappears. Eggs remain under bark. Seeds lodge in wall cracks. For the rest of the year the groves look empty again.

The silent frost reveals the deepest community only by its absence.

Before it comes, all the mobile inhabitants withdraw from exposed ground. The shell-bearers seal. The moss folds over its air spaces. Cistern filaments sink. Predators retreat farther than any reachable gallery. The birds climb above the rim, though the air there is colder. No species needs to be taught this. Even young animals born since the last such frost respond correctly.

The city pulls its water downward, but not all of it. A thin solution remains across roofs, streets, and courts. As it freezes, dissolved gases and mineral particles are excluded from the growing ice. They gather into lines above the buried fibers beneath the bowl. Around every active pore forms a bubble; above every living foundation, a pale avenue of them. The finer city suspended in the glaze is not a vision or prophecy. It is the hidden anatomy of the place made visible—the dense under-city of conduits, buds, closed chambers, and connections that the open structures only imperfectly express.

Some of those bubble-towers stand where no building yet exists.

Some stand where a tower fell thousands of years ago.

When the hum returns and the ice breaks, meltwater carries the arranged particles into cracks throughout the bowl. Moss takes some. Insects swallow some. Birds drink some from the roofs. Much of it enters the city again.

So what lives there cannot be listed cleanly.

There is a city that may be a forest, a colony, an animal, or the final living habit of the mountain. There are creatures that feed upon it, creatures that pollinate its groves, creatures that clean its cisterns, creatures that navigate by its voice, and creatures that move through its body as though through their own. Each inhabits the others. Stone enters moss; moss enters grazer; grazer enters the pale thing in the doorway; all of them return through water to the walls.

Nothing rules the bowl. Nothing built it. Nothing wholly owns a chamber or keeps the same shape long enough to call it home.

They live there by learning the city’s changes.

The city lives there by incorporating theirs.
