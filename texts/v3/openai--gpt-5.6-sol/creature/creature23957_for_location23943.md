The first inhabitants are difficult to separate from the things they inhabit.

A shelf of black stone may be the back of an animal whose face lies three valleys away. What appears to be a scatter of pebbles may have arranged itself around a warmer central pebble and may, over the course of a century, close into a protective shell. The pale seams underfoot contain old cartilage. Some of the ridges are asleep; some are extinct; some are merely taking so long to turn their heads that no living witness has yet seen the beginning and end of the gesture.

The plateau’s creatures have bodies, but body here is not a boundary. It is a tendency for matter to remain associated.

The smallest are called dust-keepers by the people of the shelters. They occupy cracks too narrow for a fingernail. Each has six hooked legs, a translucent shell, and a dark internal fleck that changes position when nobody is looking. Packed together, they resemble mineral deposits. Their colonies preserve minute differences in temperature and pressure, holding them as other animals might hold fat. In bitter pockets of air they slowly thicken; in thin air they become glassy and almost flat. When pried loose, a colony gives off the smell of shut rooms and old plumage.

No one has seen a dust-keeper walk. Nevertheless, tools left unwrapped sometimes become covered in them. The people say they do not travel across the intervening ground. They make the distance between themselves and the tool cease to matter.

Larger shell-bearers stand in the powder hollows, many-legged and low. Their plates overlap in elaborate tiers, and the edges of the oldest plates are etched with lines resembling maps. Families can read these markings—not as records of where the creatures have been, since they have been nowhere, but of the places that have passed near them. One shell may show the contour of a vanished mountain, another the plan of a settlement that has not yet been built. Such plates are harvested only after they lift from the body of their own accord. To cut one free would fasten the cutter to the event depicted upon it.

The herds are the plateau’s most visible life and, in some ways, its least knowable.

They stand in ranks across the open basins: fine-legged animals with lowered muzzles; barrel-bodied forms buried knee-deep in their own accumulated dust; tall ivory creatures whose necks carry folded fans of horn. Some have eyes on both sides of the head. Some have a single eye beneath a lid that opens vertically. A few possess no visible organs of sight, yet these are valued as watchers because their attention does not point in only one direction.

Their coats do not grow hair by hair. Instead, whole patterns deepen. A dun animal acquires a dark band over decades; the band divides; pale spots emerge within it; eventually the pattern loosens as mineral wool that can be combed away in one piece. The people make blankets from these cast-off markings. Wrapped around a sleeper, such a blanket may cause the sleeper to wake older, younger, or convinced of having spent the night as part of the herd.

The antlered beasts are slow even by the measure of the plateau. Their crowns branch until they touch and then pass into one another without wound or seam. Entire populations become joined above the ground, while their bodies remain distinct below. Nutriment, memory, and attention circulate through these aerial forests of horn. A calf accumulating in one basin may receive its final shape from an animal standing beyond the horizon. If one member of the lattice begins to die into stone, the others turn their eyes toward it over generations, preventing the change from completing until the herd is ready to inherit its mass.

In this way the herd possesses something like thought.

It is not thought made of impulses. It is made of arrangements.

One animal lowers its eyelids. Ten miles away, another’s shadow touches a cairn. A century later, a third acquires a new fork of antler. Together these may constitute recognition, warning, grief, or a decision to move the herd to a neighboring age. The shelter people understand only fragments of this language. Their oldest hand signs are imitations of it.

The predators are not more active than their prey, but they are more concentrated.

Pale cats crouch with their hindquarters bunched, each fixed upon an animal it may spend five hundred years approaching. Their whiskers are metallic and extend far beyond the muzzle as lines of sensitivity rather than matter. A whisker’s influence can lie across several bodies without touching them. Where those invisible lines cross, prey lose a little warmth.

The cats’ mouths are almost never open. When one is found with its jaws parted, the surrounding region is marked and abandoned. An open-mouthed cat is already biting something, though the victim may not yet have arrived.

There are taller hunters with folded limbs and faces covered by a single hooked plate. They feed on alignment. A person who sees one directly behind a companion must step aside without blinking, because for that instant the companion has become its prey. If the alignment is broken, the hunger may pass elsewhere. If it is maintained by frightened attention, the predator and victim can become bound into the same posture, their shadows exchanging substance until one is a shell around the other.

The greatest animals have no hunters.

They lie along the broader ridges, armored and many-porous, mistaken for fortifications by anyone who first sees them from a distance. Lichen diagrams cover their flanks. Shrubs root in the seams between their plates. Herds stand upon them, and shelters have sometimes been built in hollows that later prove to be old sensory pits. These ridge-beasts may be remnants from the time when stone was fluid. Their internal warmth reaches the surface in isolated pockets of breathable air.

The shelter people do not agree whether the great beasts are sleeping or whether sleep is merely what their waking looks like.

They call them by no names.

Indeed, the people give permanent names to very little.

A child may be called Soft Palm while being carried, Third Hood after receiving a mask, and East-Facing when old enough to stand watch. These are not stages of one name but temporary handles, discarded before repetition can make them binding. Within a family, a person is indicated by small signs made near the sternum: two bent fingers for the child who accumulated beneath an antler rack; a thumb enclosed in the fist for the woman whose shadow once failed to return; a knuckle touched to the throat for the man found inside a shelter whose builders had not yet been born.

Their faces are rarely bare. The masks are simple—felt, slate flakes, thin shell, woven hair—but each interrupts the continuity of being seen. Eyeholes are offset rather than paired. Some masks cover only one cheek or the lower half of the brow. The purpose is not disguise. It is to prevent a person from being wholly gathered into another person’s observation.

To be completely seen is considered a form of capture.

Children learn this before they learn hand speech. They are taught never to stare at a companion who has stumbled, never to watch a sleeper through the entire act of waking, never to follow the sun’s edge in the hope of seeing it move. If someone begins an important task, the others look away at intervals. A toolmaker shaping copper works behind a hanging fringe. A healer passes between patient and watcher, breaking the view. Lovers meet in bent passages, among antlers, or on opposite sides of a perforated hide so that intimacy does not harden into an unalterable scene.

Their courtesy is made of glances withdrawn.

Their cruelty is sustained attention.

The severest punishment is not confinement but witnessing. A condemned person is seated within a ring of obsidian eyes and assigned living watchers who relieve one another before their concentration fails. Food can be placed nearby, but the prisoner cannot reach for it while observed. Sleep cannot descend. Thought itself begins to repeat. Eventually the person becomes a durable posture: warm, clear-eyed, neither dead nor any longer capable of entering an unobserved moment in which change might occur.

Such sentences are rare. The people fear the resulting figures, for a fixed human being exerts a peculiar pull. Objects begin to resemble possessions arranged around them. Paths curve toward their feet. Descendants dream from their point of view.

Most plateau dwellers organize their lives around preserving small mercies of discontinuity.

Every shelter has blind spaces. There are cupboards whose interiors can be reached but not viewed, sleeping pits roofed in overlapping plates, and low chambers entered backward so that no one sees the room become occupied. Tools are wrapped in separate layers, each tied with a different knot. A knife left exposed too long becomes incapable of cutting anything except what lay before it during the exposure. A bowl watched from firing to cooling will hold only the first substance placed inside. A garment made under uninterrupted inspection cannot be removed.

Fire is possible, but delicate. Flame cannot be watched into being. Fuel is assembled beneath an opaque cap, and the person tending it listens for the change in the air. Once kindled, the fire must remain partly hidden. Fully exposed flame becomes a rigid tongue of brightness, giving neither heat nor smoke until every observer turns away. For this reason cooking stones are heated in darkness and rolled by means of long copper hooks into covered bowls.

The people eat little. They scrape gray films from shaded rock and collect the organic powder that accumulates where animals stand close together. Gas bladders from wire plants are punctured beneath the tongue, releasing a cool pressure that can sustain the body for days. Certain lichen diagrams may be ground into flakes, though doing so destroys not only the growth but whatever relation the diagram describes. A family eats such lichen only in famine. Afterward they may discover that a path no longer reaches a neighboring shelter, or that an ancestor has vanished from hand signs and memory.

Meat is almost unknown. To cut living flesh is to open mineral fibers that may take generations to close. Yet the animals shed more than horn and shell. They give up heat, posture, shadow, and age.

A person wishing to preserve youth may spend several years facing one of the pewter shrubs among the young animals. A person burdened by too many years stands on the outward side, among those turned away. Nothing visible passes between plant, beast, and human. Nevertheless, faces alter when hidden. A child may emerge from beneath a hood with the grave features of an elder. An old watcher may uncover her hands and find them small.

The people do not regard this as magic. To them, age is a substance that settles according to orientation.

Birth likewise requires careful inattention.

When a pregnancy is suspected, no one examines the body directly. The expectant parent is clothed in widening layers. Relatives leave dust, shed fibers, eggshell, and small gifts in a curtained alcove. They listen without counting days. If they become too eager—if they repeatedly lift the curtain, measure the garments, or imagine the child too precisely—the birth may fix before completion. Such unfinished children are not buried. They are placed in sheltered hollows where dust can continue its work.

Some finish after a season. Some after centuries.

A few of the plateau’s people may have begun as failed births belonging to cultures long erased. They wake within old cocoons speaking gestures no one recognizes. They are adopted cautiously and never asked for a continuous account of where they came from. Continuity, after all, is what the people have spent their lives learning not to attract.

Death is less an event than a negotiation with the ground.

The dying are carried to places where bedrock already resembles them. A broad-backed person is laid near a dome of basalt; someone with many descendants is placed beneath branching antlers; a singer, if such a dangerous person has existed, is brought to a hollow where echoes arrive early. Their family watches in turns, enough to slow the merging but not prevent it. Farewell may last for generations. The dead person’s fingers grain into stone. Their cloak becomes a mineral stain. Their last warmth enters a pocket of air that descendants visit to breathe.

No one knows whether awareness ends.

The people assume it spreads.

This is why they cover the eyes of cairns. The obsidian set atop each marker is not symbolic. It is a piece of ancestral vision, polished and given direction. Uncovered, it holds the path in one arrangement. Covered, it permits the traveler to arrive somewhere not already determined.

Journeys therefore require alternating between sight and blindness. Travelers memorize three or four steps, lower their masks, walk them without looking, then raise the mask again. They use reflections only when direct sight would be too binding. They pass through herds according to agreements embodied in the animals’ postures. If a corridor closes, they do not squeeze between bodies. That would mean the herd has withdrawn consent—or that the traveler has reached a time in which the agreement was never made.

The most respected travelers are not those who cross the greatest distances, but those who can return changed.

They carry no maps. A map observed often enough becomes stronger than the land and forces the land to conform. Instead, routes are kept as sequences of incomplete gestures: elbow east, palm under stone, two fingers hidden, eyes closed beneath suspended wings. Each telling differs. Accuracy lies in preserving enough uncertainty for the destination to remain accessible.

At the inward-facing rings, however, uncertainty is forbidden.

There the plateau’s beasts stand by the thousand, arranged around spaces that appear empty in ordinary light. Some rings are made of antlered grazers; others of birds suspended at ascending heights; a few include human watchers whose shelters and descendants exist solely to replace them. Every eye points inward.

Inside one ring is said to be a red storm still moving through the age of mountains. Inside another, an illness waits halfway between its first fever and its second victim. Elsewhere there may be a city, noisy and quick, whose towers rise whenever no gaze pins them below the surface. The watchers do not need to understand what they restrain. Their task is simply to deny it an unwitnessed interval.

Sometimes an animal’s eye clouds.

Then the nearest shelter sends a replacement watcher. They stand at the rim with one eye uncovered while the failing beast is allowed, at last, to look away and become geology. The dangerous center may flicker during the exchange. A scent of rain appears. A warm gust touches one cheek. Bells are heard from beneath the ground.

No one turns toward these things.

At the western precipice live those creatures least distinguishable from action.

They are falling animals: birds with wings caught in downstrokes, herd-beasts stepping into empty air, shell-bearers surrounded by pebbles that left the cliff with them. Some may have been falling since before the plateau wore flat. The people occasionally descend among them, using cords anchored to motionless slabs. Because nothing changes while watched, the climbers work in pairs. One watches the falling world, holding it still. The other moves only while hidden behind hanging earth or overlapping wings.

Below the cloud layer, they say, the fall may be continuing normally.

No climber has returned with certainty. Those who do return bring feathers heavy as metal, pieces of structures belonging to no known settlement, and memories of roaring air that leave them unable to tolerate silence. Such people often go to live alone beside the edge. Over years, birds gather around them in the same posture, until person and flock appear to be one suspended thought.

Yet the plateau’s life is not mournful.

It does not experience stillness as deprivation. The suspended bird is not longing to complete its wingbeat. The cat does not suffer impatience. The herd does not feel trapped inside an instant. Motion is only one way for change to organize itself, and here life has found others: substitution, accumulation, alignment, erosion, concealment, shifts of attention. The creatures grow, feed, reproduce, hunt, communicate, migrate, and remember. They simply do so without passing visibly through the space between one state and another.

Even the people laugh, though laughter is performed carefully. They cover their faces and shake in silence. The sound, if allowed out, might arrive too early and fix the joke before it is understood.

They make toys for children: nested masks, strings of shell pieces that form different animals depending on which gaps one looks through, copper disks polished on only one side. They tell stories with deliberate omissions. The finest storyteller is praised for the part no one heard, because it is there, in the unwitnessed passage, that the story can still become true.

They love by leaving room for alteration.

They survive by refusing to see everything.

And around them, to every horizon and deep beneath their feet, the other inhabitants persist in their innumerable arrangements: mineral-fleshed grazers consuming the nearness of lichen, predators drinking through intersecting shadows, birds migrating between glances, ancestral bodies settling into strata, unborn creatures assembling grain by grain in the dark.

Together they form no stable division between population and landscape.

The plateau does not carry life upon it.

The plateau is what life becomes when nothing is permitted to pass away merely because it cannot move.
