At first it seems that nothing lives there except the gardens.

They are the most legible inhabitants: rooted, growing, responsive to their surroundings. Yet even they do not divide cleanly into plant and stone. A grove may remain motionless for centuries, its mica fans sealed edge to edge, until some buried stratum tightens. Then the whole ravine opens at once. Copper fronds lift. Black fibers draw taut. The jointed columns fill with sparks that ascend against gravity and gather in their crowns.

No part of this growth is young in the usual sense. A newly appeared seed may contain laminations older than the cliff on which it rests. A trunk may be ancient at its center and not yet have come into existence around its circumference. Rings, where they occur, record not years but pressures endured: compression, release, contradiction, the long torsion of a slope becoming two slopes.

The groves respond to more than strain. They lean toward cold, toward sharpness, toward places where possibilities have been reduced. If a boulder finally chooses one shape and abandons all others, nearby leaves turn their pale undersides toward it. For several days their dust chambers brighten. If a path loses its destination, the plants along it close.

Their reproduction is less reliable than their growth. Seeds appear where compatible shadows intersect, faceted and black, each no larger than a knuckle. Most remain dormant forever. Some split when set beneath a particular weight. From the opening emerges not a sprout but a hair-thin fracture, which enters the ground and persuades the rock to crack around it. The plant develops afterward, filling the fracture with fibers until root and fissure can no longer be distinguished.

There are also things among the gardens that might be animals, although they do not graze, hunt, mate, or move continuously.

One may first be noticed as a cluster of loose slate arranged beneath a mineral cedar. Return after a few hours and the slate has gathered into a low, many-jointed shape. Return after a year and it stands at the edge of the grove, facing a cold seam. Its body is made of plates that never touch. Narrow intervals of shadow hold them together.

These beings travel only while unobserved, or perhaps observation is what prevents their travel from becoming visible. They leave no prints. Instead, the ground behind them grows slightly more probable. Fault lines straighten in the direction they have passed. Stones that had balanced against local gravity settle into one orientation.

They are usually called walkers, though walking is one of the few things no witness has seen them do.

A walker has no permanent size. Nearby, it may stand below the knee; across a ravine, it may appear taller than a tower. Its shape varies with the geometry surrounding it. On a broad facet it flattens into something like a seven-rayed star. In a narrow fault it becomes upright and slender. Under an overhang it may resemble a seated figure wrapped in angular robes. There is often a suggestion of hands: two, four, or an entire fringe of them, each divided into seven dark lengths.

Where a head should be, the plates form an empty cage. Salt slowly accumulates inside. Old walkers carry faces of white crystal, not grown according to anatomy but according to whatever pressures they have crossed. No two masks are alike. Some are smooth except for a vertical slit. Some branch outward in antlered hexagons. Some contain tiny sealed images: a softened mountain, a cloud, a falling drop.

The walkers tend the groves, although not in any way resembling cultivation elsewhere. They remove no weeds, because nothing competes. They prune only branches that have begun curling toward pressures that no longer exist. They carry seeds from one overlap of shadows to another, trying thousands of placements over centuries. Most attempts produce nothing. When a seed finally opens, every walker in the basin may turn toward it at once.

They feed neither on the gardens nor directly on stone. What sustains them appears to be resolution.

They gather around events that have almost finished becoming true: the final alignment of a crack, the instant a transparent stone loses its last opacity, the separation of one summit into two. At such places their plates draw closer. Sparks pass through the gaps. Their salt masks become more intricate. Afterward the event seems diminished, as though some portion of its certainty has been consumed.

This makes them dangerous in the cold hollows. A traveler entering one of those zones may feel not merely watched but simplified. Forgotten choices vanish first. Then unrealized intentions. The walker does not attack; it stands nearby and becomes more coherent while its witness becomes less capable of having been otherwise.

Perhaps the walkers built the huts. The tools fit their many-part hands, and the seven impressions on the walls resemble their digits. Yet the structures suggest bodies more vulnerable than theirs. The entrances are low. The salt-packed masks stored within are shaped to cover soft faces. Bowls imply something once needed to be gathered, though there has never been food or water. The stone boxes of flattened speech imply speakers who could not simply wait for meaning to return.

There may therefore be another people in the basin.

They are easiest to perceive inside the silent shadows, where the mountain’s chord turns visible. At first they seem to be imperfections in the distortion bands: upright interruptions, narrow and wavering. Then one moves against the pulse.

They have bodies only in shelter.

Outside the shadow of an overhang, they loosen immediately. Their outlines spread across the ground, their voices shear into gray flakes, and their limbs separate into several possible gestures. Inside, they gather. Dust climbs from the floor and adheres to them. Fibers creep from stored branches to braid temporary tendons. Thin pieces of slate lift and arrange themselves around a hollow torso. Last of all, a salt mask is taken from the wall and pressed over the place where a face might be.

A fully gathered one resembles a person assembled from the contents of the country: black plates, mineral cord, cold dust, and constrained shadow. It has two arms when resting, more when working. Each hand divides into seven fingers, though the outermost pair may be no more substantial than alternative positions of the others.

They do not breathe. They have no blood to spill. When cut, they open into nested membranes filled with the same dry dust found in the broad leaves. The wound closes not by healing but by being named, carefully and only once, inside a protected hut. Too much repetition is dangerous. If “arm” is spoken too often, the walls may grow an arm of their own.

These sheltered people communicate mostly by touch and placement. One sets a shard between two others; another turns it. A palm is held against a wall while the mountain’s visible noise passes through both. Complex accounts can be made from arrangements of pressure knives, seed facets, and speech flakes. A box of gray words is not an archive to be read in sequence. It is emptied onto a taut metallic leaf, and the flakes arrange themselves according to the stress beneath the hut. What emerges is less a sentence than a temporary weather of meaning.

Their lives are exceedingly long but discontinuous.

An individual may gather for six hours, work on an arch, then disperse when the shelter’s silence turns away. It may not assemble again for four hundred years. Whether this is sleep, death, or interruption is unclear even to them. They recognize one another by habits of construction: the angle at which a wedge is placed, the preferred direction of a seven-fingered mark, the manner of sorting words that have lost their speaker.

This is why the great arches remain unfinished. No builder experiences enough consecutive time to complete one. A slab may be raised by workers whose next moments occur generations apart. Their patience is not a virtue. It is the structure of their existence.

They do not remember as living creatures elsewhere remember. Memory adheres to objects. A hammer contains the labor performed with it. A doorway retains everyone who entered. The third arch, the one that gives memories of a softened future, may be their attempt to create continuity: a place where they can recall lives they have not yet managed to live.

Some of them pass through it repeatedly. They return carrying memories of rain.

This has altered them.

The oldest salt masks contain only geometric cavities. Later masks show dimples like the marks of drops. Recent ones have channels descending from brow to jaw. Those who wear them sometimes tilt their heads toward the empty sky and remain so for the duration of their gathering. They weave bowls finer than any dry world requires. They line the bowls along hut walls. They have made a word for the sound of water, though when spoken it falls as a transparent flake and disappears before reaching the ground.

The walkers avoid these people, or else accompany them at a distance. It may be that the two are stages of one life. A sheltered person dispersed too long might become a walker, its possible body hardened into plates around an empty cage. A walker brought into sufficient silence might remember hands, tools, and an unfinished task. There are carvings suggesting this, but carvings here are not reliable evidence; stone sometimes records what may happen rather than what has.

Above them all, in the grove surrounding the absent pool, something slower lives.

It is not any single trunk. The intertwined roots stop at the hollow’s rim, but their pressures continue beneath it, crossing through the absence without entering. Silver vibrations pass from crown to crown. Seeds appear constantly in the overlapping shadows and are reabsorbed before they can fall.

The whole grove may be one organism attempting to comprehend water from the tiny images sealed in salt.

It practices shapes. Leaves become concave, then release the idea. Fibers braid themselves into channels. Dust membranes form clear, flexible skins and collapse. During rare shifts in the mountain’s chord, every trunk bends inward and the hollow darkens, as though receiving weight.

The gathered people climb to the plateau when they can. They bring bowls and arrange them around the absence. Walkers stand farther out among the glass ridges, their masks turned inward. No ceremony begins, because it may have begun thousands of years ago; none ends, because the expected event has not occurred.

They wait for the first drop.

Until then, life in the basin consists largely of this waiting: gardens drinking the burden of the mountains, walkers feeding on decisions, scattered people assembling themselves long enough to continue impossible work, and the high grove teaching dry matter to imagine softness.

None of them knows whether rain will create a new world or destroy the only one they can inhabit. Still, they preserve its image.

In a country where no heart beats, that persistence is the nearest thing to hope.
