At first the basin seems inhabited chiefly by glints.

They turn in the water films, vanish against the wet stone, and reappear only when their bodies bend the light. Some are simple, transparent swimmers with a dark knot of organs suspended inside them. Others are thin flakes with ciliated undersides, grazing over the microbial skin in coordinated fronts. A herd may contain ten thousand animals and still resemble nothing more than a patch of brightness moving slowly across the floor.

They are the basin’s primary multitude: glasslings, stone-lickers, thread hunters, spring mites, and soft-bodied things that hatch, feed, mate, and die between one shower and the evaporation of its last puddle. Their eggs are nearly indestructible. Dry, they look like dust caught in the pores of the rock. Wet, they open within minutes.

The coin-shaped grazers are among the most conspicuous once the eye learns to see them. Each carries a rim of tiny feet and a mouth that continually rasps the stone. They move in dense, orderly herds, following gradients of warmth and mineral flavor. Behind them they leave branching white trails where the dark microbial velvet has been removed. These trails slowly close, but never entirely. Generations follow the faint routes of their predecessors, so that every pool contains a history written in grazing.

Their hunters live inside fractures too narrow to admit the grazers’ bodies. A hunter may be many times longer than it appears: most of it lies folded in the rock, pale and nearly boneless. Only a crown of sensory filaments extends into the water. When a grazer passes, the crown closes. There is no chase and almost no disturbance—only a small absence opening in the herd.

Higher up are the reed-dwellers. They cling in rust-colored ranks beneath the red blades, each with six soft wings pressed around its body. A whole colony can rotate around a stem to remain hidden from the sunward side. They do not fly individually unless forced. At dusk they release together, rising as a sheet that bends without breaking, passing pollen, scent, and electrical charge from one body to the next. The sheet behaves more intelligently than any one animal in it. It remembers obstacles encountered on previous evenings and divides around empty regions of air that no visible boundary explains.

White pleatbacks occupy the root hollows. They look at rest like pieces of wet fungus, eyeless except for a faint silver line beneath the skin. The folds along their backs are lungs, gills, and heat exchangers at once. When the pools shrink, the pleats close and hold water. During rain they spread wide, taking oxygen and dissolved minerals directly from the runoff. Their courtship consists of flushing colored blood through separate chambers of the skin. Two motionless animals may conduct an elaborate exchange of violet, amber, and pearl for hours without touching.

The rain-shells are slower still. Each is about the size of a thumb joint and carries a translucent mineral house secreted from what it scrapes off the basin floor. When rain begins, thousands turn upslope at once. When it ends, they return to the pools along polished routes older than the present groves. Their migrations seem modest until viewed across years: by carrying mineral dust upward and organic matter downward, they keep the soft margins from becoming soil. The basin remains bare partly because they live there.

The warm-blooded gliders sleep in the jointed trees. Folded, they resemble heavy fruit wrapped in gray leaves. Unfolded, they have four grasping limbs and a membrane running from jaw to heel, patterned with capillaries that can brighten or darken independently. Their eyes are enormous but seldom open. They navigate by sensing the pressure wakes of flying swarms and can pass through a grove on a moonless night without touching a branch. A mother carries her young inside a pocket formed by the folded flight membrane, so that in motion the pair becomes one aerodynamic body.

The long-legged waders are less like birds than walking instruments. Their feet spread into dozens of hair-fine toes, allowing them to stand on water films too shallow to cover a claw. They wait with one leg submerged, reading the tremors of larvae and grazers. The strike is made not with the beak but with the foot: the toes close around prey and lift it to a mouth hidden beneath the chest. Their eyes, bright and independently mobile, watch the sky for danger while the legs hunt below.

Above them, the air has its own layered fauna. Spore-feeders drift close to the water. Grove-circlers occupy the warm band around the trees. The highest fliers are broad, translucent creatures with almost no muscular flight. They inflate chambers along their bodies with warmed vapor and ride the basin’s updrafts for days. Colonies overlap at different altitudes, each tuned to a particular pressure. When sunlight passes behind them, internal veins flash in polarized sequences—the basin’s nearest equivalent to birdsong.

Almost none of these creatures makes deliberate sound. Their predators listen through the stone.

The largest predators are seldom seen whole. One reveals itself as a low ridge moving beneath a pool; another as a circle from which small swimmers abruptly flee. There are pale ribbon-bodied ambushers that can cross twenty meters of wet rock by throwing their own internal fluids forward. There are soft-jawed climbers that imitate the heat signature of flowers, waiting for pollinators to settle on them. In the groves live patient animals covered in moss, distinguishable from roots only when their feeding mouths turn upward.

Yet killing is not the strongest organizing force in the basin. The nine pale structures are.

No animal understands them, but nearly every species has incorporated them into its behavior. Grazers use their unseen boundaries as guides. Swarms turn along their invisible planes. Pleatbacks lay eggs where altered gravity keeps a water film from draining. Gliders sleep in the false shadow of the largest arch, even when that shadow lies across open water. Some larvae can develop only in pools whose depth has been increased by a structure without the addition of water.

Small black insects attend the structures most closely. They collect the dark beads that gather at the bases after stains and foreign matter migrate downward through the pale material. The insects carry the beads in their jaws, never eating them, and deposit them at other structures according to routes that change with weather, season, and the appearance of unfamiliar species. If one insect is displaced, it returns not to its nest but to the interrupted route. If a structure is scratched, hundreds arrive before the cut has closed.

They may be cleaners. They may be messengers. Their bodies contain threads of the same heat-drawing substance as the structures, arranged in minute branching lattices around the nerves. No one insect possesses enough of it to do anything perceptible. A colony, distributed among all nine sites, may be another matter.

Along the margins move the great many-limbed browsers. Their local name, if the basin had names, would mean something like walking ground. They are broad enough to carry pools in the furrows of their backs. Entire communities live upon them: algae near the spine, flowering creepers along the flanks, scavengers under the belly, pale fishlike symbionts in the moist folds around the legs. Young browsers begin almost smooth. Old ones become wandering islands whose passengers reproduce, compete, and migrate across them.

Their steps are extraordinarily careful. Each foot divides before touching down, spreading the animal’s weight over the stone. They eat not individual plants but portions of habitat, taking reeds, vines, nests, parasites, and pooled water together into wide filtering mouths. Much survives the passage through them. Seeds germinate in the warm chambers of the gut; small animals emerge days later elsewhere in the basin. A browser does not merely consume a community. It edits and relocates it.

They will not cross the invisible threshold around the center. A vine may bridge from a browser’s back to a central grove, but the animal itself stops. Its forward limbs stiffen. The organisms living on it become quiet. After a time it turns aside, leaving a curved track in the reeds.

The shelter at the southeastern edge produces the opposite response. There, every creature behaves as though hunger has been temporarily suspended. Predator and prey enter together. Parasites uncouple from their hosts. Territorial animals tolerate contact. The effect does not pacify them; it removes the next act from consideration. A hunter inside the shadow still recognizes prey, but cannot seem to arrive at pursuit. When the moving shadow exposes it again, the unfinished impulse returns.

At dawn the shelter’s long shade is crowded with thousands of animals resting in strange proximity: pleatbacks under the feet of waders, reed insects covering the backs of ribbon predators, gliders hanging low enough for shell-creatures to climb their folded membranes. By noon the refuge contracts. Bodies press inward until only a thin line remains, then disperse in perfect silence as appetite resumes.

There is no obvious keeper in the shelter. The dry floor bears no footprints larger than those of local animals. The tools in its recess are untouched except by curious larvae and the occasional glider, yet none accumulates dirt. The black cup’s water contains no microbes. The transparent rod grows heavy when pointed toward life, but in the basin there is no direction in which it remains light.

It is tempting to say that the builders are gone.

The basin does not quite support that conclusion.

Patterns pass through it that are too slow or too distributed to look like behavior. The black insects alter their routes. The flying colonies mirror those alterations overhead. Grazing lanes shift, redirecting minerals toward a spring. A great browser turns aside and carries a seed community to the opposite rim. One structure changes the angle of morning light by a fraction. Weeks later, a plant previously confined to one grove flowers beside another.

No command can be located. No single organism appears to know what is happening. Nevertheless, the basin responds to drought, overcrowding, new predators, and failing springs with the coherence of a body healing around a wound.

Perhaps something does live at the center—not inside any structure, but among them. Its senses are swarms, reflections, pressure changes, grazing paths, and the hesitation of droplets. Its metabolism is the exchange of water and living matter across the stone. Its memory resides partly in the impossible views held by the arches, partly in shell routes and insect nerves, partly in adjustments too small to notice until centuries have passed.

If so, it has no face and no fixed boundary. The nine structures may be its organs, or merely tools embedded in it long ago. The basin’s animals may be its inhabitants, its partners, or the moving substance of its thought.

At night this possibility becomes difficult to dismiss. Light travels through the pools in branching signals. Tree trunks kindle. The great browsers shine at the margins. High fliers answer with dim pulses from the warm air, and the nine pale structures cast their shadows upward.

For a few minutes, the entire basin appears connected: not peaceful, not unified, but listening to itself through millions of separate bodies.
