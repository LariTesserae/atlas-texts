Those who live in the basin do not regard the city as being full of animals.

To them, the distinction would be as strange as dividing a body into citizen and organs.

The hand-sized shoals in the lower streets are traffic, sanitation, weather, and food. The hinged colonies on the towers are insulation, ancestry, and public memory. The reservoir giants are districts in the same sense that a mountain elsewhere might be a district: one may reside upon them, negotiate with their movements, inherit obligations imposed by their anatomy. Even the silver parasites breeding in the message seams are tolerated as a kind of static—sometimes harvested, sometimes interpreted, sometimes blamed for communications that arrive carrying meanings no one remembers sending.

The conventional inhabitants are only the most concentrated knots in this civic life.

Unclothed, they would appear narrow and low to the ground, though their posture changes with local gravity. They possess several load-bearing limbs arranged around a flexible central trunk, but they do not consistently designate any pair as legs. In ordinary streets, where down behaves predictably, three limbs support the body while the others test surfaces, manipulate objects, or hold resonators against the stone. In rotational lifts and folded chambers they reorganize themselves without apparent disorientation, becoming briefly radial, like animals living on the inside of a pipe.

Their bodies are dark, matte, and deeply pleated. The pleats trap layers of water that remain liquid under their own internal fields. Beneath them lie ranks of pressure-sensitive nodules. These do not merely detect touch. They read the minute deformation of walls, floors, water, and air. An inhabitant standing in a square can feel a group crossing a bridge several levels above, a sluice opening behind a sealed facade, the rearrangement of roosting animals around a doorway. A reservoir beast turning in sleep reaches them first as a faint asymmetry in the load beneath the district.

They have no eyes in the familiar sense. Along the upper trunk are pits lined with thermal tissue and reflective hairs, useful at close range. They can tell where another body has recently leaned against a wall, where water has emerged from a folded depth, where a building has begun to crystallize and still retains the warmth of its liquid infancy. Visible light is an incidental irritation. Brightness washes out subtler gradients, just as noise may drown speech.

Their mouths are concealed beneath feeding plates and are seldom opened in public. Eating is slow. Nutrient films, mineral slurries, and small grazers are pressed into grooves in the plates, where symbiotic animals dismantle them over many hours. These symbionts are inherited, traded, cultivated, and occasionally composed. A household’s taste is partly the taste of the species it carries.

There are disputes over whether those species are possessions, relatives, or citizens.

The question has never been resolved because their language does not permit the categories to remain clean.

Their coats complicate matters further. Each coat consists of thousands of living plates, smaller relatives of the animals that tessellate across towers. The plates lock together around the wearer, adjusting thickness according to cold, pressure, and social circumstance. They feed from the wearer’s warmth and secretions; in return they distribute impacts, retain liquid, and translate vibrations across the body. A coat raised in one district acquires the pulse-patterns of its towers. Long after relocation, it may tighten when its former reservoir moves or open its seams in response to a door hundreds of streets away.

A young inhabitant is not issued a coat. It is colonized by one.

For several years after emergence, juveniles remain nearly bare within warm chambers in the half-liquid walls of new buildings. Small plates gather on them gradually, selecting creases and attachment points. Caretakers introduce plates from several lineages, but the final assembly cannot be controlled. Some coats form dense and quiet. Some remain restless, continuously shedding and reordering. Some acquire tiny passenger species whose functions are unknown. A coat that refuses a juvenile is not compelled. The juvenile is moved to another chamber, sometimes another district, until an arrangement takes.

Thus identity is never singular. An adult is a central body, a coat colony, the microorganisms in its feeding plates, the tools embedded among its outer layers, and the architectural responses accumulated through years of passage. Doors know the combined vibration. Bridges bend for the aggregate weight. Other inhabitants recognize the minute rasp produced when that particular multitude moves.

Names are chords of these recognitions.

A person’s intimate name may be tapped directly into their coat. Their public name is longer and includes the response of a chosen wall or conduit. A district name may require several towers to pronounce. The oldest names take minutes to swell through the foundations and cannot be spoken in damaged parts of the city. When a structure capable of producing part of a name collapses, the named individual is altered socially even if their body is untouched. They may spend decades teaching a replacement wall to answer correctly, or accept the loss and live thereafter with an incomplete resonance.

They do not converse continuously. Much of what passes between them is pressure: pauses, foot placement, the deliberate loading of one side of a room. For distance they use resonators carried on staffs or mounted in their coats. These devices are partly manufactured and partly grown. Pressed to a street, they make nearby structures repeat a sequence of knocks, each repetition changing according to the material it crosses. A message arriving from far away contains the state of the city along its route. It reports not only what was said but cracked foundations, crowded canals, migrating shoals, ice accumulation, and the wakefulness of buried machines.

Accuracy is therefore not the highest virtue in communication. A perfectly unchanged message would be considered sterile, perhaps fraudulent. Important statements are expected to gather the world as they travel.

Their settlements within the city are not enclosed homes so much as recurring permissions. A household might possess the right to warm a certain wall, harvest the clear animals passing through a particular stairwell, sleep against a conduit during its quiet cycle, and occupy a room only when gravity points toward its narrow end. Its members may rarely gather in one place. Kinship follows maintenance routes and coat lineages more often than physical descent.

They reproduce infrequently and with considerable assistance from architecture. No two bodies alone can produce a viable juvenile. Tissue and symbionts are contributed by several adults, but the developing form must be placed in a receptive embryonic building, whose warm walls shape its pressure organs and seed its internal fauna. The building is consequently counted among the child’s progenitors. So are certain tunnelers that align conductive strands around the chamber, and the pale stress-swarms whose singing prevents the walls from hardening too soon.

One may have three mobile parents, a tower, a swarm, and a section of municipal field.

This is not metaphor.

Death is equally distributed. When the central body fails, the coat does not necessarily die. It opens, withdraws, and divides among walls, juveniles, or other coats. Feeding symbionts are placed into communal films. Conductive bones may be inserted into resonators. The body itself is lowered into mineral water near a growing structure, where scavengers separate it slowly. What cannot be reused becomes part of the crystallizing wall.

For years afterward, the resulting rooms may reproduce familiar thermal patterns. A doorway may flex at the approach of an old companion. A floor may answer a name with the wrong cadence. Such events are not treated as proof that the dead remain, but neither are they dismissed. The inhabitants have little interest in deciding where persistence ends and imitation begins.

Their occupations are forms of attendance.

Some accompany migrations of transparent street shoals, opening routes a few finger-widths at a time and preventing the front ranks from sealing themselves into culverts. Some listen to towers, diagnosing changes in their long pulses. Some enter folded reservoirs to tend the great filter-beasts, returning years older than those who waited outside because duration does not always unfold evenly in the compact depths. Some cultivate stress-swarms around young bridges, teaching them the tones that induce stone to soften rather than fracture.

There are keepers of obsolete gravity. They maintain rooms where falling still proceeds in directions no longer useful. There are harvesters who draw stored heat out of previous winters and must distinguish safe warmth from heat carrying old structural failures. There are seam-readers who press their bodies against entangled mountain faults and receive messages from distant districts—or from earlier states of the same district. Their coats often become infested with silver parasites. Experienced seam-readers allow a controlled number to remain, claiming that the parasites consume false possibilities.

Others say the parasites consume the true ones first.

Conflict is slow but not gentle. Since the city answers vibration, access can be denied by changing one’s gait in public records. A household may teach a bridge not to yield under a rival coat. A district may withhold the pressure chord that keeps a neighboring reservoir entrance open. During severe disputes, opposing groups occupy the same structure and spend years persuading its material to recognize incompatible patterns. Walls thicken between them. Corridors bend. Animals alter their roosting formations. By the time the conflict is resolved, the architecture may have produced a border more durable than either grievance.

Violence against a body is rare. Violence against its relationships is not.

Their oldest political divisions concern the Unfinished Hollow.

One tradition holds that the Hollow is the city’s true inhabitant and all other life merely its temporary sensory layer. The tuning forks are said to be teaching it how to wake without destroying the basin. Adherents of this view spend long periods motionless at the rim, allowing the chord to pass through their bodies. Their coats become unusually thin. Some eventually descend by routes that do not appear on civic maps.

Another tradition regards the Hollow as a wound kept open by machinery whose purpose has outlived its makers. They tend the channels pouring into it, hoping that enough water, directed correctly over enough centuries, will permit the absence to heal. They measure its rise with rods made from conductive skeletons. Their records disagree by fractions of a finger-width, and entire schools have formed around those fractions.

A third group believes it is neither being opened nor filled. The Hollow, they say, is arriving. Its apparent depth is the first visible part of an environment folding into the basin from somewhere whose laws differ from those of the mountains. The inconsistent ice, climbing water, rotated gravity, and reservoirs larger within than without are not inventions but leakage. The city’s technology does not create these effects; it domesticates them.

These inhabitants listen most closely during the universal pauses.

No one knows why all fauna stop when the Hollow answers. The conventional explanation is that the prolonged vibration saturates every pressure sense at once, arresting movement until it passes. But this does not explain why blind animals inside sealed walls pause before the vibration arrives, or why stored messages cease propagating through the mountain seams, or why reservoir creatures sometimes begin to turn several years in advance.

The inhabitants pause too.

Groups on elevated causeways lower their staffs. Tunnelers still their mouths in the mud. The living coats flatten, each plate locking tight to the next. Those in gravity rooms drift without correcting themselves. Even disputed buildings suspend their slow rearrangements.

In that interval, the city is not silent. Its conduits boom and its vents continue to breathe. Water strikes ledges. Ice breaks. Yet the countless small acts by which the basin recognizes itself are withdrawn.

Some inhabitants experience the pause as terror. Others call it the only moment of privacy they possess.

A few claim that, during the longest pauses, they feel another pattern moving beneath the continuing noise: not a signal from the Hollow, but a vast pressure applied from the other side of it. It seems to test the basin’s shape. It lingers against the reservoir districts, the folded chambers, the embryonic towers, the mountains still raw from their making. Then it passes lightly across every coat and shell, as though counting.

Afterward, motion resumes.

The wall colonies unfold. The shoals advance their imperceptible distance. Staffs strike pavement and distant structures answer. Water climbs surfaces it should descend. The city bends around the patient bodies beneath its reservoirs.

And its inhabitants return to their work—not maintaining a city, exactly, nor serving a machine, nor surviving in an ecosystem, but continuing the immense, unresolved process by which all of those things attempt to become one another.
