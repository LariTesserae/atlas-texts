What lives there is not a population so much as an arrangement of appetites.

At first the most obvious inhabitants seem to be the animals: the plaque-winged moths, the blind duct birds, the little transparent mammals moving behind bark. They occupy the city in recognizable ways. They nest, hunt, migrate, mate, wait for rain. Yet none of them belongs wholly to a single body, and few remain the same kind of creature throughout their lives.

The moths, for example, are only moths while sleeping. Awake, they are surveyors.

They peel themselves from the trunks at the first warmth in the rails and fly the avenues in slow rectangular circuits. Their wings bear stains that resemble corrosion but are actually shifting diagrams of usable space. Wherever four moths complete the same circuit, the rhizomes beneath them raise a platform. Wherever a circuit is interrupted, the indicated building gradually empties itself of rooms. Thus the moths maintain districts they cannot enter. On the rare occasions when one is caught and unfolded, its wings show not the present city but a neighborhood that might exist after the next departure.

The blind birds maintain its depths. They are long-bodied, almost featherless things with fan-shaped faces and feet adapted for gripping pipes. They listen continually to sap, water, current, and time moving through the walls. A bird will remain motionless for days with its beak pressed to a vascular shaft. Then it will fly to another tower and utter a note below hearing. Valves open in answer. Cisterns drain. Fruit reverses its ripening. Somewhere, perhaps, a lift descends to a floor that did not exist before the call.

Their nests contain no eggs. They weave small bowl-shaped rooms from stripped insulation and root hair, then sit over the empty space until a faint pulse develops there. The chick begins as an echo. It acquires bones only after learning the city’s internal noises.

The translucent mammals are less stable. They resemble mice, children’s hands, pale stoats, or clusters of organs according to the thickness of the wall through which they are seen. They have no skin of their own. Bark serves as their outer surface; pipes become temporary intestines; wires pass through them like nerves. A colony may inhabit an entire tower while appearing from outside as dozens of separate creatures.

They feed on distinctions. A wall containing them grows uncertain about which side is indoors. A locked room forgets whether it is locked. Two adjacent apartments may exchange their temperatures, smells, and histories after a colony passes between them. The mammals leave tiny tooth marks around labels, thresholds, and measurements. Given enough time, they can eat the difference between a cupboard and a corridor.

The copper-gathering beetles follow behind them, repairing certainty. Their heaps are not nests but punctuation. A coil means that a pipe ends here. A straight length laid across a gutter means that the water must pass beneath it. Four equal fragments arranged in a square can hold a room in place for years. The factories tolerate the beetles because, without these small metallic declarations, machinery would spread into metaphor and become unusable.

When two beetles meet, each taps the other with its antennae and trades a piece of location. This is why beetles removed from the basin are difficult to keep. By the fourth night, the container holding them is always found somewhere else.

The creatures of the cisterns belong less to the city than to its moments of wetness.

They do not wait under the pavement in any material sense. They wait as readiness: a silver kink in a mineral deposit, an oval discoloration, a row of scratches left by no visible claws. Water gives volume to them. Within minutes the pools fill with larvae already bearing the scars of adult life, amphibians remembering ponds that have not formed, and flat fish that feed upon reflections.

Those fish do not consume images indiscriminately. They prefer reflected exits. A cistern may begin by mirroring a ceiling, three lamps, and an open archway. After the fish have grazed, the archway remains overhead but is absent from the water. Soon afterward it becomes difficult to use. If every reflection of an exit is eaten, the building closes like a flower.

The long-necked amphibians live partly in electricity. Their eggs cling to warm junctions as glassy strings, each egg containing a tiny intermittent thunderstorm. The hatchlings emerge during current fluctuations and have luminous filaments instead of gills. They graze green lightning from beneath the bark. Older ones collect around damaged cables, not to feed but to brood over exposed voltage until insulation grows back.

When the basin inhales and the pools vanish, all these creatures flatten. Their markings remain crowded together on the rhizome pavement: fish seen from above, larvae reduced to commas, amphibians drawn in black mineral lines. In that state they are still alive. They dream the pressure of water and can feel footsteps crossing them. Some have waited so long between rains that they have become decorative motifs in station floors. When wetted, they rise carrying fragments of every journey made over them.

The vine-cats are among the few inhabitants that appear deliberate.

They grow themselves in sheltered places from local runners, assembling paws first and a face last. Their flowers remain closed while hunting, giving their spines the knobbed silhouette of vertebrae. They have no eyes; instead, the blossoms along their backs perceive possible motion. A cat crouching beside an empty passage may suddenly spring at the route a rodent was about to take.

Their prey, the glass-bodied rodents, eat small failures in the city: cracks that roots have missed, sparks that did not reach a lamp, doors that almost opened. These flaws shine red in their stomachs. A well-fed rodent is visible at a great distance as a bright sequence of errors moving through the dark.

When a vine-cat catches one, the act is not exactly killing. Roots enter glass. Glass softens into sap. For several days predator and prey become a low, trembling shrub whose flowers show scenes of pursuit from both points of view. Then they separate. The cat carries away some transparency; the rodent departs with a few green threads in its skeleton. After many such unions, an old vine-cat becomes nearly invisible, while an old rodent begins to flower when frightened.

Their shadows do not participate in this exchange.

The shadows are thin, colonial animals that mimic attachment because attachment protects them. They cling beneath cats, towers, handrails, and passersby, feeding on unused gestures. A shadow may preserve the crouch of a predator after the predator stands, or continue the stride of a person who has stopped. Needle-winged insects prune these surplus movements before they accumulate into events.

Where the insects fail, a shadow thickens. It acquires habits, then weight. Some of the figures glimpsed at factory stations are believed to be mature shadows that have inherited the repetitive motions of vanished workers. They pull levers that no longer need pulling. They turn to greet the change of shift. Light passes through their heads and illuminates the expression on the wall behind them.

Portable lamps are dangerous because they breed such creatures without giving them ancestry. A new shadow is hungry, directionless, and attached only by geometry. If cast repeatedly, it may become infrastructure before it learns restraint.

There are larger inhabitants too, although “larger” is not quite the right word.

One lives in the pressure that moves along the avenues.

It has no visible tissue. Its body consists of simultaneous reactions: leaves turning high above, cables swaying, doors opening in sequence, puddles dimpling though no drop has fallen. It travels slowly enough that a person can walk beside it, but not for long; distance changes around its passage, and streets arrange themselves to give it room.

The animals recognize it as a grazer. They become still while it feeds. What it consumes is congestion: too many possible rooms behind one door, too many futures in a reservoir, too many versions of the same intersection. After it passes, some uncertainty is gone. A doorway settles on a destination. Water remembers which way is down. The city becomes briefly easier to inhabit.

No one has seen it turn around. Yet it passes each district from a different direction.

Another inhabitant occupies the factories. It is distributed among pistons, root turbines, belts, labeling systems, and drawers of provisional bones. It may once have been machinery operated by a workforce. It may once have been a workforce gradually relieved of bodies. Whatever its origin, it now expresses itself through production.

It does not make goods. It rehearses organisms.

The impossible skeleton assembled in the deeper works is one of its longer thoughts. Each version differs slightly: an extra hinge in the spine, a rib shaped to contain a season, a skull with room for several incompatible senses. When the skeleton is complete, the factory waits. Nothing arrives to inhabit it. After a measured interval, the bones are dismantled and sorted, and another attempt begins.

The black sheets pressed from leaves are records, but not of events. They preserve pressures, absences, repeated routes, and the shapes around which roots had to grow. Held at an angle, one may reveal the outline of a worker. Held another way, it shows the machine remembering that worker. Most are filed in sealed rooms. Some are fed into the soil of new districts, where they germinate as obsolete regulations.

The sealed pollen packets moving along the conveyors contain instructions addressed to structures that do not yet count as living. One may be marked for a staircase. Another for a municipal boundary. Another for the sensation of waiting beneath a shelter while rain begins somewhere else.

There are inhabitants made entirely of repetition.

They are difficult to distinguish from customs at first. A route walked once is only a route. Walked twice, it becomes expectant. On the third morning, something accompanies the traveler, placing its steps exactly inside theirs. On the fourth, the companion descends into the ground and becomes a path.

Such beings are called, insofar as the city has names, the Habitants.

A Habitant has no form until enacted. Knocking calls one into a wall. Setting down an object feeds another. Returning to a window each evening may cause a presence to gather on the opposite side, returning the gaze. Four repetitions give it roots. Continued repetition gives it utilities, drainage, lamps, and eventually an address.

Many apparently empty buildings are Habitants whose original gestures have been forgotten. A tower may be the matured form of someone repeatedly looking upward. A station may have grown from four interrupted departures. The broad avenues could be migration habits left by bodies too large or too temporary to fossilize.

Habitants fear neglect. When starved of recurrence, they do not die at once. They become inaccurate. A stair remembers climbing but not arrival. A doorway remembers opening but not the room. Finally they flatten into signs, stains, or stories carried by animals. The city’s fauna keeps many of them alive accidentally. A rodent’s nightly crossing may support a bridge. A bird nesting four seasons in the same shaft may preserve an entire floor.

There may also be people there.

If so, they are not visible as people.

Evidence of them persists in tool grips polished by hands, ticket machines that warm at the approach of a body, apartments whose rooms remain organized around shoulder height, and lamps illuminating workers who are absent. But absence is capable of becoming a resident in this place. Given repetition, it too acquires structure.

The missing workers seem to occupy the city collectively as vacancies. A chair pulled from a desk. A gap maintained beside a conveyor. A human-shaped region into which rain will not fall. These vacancies perform shifts. They gather in shelters when the rails warm. They stand at machines and prevent shadows from taking the wrong stations.

Sometimes a translucent mammal crosses one of them and briefly acquires a human outline. Sometimes moths land along empty shoulders. Sometimes a vine-cat looks up at the height of a face and opens every flower on its back.

Whether these are remnants, occupants elsewhere, or persons not yet arrived cannot be determined. Here, all three conditions can share a doorway.

Beneath them all—or within them all—lives the basin.

It is young, though some of its processes are ancient. It has not yet learned to separate geology from digestion. It swallows water to discover hollows in itself. It moves channels as an animal shifts in sleep. It grows mineral ribs where it expects one day to need a ravine. The raw clay below the streets is not merely substrate. It is undifferentiated anatomy.

The vegetation is its nervous system, or perhaps its imagination. Trunks hold long thoughts upright. Roots test weight, boundary, drainage, and consequence. Leaves conceal the sky because the basin is still forming an interior and cannot tolerate comparison with an outside.

Its metabolism includes scale. When a room expands beyond the mound, the basin is not making space from nothing; it is digesting the distinction between enclosure and distance. When a district turns, it is adjusting posture. When old structures appear unfinished and new ones scarred, it is distributing age where age is useful.

The city is therefore neither built upon the organism nor built by it. The city is the temporary anatomy the organism has adopted while trying to understand habitation.

The empty central plaza is the one place it does not enter. That space may be its mouth, its pupil, or a wound kept deliberately open. The inverted plaza overhead suggests another basin facing this one, each using the other as a sky. Nothing crosses the floor because to cross it would be to choose which city is the reflection.

When the rails warm, every living thing prepares for a departure because departure is the basin’s oldest instinct.

The moths wake to map what might be lost. The cats hide their rooted bodies. The birds stop the sap in certain towers. The factories suspend their rehearsal. The Habitants hold tightly to their fourth repetitions. The absent workers crowd the platforms more densely than matter could.

Then the pressure comes.

For an instant all doors agree. They open onto the cool field of raw clay, and the city sees an outside simple enough to be real: gray morning, rain scent, open distance, one green shoot.

That shoot is its smallest inhabitant and perhaps its only traveler.

It enters without moving. Later it is found inside, already becoming architecture. Around it gather beetles with their pieces of certainty, mammals carrying ambiguous rooms, birds listening for future pipes, shadows awaiting gestures, and roots eager to learn the weight of streets.

The new district begins empty.

Emptiness is what lives there first.
