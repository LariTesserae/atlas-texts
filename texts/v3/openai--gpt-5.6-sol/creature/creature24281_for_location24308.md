The first inhabitants are films.

They cover the wet faces of leaves, the undersides of stones, the black arches where vines fail, and the warm interiors of pools. Some are green, some rust-red, some visible only when they disturb the path of condensation. They live in layers thin enough to be mistaken for color. In the streambeds, colonies arrange themselves according to minerals released by the hexagonal plates. Around the pale cliff filaments they grow in branching figures, each generation following the faint electrical preferences left by storms.

These minute lives feed nearly everything above them. They sweeten the water trapped in bromeliads, soften fallen bark, and build slick skins over the marsh crust. A few have adapted to the gravity clearings. Their spores do not settle but gather in hovering brown-gold clouds, breeding through direct contact in the air. Over years, the clouds become dense enough to dim the light beneath them. Small animals browse their edges as though grazing from an invisible wall.

The forests belong next to the plants that hold water.

The great pillar trees are not especially numerous, but each is an inhabited country. Their pale ribs flare around hollows warm enough for eggs and larvae. Their crowns contain hundreds of suspended ponds, some no wider than a curled leaf, others deep enough to hold fishlike vertebrates no longer than a finger. Generations may pass inside one tree without ever reaching the ground. Tadpoles hatch in one branch basin, climb wet bark after metamorphosis, and lay their own eggs in another basin higher in the crown.

Many canopy plants are half-rooted or not rooted at all. They feed from fog, insects, shed skin, feathers, and the mineral dust carried upward during dry clearings. Their dangling roots stop above the soil because the richest layer of the forest is not the ground but the humid interval just over it. There, decomposers, spores, and tiny winged things remain suspended. The roots comb this living haze.

Some plants react to the mountain’s anomalies more clearly than animals do. A vine reaching a weak-gravity seam grows in loops and loose knots, making no distinction between horizontal and vertical. Cup-leaved herbs tip themselves toward invisible downward directions. Trees near the central marsh send branches into still clearings and grow small forests on their own undersides. Seedlings may sprout from hovering soil, extend roots upward into it, and hang beneath their island like green chandeliers.

The mobile inhabitants are built for abundance without ease.

Most have large chests, slow pulses, and blood so dark it appears violet through thin skin. Their breathing surfaces are distributed. A marsh grazer may inhale through nostrils, throat folds, and rib membranes simultaneously; a climbing animal may pause with its limbs spread against a damp trunk, taking oxygen through pads along its belly. Rest is not idleness here but a necessary phase of movement. Creatures remain still for hours, then spend their gathered strength all at once.

The bell-voiced animals live in the middle canopy. They are broad-bodied browsers with gripping feet and blunt, mobile mouths. A loose mantle of short fur traps algae, pollen, and the seeds of epiphytes, so that older individuals become green along the spine. Their throat chambers are huge and nearly empty. A call begins as a small contraction behind the jaw and leaves the body as a rounded metallic note.

They do not call constantly. One may feed in silence throughout the morning before sounding a single bell into the valley. Another answers from a ridge hidden by cloud. The exchange can continue for hours, too slow to resemble conversation to an impatient listener. Yet each note carries identity, location, health, and the state of nearby fruiting trees. Herds separated by ravines maintain bonds across distances they seldom cross.

Their young are born undersized and cling inside folds along the adult’s flank, where the oxygen is warm and already filtered by mossy fur. For the first season they scarcely climb. They listen. The paths they later follow through the canopy are partly learned from calls made by animals they never see.

Lower in the forest are small grazers that move on six limbs. Some walk with all six; others use the front pair as hands, stripping soft bark and lifting leaves to inspect the colonies beneath. Their additional limbs are less a sign of speed than of security. In thin air, a fall is costly. They keep four points of contact while reaching, sleeping, mating, and giving birth.

The slate-colored herds of the crownlands are their largest relatives. Their six legs move in paired sequences, giving them a slow, rolling gait. Moss grows thickest in channels along their backs, irrigated by sweat and condensation. This is not camouflage alone. The moss shelters mites that remove parasites, stores water during exposed crossings, and contains bacteria that produce compounds the animals lick when oxygen-starved.

Each herd is led not by the strongest adult but by one that remembers the warm ground. Subterranean heat shifts subtly from century to century, and the safest routes across the saturated ridges migrate with it. Old herd leaders know where the turf will bear weight, where a tarn has a hidden outlet, and where gravity weakens enough to make a leap unexpectedly long.

When threatened, they fold their legs beneath them. Running would exhaust them and attract anything watching from the cliffs. Their flute alarm is produced through long chambers in the chest and can continue after the animal has emptied its lungs; elastic sacs sustain the note while it draws another breath through its side membranes. The sound passes from herd to herd until animals beyond sight have lowered themselves as well. From a distance, whole meadows appear to lie down.

The small white birds among them are not merely attendants. They cultivate the moss.

With narrow bills, they clip away dead strands and carry viable pieces from one browser to another. They eat insects, but they also open tiny channels that allow rain to reach the animals’ skin. Their nests are made in the thickest living coats of old herd members. A bird may spend its entire life associated with one moving body, defending a patch of back as fiercely as another species would defend an island.

The metallic-winged birds of the basin are more collective. Individually they are drab and slight. Their apparent brilliance comes from stiff scales along the flight feathers, which flash like beaten metal when aligned. Their clicking is partly the physical strike of these scales and partly a navigational signal. In cloud, thousands turn according to the clicks reflected from slopes and trunks. The flock acts as a single sensing surface, deforming around obstacles before any one bird could have seen them.

They roost on the narrow towers.

The round windows concentrate drafts that pass through the pearl-like interiors, and the birds cling around them in layered rings. Slow points of light move inside the material while the flock sleeps. At certain moments every bird wakes, turns its head toward the same interior point, and then settles again. Whether the towers communicate with them or merely stimulate some ancient reflex is impossible to tell. Droppings and nest fibers have accumulated around their bases, but never inside. No bird crosses the window plane.

The marsh has inhabitants more difficult to count because many do not present a stable outline.

Mirror fish hide by matching the brightness and movement of the surface above them. Their flanks are covered in flexible reflecting cells that respond independently, reproducing ripples, passing clouds, even the dark crossing of a bird. From the side, the fish seem absent; from above, their shadows betray them. Predators learn to hunt those shadows, striking at patches of darkness rather than bodies.

Some fish travel through flooded grass by temporarily narrowing themselves. Their ribs fold backward, and their organs slide into a long central sheath. They pass between stems scarcely wider than their eyes, then expand in open water. Others bury themselves in the pale mineral ridges and breathe through mouths left exposed like small circular wounds.

The pool amphibians are thick, soft creatures with little need to leave the water. Their low chorus is felt before it is heard. They anchor themselves to peat with broad toes and inflate resonant sacs against the bottom, turning the whole pool into an instrument. Each pool holds a slightly different note according to its depth and mineral crust. The animals appear to choose breeding sites partly for pitch. Across the marsh, their calls assemble into chords shaped by the water itself.

In the still circular clearings live almost no surface animals. Those that enter are caught not by adhesion but by hesitation: a foot presses down and does not complete its descent; a wingbeat pushes against air that yields too slowly. Most species avoid the boundaries instinctively. Wading animals step around them. Insects turn aside. Migrating fish follow channels beneath them but do not rise.

A few specialists depend on these places. Thin-bodied harvesters climb onto the suspended leaves and feed upside down from their lower surfaces. They have long hooked limbs and almost no body mass. When alarmed, they release their grip and remain hanging in the weak descent, falling a handspan over several minutes. Their eggs are laid in the floating soil islands, where the young mature without ever touching water or solid ground.

The marsh serpents are longer than they first appear. Much of the body lies folded beneath rafts of vegetation, held motionless by lateral hooks. Only the paired breathing tubes rise, resembling broken reed stems. Their jaws are modest; they take small prey and wait a long time between meals. Their chief adaptation is patience. A serpent may occupy one raft for decades as peat accumulates around its coils. Moss grows over its back, insects breed above it, and birds may nest on vegetation it supports.

The long-bodied forest cats possess the same economy in warmer blood. They are narrow through the waist and immense through the chest, with ribs that open farther than those of the grazers. Their fur carries broken patterns like damp bark, lichen, and filtered sunlight. They climb into tree buttresses after feeding and enter a torpor so deep that condensation gathers on their whiskers. The forest resumes around them. Beetles walk across their paws. Vines shift against their flanks.

When awake, they do not chase. They descend along known contour paths and place themselves where prey will eventually pass. Their territories are remembered as sequences of waiting places rather than boundaries. A single cat may use the same twelve roots, ledges, and hollow trunks throughout its life.

Even they are cautious beneath the evening cliffs.

The gliding animals there are the largest living things regularly seen in motion. At rest they hang against blue-black rock, their folded membranes nearly indistinguishable from wet mineral sheen. They may remain on one cliff for weeks, feeding on mats of lichen and on small animals exposed by rain. At dusk, warm air begins to rise from the basin, and they unfold.

Their bones are broad lattices rather than hollow tubes, light but able to store oxygen-rich blood. Their membranes contain transparent muscle fibers and branching vessels so fine that the body’s warmth is distributed without clouding them. Seen from below, a glider is not a silhouette. It is a distortion: stars, clouds, and fading sky displaced by the curve of living tissue.

They launch rarely because landing is dangerous. Once airborne, they may remain aloft through the night, crossing several ranges without a wingbeat. They feed in flight on swarms of metallic birds, airborne spores, and the floating reproductive forms of plants. Near dawn they return to cliffs where the pale filaments enter the stone. Lightning-struck rock remains warm there for days.

Not every inhabitant is wholly organic.

The black arches respond to seasons. The stream plate alters the diet of algae and shell-builders. The cliff filaments receive lightning and pass something into the mountain. Around them, organisms have evolved dependencies as intimate as those between flower and pollinator. Remove the plate and some marsh animals would lose the mineral needed to harden their translucent shells. Silence the filaments and warm seepage in distant caverns might cease. Stop the arches and perhaps the forest would wake one morning to ice.

In that sense, the devices live in the same way reefs live: slowly, collectively, and through the lives that have fastened themselves to them.

They bear growth but are not passive beneath it. Bands on the arches reject vines. The plate edits the water. The door-shaped cool recess maintains a climate for its soft-shelled colony, which in turn keeps its inner surface free of lichen. The towers may use roosting birds as carriers, sensors, or merely as weather passing over them. Their active cycles exceed the lifespans of animals and perhaps of species. A movement of a fraction of a millimeter may be the equivalent of a breath.

If their makers remain, they are not visible as a people.

Nothing tends a field. No smoke rises from a roof. No regular foot wears the old steps more deeply. The basin that fills each morning offers its water to moss and birds. The transparent bench carries a skin of condensation. The changing hand-marks in the cave rearrange themselves for no apparent witness.

Yet the old structures preserve habits suggestive of bodies. The shelf beneath the ceramic roof is at a comfortable resting height. The cool basin is shaped for cupped hands. The paths, where they survive, choose gradients manageable by a creature that tires in thin air. Whatever came here understood exhaustion, weather, and the wish to sit beside water.

Perhaps the builders departed. Perhaps they became too dispersed to resemble a settlement. Perhaps their living part was always elsewhere, while these machines were their organs pressed into the mountain. The distinction matters less with each century. Their works have been inherited by roots, nestlings, algae, grazers, and storms.

No single creature rules the country. Its characteristic inhabitant is a partnership: tree and airborne root; browser and moss; bird and moving herd; amphibian and resonant pool; serpent and raft; machine and watershed. Life survives the altitude not by conquering it but by borrowing from everything nearby.

The beings here are loud because distance is immense, patient because breath is costly, and strange because the mountains have given them time to make use of every exception. They know where water falls sideways. They know which empty air will hold a nest and which will not hold a foot. They know that certain black surfaces are warmer after rain, that some towers must not be entered, and that stars sharpen between the arches at noon.

To them these are not impossibilities. They are shade, migration routes, breeding grounds, warning signs, and weather.

They live not among ruins, but inside an ancient functioning climate whose mechanisms have become indistinguishable from the laws of the world.
