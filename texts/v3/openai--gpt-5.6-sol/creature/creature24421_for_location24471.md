At first, what lives there appears to be only the vegetation.

The moss is the easiest to mistake for ordinary life. It lies thickly on walls, dark where the stone sweats and almost luminous where sunlight reaches it. Pressed with a thumb, it yields. Torn away, it smells of rain, minerals, and something faintly bitter. Its tiny stems can be separated and examined. They are green through and through. They have no visible seeds, no crawling inhabitants, no decay beneath them.

Yet the torn patch is still covered.

This does not happen by growth. No filaments extend across the exposed stone. The missing moss is simply present when attention returns to it, with the same branching tips and the same clear beads lodged among them. The piece in the hand remains as well. For a while both are equally convincing. If brought together, they coincide without resistance, and the hand closes on nothing but dampness.

The ferns are more elaborate. Each courtyard seems to possess its own species: black-stemmed fans, low red-flecked tongues, fronds divided into such fine lace that the roar of the waterfalls should tear them apart. None produces spores. Their curled young leaves never open, though they are not arrested buds. They are complete in the act of opening, just as the cataracts are complete in falling.

The great roof-plants bear flowers.

Some are white cups large enough to hold a person. Some grow directly from stone and have petals like wet leather. Others hang upside down beneath arches, their interiors crowded with pale structures resembling teeth, fingers, or unopened eyes. They offer nectar with the warmth and salt of blood, but nothing comes to drink it. Their pollen remains where it formed. Their fruit contains seeds, and the seeds contain folded shoots, but a planted seed neither germinates nor dies. Months later—if months can be kept—the shoot inside is found in exactly the same posture, green and viable, requiring no future.

The vines seem most nearly animal. They pass from tower to tower in bundles, divide at intersections, disappear into pools, and emerge on distant terraces. Their surfaces are marked at intervals by soft ridges that might be joints. Touching one sometimes produces a pressure in another vine far across the city. Cutting one releases sap everywhere: droplets pearl along miles of suspended growth at once, as though the entire network has suffered a single shallow wound.

But even the vines do not react. Reaction would require a before and after.

Deeper in the terraces, distinctions become difficult. Roots enter walls and continue as pale mineral seams. Bark hardens into basalt without any boundary where wood can be said to end. A column struck with stone rings like living timber near its base and like limestone higher up. Leaves spread into roofs. Roofs develop veins. Reservoirs are lined with a green membrane that contracts under the hand, although the water does not ripple.

There may be many species in the city. There may be only one.

It is possible that the moss, the ferns, the pillar-trees, and every vine are separate organs of a single life too broadly distributed to have a body. It is equally possible that each leaf is complete in itself and merely occupies the same existence as all the others. Here, identity does not depend on separation. A root can fork into two districts without becoming two roots. Two flowers on opposite cliffs may be found to contain the same grain of pollen.

The city does not house this life. The city is one of its forms.

Stone serves it as bone, though not dead bone. The cisterns are cavities within it. The vertical aqueducts are vessels whose contents need not travel because arrival and departure are adjacent states. The cataracts may be less like rivers than like an exposed circulation: white columns of pressure joining levels that geography keeps apart. Their endless roar is not a voice and not quite a heartbeat. It is the sound of continuity being maintained.

The life has no center. The high court is no brain, heart, nest, or sanctuary. The reflective pool governs nothing. Damage done there is no more significant than damage done to a fern in a lower alley, and no less. Every portion seems to contain the arrangement of the whole, altered to suit its local stone.

Nor is there evidence that it thinks.

It does not watch through flowers. It does not reach toward warmth. It sets no traps and offers no guidance. The repeated streets are not lures; the mismatched stair counts are not deceptions. Those imply intention, and intention implies an absent condition desired in place of the present one. Nothing here desires. Desire cannot live where lack is impossible.

Still, a traveler becomes aware of being accommodated.

Sweat begins to gather on the skin in the same fixed patterns as condensation on leaves. A bead brushed from the wrist returns to the wrist. Hair lies still despite movement. The pulse becomes difficult to count: sixty beats upward, fifty-nine downward. Hunger arrives but does not deepen. Cuts remain fresh without bleeding further or closing. Memories cease to arrange themselves by age. An event from childhood may feel no more distant than the most recent footstep, while the act of entering the city becomes impossible to place.

The vegetation does not approach. Rather, the distinction between traveler and vegetation loses some of its usefulness.

A footprint in moss acquires a green lining. A hand rested too long on a wall leaves warmth in the stone, and the hand afterward contains a faint mineral band visible beneath the skin. A person sleeping beneath the pillar-trees may wake with several shadows, each attached correctly, each carrying a slightly different posture. One rises when the person rises. Another remains asleep. A third is already walking toward a district the person has not chosen.

None is a duplicate. Duplication is not among the city’s habits.

What lives there may therefore be called the green things only because green is the part of it that still resembles life elsewhere. The mountain, water, buildings, and sound participate just as fully. Its living is not growth, reproduction, appetite, adaptation, or decay. It does not persist through time. Persistence is what time looks like from inside it.

It lives by being complete in every direction at once.

The plants are always becoming what they already are. Water circulates without leaving. Stone reaches its proper age without having passed through youth. Every possible terrace occupies its own version of the same height. The whole crown is not frozen, because frozen things have been interrupted. Nothing interrupted it. This is its activity.

Travelers bring the one substance it does not otherwise contain: consequence.

For a while, they alone can make one event follow another. They choose streets. They remember touching water. They become tired. They distinguish the vine before cutting from the vine after. The city accepts these differences without preserving them as changes to itself. Instead, it places them in the traveler. Each contradiction becomes another memory, another shadow, another route simultaneously taken.

Perhaps this is how it feeds, if feeding is the word: not on flesh, heat, or fear, but on sequence. A visitor enters with a life arranged as a narrow line. The terraces open that line into avenues, stairs, overlapping plazas. Past and possible acts begin to stand beside one another. The traveler is not consumed. Nothing is removed.

They are made spacious.

Eventually there may be no single figure left who can be said to have arrived. There may be a person on every terrace, each carrying the same body toward a different square; a sleeper beneath the trees; a face visible under upright water; a set of footprints continuing where no one walks. All remain one traveler, just as all the vines may be one vine.

Then the city contains them without having gained an inhabitant.

That is what lives there: something for which existence is not a passage from birth toward death, but an arrangement too complete to move through. It has no name because names separate one thing from another, and separation is only locally true within it.

The green is merely where its completeness shows through.
