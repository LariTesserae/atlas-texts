The people of the plateau are slight, deliberate, and difficult to count.

At a distance they appear almost uniform: pale figures in layered filter-cloth, moving single file along rails and maintenance ledges. Up close, no two are assembled quite alike. Their coats are cut around inherited braces, tool harnesses, breathing folds, insulating pads, and small ceramic plates worn over the sternum. Some have six fingers. Some have transparent nails through which fine dark numerals can be seen. A few possess narrow openings behind the jaw that flutter near active machinery. These are not treated as deformities. They are called accommodations.

They do not know whether their ancestors arrived on the plateau or were found inside it.

Birth records are unreliable. Families keep better accounts of where a child first appeared than of who bore it. Most are born in the ordinary way, in warm chambers lined with discarded filters, but others are discovered sleeping in locked cabinets or standing ankle-deep in the clear water of newly emerged districts. If such a child knows the household signs, accepts food, and casts the expected number of shadows, it is adopted without ceremony. Questions are postponed until the child is old enough to lie.

Their skin tends toward colorless brown, ash, or the faint gray-pink of unglazed ceramic. Generations spent beneath hard reflected light have given many of them dark inner eyelids, which slide sideways across the eye when they cross exposed roofs. Their pupils contract into small geometric shapes. Among cistern workers the eyes are often filmed silver, and among those who tend the summit mirrors they may become black enough to show stars at noon.

Age does not bend them so much as make them still. Elders can remain seated beside a hatch for hours, apparently asleep, then reach out at the precise instant a pressure gauge trembles or a migrating beetle arrives with a polished screw. Their memories are long but nonsequential. They may describe tomorrow accurately and forget a marriage of forty years. Because dates are unstable, age is measured by durable events: three piston descents, nine eel ascensions, the duplication of the eastern warehouse, the winter when rain fell upward in the school.

Names are similarly practical. A person has a home-name, a work-name, and a name used only in reflective surfaces. Home-names are brief and soft. Work-names may be entire instructions: *She Who Opens the Third Valve After Frost*, *Keeper of the Dry Ladder*, *Do Not Follow Him Through Blue Doors*. Mirror-names are never spoken aloud. Children are taught to mouth them silently if the watching animal appears behind them.

Most inhabitants are rearrangers.

They travel with flat tools, sealant knives, coils of wire, chalk, little hammers wrapped in cloth, and cases of sorted fasteners. Their craft is not to create but to recognize what an existing thing is becoming. A cracked reservoir wall may be declaring itself a row of houses. An obsolete lift cage may already contain the disposition of a shrine. The skilled rearranger does not impose a design; she removes whatever prevents the new use from being obvious.

This makes demolition a form of listening.

Before taking a panel, people press their foreheads to it. Before cutting a pipe, they taste the condensation. If the metal vibrates against the teeth, the pipe is occupied, load-bearing, or dreaming and must not be disturbed. Every settlement has stories of impatient workers who dismantled a harmless-looking staircase and caused a distant district to lose its rain.

There are specialists in nearly invisible labor. Rivet readers interpret the stress patterns around old bolts. Echo shepherds release words into acoustic valleys and collect them hours later in padded jars. Weather clerks map the narrow corridors along which rain chooses to fall. Delay porters carry messages across bridges whose ends belong to different years. Pond witnesses sit beside temporary water until it evaporates, recording what appeared in the reflections.

None of these professions is regarded as more mysterious than cooking.

Food comes from many levels of the works. Red grass is ground with mineral salts into bitter cakes. Reservoir eels are eaten only after they have shed their charge, and their translucent bones are woven into lamp cages. Moth colonies provide heat, wax, and, during lean months, a flour that tastes faintly of hot dust. Pale fruits grow inside certain cable housings, each one printed with a different serial number. The fruit is nourishing, but families avoid eating two with consecutive numbers.

Meals are taken around converted pressure doors. No one sits at the head, because a pressure door has no head. A place is left open for anyone delayed by faulty distance. Sometimes the missing person arrives years younger than expected. Sometimes a pipeline fox occupies the place instead, curls its tail around its feet, and receives a bowl.

The foxes live nearest to the people without belonging to them. They are narrow, soot-colored creatures with bright jointed whiskers and paws that adhere briefly to metal. They follow safety markings even when the paint ends, continuing along invisible lines over open air. Rearrangers watch them closely. Where a fox refuses to step, a connection is about to fail. Where three foxes sleep together, a new passage usually appears.

Children imitate them. They balance on rails, leap between painted stripes, and crawl through ducts adults can no longer enter. The plateau’s young are loud by local standards, though their loudness consists mostly of clicks, whistles, and palm taps against hollow surfaces. They learn the resonances of their district before they learn its map. Blindfolded, a child can often identify home from the pitch of rain in a bolt socket.

The six-legged hares are less trusted. Their extra forelimbs are small and deft, capable of loosening clasps and opening food tins. They nest among warm cables and collect objects that have been misplaced but not yet missed. During certain seasons they build elaborate mock control rooms beneath the plates, arranging bottle caps, washers, teeth, and beetle shells in exact imitation of panels above. Whether they are copying the machinery or operating it at another scale is disputed.

The warm moths are treated almost as a civic population. Settlements conduct annual counts by dimming every lamp and waiting for the insects to settle into switch shapes. Missing moths indicate a broken circuit somewhere nearby. Excess moths suggest that a room is about to duplicate. Nursery keepers allow them to cover sleeping infants in winter, leaving only the mouth and nostrils clear. In the morning the moths depart, and faint diagrams remain on the child’s skin.

Birds carry news farther than people can travel. Cliff-swifts return with washers from unknown structures. Reservoir birds bring eels whose bodies contain glimpses of other waters: black seas under green stars, flooded corridors lined with shoes, cisterns hanging upside down above an endless city. The inhabitants do not kill a bird that arrives with a numbered band. They copy the number onto a wall and wait. Eventually a door bearing the same number appears somewhere in the district.

There are also human beings who are becoming less easy to distinguish from their work.

Long service in pressure chambers can make a person’s breath emerge as white vapor even in warmth. Mirror tenders develop reflective patches along the arms. Those who maintain the silent carts sometimes lose their footfalls entirely. The oldest cistern divers grow flexible ribs and can remain underwater for days, returning with rust in their hair and memories that belong to drowned machines.

They are not feared. Transformation is considered one of the plateau’s ordinary forms of employment, though families may grieve when it progresses beyond conversation. A beloved worker who can no longer leave a ventilation bank is visited there. Cloth is tied around the warm pipe through which she answers. Meals are shared beside her access panel. Her descendants consult her when the weather enters the building.

Death is defined not by stillness but by disconnection.

A body may cease breathing and remain socially present if lamps brighten at its touch or nearby gauges continue to display its pulse. The dead are rarely buried, since there is nowhere to bury them. They are wrapped in signal cloth and placed in useful cavities: beneath resonant floors, inside empty switchgear, along the walls of condensers. In time their bones acquire mineral coats and become part of the district’s acoustic character. Families visit by tapping known rhythms on the nearest plate.

Some bodies disappear before this can be done. Doors close. A shadow walks away without its owner. A rain-filled room is found empty except for clothing folded around a warm patch of floor. Such people are called transferred, not dead. Their place at the table remains available.

The inhabitants have governments, though these are modest beside the systems they inhabit. Councils assemble when enough representatives arrive in the same version of a room. Decisions are marked on ceramic tiles and distributed among several districts so that no single alteration of reality can erase them. Laws concern load, water, passage, and warning. It is forbidden to conceal a migrating triangle, to divert rain from a nursery, to count another person’s teeth in a new region, or to open a box from the inside.

They have no law against entering the new districts. Curiosity is understood to be stronger than law.

Explorers go in pairs connected by loose red thread. They carry bells, dry chalk, opaque mirrors, and a domestic beetle in a ventilated case. If the beetle begins polishing something that is not rusted, they turn back. If the thread becomes taut while the companions stand side by side, they cut it immediately. Survivors return with objects that resist ownership: keys that unlock childhood rooms, cups that fill with rain from other seasons, office ledgers listing residents not yet born.

A few explorers come back immaculate. Their clothing is unworn, scars have vanished, and every tooth is whole. They speak correctly but pause before familiar names. Animals avoid them except for moths, which cover them in dense, warm layers. Such returnees are welcomed, fed, and never left alone near sleeping children.

Far below all these lives are the plate-bearers.

No one has seen an entire one. Divers and maintenance crawlers report surfaces like skin, stone, rubber, and old white metal, rising through the luminous depth. The slow creatures press upward beneath districts, supporting or testing them. Some believe they are animals that grew around the plateau’s foundations. Others say the plateau has no foundations and the creatures are carrying it voluntarily.

The people lower gifts through inspection shafts: salt, charged wire, bowls of rainwater, recordings of small sounds. The gifts vanish. Months later, a neighborhood lifts slightly and settles in a better alignment.

Children are told that the great breath beneath the plates belongs to one being. Engineers insist there must be thousands. The oldest elders say both ideas are too small.

And there may be another inhabitant altogether: the unfamiliar animal seen in every reflection on certain mornings. Descriptions never agree. It has antlers, or cables; fur, or polished plates; one eye, or a multitude of dark windows. It always stands behind the observer, attentive but not predatory. Pipeline foxes acknowledge it. Birds leave space for it on reservoir rims. The moths sometimes form its outline across dead consoles.

No one has met it by turning around.

Some say it is the plateau itself, trying patiently to acquire a body. Others say it is what the factories make from the migrating animals and release where no direct eye can see. A smaller sect believes it is the only creature that truly lives there, and that people, birds, eels, trees, and the enormous breathers below are organs by which it senses its bright, unfinished world.

Whatever it is, it appears a little closer each year.
