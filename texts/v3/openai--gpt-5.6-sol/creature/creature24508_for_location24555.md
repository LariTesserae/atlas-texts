The chief inhabitants are hidden.

For every animal crossing the sunlit crust, thousands live inside it: in walls, root chambers, ventilation throats, seed pockets, dung vaults, and the cool seams beneath hardened clay. The plain’s apparent surface is only its roof. Below it lies a shallow, inhabited depth, seldom more than a few body lengths thick but continuous across immense distances.

The tallest builders are pale, soft-bodied termites whose queens never see daylight. Different castes perform different kinds of masonry. Some chew mineral dust with resin and saliva to make waterproof outer plaster. Others lay porous internal walls through which moisture can pass and condense. Broad-headed soldiers stand in the narrowest gates, not merely defending the colony but sealing it with their own bodies when hot wind drives inward. Their mounds are less like fortresses than respiratory organs: warm chambers rise, cool galleries descend, and the entire structure inhales through shaded apertures near the ground.

No mound belongs entirely to its makers. Tiny silver roaches graze its inner molds. Blind beetles feed on shed termite skins. Threadlike worms inhabit the damp films around fungal chambers. Lizards leave eggs in abandoned ventilation pockets, where the temperature scarcely varies. When a colony dies, its architecture may remain occupied for generations, changing tenants so gradually that there is no moment when the mound can be called empty.

Among the lowest roofs live the burrowers: dense-furred mammals with blunt faces, broad incisors, and forefeet like little shovels. Their eyes are small, but their whiskers spread wider than their bodies and read the pressure of the tunnel air. They feed mostly on buried stems and water-storing tubers. They do not consume a plant outright if they can avoid it. Instead they bite channels into a tuber, drink the slowly welling sap, then pack the wound with chewed fiber and clay. A carefully tended root may supply one family for years.

Their warrens are communal but not peaceful. Families defend sleeping chambers, food knots, and cool shafts, while tolerating one another in the main passages. Boundaries are marked with pellets of dried scent, renewed at dusk. Disputes consist largely of shoving, tooth-clicking, and the deliberate blocking of doors. Actual wounds are dangerous in so dry a place; blood cannot be wasted. Even aggression has become economical.

Above them, in clay banks and beneath the ledges of old mounds, live small communal birds. They are dust-colored except for pale throats that flash when they turn. Their nests begin as individual cups, but each season new cups are attached to old ones until the colony becomes a wall of chambers. The birds recognize their own openings by the arrangement of pebbles, fibers, feathers, and beetle cases around the rim. A large nesting wall may contain hundreds of such signatures.

They fly mostly at dawn and evening. In the hottest hours they cling inside their chambers with wings slightly raised, letting air pass over the skin. They carry food for their young in dry pellets, but water in specialized folds of the throat. A returning bird may be mobbed not for its insects but for the moisture beading at the corners of its bill. Adults allow nestlings to drink first. Old birds, when water is scarce, often cease breeding and become carriers for the colony’s young.

The plants are inhabitants and architecture together. The great resin shrubs begin as separate thorny bushes, but their roots graft when they touch. Insects stitch adjacent branches with silk and chewed pulp. Wind packs dust into the mesh. Fine roots invade the trapped soil, and in time a thicket becomes a single roofed mass containing courts, galleries, and pockets of deep shade.

Whether such a grove is one organism or many is impossible to say. Individual trunks die while the joined crown continues. Sugars move through grafted roots. Water taken up in one quarter may sustain leaves far away. A grove can slowly abandon its windward edge and grow from its sheltered side, migrating across generations without any one plant moving.

Within these living halls are the larger grazers: slight, long-eared animals standing somewhere between antelope and hare. Their legs are narrow, but their feet spread into soft pads that bridge fragile crusts. They crop only the tips of cushion plants and vines, moving after a few bites so that no patch is opened to the sun. Their nostrils close against dust. During the day they rest shoulder to shoulder beneath the thorns, each animal lowering the exposed surface of the others.

Their young are born without strong scent and remain in nursery warrens excavated beneath the groves. Several mothers share a chamber. They return at different hours, guided through the branching tunnels by touch and low vibrations transmitted through the floor. Milk is concentrated, almost paste-like. The young lick it rather than suckle freely, and none is spilled.

Smaller harelike grazers use the mounds themselves. They know the daily movement of shade with extraordinary precision. A family may follow one tower’s shadow from west to east, feeding along its margin and retreating into a slit at its base whenever the light sharpens. Their ears serve as heat exchangers at night but fold close beneath insulating fur by day.

The predators are equally restrained. Clay cats, narrow-bodied and long-tailed, lie on parapets with their forelegs hanging down like roots. Their coats are patterned not in spots or stripes but in irregular patches matching cracked plaster, lichen, and shadow. They can remain still through most of a day. A hunt may consist of one silent drop onto an animal passing below. If the first attempt fails, the cat rarely pursues. Speed generates heat, and heat demands water.

Vent snakes are slimmer than a finger for much of their length. They enter mound shafts headfirst, anchor themselves around a bend, and wait with their jaws beside a well-used crossing. Some carry sensory scales along the lower jaw that detect the faint footfalls of animals through clay. They may swallow only a handful of prey in a season. Between meals they become nearly inert, part of the tunnel’s geometry.

The thorn owls are more visible. They sit upright in dead forks at twilight, pale-faced and nearly featherless around the eyes. Their wing feathers have softened edges, and their toes are cushioned underneath. Even their droppings are quickly claimed: moths drink from them, beetles bury them, and roots gather beneath favored perches.

Much of the plain’s communication passes through the ground. Burrowers drum warnings with their heels. Termites alter the rhythm of work when a wall is breached. Grazers freeze when they feel a sudden cessation of ordinary vibration. A predator may be revealed not by any alarm call but by a spreading stillness: digging stops, mandibles pause, wings close, and the silence travels outward faster than the hunter.

Scent is used sparingly because every vapor carries moisture away. Many animals store scent compounds in wax and deposit them by touch rather than releasing them into the air. Others communicate with color: a throat patch briefly exposed, a flank turned pale-side outward, the opening and closing of patterned wings. At night, some beetles produce dim green points of light under the thorn canopy. The signals are directional and hooded, visible only from below, so that owls do not see them from their perches.

The smallest creatures govern the water economy. Velvet mites comb dew too fine for larger tongues. Flat-bodied beetles climb the warm sides of towers before dawn; moisture condenses on their cooler shells and runs into grooves leading to their mouths. Pale tunnel crickets gather around breathing animals and drink from fur. Certain moths visit sleeping grazers not for blood but for tears, touching the corner of an eye with long, soft mouthparts and leaving without waking them.

Nothing drinks from a pool. Most inhabitants would not recognize one.

Rain, when it comes, is almost catastrophic. The first drops strike like falling stones. Entrances are sealed. Birds plaster themselves against nesting walls. Burrowers retreat upward from tunnels that may suddenly flood. Termites open emergency channels, directing water into sacrificial chambers packed with dry fiber and dormant seeds.

Then the plain changes for a few hours. Leaves flatten to receive the rain. Shrubs uncurl tissues that have remained folded for years. Animals lick roofs, backs, whiskers, and one another. Every depression is crowded before it can fill. The water vanishes rapidly into bodies and structures, wicked downward by roots, swallowed by swelling clay, sealed behind fresh plaster.

The generations afterward bear the event within them. Tubers grow thick. Queens lay heavily. Burrowers enlarge their nurseries. Birds produce larger clutches. Seeds that have waited beyond the lifespan of any animal open all at once, covering roofs and causeways in a brief low bloom. Young creatures may grow to adulthood having never felt rain, yet their abundance is its delayed expression.

Death is similarly dispersed. There are no enduring corpses. Hide beetles enter first, followed by mites, larvae, ants, and small gnawing mammals. Bones are rasped for mineral, splintered, carried underground, and packed into nursery walls. Horns become tools for no one, yet still acquire use: termites tunnel through them, birds wedge them into nest foundations, roots dissolve them slowly. The individual disappears, but almost none of its substance leaves the neighborhood.

No species rules the plain. The termites may appear to, because their structures are conspicuous; the thorn groves may appear to, because they hold the shade; the burrowers may appear to, because they rearrange the ground. But each is dependent on the others’ waste, shelter, restraint, or accidental labor.

Together they form something that behaves almost like a single creature without possessing a single mind. Its towers breathe. Its roots hoard. Its animals circulate nutrients and seeds like wandering cells. Its wounds close quickly. Its outer skin is forever broken and forever repaired.

Yet within that larger order, every inhabitant remains stubbornly itself: the queen in her sealed chamber; the old carrier bird returning with a throatful of water; the clay cat lowering one paw toward a shadow; the beetle waiting for condensation; the buried seed listening, in its fashion, for rain.

They do not know they inhabit a city. They know only the nearest wall, the nearest root, the hour of shade, the scent at a doorway, and the minute quantity of moisture entrusted to them.
