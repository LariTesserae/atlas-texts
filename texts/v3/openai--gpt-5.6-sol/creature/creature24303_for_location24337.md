No single people inhabit the city. Its citizens are arrangements of appetite: mouths facing the current, arms laid across stone, shells clamped against pressure, soft bodies borrowing whatever hardness death has left behind.

The newest arrivals live nearest the fissures. They are drifters caught by accident—larvae finer than grit, transparent crustaceans, spores that will never germinate, jelly fragments still twitching with undischarged stings. Most are eaten before they touch a wall. Those that settle must choose quickly, though they possess nothing recognizable as choice. A chemical taste, a patch of turbulence, the slime of an earlier colony: these are invitation enough. They fasten themselves to black glass and begin changing it.

Among the first are the tube worms. Their exposed crowns are intricate and festive, red or cream or banded like tiny ceremonial fans, but the animals beneath are pale, compressed things folded into masonry of their own secretion. At the brush of a pressure wave, every crown disappears. The withdrawal passes down a whole street in sequence, so that a flourishing grove becomes, in an instant, a district of empty chimneys. They are builders only because they cannot help leaving their houses behind.

Mussels occupy the stronger currents. They bind themselves together with dark threads until individuals are difficult to distinguish. Old ones form the lower courses, their shells scabbed with bryozoans and minute limpets; younger ones wedge into the narrowing gaps above. Deep inside the wall lie the dead, still locked in place. Amphipods move through them constantly, picking edible films from hinges and crevices. Pea crabs live between mantle and shell. Worms enter through old boreholes. A mussel wall is less a colony than a crowded vertical country, with most of its population hidden inside the bodies or remains of something larger.

The sponges appear motionless, but they are among the chamber’s great movers of water. They stand as knuckled towers, low crusts, translucent cups, and branching forms the color of old bone. Every surface is perforated. Water enters them cloudy and leaves altered—emptier of food, richer in waste, bearing traces of the sponge’s chemistry into neighboring streets. Small animals shelter in their canals. Some are guests, some thieves, some prey that has not yet been digested. If a tower is torn, its fragments may attach elsewhere. Here, dismemberment can resemble migration.

The shrimp are more visibly social. They gather wherever the current narrows, all facing the same way, their legs opening into delicate baskets. They seem orderly until food arrives. Then the rows collapse into a pale commotion of antennae and transparent abdomens. Each animal snatches from the water and from its neighbor. Their shells show the color of what they have eaten: greenish when feeding on bacterial clumps, pink after flesh, brown with silt. When they molt, they back out of themselves and leave perfect empty citizens clinging to the walls. For a few hours afterward they hide, soft enough to be punctured by almost anything.

Brittle stars own the upper ledges, if ownership can be said to consist of holding on. Their central discs remain concealed while their arms extend across one another in a living mesh. They pass food inward by a series of small contractions. A severed arm continues to curl and seek purchase long after it has ceased to belong to the animal. Beneath the mesh, eggs develop, copepods shelter, and young brittle stars—small as punctuation—cling to the spines of adults. At times the entire ceiling seems to be one creature with thousands of undecided limbs.

The grazers move through this fixed multitude.

Nudibranchs travel with an almost extravagant slowness, carrying exposed gills and soft horns where armor should be. Their colors are not decoration in the darkness but consequence and warning. One has eaten orange hydroids and wears their pigments through its body. Another stores the stinging cells of its prey at the tips of blue-white processes, turning borrowed weapons outward. Their trails are visible mainly as absences: clean ribbons through films, cropped paths across furry colonies. Behind them, bacteria immediately begin to close the wound.

Urchins rasp at the walls with five hard teeth. Their feeding can be felt farther than it can be heard. Shell chips and mineral grains pass through them and emerge as finer sediment, filling the chamber’s lowest seams. Limpets grind smaller circles. Snails browse the bacterial glow until their shells are filmed with it. Each grazer erases a surface and prepares another.

In the coldest water live those whose bodies seem designed for a different set of directions. The head-down fish wait above the brine as if suspended from their tails. Their eyes are reduced, clouded, or useful only for distinguishing glimmer from complete dark. Along their flanks, pressure-sensitive lines read the passing of shrimp and worms. They do not pursue far. A movement comes within range; the mouth opens; the fish becomes still again.

The long-legged crabs map the city by repeated touch. Their front limbs tap shell rims, tube mouths, anemone flesh, fresh fractures. They know no street as a route seen from above. They know sequences of contact: smooth shell, branching sponge, three worm tubes, open water, sharp basalt. If a colony grows across the sequence, the crab hesitates as though part of its memory has been removed. Its disguise is similarly tactile. Folded among calcareous tubes, it suppresses movement and becomes a shape not worth testing.

Hermit crabs are the city’s most conspicuous inheritors. They carry old rooms through streets that barely accommodate them. Their adopted shells bear several eras at once: the spiral made by a dead mollusk; a borehole left by a predatory snail; a bryozoan crust; hydroids planted on the upper surface; a ribbon worm folded into the innermost whorl. When the crab changes shells, the attached settlement changes address with it. What looks like one animal walking is a tenement in transit.

At the rear is the great anemone, though “great” is a local measure. It has no conception of the city around it, yet much of the city lives according to its breathing.

Its body is clear enough to reveal digestion as weather. A captured copepod darkens the throat. Shell pieces descend and later rise again, polished of flesh. Muscles contract in slow bands along the column. When the anemone fills with water, its tentacles extend into the lanes, tasting every surface they touch. Permanent neighbors pass among them with little harm. Their scent has become ordinary. Strange flesh provokes an immediate folding inward: tentacle, prey, and surrounding water drawn toward the mouth.

Shrimp groom the anemone and steal from it. Small fish rest against its column. Isopods shelter beneath the margin of its base. Worms build tubes in places the tentacles cannot quite reach. Thus the largest predator is also housing, obstruction, refuge, feeding ground, and clock. No one obeys it. Everyone accommodates it.

Above and between the larger bodies live populations too numerous to appear as individuals. Copepods pulse in loose clouds. Ostracods creep like seeds on jointed legs and flash green when struck. Flatworms pour themselves through the shellwork, flattening until they seem no thicker than a stain. Hydroids raise forests of mouths on hair-fine stalks. Bryozoans reproduce by adding rooms to themselves, each chamber occupied by a minute feeding animal genetically continuous with its neighbors. They blur the distinction between citizen and building. A colony may begin on a shell, survive the shell’s occupant, spread onto basalt, bridge to a worm tube, and persist after all three supports have broken apart.

Bacteria are everywhere, and in a sense they are the oldest and largest inhabitant. They coat eggs, line guts, soften rock, digest corpses, harvest minerals, and make edible what would otherwise pass unused through the chamber. Around the seepage they assemble into pale veils and rust-colored mats. Some live without light on the chemical difference between one kind of water and another. The animals graze them, host them, depend upon them, and become food for them in turn. Every visible creature is also a moving habitat for invisible ones.

Brooding occurs wherever predation is least immediate. Eggs are glued beneath ledges, packed into empty tubes, carried under tails, held inside gill chambers, or buried in sponge canals. They are pearl-white, amber, glass-clear, or red with yolk. Many contain dark points that turn before hatching. Whole courtyards may be filled with them, guarded by a parent or abandoned to current and chance. Most become meals. A few become the next layer.

There are no permanent occupations. The feeder becomes prey; the predator becomes shelter; the shelter becomes sediment. An animal may spend its life defending a crevice only for its shell to seal that crevice after death. A colony may smother its foundation and collapse under its own success. Even the most settled inhabitants are temporary phases of material.

What lives there, finally, is not merely the shrimp, the worms, the mussels, the blind crabs, or the anemone. It is tenancy itself: the ceaseless passing of space and substance from one body to another.

Nothing possesses the hollow. Everything borrows it.

And in borrowing it—filtering its water, cementing its fragments, eating its films, dying in its passages—the inhabitants steadily replace the hollow with themselves.
