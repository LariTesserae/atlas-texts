The first inhabitants one notices are not the people but their consequences: a hand withdrawing behind a reed screen, a basket rising without a visible bearer, flour dust shaken from an upper balcony, three pebbles knocked from a stair by someone climbing barefoot above. The settlement seems to move before it seems inhabited. Then the eye adjusts, and figures separate from plaster, shadow, and hanging vegetation.

The people are generally small and spare, though not uniformly so. Their narrowness is partly inheritance and partly the shaping pressure of the cleft. Broad gestures are impractical. Heavy bodies suffer on ladders. Children learn to turn sideways almost as soon as they walk, and adults pass one another on stairs with an unconscious sequence of tilted shoulders, lifted elbows, and hands placed briefly at the other’s waist. They climb with their toes as much as their feet. Even the elderly retain strong fingers.

Their skin is darkened less by constant sun than by mineral dust, smoke, plant dyes, and the glossy salves they use against dryness. Faces are lined early around the eyes and mouth. Hair is usually bound close to the head or braided into cords, since loose hair catches on thorns, rope, and clay hooks. Many shave portions of the scalp where carrying bands rest. Their clothing is layered but scant: wrapped fiber cloth, soft hides, netted vests with many small pockets, short capes against falling grit. Knees, palms, and the inside of the forearms are protected by patches of leather. Almost everyone carries a lidded cup attached to the body by cord.

They smell of smoke and bitter sap.

They are not silent people. Privacy cannot be maintained by quiet in such a crowded place, and so it is maintained by convention. One does not listen when a neighboring family speaks behind a hanging mat. One does not look through the smoke hole above another household. Names called across levels are answered promptly, lest the whole cleft continue shouting them. News travels vertically: from rooftop to bridge, from bridge to stair, from stair to the low chambers where elders and infants spend the heat of day.

Their voices tend toward sharp, carrying notes. Much of their speech is accompanied by taps on stone, whistles, and rope signals. A person below can ask for an empty basket, warn of a snake, announce a returning flock, or demand that a bridge be cleared without seeing the person above. During windstorms, when speech is torn away, households communicate by pulling lines strung through holes in shared walls. Children know these patterns before they know all the spoken words.

No one lives alone in the ordinary sense. A household may contain blood relatives, widowed neighbors, apprentices, fostered children, two or three animals, a tolerated colony of geckos, and the descendants of birds whose nests were built into the wall generations earlier. Kinship follows care more reliably than descent. The person who feeds a child through a dry year acquires a claim on that child’s loyalty. The person who tends another’s thorn tree may inherit a portion of its pods. An abandoned room passes not to the nearest relative but often to whoever has kept its roof sealed.

Their social world is arranged by height, but height is not simply rank. The upper dwellers are climbers, bird keepers, wind-watchers, and collectors of condensation. They endure the greatest heat, the hardest gusts, and the danger of falcons. The lowest families work among pens, refuse beds, fungi, insects, and old fill. They know the buried city better than anyone and can find cool chambers where others see only blocked doors. Between them live most craftspeople, growers, cooks, and traders. A household may possess spaces on several levels: a sleeping alcove below, a garden basket in the middle reaches, a few precious cloths stretched near the rim.

Ownership is rarely absolute. A ledge may belong to one family for sleeping, another for drying fruit, and the whole settlement during a storm. Thornwood roots belong to whoever keeps the plant alive; its shade belongs to those beneath it; branches crossing a doorway may be cut only after negotiation. Nesting birds are claimed by the wall they occupy, but birds taken in flight belong to the catcher. Such distinctions produce endless argument. The city has people whose chief talent is remembering old agreements.

Water governs manners more strictly than authority does.

To drink in front of another person without offering is an insult unless the drink is medicinal. To spill is shameful, but not because liquid is sacred in any simple sense. It is shameful because spilling reveals inattention. Cups are passed with both hands. A host does not fill one to the brim; generosity is shown by offering again. Tears are noticed and politely ignored. Spitting in anger is considered more violent than striking. Bloodshed within the inhabited cleft is especially abhorrent, both for practical reasons and because every fly, ant, dog, and lizard responds to it.

Their language distinguishes moisture by source and behavior. There is moisture that forms on cloth, moisture held in pulp, moisture that can be pressed from flesh, moisture spoiled by smoke, moisture promised by swollen roots, moisture lost invisibly through breathing. They have fewer useful words for rivers. To many of them, a river is not primarily a body of water but an old story about waste on an impossible scale.

Those who know how to open living things without ruining them are highly regarded. Plant cutters remove sections from great succulents and seal the wounds with ash, clay, and resin. Animal keepers can draw milk without exhausting a mother or bleed a goat from a shallow vein and close it again. Seed women split gourds so that rind, fiber, pulp, and kernels fall into separate vessels. Butchers work rapidly under nets while children and dogs hold covered bowls. Nothing wet remains exposed longer than necessary.

Their healers share these skills and are difficult to distinguish from gardeners, midwives, poison workers, and cooks. They treat fever with shade and scraped cactus flesh, close wounds with ant jaws or thorn stitches, and diagnose illness partly by the smell of a person’s breath. They are skilled at removing barbs, mites, eggs, and broken splinters. Serious sickness alarms the entire vertical neighborhood, because the cleft has little defense against contagion. Bedding is burned reluctantly. Rooms are smoked with bitter leaves. A colored cord may be tied across a ladder to prohibit passage.

Children are everywhere, but seldom underfoot. The dangerous routes discipline them early. Toddlers are tethered in open rooms or carried in back slings. Older children crawl through storage spaces, gather dung, pick insects from leaves, chase grain moths, and retrieve eggs from holes too narrow for adults. They keep beetles in pottery shards and race them along scratched tracks. Their games imitate the city’s hazards: spotting a painted scorpion among pebbles, crossing a rope without touching the ground, stealing a seed from beneath a mock falcon’s shadow.

They are warned never to follow a lizard blindly into a crack, never to place a hand where air feels unexpectedly cool, and never to climb toward the rim when the wind has gone abruptly still.

Adolescents begin to range farthest. They cross the plateau at night to gather stone, thornwood, mineral pigments, salt, shed horn, and whatever dead creatures the desert has preserved. They travel in low, roped groups, carrying no flame when the moon is bright. The plateau frightens them in a way the cleft does not. Its openness offers no handholds, no roofs, no voices beyond the next wall. Some become addicted to that emptiness and volunteer repeatedly for journeys. Others return trembling and refuse ever to go above again.

The oldest inhabitants tend to move downward. Their ladders are shortened; their sleeping places shift to cool rooms near the floor. There they sort seed, twist cord, watch infants, and preserve memories of structures now buried. They can identify families by the rhythm of footsteps overhead. Some are nearly blind yet know every wall by touch. They tell of doors beneath the ground, of gardens that once occupied places now roofed over, of a time when a certain thorn tree was only a sprout in a cooking pot.

The dead are not permitted to keep much moisture. Bodies are washed sparingly with smoke, dust, aromatic oils, and crushed leaves, then wrapped and dried in upper chambers where insects are kept away by resin. Some bones are later placed in sealed wall niches. Others are ground and mixed into plaster for a descendant’s house or into the soil basket of a family plant. This is not understood as consumption. The dead become structure, shade, and root nourishment: the three forms of shelter the cleft can offer.

Their shrines are correspondingly small. A polished handhold may be a shrine. So may a clay cup sealed into a wall, a bird skull wrapped in blue fiber, or an ancient succulent scar that resembles an open eye. They do not imagine holiness as descending from the sky. What is valuable is concealed: moisture in flesh, roots in stone, children in the body, ancestors in walls, the city in the plateau. To expose a thing is to endanger it. To open it correctly is an act requiring permission, skill, and gratitude.

Their most persistent sacred figure is not quite a god and not quite an ancestor. It is described sometimes as a root, sometimes as a burrowing animal, sometimes as an old woman folded into a crack. It moves downward where no one can follow, finding the coolness hidden in stone. In carvings it appears as a branching line ending in many hands. People invoke it when cutting plants, digging postholes, entering blocked rooms, or assisting births.

Not all inhabitants accept such rites. There are skeptics, mockers, and people too busy to care. A roof repair can provoke more passion than a festival. The settlement’s ceremonies are repeatedly interrupted by animals, wind, crying infants, broken ropes, and urgent calls from another level. No ritual remains pure for long.

The nonhuman population shapes the city as decisively as its people do.

The goats are wiry, sharp-hoofed creatures with narrow bodies and oversized ears. They are valued for milk, blood, dung, hide, sinew, horn, and their ability to eat what humans cannot. They are also thieves and vandals. A goat freed from its tether can destroy a season’s wall garden in moments. Their keepers paint their horns and fit the worst offenders with wooden neck frames that prevent them from squeezing through doors. Old females know the bridges better than visitors do.

The penned rodents are soft-furred, nervous, and prolific. Children handle them; adults pretend not to be fond of them. Certain breeding mothers are known by name and buried rather than eaten. Dogs occupy an uneasy category between guard, scavenger, companion, and threat. They are lean, sand-colored, scarred by snakes and one another. Each belongs nominally to a household but sleeps wherever passage is warm. A dog that reliably smells vipers is fed before many people.

The kitchen lizards are almost citizens. They have learned the sounds of chopping and the opening of fat jars. Some wear tiny daubs of pigment on their backs so households can argue over which one belongs where. Geckos are left unmarked and unnamed; it is believed that counting them invites mites.

Birds constitute another city above the human one. Doves feed families with eggs and occasional meat, finches consume seed and steal it, parrots chew beams and imitate rope calls badly enough to cause confusion. Swifts belong to no one. Their arrival and departure mark seasons more reliably than weather does. Bird keepers read the health of the plateau from what returns in crops and droppings: distant seeds, insect wings, unfamiliar dust, once in a generation a fleck of green from somewhere beyond the horizon.

The bats are treated with wary respect. Their guano is precious, but disturbing a roost can bring bites, panic, and a rain of parasites. Specialized gatherers enter the seams wearing clay masks and slicked coverings. They scrape beneath the colonies while the animals sleep overhead in a dense heat of fur and membrane. These gatherers are recognizable by the sharp musk that never quite leaves them.

Insects are divided less into species than into occupations: those that drink, those that bore, those that spoil, those that pollinate, those that clean, those that announce dampness, those whose appearance predicts a plant’s flowering. Ants are enemies in storerooms and allies in refuse beds. Wasps are tolerated until their galleries cross a handhold. Scorpions are removed if possible, killed if necessary, and sometimes kept for venom. Heavy dusk moths are gently caught, dusted with pollen, and released among household flowers when natural pollination fails.

At night, these many populations rearrange themselves.

Human paths contract to the safest stairs. Dogs take the junctions. Geckos descend. Spiders occupy the warm rims of lamps. Bats claim the open air between bridges, and moths batter themselves against pale walls. Snakes emerge into routes that belonged to children an hour before. Families lower reed screens but leave gaps for ventilation, making every dwelling a lit box with darkness moving around it.

The people sleep lightly. They know the difference between settling plaster and a rodent in the wall, between a goat shifting its weight and a ladder rope beginning to fail. A flutter means one thing, a soft scrape another. The city has trained them to interpret disturbance. Stillness is what wakes them.

Seen together, they are not a people who have conquered thirst or mastered the cleft. Their endurance is more intimate and less triumphant. They survive by making themselves useful to processes they cannot control: birds fertilize gardens, gardens feed goats, goats feed people, people repair bat roosts, bats carry seeds and eat moths, moths open flowers, termites hollow beams, and hollow beams are replaced by thornwood grown from dung-enriched cracks.

Every resident consumes the city, and every resident builds some portion of it.

From above, none of them can be seen. Yet below the seam they climb, quarrel, breed, mend, bleed animals, sing over grinding stones, curse termites, trade eggs, flirt across bridges, dry their dead, rescue fledglings, and wake in darkness to the sound of something drinking from a cup that was not covered tightly enough.
