The first answer is that plants live there.

The answer does not remain sufficient for long.

There are growths that can be distinguished as individuals, at least for a season. In the shallow reservoirs live cup-mats, each cup a small translucent mouth turned upward to collect mist. They float in contiguous colonies, joined beneath the surface by threads fine enough to pass through the pores of the breathing pool-floor. When mineral concentrations rise, the cups darken and detach from one another. For a few hours each becomes a separate vessel, drifting toward a favorable chemical gradient. Then their rims touch, recognize compatible tissues, and seal. By evening there is a single mat again, though not the same one.

Violet skins spread among them. These are thin, many-layered organisms, slick as oil but tough enough to bear the impact of heavy drops. They regulate the pools beneath, opening clear windows when the water requires cooling and drawing shut when useful light is scarce. Slow waves pass through them without wind. At times a skin folds itself into ridges and travels across a reservoir, carrying captured warmth from one end to the other. Its underside is crowded with pale digestive tassels that do not eat living prey—there is none—but dissolve abandoned membranes, mineral flakes, and old reproductive chains fallen out of the cloud.

The hollow-stem forests are more readily mistaken for familiar plants. They rise straight from the marsh in close ranks, jointed and smooth, their walls strengthened by flexible glass. Yet each “stem” is a temporary tower grown by a buried braid of tissues shared among thousands of towers. The knocking crowns are valves. By leaning together and separating, they pump mist down their interiors, sorting the air into water, gases, spores, and charged dust. Their music is part accident and part regulation. A stem that sounds at the wrong frequency is thickened, drained, or allowed to split. Its empty shell remains among the living pipes and continues to participate after its metabolism has ended.

Lower down, where daylight has been filtered almost to extinction, grow the listeners.

They are black fronds no broader than a hand, arranged in spirals around warm seams in the ground-skin. Their surfaces absorb little light. Instead they attend to vibration: rain on the high canopy, pressure changes in distant reservoirs, fractures moving through mineralized supports. Each frond is veined with conductive fluid and edged by droplets held in precise intervals. The droplets tremble differently for different sounds. These tremors become pulses in the root mesh.

A grove of listeners can retain a storm.

Long after the rain has passed, its sequence persists as changing tensions in the fronds, then as mineral deposits laid down inside their bases. Months later the grove may reproduce some portion of the pattern—not as sound, but as a release of humidity, a darkening of nearby pool-skins, or an instruction sent upward for the canopy to close. Whether this is memory or merely prolonged response cannot be cleanly decided. Here the distinction may have no use.

There are also pale things that resemble fungi, though they are wholly of the same botanical ancestry as the rest. They inhabit the compressed layers beneath the visible surface. They have no leaves. They move by growing at one end and surrendering tissue at the other, flowing through the continent at the pace of cold wax. These underthreads carry salts, repair torn conduits, and connect regions whose surface growths appear unrelated. Where several threads meet, they form soft knots filled with metallic granules. Signals enter such a knot, circle within it, interfere with one another, and leave by different routes.

The knots are not brains. They are too numerous, too replaceable, and too widely dispersed. But decisions seem to accumulate around them.

A flooded region begins to sour. Thousands of kilometers away, a canopy fan narrows its stomata. Warmth is redirected through deep sapways. A mineral-rich seam is opened, and a population of cup-mats disassembles to spread across the troubled water. No command travels intact from a center. The correction emerges in fragments, each local act changing the conditions encountered by the next. Intelligence, if that is what it is, lives in the delay between them.

Above all of these are the great canopy makers.

No one of them can be seen entire. What looks like a trunk is a congress of trunks: braided columns fused so thoroughly that their old boundaries survive only as variations in grain. Some strands draw water, some bear weight, some carry electrical discharge, and some are already dead, retained as resonant chambers or mineral reinforcement. The columns divide far overhead into fans whose leaves may cover the area of lakes.

A canopy leaf is itself a landscape. Its upper surface bears low forests of moisture combs, reflective scales, transparent vents, and hairlike lightning rods. Channels migrate across it as tissues swell and relax. Along its margins, ice is grown deliberately on cold nights. The crystals extract particles from the mist, after which the leaf warms them, drinks the meltwater, and folds the captured material into new veins. Tiny surface organisms live there too, though “on” the leaf is misleading: they bud from it, perform specialized work, and are reabsorbed. They are organs that temporarily assume the shape and freedom of creatures.

During reproductive weather, the canopy makers loosen chains of transparent cells into the clouds. These are the nearest things the place has to migrants. They possess no wings and choose no direction, but they are not passive. Each chain changes charge in response to temperature and electric fields, climbing or descending through the cloud layers. It may join another chain, exchange segments, break apart, or incorporate fragments of free mineral lattice carrying patterns from an extinct grove. For weeks or months the chains circulate above the continent, becoming less like offspring of any parent and more like negotiations among distant regions.

They return in rain.

Most are opened and read by the first leaf they touch. Some are dismantled for nutrients. Some are passed downward through successive membranes. A very few reach damaged tissue and begin an unfamiliar kind of growth: a new pigment, a revised vessel wall, a different rhythm for releasing heat. Thus heredity falls everywhere, and descent belongs as much to weather as to lineage.

At the margins dwell the hangers, immense curtains of root tissue suspended into cloud. They have no stable distinction between root and leaf. Their outer fibers drink mist and light; their inner cords carry the continent’s weight and accumulate mineral bone. Long translucent bladders swell among them, filled with water under tremendous pressure. When winds pull the curtains outward, the bladders contract in sequence and draw them back, a movement so slow that it resembles a change in landscape rather than an act.

The hangers are the first to encounter emptiness. They respond violently to it.

When a mineral bud tears loose and falls, wounded fibers release a signal through water, electricity, and sound. Nearby roots branch into the gap. Airborne threads adhere to the new surfaces. Bladders empty to wet the exposed tissues. Black fronds orient toward the altered resonance. Even far inland, pool-skins tighten as pressure is diverted toward the margin. The entire living plateau participates in closing the loss.

Nothing appears to grieve, yet the response has the scale and unity of grief.

Death among these beings is rarely an ending that preserves an individual outline. A failing stem is entered by its neighbors. Soluble tissues are withdrawn. Stored patterns are copied imperfectly into younger mineral lattices. The remaining shell becomes support, channel, resonator, or nursery. A dead canopy fan may continue to alter the weather for centuries because air still passes through its veins and living growth still opens and closes around it.

Consequently the oldest inhabitants are not organisms but continuities: a chord maintained through successive hollow trunks; a branching geometry copied from tissue to tissue; a storm pattern stored first in droplets, then in root tension, then in crystal; a route by which water has traveled despite every vessel along it having been replaced.

These continuities migrate without moving. They reproduce without bodies. They can outlive forests.

It may be that the plateau contains innumerable species woven into compulsory intimacy. It may instead be one organism with countless temporary anatomies. Both descriptions fail at certain scales. A cup-mat can reject another mat. A root knot can isolate diseased tissue. One canopy region can deprive another of light or minerals for decades. There is competition, error, parasitism, and something like rebellion: tissues that hoard electrical charge, vascular lines that reroute water for their own expansion, reproductive chains that return bearing instructions the receiving leaves destroy.

But even these conflicts occur inside a shared circulation. There is no outsider to eat the rebel growth, no animal to carry it away, no bare earth into which it can escape. Victors and failures alike are eventually inherited by the same wet fabric.

So what lives there is not peaceful, although it has no predators. It is crowded with negotiations over light, pressure, temperature, dissolved metal, structural weight, memory, and rain. Its dramas are slow enough to look like climate and large enough to look like geography.

Perhaps the closest thing to a person in that place is a temporary alignment.

A pressure wave enters from the western margin. Listener groves translate it into electrical pulses. Root knots compare those pulses with old mineral patterns. Reservoir skins release stored heat. High leaves alter their charge, and the cloud ceiling thickens along a branching line. For several minutes, systems separated by vast distances act as one coherent body. The marsh darkens. Hollow trunks fall silent at once. Droplets lift from the fronds and hang motionless.

In that interval, something exists that did not exist before and will not remain afterward: not a mind located anywhere, but an act of attention distributed across weather, water, and living tissue.

Then the discharge comes. The alignment dissolves into local processes. The canopy resumes its surf. Stems knock together. Pools breathe through their dark membranes.

Whoever was there has become a continent again.
