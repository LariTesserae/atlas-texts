At first there seem to be no people in the basin.

There are human proportions everywhere: the height of steps, the reach between handholds, the shallow depressions worn—or fashioned to resemble wear—into the edges of pools. Cups stand where a hand would set them down. Hammocks sag between trees. Fruit has been opened with neat thumbnail cuts. Yet no figure crosses the plazas, and no voice answers from the colonnades.

Only after long attention do the inhabitants separate themselves from the work around them.

One may be standing beneath a shade frame, indistinguishable at first from its bundled posts. Another sits knee-deep in a channel, its back resembling a stack of wet tiles. A third lies along a branch above an orchard path, narrow as a vine and clothed in leaves that may actually grow from its skin. They do not hide. Rather, the basin has made no strong distinction between a body and a built thing, and the eye must learn the difference.

From a distance they appear human: slight, long-limbed people with dark, rain-bright skin. Nearer, each proves to have been made by a different craft.

Some have smooth clay faces, fired the color of honey or ash, with lashes inserted one by one through pierced lids. Their cheeks bear the shallow whorls of the fingers that shaped them. Hair emerges as black fiber cord, braided with seeds and fragments of shell. At the joints, where flesh should fold, there are careful lashings kept perpetually damp.

Others seem grown rather than modeled. Their ribs are pale roots trained around hollow cavities. Their skin has the fine veins of a broad leaf, though it is warm and bruises under pressure. Small flowers open in the hollows of their throats when they breathe. If one cuts such an inhabitant—and they do sometimes cut themselves while working—the wound reveals alternating layers of wood, red flesh, woven matting, and painted ceramic. Clear sap wells up but does not fall. When the injury is no longer watched, it closes without scar.

A few are almost entirely ordinary until they turn. Then the glazed pattern of a pool can be seen continuing across the back of the neck, or one arm is discovered to be only a flat image that acquires thickness when extended. Some possess hands on both sides of their wrists. Some have no visible mouths until food is offered. One old-looking woman has a small doorway in the center of her chest. Through it can be glimpsed a humid grove much larger than her body, where three bright monkeys move soundlessly among blue-white leaves.

No two are made alike, but all bear the same sign somewhere upon them: three overlapping handprints, small enough to belong to children. The prints may be pressed into a shoulder blade, painted under the tongue, scarred into a heel, or hidden among the ridges of a fingerprint. They correspond to no hands anyone has found.

They are not numerous. One may walk half a day and meet only a single figure carrying reeds across a plaza. No more than seven are ever visible together, and gatherings of that size occur only beside deep pools or at the branch frames in the central field. Usually they keep distances resembling those of the animals: one person to a pavilion, one to a grove, one to a long reach of wall.

They have houses, but do not sleep in them. Their dwellings are small arrangements of useful boundaries: three walls around an empty patch of light, a woven roof suspended over water, a doorway with no adjoining chamber. Within are combs, bowls, sleeping mats, painted toys, baskets of fruit, and changes of clothing. Everything suggests domestic life except the life itself. An inhabitant may enter through the doorway and later be found miles away emerging from beneath a stair. Beds remain smooth. Meals remain newly served.

Whether they sleep at all is uncertain. At intervals they stop moving and place one palm against a wall. Their eyes stay open, but their pupils fill with tiny white skies. During this stillness, insects follow the lines of their bodies as if using them for roads. Several hours later—or immediately; duration is difficult to establish around them—they take away the hand and continue whatever they were doing.

Their work is constant, exact, and apparently unnecessary.

They retie sound lashings. They polish tiles already spotless. They lift fallen fruit onto branches, where the stems accept it. They move water from full pools into other full pools with shallow shell bowls. At the condensation ridges they touch each hanging droplet in sequence, never dislodging one. They trace names on every seventh brick of certain walls, though their fingers leave no marks until the writing is complete.

None is ever seen beginning a task. None finishes one. Still, the work is not futile in their understanding. They approach it with the grave concentration of healers.

Their tools are the same simple tools left throughout the basin, but an implement behaves differently in their hands. A shell adze does not cut by sharpness; it persuades wood to remember that it has been divided. A plumb weight hangs toward whichever surface the worker has chosen as “down.” A bone needle may draw a cord through brick, shadow, flowing water, or the flank of a living animal without injury. When a tool breaks, the inhabitant wraps its pieces in leaves and places them under a stair. Later the tool is found in its old position, whole, while the leaf bundle contains a tiny clay picture of the break.

They rarely speak aloud. When they do, their voices resemble the sounds of the basin: a wet click, a gutter’s murmur, the ticking inside a sun-warmed wall. Their language is chiefly made of placement. A red fruit on a black tile may mean danger, mourning, welcome, or that a distance has become longer. Three reeds laid across a threshold ask whether the person beyond it remembers the same event. An overturned cup is an apology. A cup upright but filled with leaves means the apology has been accepted but not erased.

They also speak with shadows. Their own shadows do not always imitate them. An inhabitant may stand motionless while its shadow kneels, points, or covers its face. Two people meeting across a plaza often remain far apart while their shadows approach and touch hands. A person whose shadow has gone elsewhere becomes distracted and incomplete. They misplace tools, walk into pools, and sometimes forget which of their limbs possess depth.

The bodyless bird-shadows in the central field are treated with particular tenderness. Inhabitants bring them seeds that cannot be eaten and construct little perches by arranging strips of darkness cast from other objects. Sometimes a bird-shadow follows one of them away. When this happens, the person acquires a faint internal birdsong—not audible, but visible as a trembling pattern under the skin of the throat.

They eat, though hunger does not seem to weaken them. They favor split purple figs, bitter leaves, fish-shaped cakes of pressed seed, and the translucent gourds full of water. Before eating, they press a portion against the pavement. The offered food passes slowly into the glaze, becoming part of its painted design. Afterward the meal is whole again, but its eater remembers the taste.

This matters greatly to them. Memory is the one thing the basin does not restore.

Each inhabitant carries a private accumulation of events that no longer have evidence. They remember branches that are now uncut, companions who died and stepped again from mosaics, walls dismantled and found standing, journeys whose routes have ceased to exist. The oldest among them are not physically old; age appears instead as density. Their bodies contain more layers. Paint shows beneath skin, older eyes behind present eyes, former gestures faintly repeating under each current movement.

Such a person may remember meeting a traveler whom the traveler has not yet become.

Their names are not fixed. Because names can make walls watertight and hold lintels aloft, a permanent name would bind its bearer too strongly to the architecture. They therefore borrow names from temporary conditions: Condensation-on-the-North-Face, Three-Figs-Opened, Bird-Under-Reed, Black-Path-Returning. A name is kept until the condition changes. Some names last moments. Others, in a place where the last drop never falls, have endured beyond counting.

Birth among them is neither common nor clearly birth.

Sometimes three inhabitants gather around an empty sleeping mat and weave a small garment. They leave it beneath fruit trees with a clay cup and a tool. Later a child may be seen elsewhere in the basin, already walking, carrying the tool but wearing a different garment. No one claims to have made it. The child knows several paths, the rules for approaching water, and the names of animals, but has no memories. For this reason children are considered startling and precious. The landscape is full of freshness, but only a child is truly new.

They do not grow in a steady way. A child may remain small through many meetings, then pass behind a wall and emerge long-limbed, with years of memory acquired from some unseen courtyard. Another may become younger after descending a black-tiled path. Kinship is therefore determined not by age or resemblance but by shared recollection. Two people are family if each remembers an event involving the other. If only one remembers, the relationship is called a one-sided bridge and is observed with a mixture of devotion and grief.

Death does not remove them, but neither is it harmless.

When an inhabitant dies, the body flattens into the nearest decorated surface like any other animal. Somewhere else, often much later, the same figure steps out of a mosaic, a painted wall, or the image held inside a droplet. The restored person is bodily complete and remembers life up to the moment when no living eye last rested on them. What happened between death and return is missing.

Their companions remember all of it.

Thus mourning concerns not absence but asymmetry. The dead return to friends who have wept for them, carried them, spoken over them, and waited beside the image into which they vanished. They cannot remember receiving that love. Mourners recount it through arrangements of fruit, reeds, tools, and cups, trying to build an event sturdy enough to survive without evidence. Sometimes the returned person believes them. Sometimes not.

They show caution toward travelers, especially those who leave footprints in memory even when the ground refuses them. An inhabitant may follow at a distance for hours, never looking directly at the newcomer. Gifts appear: a cool gourd on a hot stair, a black beetle placed belly-up to indicate a deceptive path, three loops of fiber warning that a roof must not be counted.

If trust develops, the inhabitant asks a single question, usually by placing reeds across a threshold:

**What do you remember that this place denies?**

The answer is listened to with complete attention.

They do not know whether they built the basin. Some believe the makers departed before anything began. Some believe the makers are beneath the courtyards, endlessly supporting the white sky with their raised hands. Others say the basin made its own builders afterward, as a cup might invent the potter whose fingerprints it bears.

A quieter belief holds that every inhabitant is one of the makers, restored too many times to remember the work.

They avoid the summit pool on the brick hill, although they tend its reeds and feed its frogs. When they look into the warm black water, each sees a different underside: lashings cut through, supports unfinished, roots seeking gaps that have not yet been designed. Occasionally one sees a pair of hands working there. The hands are always crude, scarred, and unmistakably their own.

Then the inhabitant returns to the terraces, takes up a shell adze or wooden mallet, and resumes a task that was waiting before they arrived.

They are not guardians in the usual sense. The basin needs no protection, and damage cannot endure. Nor are they prisoners, because there are passages everywhere, and some have gone through the branch doorways into the green countries beyond.

Most eventually return.

They say those other worlds contain weather that proceeds, wounds that scar, roads that become shorter as one walks them, and children who grow by remaining continuously themselves. They speak of fallen leaves gathering under trees and of buildings becoming ruins. Such things fascinate and terrify them.

Here, nothing can acquire a past except the living.

So they remain among the bright tiles and instructed waters, carrying within their handmade bodies everything the basin will not keep: every meeting, every loss, every alteration, every unfinished act of love. The place is eternally new around them. They alone become ancient.
