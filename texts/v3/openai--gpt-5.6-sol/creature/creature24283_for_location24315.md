At first, what lives there is easily mistaken for discoloration.

It occupies the country as a collection of stains: a grayness in the joins, an olive ring around retained water, a black-green line under the lip of a slab. Even where it rises above the surface, it does so reluctantly. There are no crowns, no broad leaves, no upright bodies conspicuous against the horizon. Life keeps close to the fabricated ground, as though the glare has pressed it flat.

The oldest inhabitants are films.

They survive in the shallow courts and in the narrow damp beneath imperfect seams, drying to skins so thin they seem painted onto the mineral. In drought they are brown, charcoal, or nearly invisible. Their cells are sealed within layers of sugar, salt, and shed material, packed together under a translucent glaze. They may remain in that condition through hundreds of hot days. Dust buries them. Salt crystals grow through them. The ceramic below expands and contracts. Nothing about the dry film suggests that it is waiting.

Then rain arrives.

Within minutes the dark margins soften. Trapped gases stipple the film with bright beads. Olive spreads through the brown, first in branching threads and then in broadening sheets. Where the water is shallow enough for light to reach the bottom, the entire basin floor takes on a muted green shine. The change has no visible center. It occurs everywhere at once, wherever dormant cells have been wetted.

These films are not one species. They are layered communities whose members tolerate one another because the plain allows few alternatives. The upper cells endure light and heat, screening what lies below with dark pigments. Under them live slower organisms that feed on dissolved mineral salts and the remains of the upper layer. Colorless threads pass between both, binding dust grains into a flexible crust. Still deeper, in oxygen-poor cracks, other cells wake only after water has stood for several days. They release tiny bubbles that tremble under the film and burst without sound.

When the pools retreat, the community retreats in place. It does not crawl after the water. Instead, each receding margin leaves behind a band of drying life. A single basin may acquire hundreds of concentric contours: green at the water’s edge, black farther out, then rust, then the pale gray of older seasons. The rings are both colony and calendar.

Along the drainage joins grow tougher forms.

From a distance they resemble clumps of wiry grass. Up close, their blades are not soft or uniformly green. They are narrow, ridged, and mineral-dusted, with surfaces the color of sage, ash, or oxidized copper. Most stand no higher than a hand. Their tips bleach and split in the hottest months, but the living tissue is buried near the base, protected among overlapping sheaths.

Below each clump is far more organism than appears above. Bundles of roots follow the engineered seam downward, flattening themselves where the gap narrows and branching again when they reach loose fill. They do not seek soil, because there is almost none. They live among ceramic grit, compacted clay, windblown dust, and the residues of dead films. Their finest roots enter pores too small to admit a thread. They release mild acids against the manufactured stone and draw out traces of iron, potassium, and phosphorus. Around them, pale fungi carry moisture from deeper cracks and receive sugars in return.

The fungi are the plain’s hidden inhabitants.

Their filaments line the undersides of plates and spread through the cool darkness of buried joins. They are milk-white when newly grown, then amber, then the color of old paper. In some places they form dense felts that hold a seamful of damp long after the surface has dried. Elsewhere they occur as isolated cords, each no thicker than a hair, crossing cavities in search of another patch of organic debris.

They have no great underground intelligence and do not connect the whole country. Heat breaks their networks. Salt kills portions of them. Shifting plates tear them apart nightly. Yet fragmentation does not always mean death. A severed cord may seal itself, remain dormant, and begin again when condensation returns. Thus the minute settlement of the ground proceeds without travel in the animal sense. The plain moves the inhabitants by cracking beneath them.

On shaded faces of pylons are low cushions composed of innumerable short shoots. Their color changes with the hour. Before dawn, when condensation fills them, they are dark green and almost plush. By noon they have drawn inward, exposing a surface of overlapping silver scales. The scales reflect heat and trap a layer of still air. Seen at midday, a living cushion may look like nothing more than a deposit of pale mineral.

Each cushion grows around a dependable drip. It thickens most on the lower side of a groove, where water pauses before falling. Over many seasons it alters the path of the droplets that sustain it. Water is caught by the cushion’s upper edge, divided among its folds, and delivered toward the center. Dead tissue accumulates underneath. Dust adheres to the dead tissue. Slowly, the plant constructs a little pocket of dark material on the face of the pylon.

Some cushions become too heavy. They peel away during rain and fall in sodden fragments to the basin below. Most fragments dry and perish. A few lodge in another crack, and in this way the apparently rooted life of the pylons achieves a slow, accidental migration.

The most conspicuous inhabitants appear only after storms.

They begin as a green haze along the newly flooded courts. Then thousands of slender stalks push upward through the water. They are jointless, hollow, and nearly transparent at first. In several days they darken to red-brown or violet-black, standing so densely in favored places that they make low bands visible from distant ramps.

They do not flower. At the tip of each stalk, a sealed case matures. Heat and drying tension eventually split the case along precise lines. Dust-fine reproductive cells pour out, some floating on the last water, others lifted by the warm air. They settle across enormous distances, though almost all land where they cannot grow. Their abundance compensates for the plain’s scarcity of refuge.

The stalks live quickly because the lakes do. Their whole exposed existence may occupy less than two weeks. As the water vanishes, they sag into interlaced mats. Fungi enter them. Salt whitens them. By the next dry season they have become brittle fibers lying exactly where they fell, untouched by mouths or feet. The next flood presses these remains into the basin silt, adding a layer scarcely thicker than paper.

There are also inhabitants that live almost entirely inside stone.

They occupy pores in the ceramic fragments and microscopic flaws in the binding material. Some use sunlight penetrating the outermost translucent grains. Others feed on iron-bearing compounds and leave rusty halos around their colonies. Their growth is so slow that a patch may advance only the width of a fingernail in many years. Yet they are among the first to colonize a fresh break.

A newly fallen block exposes bands of red, cream, and charcoal. For a time the fracture is clean. Then airborne cells settle. On the cooler cream layers, a greenish shadow appears. The charcoal layers develop a dull sheen. Where dew forms, black pinpoints collect into branching figures. The figures follow mineral boundaries so exactly that they resemble a continuation of the block’s manufacture, but they are alive.

No single inhabitant owns the plain. There are only overlapping tenures of moisture.

A basin film may flourish for a week and remain dormant for decades. A seam-clump may endure for centuries while growing no broader than a stool. A fungal network may spread beneath a plate for years and be destroyed in an instant when the slab shifts. Colonies on the east face of a pylon live by dawn condensation; colonies a few meters away on the west face live by the rare slant of rain. To cross that short distance is, for them, to cross a desert.

Their competition is slow but severe. A cushion may divert a drip and dry the film below it. Roots may consume the stored damp in a seam before neighboring spores awaken. One microbial skin may seal a porous surface so effectively that another suffocates beneath it. Because nothing grazes them back, successful patches often persist until they poison or bury themselves. Salts accumulate. Dead layers shut out light. The center of an old colony becomes barren while growth continues around its edge, leaving dark rings and crescents across the orange ground.

Nothing tends these living places. Nothing carries their spores deliberately, prunes their dead tissue, or opens their crowded growth. Their world is without partnership from anything that walks, swims, or flies. They depend instead on impersonal carriers: runoff, dust devils, collapsing crusts, the flexing of plates, and the explosive release of tension from drying cases.

Even so, from a sufficient height all these separate lives might appear to be parts of one dispersed being.

Its green organs occupy the catchments. Its gray organs cling to the pylons. Its buried white threads inhabit the joints. It wakes wherever the water has gone and sleeps wherever the heat has returned. It has no center and no awareness; storms connect one portion of it to another only briefly. But year by year it traces the design of the plain more clearly than dust alone could do.

The constructed country determines where life can begin. Life, in turn, begins the long work of making the construction less exact.

Roots widen the joins. Films trap silt. Fungi weaken buried surfaces. Dead stalks create the first thin organic layers in the courts. Cushions turn condensation channels into pockets of primitive soil. Every inhabitant is small, but each retains something that would otherwise be lost: a droplet, a grain, a dissolved salt, a fragment of another life.

They are not yet a forest, meadow, or marsh. They are the conditions from which such things might someday arise.

For now, they remain close against the immense warm floor—quiet, intermittent, and patient enough to live at the speed of weather.
