At first, nothing there appears to live.

The dark plates beside the condensation hollows seem no different from the basalt pavement: low mineral lumps, reddish at the edges, dulled by ages of warm rain. Some carry pale bands like deposits left by evaporating brine. Others are black and faintly metallic. Dust rests on them in the same stationary ripples that cover the surrounding stone.

Only after long observation does one of them cease to be a rock.

Its shell lifts—not enough to reveal the body, only enough to admit a thin darkness beneath the rim. The movement is so slight that it may first be mistaken for a change in shadow. At once the hum around it alters. A high tone diminishes; a lower one becomes audible through the soles of the feet. Several droplets clinging to the nearby quartz face lengthen toward the creature before falling.

Beneath the shell is a crowded fringe of legs, finer than wires and nearly transparent. There may be hundreds. They do not step in any familiar sequence. Some touch wet stone, some dry stone, some the metallic seams crossing both. The creature spreads itself across these differences as an animal elsewhere might spread its jaws over food.

It eats no substance. It completes a circuit.

Its upper shell is built from iron compounds, silica, and dark conductive crystals gathered from the water without excavation. The layers are not carried as armor so much as grown through the tissues, each plate continuous with a different internal membrane. From above, the shell is a single broad shield. Within, it is a stack of unequal conductors separated by living films. Charge enters through the legs on one side, passes through slow chemical gradients in the body, and leaves through the other.

This passage is enough to sustain it.

There is no heartbeat in the ordinary sense. Instead, bands inside the shell alternately bind and release ions in time with the deep pulses of the basin. Its fluids move because the ground moves electrically. Its respiration is not an exchange with the air but a reversible staining of its own internal metals. For part of the cycle they darken; for the next they clear. What little energy remains is spent maintaining the membranes between them.

The creature has no head that can be distinguished from its rear. Around the entire underside runs a ring of sensory pits responsive to moisture, voltage, pressure, and vibration. Light matters little. Sound, as a separate phenomenon, may not exist for it at all. The basin’s roar is perceived as a total arrangement of stresses passing through shell, limbs, and stone. A changed overtone means a drying seam. A minute phase difference means that a brine film has formed nearby. The distant fall of one regularly timed droplet is as definite to it as sunrise might be to another animal.

Its life consists largely of holding the correct posture.

A leg may remain against the same crystal grain for decades. A narrow fan of tissue may rest in one wet fissure until the fissure dries centuries later. Adjustments pass around the body slowly: one filament curls, another extends, the shell settles by the width of a dust grain. These motions are not hesitation. The animal has no need to be quicker. Nothing pursues it, and its food does not escape.

When a condensation pocket begins to fail, the creature detects the loss long before the last water vanishes. Then it migrates.

The shell rises on its concealed multitude of legs. What looked heavy proves almost weightless, much of it a porous mineral foam stiffened by metallic ribs. The animal slides rather than walks, keeping several limbs in contact with the ground at all times. Its passage may cover only a few handspans in a year. Behind it remains a shallow line where the dust’s standing-wave pattern has been interrupted.

That line is the nearest thing the basin has to a path.

It does not point toward any visible destination. The traveler follows an electrical slope too faint and too extensive to be measured locally, curving around quartz seams, pausing at damp stains, sometimes reversing a portion of its own route when the underlying current changes phase. The journey may last longer than the life of a forest. At its end, the creature lowers itself beside another dependable bead of water and becomes a nodule again.

Whether it remembers the journey is difficult to say. It has no centralized brain. Its conductive plates retain slight differences in composition, and its membranes become more permeable along pathways repeatedly used. Experience may exist in it as an alteration of resistance: not an image of where it has been, but an increased ease with which certain charges pass. Yet the basin slowly reverses even these changes. Given enough time, the animal’s internal history is discharged back into the ground.

Two of the plated creatures almost never meet. When they do, they show no alarm and no recognition. Their outer legs overlap. Current divides between them. For a while they function as one broader bridge across the stone.

During this contact, minute packets of layered tissue sometimes pass from the underside of one shell to the other. No fertilization follows. Each packet contains alternating mineral membranes arranged in an incomplete sequence. The recipient contributes the missing layers, then releases the finished packet into a wet crack.

There it remains.

Sediment gathers around it and is later carried away. Iron darkens it, then clears. Silica enters its outer membrane molecule by molecule. For hundreds or thousands of years it may be impossible to distinguish from precipitate. Eventually, if the local voltage and moisture remain within a narrow balance, fine legs unfold from one edge. The young creature is no larger than a flake of rust. It begins by bridging the distance between adjacent mineral grains.

Growth is almost imperceptible and may never truly end. Nor is death easy to locate. A damaged animal can cease coordinating its membranes while portions of it continue conducting. The shell separates into plates; the plates become part of the pavement; living films survive in damp pores and may assemble new layers later. What died, and when, has no single answer.

The brine pockets contain a second kind of resident, though “resident” may grant it too much individuality.

In clear water under the overhangs hang colorless threads, visible only when they turn the light. Each is thinner than hair and divided at intervals by metallic knots. They neither swim nor anchor themselves. They drift into parallel arrangements determined by the basin’s charge, forming loose veils in which no thread can be called the first or central one.

Dissolved iron, copper, sodium, and other ions collect along them. For a time the threads build precise lattices: branching grids, hexagonal cages, long repeating chains of crystal too delicate to survive in air. As the local current reverses, the same threads release what they gathered. The lattices dissolve in the order opposite to their formation.

Nothing is gained. Nothing accumulates.

A thread can divide, but division does not increase the amount of thread in the pool. One portion lengthens while another shortens. If two touch, they may merge without leaving a seam. Their chemistry is active, responsive, and self-restoring, yet they do not compete for the available metals. The pocket always contains approximately the same quantity of them, distributed through different forms.

Perhaps they are colonies. Perhaps each pool holds one organism repeatedly taking itself apart. Perhaps the distinction has no use there.

The plated creatures sometimes lower their filament legs into these pockets. The transparent threads gather around the limbs and align with them. Metals pass from water to animal and from animal back to water. Neither consumes the other. For several cycles they become a single conductive structure, after which the shell withdraws and the threads resume their suspended geometry.

These are the basin’s inhabitants: lives with almost no appetite, no haste, and little separation from the processes sustaining them. They do not oppose the landscape’s equilibrium. They participate in it so completely that their bodies resemble temporary details of geology.

Nothing hunts because nothing flees. Nothing decays because every dissolution is also a preparation. There are no calls beneath the roar, no courtship displays, no territorial marks. The creatures need not announce themselves to a world in which every useful signal is already present as voltage, pressure, moisture, and tone.

To an observer, they may seem impoverished—animals reduced to endurance, organisms denied event. Yet their stillness is not sleep. Beneath every shell, innumerable membranes sort and release ions. Along every thread, crystals appear and vanish. Each inhabitant is busy at the same immense task as the basin itself: allowing motion to continue without permitting it to become change.

They live there not as visitors on the stone, but as small places where the stone’s circulation has learned to hold a shape.
