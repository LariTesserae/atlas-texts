What lives here is not a population in the usual sense. There are no bodies quick enough to startle, no eyes reflecting the maintenance lamps, no hidden mouths. The district is inhabited by a green continuity: innumerable plants living so closely and repeating one another so thoroughly that they have ceased to resemble a collection.

At first it seems that the figs are the principal inhabitants. Their roots enter from above as narrow cords, pallid where they have grown in darkness, then darken and divide in the cistern air. They descend along pillars, cross arches, and gather around pipe joints in dense wooden fists. Some reach the sediment of the lower tanks. Others never find earth at all. They live clasped to brick, feeding on seepage and the thin material accumulated in mortar seams. Their trunks are distorted by the district’s geometry—flat where pressed against walls, forked around conduits, ribbed by cable trays they have swallowed but not displaced. They bear occasional fruit, small and sparse. It ripens unvisited, bruises itself in falling, and becomes part of the floor.

Yet the figs are less rulers than scaffolds. Maidenhair ferns rise from the wet pockets between their roots, each black stem holding a delicate fan of leaflets above the machinery. Larger ferns crowd the warmer chambers. Their fronds arch over walkways and valve platforms, uncurling slowly in the amber light, then remaining extended for months. When they shed spores, the air takes them. Fan drafts carry them through grilles and along pipe galleries; pump vibration loosens them from undersides; condensation catches them against walls. Thus the ferns migrate without footsteps, appearing far from their parent growth in the rust hollow of a cabinet or the recessed numeral of a sector door.

Moss lives nearer the surfaces and may be the district’s most complete occupant. It has no need to span a chamber. It accepts each bolt head, stair edge, and mortar joint as a sufficient world. Around the hottest pipes it forms dark, close cushions. In cooler seepage it lengthens into loose green fur. It fills the letters on warning signs until the words stand out as darker growth. It rounds the teeth of corroded gears and makes soft islands of abandoned tools. Where iron flakes away beneath it, the moss settles lower without losing its shape, as though the object were slowly passing through the colony.

Within and below the moss are liverworts, algal skins, molds, and pale fungal threads. They inhabit scales too small to alter the district’s silhouette, but they perform much of its dissolution. Fungi enter fallen stems without visible ceremony. Since no animal cuts or grinds the dead tissue, decay begins from the wet interior. A branch remains outwardly whole while its center turns dark and soft; then one day it folds under the weight of a drop or a new root. Fungal filaments bind the collapse into the surrounding litter. They make soil where there is no ground.

The flowering plants are stranger here. Some are remnants of deliberate cultivation: old lines of reeds in rectangular beds, surviving herbs beneath broken labels, pale descendants of ornamentals once selected for color no lamp now reveals properly. They bloom according to warmth, internal rhythm, or the schedules of lighting circuits. Their flowers open perfectly into the vacant air.

When the weekly carriage moves, it comes to them like weather.

The rail shudders first. Condensation drops from it in a brief artificial rain. Then the carriage advances, its soft paddles passing through the rows at the speed of a growing shadow. Blossoms incline beneath the fibers. Pollen lifts and settles. Stems bend, touch neighboring stems, and rise again after the machine has gone. Whatever plants still depend upon this passage live in a narrow partnership with a device that does not know they are there. Their reproductive season is not attended by wings but by a corroded motor, limit switches, and the buried persistence of a municipal calendar.

Many such partnerships have already failed. Empty bed frames remain where more demanding species once grew. Their irrigation nozzles still mist at intervals, watering moss and volunteer reeds. Their labels survive longer than they did. Other plants have escaped the intended beds and become structural. Willows rise where cut branches lodged in drain silt. Their young shoots are yellow-green and almost translucent; their older roots travel laterally beneath paving, levering up brick courses in long patient waves. Reeds form walls wherever the water is shallow enough. Their rhizomes penetrate filter gravel and knit whole beds together. When dry stalks fall, they are not carried off or consumed. They lie among the new stems until damp and fungus reduce them to a fibrous platform, raising the colony by fractions.

In the black tanks, duckweed lives as a surface several leaves thick. The uppermost layer faces the weak lamps and gray shafts. Beneath it are shaded leaves, yellowing leaves, drowned leaves, and a suspended felt of roots. The raft closes after every disturbance. A falling drop opens a dark round eye in it; within moments the leaves drift back together. When a sluice gate lifts and the water level changes, broad portions of the colony slide over one another, wrinkle against the tank walls, or pour in green ribbons across a weir.

Nothing swims below them. The roots hang in stillness through tannin-dark water, accompanied only by rising gas bubbles and the remote tremor of pumps. The tank is inhabited all the way down, but not by anything that pursues or flees. Algae occupy the submerged masonry. Bacterial films work within the sludge. Roots enter and branch into finer roots. Life occurs as layers, gradients, and slow chemical exchanges.

The machines also seem to live here, though differently and perhaps only because every living thing has incorporated their behavior. Pumps take turns sleeping and laboring. Valves wake to rotate by a few degrees. Fans continue with damaged rhythms, striking once per revolution like enormous imperfect hearts. Sensors buried behind mineral crusts still report pressure and depth. A float rises in a dark chamber; elsewhere a relay closes; a motor begins. The response may travel through walls and water before becoming visible as a trembling pipe or a current under duckweed.

Plants know these actions without understanding them. Roots thicken where a pipe repeatedly warms. Moss retreats from a casing that vibrates too violently and flourishes along the quieter bolts. Reeds lean with the periodic pull of overflow. Ferns around ventilation grilles orient themselves to the faint reliable breath. Colonies establish around scheduled leaks, timed mists, and the condensation produced during a pump’s working cycle. What began as machinery serving cultivation has become climate, season, and terrain.

Conversely, the machinery now depends upon the plants. Root mats seal cracks that would otherwise widen. Reeds hold sediment out of intakes. Moss keeps surfaces wet but protects them from abrupt changes in temperature. Vegetation slows runoff enough for old drains to accept it. The arrangement is not peaceful: roots crush conduits, fronds foul sensors, rhizomes lift paving, and algae narrow channels. But each injury changes the flow, and the growth follows the change. Over decades, opposition has become interlocking habit.

No single plant possesses the district. Even the largest fig is only a temporary concentration in the larger fabric. Its upper roots share water with moss; its bark holds ferns; its fallen leaves feed fungi; its lower fibers slow silt that supports reeds. If it dies, it will remain standing for years, propped by pipes and neighboring roots, becoming wetter and more inhabited as its own life recedes. The vacancy inside it will immediately fill.

This may be the true resident: not fig, fern, reed, algae, fungus, or machine, but the accommodation among them. It has no center corresponding to a brain and no boundary finer than the sealed gates. Rain reaches it through brick. Heat reaches it through mains. Electricity enters in armored cables and emerges as light, rotation, vibration, and the weekly stroke of brushes. In return, the district retains water, catches mineral silt, exhales damp air, and presses roots into every weakness available to it.

Its movements are distributed. A valve turns. A frond uncurls. A mineral lip thickens around a leak. Duckweed closes over disturbed water. A willow root widens beneath a street. None of these actions answers another immediately, yet over years they compose something like behavior. The district gathers around water. It braces what shelters it. It obstructs excessive flow and redirects what it cannot stop. It occupies damage before damage can become emptiness.

It does not hunt, mate, call, or watch. It has no silence in which to listen. Its form of presence is pressure: root against brick, moss against iron, water against gates, warm air held beneath arches. Its form of memory is physical. Every former leak is recorded by a vertical seam of growth. Every period of flooding leaves a line of silt. Every long-running motor is surrounded by plants suited to its heat. The oldest events have become indistinguishable from architecture.

Whoever lives there is therefore both crowded and solitary. Countless organisms touch, overlap, grow through one another, and exchange the products of decay, but none encounters an animal presence. Flowers offer themselves to mechanisms. Fruit falls without being eaten. Leaves complete their shapes without ever being bitten. The plants inhabit a world in which nothing approaches them except water, air, machinery, fungus, and other plants.

They have filled that world so completely that absence itself has become overgrown.
