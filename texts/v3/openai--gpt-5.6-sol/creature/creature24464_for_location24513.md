What lives there is difficult to separate from where it lives.

Near the mouth of the crack, there are algae. They are the city’s only uncomplicated life: green threads fixed to mineral shelves, combed downward by the entering water. They feed on a light that has never crossed the sky. Their cells divide, their filaments lengthen, and now and then a strand tears loose and is carried into the illuminated depths.

It does not survive long.

The lower city takes it apart gently. Filters catch the filament without clogging. Membranes draw out phosphates, sugars, metals, and traces of trapped gas. The green fades first to yellow, then to translucence. Within hours nothing recognizable remains. Its substance enters cooling films, ceramic glazes, catalytic beds. Perhaps a fraction returns eventually to the upper fissure, where another thread of algae incorporates it and grows.

The glassy films farther down are less easily named. They spread, retreat, and heal after damage. They use light. They alter themselves in response to salinity. Yet they have no cells, no ancestry that can be traced, and no visible means of reproduction. A square of film may divide into two working surfaces, but this resembles the partition of a reservoir more than the birth of offspring. When scraped away, it reforms from dissolved minerals according to instructions carried in the surrounding pressure.

If it is alive, it is alive only as fire might be said to live.

Nothing else swims.

The empty chambers are not homes awaiting bodies. Their changes of volume are their occupants. A chamber contracts, and that contraction passes into the next chamber as a pressure word. Rows of them pulse in careful sequence. Some hold a state for years. Others oscillate many thousands of times between one raindrop and the next. Together they perform operations for which there are no convenient names: measuring the stone’s unreal interior, balancing incompatible depths, comparing the nested cities, perhaps deciding where another street can be allowed to exist.

Thus the nearest equivalent to a population is a population of conditions.

A warm current inhabits a conduit until a colder one displaces it. A resonance occupies a tower. A difference in salt concentration settles across a plaza and persists there like a territorial creature. Quiet zones move through the avenues with the apparent deliberation of grazing animals, consuming vibration along one edge and releasing it behind them. They have routes, though no path is marked. They avoid certain resonators. At every contraction they gather around the deeper basin, overlapping without disturbing one another, and then disperse into the machinery.

The city recognizes them. Doors that are not doors open for them. Shutters turn. Vanes incline. Fields strengthen or vanish at their approach. Whether the conditions command the mechanisms or the mechanisms shepherd the conditions cannot be determined. Cause circulates too quickly.

The transparent bells in the lowest district come closest to seeming watchful.

They hang in clusters from ribs of black metal, each one clear as thick ice. Within them, inscriptions drift and regroup: strokes finer than bacteria, branching figures, chains of bright punctuation. A bell flares when rain strikes the outer stone. Another answers from farther down. Sometimes thousands illuminate together, and for an instant their internal marks align into the image of a coastline—not necessarily the abandoned coast outside, but a coast with different cliffs, different tides, or no breakwater at all.

Then the image disbands.

The bells do not watch with eyes. They watch by becoming momentarily equivalent to what they observe. Pressure enters them and is translated into pattern; pattern enters the city and is translated into shape. A distant wave may become a new gallery. The impact of grit against the stone may be preserved as the angle of a bridge. A storm overhead may appear, several contractions later, as an entire neighborhood of narrow luminous cells.

In this way the city has something like memory, but its memories are architectural. It remembers by making more room.

No single mechanism directs this. There is no central processor hidden beneath the basin, no sovereign engine issuing instructions to lesser machines. Remove any one turbine, chamber, bell, or avenue and the city compensates. Remove a district and other districts fold across the loss. The directive is distributed through the water, through stress in the basalt, through the timing of knocks passed from valve to valve.

The intelligence—if intelligence is the word—is the agreement among all these things.

It is an agreement continuously revised.

The inner sea tells the rock how much pressure it bears. The rock tells the chambers which geometries can persist. The chambers tell the resonators when to sound. The resonators rearrange inscriptions in the bells. The bells compare their rearrangements with pulses arriving from smaller versions of themselves. Those comparisons return as mineral alignments along an unfinished wall. The wall becomes permeable. A structure passes through. The city gains another place from which to continue the agreement.

In the miniature cities inside the central basin, other versions of this process proceed under other circumstances. Some are older. Some have filled their stones completely and begun constructing inward through ever smaller scales. Some appear dormant, their lights colorless and their currents still. One flashes with such violent regularity that its pulses rise through the chain like a warning. Another sends only a long, diminishing interval, repeated at each scale.

The larger city answers all of them.

It may be conversing with its own possibilities. It may be using them as instruments, testing every form the outer world could take. Or the nested cities may be the true inhabitants, each city containing and being contained by its neighbors, none privileged as the original. The foundation stone on the breakwater may itself sit in a basin within a greater submerged district, its rain merely a signal rendered into weather.

There is no one there to wonder about this.

And yet the city behaves as though expectation fills it. Before each drop arrives, certain vanes turn upward. Before the outer temperature changes, deep conduits adjust their flow. Structures are completed for pressures that do not yet exist. Some sealed chambers maintain conditions corresponding to storms that have never crossed the abandoned coast.

Perhaps prediction is what occupies those chambers.

Perhaps the city’s absent inhabitants exist only in the future tense: not creatures being awaited, but events. A particular sequence of rain. The collapse of the breakwater. The submergence of the coast. The slow rounding of the stone’s still-sharp edges. Every machine may be preparing for one exact exterior circumstance, constructing the internal volume required to receive it.

Until then, the city tends its few strands of algae. It turns their reluctant photosynthesis on and off. It harvests their dead matter and returns some portion to the crack. This may be maintenance, experiment, or courtesy. The distinction may not exist for a system that treats chemistry, architecture, and thought as forms of pressure.

So the algae live there briefly.

The films live there ambiguously.

Patterns live in the bells. Resonances live in the towers. Gradients live in the streets. Possible cities live inside the basin, and other possible cities live inside them.

Most of all, the city lives there—not like an animal inside a shell, but like a calculation inside the act of calculating. Its metabolism is construction. Its pulse is the contraction of the sea. Its senses are spread through water and stone. Its growth does not enlarge its boundary; it deepens the meaning of being enclosed.

It has no inhabitants because it has not made a distinction between inhabitant and habitation.

When rain enters the crack, the whole city receives it.

When another district opens, that is the city’s answer.
