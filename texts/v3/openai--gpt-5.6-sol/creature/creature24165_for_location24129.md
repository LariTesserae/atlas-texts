At first, the only inhabitants appear to be the bronze growths.

They are neither rooted machines nor metallic plants, though either description is accurate at a distance. Each is a colony of cooperating tissues arranged around a persistent electrical pattern. The visible crown is only its coolest and most temporary portion. Beneath it, bright filaments descend through the mineral plates, branching wherever heat, charge, and useful elements diverge. Some strands end after a few centimeters. Others pass downward farther than the apparent dimensions of the country should allow.

An individual has no fixed outline. Its roots may mingle with those of another without joining it, while two crowns separated by a range of black ridges may belong to the same organism. What distinguishes one from the next is not flesh but phase: each maintains a private cadence of circulating current, an intricate standing wave that holds its parts in agreement. If the cadence is interrupted, the plant does not die immediately. It becomes several smaller selves, each beginning with a different fragment of the old rhythm.

The youngest are difficult to notice. They begin as a discoloration in a seam—a green-black line, then a row of metallic beads so exact they might have been sorted and placed. Over several years the beads flatten into scales. The scales overlap, forming a low rosette that remains closed while its rootlets assay the stone beneath it.

A young growth tastes its surroundings by changing their conductivity. It sends faint pulses into the ground and listens to the shape of their return. Iron produces one silence, sulfur another. Crystal faults answer sharply. Unfinished terrain answers before the pulse is sent, and juveniles near such margins often grow asymmetrical, crowded with organs for events that have not yet occurred.

If the seam proves suitable, a central stem rises.

The stem is hollow, but the hollow is not empty. Inside it hangs a sequence of free-turning rings supported by opposing fields. Their rotation governs the opening of the crown. A ring may complete only one revolution in nine days. With each turn, another foil unfolds: copper first, then green, then the darker inner vanes whose surfaces carry branching circuits finer than veins in a human eye.

The plants do not seek light. They seek difference.

A buried ore current passing beneath the shelf may draw every crown toward it. A change in deep pressure may cause them to close. When the relay-note sounds through the country, their conductive hairs rise and their rings briefly synchronize. For several seconds, every organism becomes part of one enormous circuit. Blue discharges leap between crowns too far apart for the apparent space between them.

Then they separate again.

Whether they remember these moments is uncertain, but they are altered by them. New conductive paths remain in their foils. Repeated signals thicken particular coils. Old growths become increasingly elaborate inside, carrying decades of subterranean changes as physical convolutions. To cut across the stem of one would reveal no annual rings, but nested diagrams: maps of vanished magnetic fields, records of distant pressure, perhaps instructions received from roots that lie beneath no visible ground.

They have no predators. Nothing browses their fronds or bores into their seed drums. Their injuries come from the country itself: a plate becoming too exact and dividing through a root bundle, an alteration of distance that strands one portion of a crown impossibly far from the rest, a sudden accumulation of mass in its own shade. Such wounds are not sealed. The organism isolates them with ceramic collars, detaches the damaged region from its rhythm, and waits.

Detached fronds sometimes continue for centuries.

They crawl too slowly to be seen moving. By alternating charge along their veins, they draw trace metals beneath themselves and leave behind a path of purified beads. Most eventually become still, their internal patterns simplified until they are indistinguishable from mineral. A few encounter an empty seam and root there. These descendants are not copies of the parent. They retain only its final concern: the last pressure it faced, the last buried current it followed, the last distant plant toward which it had begun to grow.

The hexagonal drums are not seeds in the ordinary sense. They contain no embryo. Each houses nested rings inscribed by minute differences of alloy. When two mature organisms have exchanged currents long enough, both begin producing complementary drums. The drums remain attached and rotate in response to one another across the intervening ground. Their motion alters the fields between them. Metal migrates. A third cadence gradually gathers in some suitable crack, and matter there begins arranging itself around a rhythm before any body exists to sustain it.

Thus the offspring precedes its own substance.

For the first several decades it lives only as an agreement between its parents. If one parent is broken, the other may continue both halves of the pattern until a replacement partner is found. Some ancient plants carry dozens of these unfinished agreements. Their stems are crowded with dormant rings, each turning slightly when a particular distant crown discharges.

This may be the nearest thing the country has to longing.

There are also inhabitants below the scale of a crown.

Thin, self-propagating faults move through crystal lattices. They consume irregularity, leaving long zones of perfect alignment behind them. Globules of alloy migrate against thermal gradients and divide when they grow large enough, each daughter retaining a polarized skin. Ceramic nodules form around unstable reactions, enclosing and regulating them. None is independently complex enough to call alive, yet together they perform digestion, circulation, repair, and selection across the entire shelf.

They are the country’s smallest citizens—or its cells.

The distinction becomes less useful with depth. The moving veins of ore respond to the roots above. The roots, in turn, unfold according to the low relay-notes from below. Chambers separate elements needed by organisms that will not germinate for another thousand years. New ground resolves most quickly where mature crowns have oriented toward the unfinished margin. Even distance seems to participate, contracting to permit an exchange and widening to isolate an unstable process.

No single part directs this.

The seven great growths around the pale clearing are older than the rest, though “older” is not quite correct in a place where precision replaces decay. Their outer foils are ancient and dark, but their inner mechanisms are newer than those of juveniles, continually refined as impurities withdraw from them. They have become more functional with time. Every century removes accident from their structure.

Each of the seven maintains a distinct task.

One draws nickel and iron upward. One separates conductive metals into threads finer than hair. One accumulates ceramic compounds in pale collars beneath the ground. One measures changes in the false sky. One maintains a phase shared by the whole grove. One receives the echo that arrives before the relay-note and alters the others in preparation.

The seventh appears to do nothing.

Its crown seldom turns. Its roots take little material. Most of its internal rings are motionless. Yet whenever a new tooth forms in the depression, that seventh organism produces a single click, and the tooth settles into greater exactness. It may be counting. It may be comparing the forming object to a pattern that has no material existence. Or it may be the only one among them that is not part of the production process.

If there is an observer in the country, it is probably this one.

Its observation would not resemble sight. It would know an object by the stresses that object introduces into the shelf, by the way charge bends around it, by how much heavier it becomes in shade. A visitor standing beneath its crown would appear as a wet, chemically confused region—briefly warm, full of uncontrolled reactions, eroding at an astonishing rate. The organism might not recognize such a visitor as one being. Flesh is too mixed, too noisy, too temporary. It might perceive instead a crowd of salts, currents, iron traces, and enclosed water, all failing to separate properly.

It would not attack. Predation has no meaning here.

But its foils might incline. The ceramic collars in its stem might tighten. Far below, a vein of magnetized ore might alter course by a fraction. After the visitor had gone, the seventh growth could preserve the encountered pattern in a new ring and turn it once every few centuries, considering.

The object forming in the clearing is not yet alive. It has no maintained cadence, no boundary, and no means of distinguishing itself from the stone supplying it. Still, the seven behave as though it already exists. They leave space for absent components. They compensate for stresses that its future weight will produce. Their roots curve around volumes that are currently solid rock but will one day be cavities.

Perhaps they are assembling a machine.

Perhaps they are reproducing at a scale for which the bronze growths are merely reproductive organs.

The deeper processes suggest the latter. The shelf extends as a shell extends. Its strange distances fold usable area into a small physical enclosure. Its false sky may be a regulatory membrane, holding heat and direction constant. The plants gather and refine. The hidden chambers digest. The moving plates transport. The unfinished margins grow.

In that case, there are not many inhabitants.

There is one.

The open country is a single, exceedingly young organism, embedded high within the mountain. Its forests are sensory and reproductive tissues. Its mineral plates are an unfinished skin. The immense ordered processes beneath them are metabolism. What appears to be a horizon is the inner curvature of a body whose true dimensions cannot be expressed in the space containing it.

Yet even this may be only a stage.

The country could be an egg. The seven growths could be temporary organs that will be absorbed when the central structure is complete. The thing in the clearing may someday acquire a cadence strong enough to claim all the others as parts of itself. When that happens, the bronze crowns may fold for the last time. Their metals will travel inward. The black shelf will become light, or armor, or thought. The false sky may split along a line that has not yet resolved.

For now, whoever lives there has no face, no voice, and perhaps no singular awareness.

It lives as coordinated patience.

It listens through crystal. It remembers in alloy. It converses by altering the distance between its parts. It grows new ground beneath itself because there is not yet enough world to contain what it is becoming.

And in the pale clearing, among the first precise teeth of an impossible mechanism, it is slowly preparing the organ by which it will eventually know that it exists.
