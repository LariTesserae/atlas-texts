The people are easy to overlook.

At a distance they resemble bundles of bark and moss moving between the houses: low shapes, dark against dark stone, stopping whenever the animals stop. Their clothing is layered rather than tailored—felted hair, softened hide, woven nettle, panels of dry lichen stitched beneath the outer wraps for warmth. Over everything they wear short mantles of overlapping bark scales. These shed mist without rustling much and can be replaced one scale at a time.

They go bareheaded in all but the hardest frost. Most wear their hair long, not loose but braided into cords useful for tying tools, filtering water, repairing condensation nets, or marking promises. Cutting one’s hair is less a matter of appearance than expenditure. A household that has recently repaired a roof may be recognized by the uneven heads of everyone in it.

Their faces are broad across the cheeks and narrow at the chin. Noses run often in the cold. Their eyes appear unusually dark because they do not widen them in surprise; surprise is a dangerous thing to show abruptly when so many creatures attend to movement. Even their expressions have become gradual. Amusement begins at one corner of the mouth and travels slowly. Grief may first be visible in the hands.

They are not mute. Indoors, with hides dropped over the doors and the hearth pebbles ticking softly in their pot, they speak in low voices. Their language has many words for intervals: the time between a drop forming and falling, between the first shiver of an antler and its movement, between the silence of moths and the arrival of foxes. It has few true synonyms. Similar words, they say, invite the mountain to misunderstand.

In public they speak mostly with fingers, wrists, shoulders, and the direction of the gaze. These signs are not merely substitutes for speech. Some thoughts belong properly to the hands. Ownership is signed against the sternum; temporary possession against the palm. Water is indicated by touching the lower eyelid. A moving grove is described with both hands, one representing its visible edge and the other whatever intention may be traveling beneath the stone.

Children sign before they talk. Infants are swaddled against the chest, where their cries can be muffled quickly by skin and cloth, though the adults do not punish them for crying. Instead, each family keeps a small room lined with moss, root matting, moth wings, and old felt: a soft chamber into which an inconsolable child may be carried. The chamber is called a thunder-house. Adults use it too.

A child’s first civic duty is to carry an empty bowl. Later comes carrying a full one. Only after learning the difference in posture are they trusted to walk near the cisterns.

They grow up sure-footed, with strong toes and a habit of testing every surface before placing weight upon it. Most have pale horizontal scars on their shins from fresh rock and dark stains on their fingers from handling cold-leaved plants. Their teeth are worn by mineral grit. Their hearing is no sharper than that of other people, but their attention is. They notice the withdrawal of sound as readily as sound itself.

No one walks through the city alone in quite the ordinary sense. A person crossing a court may have moths sleeping under the back flap of their mantle, two beetles lodged in the warm seam of a boot, and a hairless mouse coiled in the basket they carry. The people distinguish between animals that are owned, animals that are hosted, animals that are tolerated, and animals whose claim on a dwelling predates the human one. These categories change with the season.

The hairless mice are hosted. At night they braid themselves together for warmth, tails and limbs interlaced, and families place these living ropes beneath sleeping platforms. They smell faintly of wet clay. A mouse that repeatedly sleeps beneath the same person is sometimes given a private name, but the name is never spoken near it. Mice are believed to take names into cracks.

The frogs of the water jars are tolerated and watched. Their health reveals changes in the collected mist. A frog with a silver film over its eyes means the water has touched new stone. A frog that refuses to leave a jar means the jar should not be emptied, no matter how thirsty the household. There are stories of families saved during dry seasons by obeying such warnings and stories of families poisoned by assuming every stubborn frog was wise.

The glass-backed beetles belong to no one. Beneath their transparent shells, pale organs turn slowly around a central dark bead. During ground tremors they align themselves with nearby fissures. Surveyors carry them in slotted bone boxes, though the beetles frequently escape and return years later inside sealed walls.

Moths are counted as weather.

Their larvae feed on fungi cultivated in chimney gardens, and their adults sleep in such density that a bare patch on a wall can be alarming. They have no mouths. During the few days of their winged lives, they seek warmth, mist, and the faint stored vibration of the antlers. Sometimes thousands arrange themselves along a white branch in bands corresponding to sounds no person remembers making.

The pale lizards eat them. These lizards are nearly weightless, all rib and toe, with loose skin along their flanks that catches the air when they leap. Children tempt them onto sleeves with polished chips of mica. They cannot be domesticated, but some become accustomed to particular roofs and will defend them from other lizards by opening their mouths in perfect silence.

The ash birds are less welcome. They stand as high as a person’s knee and have wings too long to fold neatly. Fine branching vanes grow from their feathers, collecting mist and directing it toward the corners of their mouths. Their legs end in broad, soundless pads rather than grasping claws. They watch the people with neither fear nor familiarity. If a sleeper exhales with their mouth open, an ash bird may lower its narrow beak to drink the condensation from their teeth.

Above them in the city’s hungry order are the six-legged foxes.

They are not truly foxes, the scholars say, though no alternative name has lasted. Their front pair of limbs are small and handlike, usually folded against the chest; the middle and hind pairs carry them in a fluid, level gait. Their fur is gray at the tips and nearly colorless beneath. When they crouch on bare rock, they become a patch of mist with eyes.

They do not bark. They hunt by resting their palms on stone and feeling the motions of prey. Before entering a lane, one may remain motionless for an hour with all six feet spread. People discourage them with smoke and hanging nets but do not kill them. A fox carcass is said to draw something without feet.

Among the human citizens are water listeners, joint looseners, grove witnesses, roof cultivators, animal counters, and keepers of returned sounds.

Water listeners make their rounds before dawn. Each carries a sequence of shallow cups fixed to a wooden stave. By placing the cups beneath drips and comparing their notes, they determine which channels have opened or narrowed inside the rock. They have the privilege of waking any household without apology. Their ears often become damaged with age, not from loudness but from decades spent attending to tiny, hard sounds.

Joint looseners maintain everything that touches an antler. Their wedges are of willow, horn, wet root, and layered fungus. They know how to distinguish the ordinary strain of timber from the deeper pressure preceding growth. When a white branch begins to rise, they move through the affected district taking houses slightly apart. Roofs tilt open. Bridges lift free. Stairs separate at their soft joints. The city seems briefly to become a collection of pieces, held in readiness around the emerging bone.

Grove witnesses do not prevent vegetation from appearing. They sit beside new thickets and record what arrives first, what direction the leaves face, what animals enter, and whether the grove casts the correct shadow. They wear waxed leggings against the cold foliage. If a grove contains a plant not previously known, the witness remains until it collapses or until another person can learn its signs.

Sometimes a witness does not return. This is not always treated as death. Certain groves are larger inside than their circumference permits, and voices have occasionally been felt—never heard—in the antlers after someone disappears among the leaves.

The keepers of returned sounds have the most uneasy occupation. They sleep in rotating shifts near cisterns, antler forks, fissures, and old foundations. When a noise emerges, they record its rhythm with knots in string and scratches on slate. Most are ordinary: tool blows, dropped stones, coughs, the slap of a wet roof panel. Others cannot be placed.

A recurring sound beneath the western houses resembles fingernails running over the inside of a shell. One antler gives back the breathing of a large animal every forty-three cold seasons. The oldest cistern sometimes releases a conversation between two people using words that have not yet entered the language.

The keepers are forbidden to interpret while recording. Interpretation belongs to the council, which rarely agrees.

Authority in the city is temporary and specific. One person may direct the rationing of water yet yield to a child who has seen a fox beneath the paving. During antler growth, the joint looseners command entire districts. During cloudfall, animal counters determine which doors may remain open. The council settles disputes over hearthstones, roof frames, jars, and sounds.

Sounds can be owed.

If a person makes a dangerous noise and it returns beneath another family’s house, the maker may owe them moss lining, sleep, or watch duty until the sound has fully emerged. Some debts persist for generations. One family still maintains the northern condensation net because an ancestor shouted beside the largest antler and the shout has never been conclusively heard again.

The inhabitants do not bury their dead in the basin. The ground cannot be trusted to keep a body separate from other things. Nor do they burn them, since fuel is scarce and smoke has consequences. Instead, the dead are carried beyond the rim to a narrow platform above the clouds.

There their spoken names are released outward. Their bodies are wrapped in condensation netting and left to the cold, the ash birds, and whatever climbs the mountain from below. A strand of hair is retained for repairs, but no object is kept solely as a memorial. Remembering, they believe, should remain light enough to move.

Yet not every inhabitant can be carried beyond the rim.

The antlers themselves are inhabited. Their pores contain minute red worms that withdraw from daylight. Snails graze their surfaces. Moths drink their warmth. In some forks, enclosed chambers can be detected by tapping, though tapping is discouraged. Once, when a branch cracked during a frost, those nearby saw layers inside it: white outer bone, a pink and fibrous depth, then a glossy dark hollow in which points of light moved as though very far away.

The crack closed warm around the wedges meant to hold it open.

Below the paving live entire populations the city knows only through effects. Something gathers lost bone awls into ordered fans. Something turns sleeping mice so that all their noses point downhill. Soft-bodied animals push up through fresh fissures and perish in the cold before anyone can decide what they are. They have translucent limbs, unfinished mouths, and patches of fur growing on the inside of their skins.

These may not be species. They may be attempts.

The scholars sometimes suggest that the mountain is producing life the way young rock produces crystals: under pressure, repeatedly, without yet knowing the final form. The groves are trials of landscape. The animals are trials of motion. The antlers are the first durable part of some body that has not fully entered the world.

Others say the creature already exists and the bowl lies within it rather than upon it. The black stone is only a hardened lining. The cloud below conceals the rest of the anatomy. What people call tremors are adjustments in sleep.

Still others insist there is no single creature. The mountain, antlers, groves, clouds, animals, and city are assembling one another. Nothing here is the host and nothing merely the parasite. The human settlement is another emergent organ: a small, clever arrangement for moving water, loosening joints, storing observations, and preventing catastrophic sound.

The people dislike this last theory most, perhaps because it gives them too much importance.

They know themselves to be recent. Moths are impressed in stone beneath their oldest hearths. Snail tracks cross antler surfaces that had not yet emerged when the tracks were made. Warm breath rises from cracks far too narrow for lungs, and in the hour before morning the entire basin sometimes tightens by a fraction—the cisterns trembling, fern islands drawing inward, every sleeping mouse clenching its tiny feet.

Then comes the drop into the oldest cistern.

The people pause with the animals. The fox on the rim lays six feet against the rock. The frogs swell their throats. Moths raise their wings without taking flight. In every house, those awake place a palm on the floor.

The answer travels through the antlers too slowly to be called a note. It is more like a posture assumed by the whole white stand. Condensation shivers along the branches. Somewhere deep below, many soft things rearrange themselves around a larger warmth.

Only when that movement has ended do the inhabitants breathe normally again.

Whether they are listening to the city, the mountain, an animal, or something not yet divided into those categories, none of them claim to know.

They know only that it is alive enough to answer—and that, for now, it permits them to live inside the answer.
