At first, the chamber seems inhabited chiefly by things that cling.

Everything has a holdfast, a hook, a cup, a gripping tail, a thread of mucus, or a habit of wedging itself into a seam. Even the swimmers are only temporarily unattached. They cross the open water in nervous bursts, traveling from one certainty to another, and the instant the pumps change pitch they fasten themselves to the works.

The green ribbon weed holds by rivets. Its roots are not roots so much as fitted collars, grown to the exact profile of the metal beneath them. Pry one loose and the underside preserves every stamped number, every pit and burr, in soft green relief. Older plants have followed the erosion of their anchors for generations. As a bolt head narrows, the collar tightens. When the bolt finally shears, weed and metal drift away together, the plant unwilling or unable to distinguish between substrate and ancestor.

The broad leaves around the coolant leaks are more animal in their behavior. They furl against cold currents and spread when warmth returns. Tiny mouths stipple their undersides. These mouths collect oil droplets, rust bacteria, and the loosened skins of shrimp. A leaf deprived of light may crawl several finger-widths in a day, inflating one edge and collapsing the other until it reaches the brightness of a glass panel. Whole patches migrate this way, slowly enough to appear fixed unless measured against the screw holes.

Among them live the tapping shrimp.

They are translucent except for their hearts and the copper-colored plates over their skulls. Each shrimp claims a small resonant territory: a pipe elbow, an empty gauge cup, the hollow back of a handrail. It strikes the metal with one enlarged claw and listens through its feet. The resulting pattern tells it more than sight could. It hears food moving inside gasket fiber. It hears the approach of larger creatures. It hears the next freight carriage while the roar is still kilometers away.

During the false days, the shrimp tap constantly, filling the garden with dry, delicate industry. During long violet darkness they fall silent all at once. No one knows what signal passes between them. Perhaps one shrimp stops and its neighbors imitate it until the hush has traveled around the room. Perhaps they all hear something deeper in the elevator—the movement of a buried relay, the turn of a clock whose hands have vanished.

The blind eels are the oldest-looking inhabitants, though they are born soft and pink and live only a few years. Their skin accumulates gray dust until they resemble lengths of forgotten insulation. They browse head-down over gasket moss, rasping it from joints without damaging the seals. This restraint is instinctive and exact. An eel will clean a corroded flange to the edge of failure, then leave a dark ring of growth untouched around the weakest point. Young eels that bite too deeply are killed by the sudden jets. The survivors inherit no teaching except the shape of caution in their bodies.

They nest in cable sleeves. Their eggs are wrapped around one another in long gelatinous fuses, and whenever a pressure door begins its hours-long opening, the adults gather to move them. Hundreds of eels pull in the same direction, laying the egg-rope in a safer groove before the latch completes its turn. Once the clutch is secured, they return to feeding. They do not seem to recognize their own offspring individually. What they protect is the continuity of the rope.

The glass minnows are less cautious.

They inhabit dead instruments because those offer the illusion of boundaries. In the transparent level tubes they school vertically, rising and falling with the seepage. Their bodies carry little organs of cold light. When they turn together, faded measurement marks reappear across them: half, full, danger, purge. For a few seconds a gauge may seem restored to duty, though what it measures now is fear, hunger, or the passage of the crab.

There are thousands of minnows, but the chamber rarely shows more than a few hundred at once. The rest occupy hairline cracks, hollows inside valves, and the flooded conduits behind the wall panels. Their migrations extend into spaces no larger creature can enter. They know the elevator as a mesh of narrow internal seas. A shoal may disappear into a rust hole beside the floor and emerge weeks later from a lamp housing overhead, reduced in number and carrying unfamiliar silver parasites.

The coin-rays follow the light.

They settle flat against warmed plates and alter their mottling until they resemble corrosion. When a panel brightens, one can sometimes see dozens detach from the wall at once—not stains after all, but thin bodies lifting into the water. They swim with gentle ripples and graze on the organisms awakened by illumination.

Each ray carries a copied fragment of its preferred wall: a crescent of verdigris, a black ceramic crack, the pale shadow where a warning label used to be. As the chamber wears away, patterns survive on the animals after disappearing from the structure. There are rays marked with letters no plate still bears. Others preserve diagonal stripes from an ancient hazard border. In this way the school retains an incomplete, swimming archive of the room’s former surfaces.

Above them hang the bell spiders in their communal air.

Seen from below, a nest appears to be a tarnished mirror bulging from the ceiling. Within it the crustaceans move upside down, their hooked feet dimpling the water’s skin. They are pale, long-jointed things with feathery breathing plates along the abdomen. Their young hatch into air, cling to the inner surface of the bubble, and spend their first days looking downward at the garden they will later enter.

The colony maintains its atmosphere patiently. Individuals bite ripe bladder-fruit and herd the released gas upward beneath their bodies. Others comb condensation from the bubble’s interior. When the air fouls, they open a temporary vent and let a silver portion escape into the ceiling’s seams. The nest shrinks, trembles, and is rebuilt.

Their descending lines resemble silk but are secreted as clear elastic cords. Bell spiders lower themselves through open water with all their legs spread, hunting leaf larvae and the smallest shrimp. A passing carriage sends them climbing frantically home. Those caught too far below do not attempt to swim. They pull their mucus lines taut and let the chamber’s violent current throw them like bells on ropes.

The pulley crab hunts beneath them.

There may be only one. At times two different arrangements of washers have been seen in distant parts of the chamber, but never together, and the crab sheds frequently. It could be a succession mistaken for an individual, or one animal old enough that its molts are epochs in the lives of minnows.

Its true shell is blue-white and nearly smooth. Nothing sees that shell for long. The crab decorates itself with whatever the room has recently loosened: red vine leaves, ceramic flakes, screw heads, eel skin, the calcareous fans of dead worms. It selects pieces not merely for concealment but for balance. A heavy nut on one side is answered by a coil of wire on the other. The completed armor makes the crab resemble a heap swept together during a repair that was never finished.

It waits where the cable’s tremor is strongest. Its feet can lock around grating bars, and the joints contain fluid bladders that stiffen before a carriage passes. In the rising roar, it opens its arms. Stunned fish arrive in the current. Loose growth arrives. Sometimes an object comes too—an identification tag, a snapped tool, a bone polished beyond recognition. The crab accepts or rejects each by touch.

After feeding, it often visits the alcove.

It enters sideways through the crimson vines, shedding leaves and washers, and folds itself into the chair. The fit is too good to be accidental, though the chair was plainly made for another body. Two rear legs grip the seat. Its middle legs brace against the walls. One claw rests near the lever.

There it watches the impossible window.

Whether the crab understands the view cannot be determined. Its eyes track cloud shadows. They follow lights moving through unknown cities. When birds cross the daylight, it raises its feeding arms as it does for minnows carried by the pumps. Once, during a view of snow-covered mountains, it scraped frostlike algae from the inner glass and packed the white material along its shell.

Sometimes it touches the lever.

The chamber tilts. The gas meadow rolls. Every swimmer corrects its body, every leaf changes its angle, and the vast cable groans in its hidden course. The crab remains in the chair until balance returns. Then it backs out and resumes its ambush, leaving the window to the grazing fish.

Beneath these conspicuous lives are colonies that cannot be separated cleanly from the machine.

Fan-worms pulse in the ruined pump. Heat polyps inhabit the thermostat. Filaments of conductive slime bridge broken contacts and retract when current becomes too strong. Limpets with iron teeth scour oxide from rails. Pale tube animals cement hairline fractures from the inside, dying when the leak that fed them is sealed. Their descendants wait nearby for the next failure.

None of them knows it is repairing anything.

The worms contract because electricity irritates them. The polyps close because heat expands their flesh. The slime reaches toward the mineral taste of a charged terminal. Yet together they perform the gestures of absent workers. They regulate, clean, seal, vent, and brace. They have inherited the elevator’s maintenance not as knowledge but as appetite.

And the elevator, in turn, tends them.

It brings heat. It circulates food. It brightens occupied panels and darkens empty water. Its valves create spawning seasons; its warning pulses trigger migrations. Even the freight carriage’s approach has become a kind of weather. Creatures feed before it, shelter during it, and emerge afterward to harvest what the violence has dislodged.

Thus the chief inhabitant of the Drowned Lift-Garden is difficult to point to.

It is not the pulley crab in the chair, though the crab resembles a patient custodian. It is not the vines, the minnows, or the silver nests overhead. It is not the machinery, though machinery supplies its bones and laws.

What lives there is the arrangement between them: a distributed creature occupying the whole chapel-shaped interruption. Its nerves are control currents and shrimp knocks. Its circulation is warm leakage. Its lungs are the bell spiders’ bubbles and the slow pressure doors. Its memory survives in ray-skin markings, eel caution, worn handrails, and the habits of worms inside dead pumps. The descending cable is not quite its spine, but when the cable falters, the whole room suffers as one body.

At the first vibration of an approaching carriage, this larger life contracts.

Shrimp stop tapping. Eels knot themselves into sleeves. Rays flatten against the plates. Bell spiders reel in their lines. The pulley crab lowers its weight and locks every leg. Leaves curl around bolts. Polyps close valves. Minnows pour into the level tubes until the old gauges shine with living alarm.

For a moment, everything holds fast.

Then the carriage passes, the chamber thunders, and the creature made of garden and mechanism endures its own enormous heartbeat.
