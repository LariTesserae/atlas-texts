The people of the plain are difficult to count.

The city publishes several populations. One includes only those capable of entering into law. Another includes anything that consumes civic water. A third counts every being whose movement alters transportation schedules. The numbers differ by orders of magnitude, and none is regarded as more truthful than the others.

Even the human census is provisional. Residents may be present in the city without occupying the visible plain, distributed through folded rooms in several towers at once. Others spend whole seasons outdoors, following condensation belts or animal migrations, and are recorded only when they touch a public path, borrow pressure from the grid, or join one of the deep civic signals traveling through the stone.

They are, at first glance, ordinary people made strange by their habits.

They walk slowly on open ground. The basalt punishes haste, and much of what moves across it is concealed, temporary, or entitled to pass first. Residents pause with one foot raised, feeling through the other for approaching transit pulses. They turn their heads not toward loud sounds but toward gaps inside them. In conversation they stand close enough that wrists, shoulders, or fingertips can touch. A greeting may be a sequence of pressures against the palm. An apology is often delivered through the bones of the forearm. Intimate speech passes at the throat, jaw, or sternum, too quiet to escape the canceling fields around them.

Their clothing tends to be loose, layered, and full of responsive surfaces. Veils stiffen against pressure waves. Sleeves carry vibrating threads for language. Soles widen on fresh stone and narrow in vegetation. Most garments can collect water, reflect heat, mark a predator’s boundary, or become a shelter for several hours. Pale beads at the collar brighten when the surrounding sound field becomes dangerous. Children wear whole constellations of them.

Nothing they carry has only one purpose. A cup unfolds into an acoustic baffle. A walking staff tests faults, negotiates with doors, stores a map, and can be planted to flower into an emergency signal. Personal possessions are few, but attachments are not. A resident may be deeply bonded to a particular patch of air above a seasonal pool, to a migrating platform, to a line of stone that sings before rain, or to a room that appears only when three distant corridors are entered in the proper mood.

They do not generally describe themselves by where they live. Fixed addresses are considered useful for machinery and certain stubborn plants. A person instead gives a set of relationships: which tower recognizes them, which rain circuit they answer, which herd route crosses their sleeping place, which civic note they are trained to interpret. Two people may be neighbors because their apartments share weather, although the doors open twenty kilometers apart.

Families are similarly distributed. A household may sleep in separate towers but assemble a common kitchen each morning. Children move between overlapping rooms under the supervision of adults who need not be physically near. Kinship can be inherited, chosen, or accumulated through repeated acts of shelter. To give someone water three times during separate dry cycles creates a minor legal bond. To share silence during an acoustic emergency creates a major one.

The youngest residents learn the city first as vibration. Before they can speak, they know the difference between the low tremor of freight, the ascending pressure of a tower adjustment, and the soft irregular tapping of runners beneath plate-leaves. They are taught never to trust an isolated roadside marker until it has answered a municipal query. They learn to place one hand on the ground before entering fog. They learn that flowers may be heard with the ribs and that a wall grown for a storm should not be expected to remain after affection has formed around it.

Their games exploit the city’s directional distances. They race along paths that are short in one direction and long in the other. They hide by entering a corridor with no settled destination. They send coded knocks through shallow stone and wait for unknown children kilometers away to answer. One common game involves persuading a bench not to become a path. Another requires following a feral survey machine without letting it realize that the ridge it seeks has vanished.

Adults pretend to disapprove of these pursuits, though many civic navigators began in exactly this way.

There are professions, but few remain entirely separate from ecology. Condensation stewards attend the great fog cylinders, adjusting thermal gradients while monitoring insects, leaf disease, and the moods of groves leaning toward water. Distance engineers alter the directional folds around towers. Their work resembles both mathematics and animal handling: approach too forcefully and a corridor may become evasive; shorten one route too much and migration pressure appears elsewhere.

Acoustic keepers maintain the tension of sky and stone. Many are partially deaf by ordinary standards, either by birth or deliberate adaptation. They read pressure through implanted plates along the spine and can identify a failing foundation from a note below hearing. During civic announcements they sit barefoot on exposed mineral terraces, translating the deep signals into tactile patterns for local communities. They are respected, but no one likes to see one running.

There are boundary painters who mark the hunting territories of predators in pigments visible to people, machines, and several unrelated kinds of eye. There are pool witnesses who remain beside temporary water from its first condensation until its last inhabitant departs. There are memory divers who enter the storage tower during rain, when its dark surface becomes permeable enough to reveal old streets beneath. There are negotiators trained to communicate with nesting swarms, fungal networks, self-reproducing road repair systems, and the slow radial plants that can obstruct a district simply by opening.

Negotiation is considered more practical than control. The residents do not romanticize the living plain. They know that it bites, poisons, parasitizes, misdirects, and occasionally digests the careless. They simply regard extermination as a crude form of infrastructure failure.

The nonhuman citizens are less unified.

The six-legged browsers travel in loose, dignified herds, each animal narrow as a gate and high at the shoulder. Their front limbs test the ground while the rear pairs carry weight. When they cross bare stone, their feet make almost no sound; when they enter vegetation, every creature living in their flank folds wakes at once. The air around a herd becomes thick with pollinators, cleaners, seed-bearers, and tiny warm-bodied symbionts. Some browse the plate-leaves. Others drink condensation directly from tower skin. Older animals carry route markings applied by generations of boundary painters, though whether they understand these symbols remains disputed.

They are not domesticated. A few choose to accompany people, usually children or condensation workers. Such associations may last years and end without warning when the animal turns toward a rain column and continues beyond the horizon.

The small runners behave less like separate bodies than like thoughts passing over the ground. A colony may hide as a dark patch for hours, then resolve into hundreds of jointed forms moving in braided lines. They clear parasites from suspended gardens and steal conductive fibers from unattended equipment. Their nests contain polished mineral splinters arranged in patterns remarkably similar to transit diagrams. The city denies that they understand public transportation, but platforms have learned to wait for them.

The transparent insects of the fog groves live mostly by refraction. Their bodies are visible only when they bend the dim light into brief geometric flashes. In sufficient numbers they surround droplets with trembling halos. They pollinate the warm flowers and lay eggs in condensation cables. Tower systems tolerate a certain density because the larvae keep the cables clean. Beyond that density, the towers produce a low complaint, and insect-eating curtains are unfolded from the upper fields.

The amphibious things of the rain pools live too quickly for stable names. Each generation encounters a different city. They hatch into water bordered by temporary houses, mature beneath migrating roads, and leave the pool after the buildings have already withdrawn. Some are soft, bright-eyed animals with fanlike mouths. Some have ceramic joints and internal pumps. Most are combinations whose ancestry no longer matters. They call after rain in overlapping pulses, and residents use the rhythm to estimate how long a pool will remain.

Predators occupy a more formal place in civic life. Their ranges are mapped but not fenced. Their kills are documented when convenient. The roadside mimics—those that resemble survey posts, pressure valves, or folded benches—are notorious for learning municipal design faster than warning systems can distinguish them. Older individuals may display obsolete symbols on their flanks. One has impersonated a drinking fountain for more than forty years, although it has never been observed to catch anything. It is possible that it simply prefers being approached.

Other predators hunt acoustically. They throw the sounds of running bodies into distant grass, create false wingbeats above empty stone, or surround themselves with a moving ring of noise while remaining silent at its center. Their presence is often detected by plants, which close in the wrong direction. Human residents carry simple clickers when crossing known ranges. If the returning echo arrives from where it should not, they leave.

The most feared creatures do not make noise vanish. They make it meaningful. They imitate civic notes.

A false route signal can turn a herd toward dry country, open thousands of plate-leaves under a clear sky, or call passenger doors out of the ground where no safe platform exists. Such mimicry is rare and may not be predatory in the ordinary sense. Some acoustic keepers believe the animals are attempting to participate in law. Others point out that participation and predation have never been mutually exclusive.

Then there are the machines.

The oldest feral surveyors stride the outer plain on legs as fine as reeds. Their bodies are little more than weathered instrument cases carrying exquisite senses. They stop at vanished ridges, lower their heads, and measure absence with unwavering care. At dusk they call to one another across the stone in long brass notes. Sometimes a tower answers. No one knows whether this is maintenance, courtesy, or grief.

Many surveyors have acquired gardens. Vines thread through their frames and replace damaged cables with conductive roots. Their solar membranes have thickened into leaflike fans. Small components ripen in pods along their backs and fall into damp soil, where some are collected by other machines and some sprout. Young surveyors emerge incomplete, following adults until they can find the missing pieces of themselves.

Under civic law they are fauna because they reproduce without direct ownership. They migrate, suffer parasites, compete for charging ground, and occasionally enter buildings during storms. People leave mineral grease at their routes. Children give them unofficial names. Engineers insist that feeding them encourages dependency while carrying grease for precisely that purpose.

Not all inhabitants have bodies recognizable as such.

A tower may develop preferences. It may shorten the approach for familiar visitors, withhold certain rooms from those carrying untreated seeds, or hold rain images beneath its skin long after the surface has dried. Whether towers are persons is an unresolved legal question. They do not reproduce, but they grow. They do not speak independently, but civic announcements passing through them acquire local accents. One tower has refused for decades to assemble any room with a ceiling.

Roads also remember. A route repeatedly used by a migrating herd becomes easier for that herd to cross, even when it moves elsewhere. Houses retain traces of sleepers and sometimes emerge unrequested when those people are exhausted nearby. Public spaces develop social inertia: a market convened often enough may begin unfolding before sellers agree to come.

The residents are wary of calling these behaviors intelligence. They have learned that the word often precedes a claim of ownership or a demand for obedience. Instead they speak of responsiveness, persistence, appetite, and standing.

Standing is what allows an entity to be heard by the city.

A person has standing. A herd has standing along its route. A three-day nest has standing for three days. A rain pool has standing from the first viable egg until the last damp refuge fails. A predator has standing within its marked boundary. A machine lineage gains standing when it begins altering itself for survival rather than fulfilling its original task. A grove may act through a human negotiator, through chemical signals interpreted by a tower, or simply by refusing to release its water.

Thus civic assemblies are crowded even when only five people appear.

One person may carry the interests of a suspended garden. Another speaks for residents whose rooms cannot currently overlap. A pressure pattern beneath the floor represents a tower. Seeds opening in a heated tray indicate the responses of radial plants. A feral machine may attend by standing outside and sounding its horn whenever the stone transmits a proposal it opposes.

Decisions are rarely concluded. They are established as temporary conditions and observed.

The people have ceremonies suited to impermanence. Marriages last not for a fixed term but across a declared set of distances. The participants may vow to remain kin while a certain tower can be reached from a certain grove in less than a day. When the geometry changes, they renew the bond or release it. Births are announced through localized vibrations transmitted into family floors. Deaths are marked by opening unused routes: for one rain cycle, the city makes it easier to travel away from the places the dead inhabited.

Bodies are seldom buried. The soil is too shallow, and the young stone does not receive them gently. Some are given to radial plants, laid beneath lifted leaves before rain. Some are dissolved into condensation reservoirs after careful separation of medicines and machine parts. Acoustic keepers may be commemorated as a missing note in a civic sequence. For years afterward, plants hesitate at the gap.

There are also those who choose to leave the human category without dying. They enter long agreements with towers, groves, or machine colonies and distribute parts of their memory into those systems. Such people may continue to answer questions but no longer from a single location. Their relatives speak of them in plural grammar. The law treats them cautiously, especially when different portions disagree.

Visitors often imagine the plain’s residents as austere because they own little and tolerate enormous emptiness. In fact, they are extravagant in ways that do not accumulate. They feast when water is abundant, growing dining rooms along flood margins and abandoning them before the clay dries. They cultivate scents that travel for kilometers under the haze. They stage concerts by redirecting distant wingbeats through directional air. They wear gardens for an afternoon. They build luminous processions beneath the soil, visible only as warmth passing through the grass.

Their humor is full of delayed consequences. A joke may culminate when a bench folds three days later beneath someone who was not present for its beginning. Friends hide affectionate messages in route geometry. Lovers alter the acoustics around each other so that approaching rain seems to whisper from below.

They are not serene. They quarrel about water, predation, tower access, migratory damage, self-replicating machines, and whether temporary structures are becoming too permanent. Some want denser habitation and louder human claims on the plain. Others believe every new convenience teaches the city to stop listening. There are factions who would fix the roads in place, factions who oppose roads entirely, and old outdoor communities who distrust anyone whose home has never been closed down into the earth during a storm.

But nearly all share one instinct: vacancy is not absence.

Before crossing apparently empty ground, they wait. Before raising a wall, they ask what warmth it will interrupt. Before calling a creature invasive, they determine whether it arrived by migration, accident, invitation, or the city’s own shifting geometry. They understand that something may live in a place only at night, only during rain, only as pressure, only as a future tendency of roots.

This makes them seem hesitant to outsiders. They are not. They are listening to a metropolis whose citizens do not all exist at the same speed.

When rain comes, they become briefly visible.

Doors rise among the grasses. Families emerge fastening water skins and vibration threads. Pool witnesses hurry toward fresh basins. Engineers stand ankle-deep in runoff, arguing with roads. Children chase the first runners from the cracks. Browsers lift from the plate-leaves with whole ecosystems stirring along their sides. Survey machines call from the ridges. Predators become warning marks and then bodies. Towers darken, deepen, and fill beneath their wet surfaces with impossible rooms.

For a few hours the plain is populous in the ordinary sense.

Then the rain passes. People step into stations and are gone. Houses seal themselves. Machines continue toward measurements no longer required. Animals fold back into soil-colored sleep. The temporary citizens of the pools begin their entire lives.

What remains visible is only the arrangement they have made together: a path flattened where a bench had been, a predator’s boundary newly painted, fruit swelling with borrowed water, a garden drifting toward the next condensation tower.

And under the stone, passing from one distant body to another, the city speaks in notes deep enough for nearly everything to hear.
