No single creature can be called the inhabitant of the Rain Court. The Court is occupied in layers, each life fitted into another life’s absence. What lives there does not so much dwell in rooms as take up duties in a machine whose plan has been lost.

The most visible inhabitants are the rain crows.

They are waist-high when they stand erect, though they dislike doing so. Usually they move with their backs humped and their heads thrust low, black feathers breaking the gray light into green, violet, and dull copper. Their wings have narrowed over generations into stiff, fingered paddles. They cannot lift the birds into flight, but spread them while running to keep balance against a surge of water. Their feet are broad and soft-soled. Each toe can close over the rim of a gutter or flatten across wet tile without slipping.

Their beaks appear clumsy until they work. A rain crow can wedge the shovel tip under a fitted tile, brace both feet, and tip the stone without cracking it. Beneath, it harvests pale worms, beetle larvae, and soft-bodied things that retreat from light. It then lowers the tile carefully—sometimes into its old place, sometimes beside the opening. This habit rearranges the Court by degrees. Channels open where none existed. Forgotten vents are covered. Water begins to favor one path over another.

The crows remember these changes.

They have no fixed nests. A group claims a moving territory made not of space but of sequence: the third gutter after the copper bowls ring, the eastern steps during fine rain, the bridge when the round cistern spills. To a human observer they seem to patrol randomly. In fact, each flock follows the Court’s timetable, anticipating where minnows will strand, where snails will emerge, and which seam will foam before a release.

Their pebble-clicking is part tool use, part announcement. Certain stones are carried for years. A bird may inherit one from a dead flock-mate and keep it tucked in the loose feathers beneath its throat. Smooth shell pieces produce a feeding call. Brick chips warn of eels. Copper fragments are struck only during the deepest floods, when their clear notes travel through water better than the birds’ own voices.

Young rain crows are dull brown and nearly silent. They learn the Court by following adults through storms. Many drown. Those that survive know hundreds of water paths, including paths that no longer function. Old birds can sometimes be found waiting beside a dry wall-mouth, clicking patiently for a flood that has not come in decades.

Beneath the birds is the more populous nation of frogs.

They live in holes bored into the masonry, each cavity just large enough to admit a folded body. When withdrawn, a frog seals its doorway with its back. Mineral deposits harden on the skin until the animal resembles a clay plug. Some remain in this position for months, breathing through wet stone and waking only when a particular vibration reaches them.

Their calls are not uniform. Frogs near the cisterns utter low, vessel-like notes. Those in the upper gutters make thin glassy ticks. The palest frogs, which live close to the drain house, swell their throats without producing any sound that a person can hear. At their calling, however, minnows turn in the channels and pulley bats lift their heads. They may be sounding through the pavement rather than through the air.

During the daily flood pulse, the frogs leave the water before it arrives. They climb walls, pillars, crows, ropes—anything that rises. A rain crow may run screaming through a surge with six frogs adhered to its back. The frogs release afterward without gratitude. They are eaten whenever the birds can catch them.

The needle-legged crabs are less dramatic and more indispensable. Their bodies are no wider than coins, but their legs span a hand. They move sideways along vertical joints, combing brick film with delicate mouthparts. Every adult maintains a particular pipe mouth. It removes sediment grain by grain and stacks the unwanted material nearby in crescent embankments. Whole families inherit the same opening. Older crabs have claws worn blunt from masonry work.

They are compulsive sorters. Fish scales go in one heap, lime flakes in another, seeds and bits of fiber in a third. The order means nothing beyond the crab, yet some of these heaps divert rivulets, shore up broken corners, or provide beds where translucent grass takes root. A crab colony may unknowingly preserve a failing wall for generations.

The fist-sized snails perform slower labor. Their shells are flattened and ridged, shaped to resist being rolled by sudden water. They spend their lives traveling fixed circuits along the parapets. Where they pass, algae disappears, lime is shaved smooth, and mineral crust never closes the narrow vents.

Their rasping travels astonishing distances through stone. A snail at the eastern huts can be heard from the triangular pool. During long mist-rains the Court murmurs with thousands of tiny teeth.

Pulley bats live between animal instinct and civic office.

They are small, long-armed creatures with rust-colored faces and dark membranes too narrow for sustained flight. They glide well enough from wall to rope, but climb more often than they fly. At rest they hang in evenly spaced rows, each gripping the braid with hind claws and the bat above it with one forefoot. Their combined weight raises the sluice counterweights.

No bat understands the gate.

Each knows only that the roosting rope is safe, that water will soon bring insects, and that releasing at the dimming hour produces a cool rush followed by abundant food. The oldest females choose when to let go. Sometimes the whole colony drops at once. Sometimes the decision passes along the rope as a shiver, one body releasing after another.

They mend the ropes because frayed ropes are difficult to grip. The bats pull loose fibers free and weave in replacements—gut, bark, hair, reed, even the flight feathers of dead rain crows. Their repairs are crude in isolation but strong in accumulation. Generations of wrapping have made some ropes thicker than a human torso.

The eels live higher than any of them.

Translucent and nearly boneless, they inhabit downspouts in the walls and black plumbing overhead. They wedge themselves across ceramic throats with a fringe of backward-pointing ribs. In heavy rain they allow water to pass around them; in drought they relax, plugging the pipe and preserving a pocket of moisture above.

No one knows where they breed. Very small eels occasionally fall from animal mouths in the walls, knotted together like wet glass threads. The crows gorge on them. Others vanish immediately into cracks scarcely wider than scratches.

Some old eels carry objects inside their bodies. A hook. A blue bead. A child’s tooth. These can be seen when the animals press against thin ceramic plugs, their flesh illuminated by the weak light. Whether the eels swallow such things or grow around them is uncertain.

The minnows belong less to the water than to its motion. In still cisterns they become sluggish and sink until they resemble metal filings. The moment a current begins, they brighten and align. Whole schools pass through channels only a finger deep, silver backs breaking the surface. Their eggs adhere to copper. Their young shelter in the moss bruising the northern gutters.

They have learned—or inherited—the impossible routes. A school entering a low grate may emerge later from a high spout. The fish never hesitate before these passages. They seem to feel connections through pressure changes that the eye cannot follow.

Then there are the creatures that belong to no ordinary food web.

The pale fish beneath the triangular pool is one, if it is a fish.

It is seen only in portions: an eye turning far below, a length of colorless flank, the suggestion of a fin passing beneath the curb. Its scale cannot be reconciled with the pool. The visible eye alone is sometimes as broad as a copper bowl. At other times it is no larger than a frog’s throat.

Nothing feeds there. Nothing drinks. Insects striking the surface do not float but continue downward, shrinking without sinking. Rain enters without disturbing it. Very rarely, after the hammering below, the pale fish rises close enough that markings can be seen along its head. They resemble fitted masonry—lines, squares, intersecting channels—or perhaps scars left by a net too geometrical to have been woven.

The frogs lower their voices around it because loud calls return changed. A single croak may come back as many croaks, delayed by minutes and sounding from under the caller’s own feet.

There are also the white shrimp from below. They appear only when the drain house answers the hammering. Eyeless and warm to the touch, they thrash in the seams until ordinary water cools them. Most are quickly eaten. A few find cracks and disappear downward again. Their stomachs contain charcoal, reddish wood fiber, and occasionally tiny curls of copper.

Last are the maintainers.

Whether they are people is not settled.

They are small enough to pass through spaces the rain crows cannot enter, yet the thumbprints left in wax are human in shape. Their footprints show five toes. Some are the tracks of children. Others are stranger: the same size, but too deep, as if made by a small body carrying a great weight.

They are never seen crossing the open Court. Their presence is inferred from completed acts. A split peg is replaced. A clay jar counterweight is emptied and filled with a different measure of stone. Wax is warmed and pressed into a leak. Dry fibers appear in a hut after rain that should have soaked every corner. Once in a great while, seven pale stones are found rearranged into six and one.

They may live inside the walls, occupying cavities the builders intended as reservoirs. They may come upward from beneath the drain house. They may enter by water routes, emerging only when the Court falls silent before a release.

Evidence suggests bodies adapted to dampness: scraps of shed skin as thin as onion membrane; hair coated in wax; little sandals woven from eel hide and frog gut. Yet some repairs require more strength than such beings should possess. Copper patches are hammered flat from the underside. Stone rings shift between one rain and the next. The timber bridge, absurdly reinforced across its hand-wide channel, has fresh load marks in the middle of its span, as though something extremely heavy crosses there on careful feet.

The hammering below may be theirs: three blows, a pause, two.

The pattern is sometimes answered by a rain crow striking a pebble. Sometimes by a copper bowl. Sometimes by another hammer far behind the western wall.

Perhaps the maintainers are descendants of the Court’s builders, diminished by generations in narrow passages. Perhaps they are not descendants but products of the place, assembled gradually from the materials the water carries away: lime for bone, fiber for hair, wax for skin, copper salts for blood. Perhaps each apparent person is only a collaboration of smaller creatures—a moving sleeve of frogs and crabs, briefly taking the shape of hands because hands are what the old mechanisms require.

No inhabitant possesses the whole design. The bats lift gates. Crabs clear mouths. Snails preserve vents. Birds move tiles. Frogs announce pressures. Eels regulate the upper pipes. Hidden hands patch what these labors cannot. Each species follows appetite, fear, memory, or habit, and the result resembles maintenance.

Or maintenance has learned to resemble life.

The truest inhabitant may therefore be the arrangement itself: the Court distributed among thousands of bodies, using claws where it once used rakes, wings where it once used counterweights, shells where it once used scrapers, instinct where it once used instruction.

When a creature dies, its task does not end. A crow’s feather enters a bat’s rope. A crab’s shell becomes a channel wedge. Frog bones collect in a leaking seam and catch sediment until the leak seals. Snail lime enters the manufactured mud. The Court takes each resident back not as burial, but as building material.

Thus everything there is both animal and implement, tenant and repair.

Everything is being worn away.

Everything is learning, without knowing it, how to hold the rain.
