What lives there is easy to mistake for scenery.

The green stalks in the basins appear, from a distance, to be reeds. Up close they have no true stem or leaf. Each is a sequence of water-filled joints enclosed by a translucent mineral skin. New joints form at the warm end of the stalk while the oldest joints close, harden, and merge with the substrate. The plant does not so much grow as pass slowly through itself. A stand may lean toward a heating conduit for centuries without advancing by the width of a hand.

The broad ground-leaves are stranger. They lie flush with the stone, black-green and slightly warm, their surfaces divided into fine radial channels. Condensation collects in the center and is drawn through the leaf into a network of pale fibers beneath it. During cooler intervals the fibers brighten. At no point does the leaf visibly open or close, yet over several days its outer edge will have become inner tissue while an equal margin on the opposite side returns to mineral film. In this way it turns without moving.

The mosses are the nearest things to abundance. They cover retaining walls, loading bays, bridge piers, and the lips of flooded foundations in deep cushions of green and gold. But even they do not spread freely. A colony ends at a precise line of temperature, vibration, or dissolved metal. Where several kinds meet, there is no struggle for space. Their borders fit together like surveyed districts.

They have no wounds because nothing feeds on them. They have little use for poison, speed, concealment, tough bark, or sacrificial seed. They do not advertise themselves. No color calls across the marsh to an eye; no scent seeks a moving body. Reproduction occurs almost invisibly, by a sheet dividing underneath, by a knot of cells mineralizing and appearing elsewhere along the same seam, by spores released into ventilation currents at exactly the rate that other spores settle out.

Their dead do not accumulate. A paling stalk separates into membranes finer than paper. These lie flat, admit water, and become glassy grains. Later they may be drawn into a rootlike fiber, carried through a pumping station, deposited as scale inside a pipe, cut away by an automatic tool, melted, extruded, and returned to the marsh as part of a warm ceramic bank. It is difficult to say where the plant ended.

The machines are more readily mistaken for inhabitants because they perform recognizable gestures.

Cranes reach down. Doors admit trains. Presses strike. Inspection lights pass over finished surfaces. Small service vehicles emerge from wall recesses and travel in groups along marked lanes, slowing at crossings where nothing else is present. In the higher cities, articulated devices move beneath the towers on six or eight narrow legs, placing their feet carefully among the moss. They resemble animals only until one watches long enough. They never search, startle, hesitate, or choose a shorter path. If a strand of luminous reed grows across their route, they stop at the appointed distance. Somewhere else another device begins moving, and the total work continues unchanged.

No machine appears to command another. There are cabinets full of relays and processors, but no privileged chamber where the country becomes aware of itself. Signals pass through buried fiber, through pressure changes in pipes, through timed pulses in the electrical grid. A message may cross the plateau, alter a valve by a fraction, and return transformed to its point of origin. Yet it is never possible to isolate a request from a response. Each seems to have begun before the other.

The factories make components for the factories. Bearings leave one district and enter another. Worn bearings emerge elsewhere, not damaged but at an earlier stage of manufacture. A cutting head is sharpened as another is blunted. Whole bridge sections are cast, carried for months across the high plain, lowered into fog, and later seen entering a furnace as raw feedstock. The system repairs itself, but “repair” is misleading. It does not restore departures from an ideal condition. The departure is part of the condition.

If there are workers, they are these intervals: the hook descending, the wagon passing, the arc striking behind blue glass. Their bodies last only as long as the action. Then the action occurs elsewhere.

Around the central basin, the distinction between vegetation and machinery weakens further. Translucent reeds enter grilles and continue as insulated cable. Moss sends pale filaments into concrete, where they join capillary channels laid down when the concrete was poured. Foundry towers vent mineral vapor that settles as living skin on distant cooling ponds. Some conduits branch with the regularity of roots; some roots carry current with the regularity of conduits. Neither has invaded the other. They appear to have been specified together.

The most elaborate organisms may be the stationary walls of fog. They possess boundaries, circulation, and a kind of metabolism. Warm exhaust enters low, cold droplets descend, and water leaves through gutters without the wall shifting from its place. It reacts to every local change by distributing that change through millions of replacements. One can walk into it and feel its minute processes collecting on the face and hands. One can walk out the other side, look back, and see no evidence that it was entered.

Nothing here observes in the ordinary sense. There are lenses above doors, sensor arrays on masts, polished apertures moving along rails. They register heat, stress, water level, alignment, chemical proportion. But no image seems to be retained as an image. A traveler passing beneath a lens becomes a temporary adjustment in illumination, drainage, load balance, and airflow. The country does not identify the traveler. It accommodates the disturbance until the disturbance has been translated into its cycles.

This may be the closest thing it has to recognition.

Near the shaft, where the deep note enters bone and teeth, even individual processes become hard to name. Cables descend and return as different cables. Water falls, rises as mist, condenses on the black columns, and falls again. White lights move far below, sometimes outlining an arrangement like scaffolding, sometimes a crystalline wall, sometimes an enormous ribbed surface that might be growing or being machined. Each glimpse suggests a body. None lasts long enough to establish one.

Perhaps there is no resident in the singular. Perhaps the whole plateau is occupied by innumerable brief residents: each droplet while it falls, each railcar while it crosses a span, each green joint while it holds water, each pulse while it passes through a buried line. They come into local existence, perform one portion of an exchange, and cease without leaving a vacancy.

Or perhaps there is only one inhabitant, too distributed to possess a point of view. Its mountains are not its skeleton, nor are the cities its organs; those comparisons imply a body assembled from specialized parts. Here every part can become material for another, and every function is balanced elsewhere. The inhabitant would be not the plateau, not the machinery, not the marsh, but the exact relation among them—the invariant that survives all their motion.

It has no face because a face is an exposed history. It has no hunger because nothing can be removed from it. It has no offspring because it is always at the moment before succession becomes necessary.

Its only behavior is continuation.

At the viewing platform, droplets gather on the rail. One swells until it can no longer hold its shape. It falls into the shaft.

At the same instant, though far beyond sight, another droplet forms.
