At first there seem to be no inhabitants.

There are movements, but all can be assigned to currents. A ribbon folds around an unseen obstruction and opens again. A patch of velvet darkens in five long marks, then slowly returns to green. Hollow reeds incline one after another, preserving a passage for something that cannot be seen. In the air chambers, pressed leaves have been rearranged since the last inversion, though no fragment is torn and no sediment disturbed.

Only after remaining still for a very long time does one begin to distinguish them from the growth.

They are low, wide beings, built for a world in which weight may arrive from any direction. Most have five or seven limbs arranged around a central body, but neither number is reliable. A limb that finds no use may flatten, green, and become a leaf; a root-vein may thicken over years until it serves as an arm. Their bodies have no permanent upper side. What appears to be a back can open into a soft, many-folded mouth, while the old mouth seals and develops a clear lid through which they perceive pressure.

Their skin resembles the plateau: red-brown, smooth, and filmed with living color. Colonies of velvet growth inhabit them from birth, changing from olive to violet wherever their muscles tighten. Thus their gestures often appear on the skin before the limbs move. A ring of darkness passes across one flank; several minutes later, the creature turns. Pale lines gather around a hand; afterward, it reaches toward the stone.

The oldest are nearly indistinguishable from worn boulders. Forests grow from them. Mineral shelves form along their sides. Small transparent animals occupy chambers in their limbs and leave their molts beneath the skin. Such an elder may remain in one place for centuries, speaking only by altering the flow around itself. A traveler who rests against it may not discover until years later that the “rock” answered.

They have no name that survives translation into sound. Their communications are made of pressure, posture, temperature, and delay. One presses a palm to stone; another, several kilometers away, feels the answer through a root mat. A question may consist of warming a current by less than a degree. Refusal is expressed by becoming perfectly streamlined and permitting the water to pass without alteration.

They are patient, but not because they experience time slowly. They experience too many portions of it at once.

A young one may remember an air chamber that has not yet been built. An elder may grieve for a companion who is present and will remain present for another thousand years, because the absence has already reached them. They do not expect causes to precede consequences, and find no contradiction in tending a wound before the body that will receive it has arrived. When footprints begin deepening in the moss, they gather nearby—not to discover who made them, but to welcome whoever eventually will.

This makes their settlements difficult to recognize.

The low stone circles are not houses. They are places where events are encouraged to meet in the proper order. Within one circle, a traveler may recall departing before deciding to leave. In another, spores adhere to the water itself and grow into a temporary floor. The inhabitants sit around these boundaries with their limbs touching the stone, gently resisting whatever sequence the landscape attempts to impose.

The leaned-slab chambers are used during periods when a body requires only one time at once. Those containing air serve the young, the injured, and the dying. In air, their broad forms collapse inward. Their skins wrinkle and darken. They become smaller, firmer creatures, supported for the first time by bones concealed beneath their soft tissues.

They do not breathe air well. They enter it because sound behaves differently there.

Within the sealed chambers, they make voices.

The voices are frail, dry things: clicks of palate and stone, small exhalations, the rubbing of one damp limb against another. To an outsider the result would seem scarcely more elaborate than rain beginning on leaves. To them, accustomed to meanings spread through kilometers of water, the confinement is exquisite. A word can remain close to the one who spoke it. A reply can follow instead of surrounding it. For a little while, they may tell a story from beginning to end.

No two leave an air chamber remembering the story in quite the same sequence.

The layers of pressed leaves are their records. Each leaf has grown under a particular pressure and bears that pressure in its veins. Set one against the skin and an arrangement of currents returns: a migration, an overturning, the approach of a broad pale grazer. Some records preserve individual gestures. Others contain centuries of warm fronts combing one region of forest. The plants that renew the leaves are not cultivated in the ordinary sense. They are descended from limbs voluntarily shed by the record-keepers, and perhaps remain part of them.

Whether the inhabitants are animals or vegetation is a question the sea has worn away.

They begin as black spores during the intervals of darkness. Most settle and become ordinary growth: ribbons, moss, hollow stalks, parasols drifting sideways. A few fall upon stone circles or the bodies of elders. These do not root immediately. They gather dust, form small radial shapes, and wait for a pressure-pattern that may take decades to return.

When it comes, they loosen themselves and crawl.

For their first years they are delicate, almost transparent. They hide inside reed clusters, moving only when no gaze or attention rests upon them. The clear creatures said to inhabit the reeds may be their young, or merely the molts from which the young have escaped. Observation presses heavily upon an unfinished body here. A young one looked at too directly may become fixed in the form in which it was seen, unable to grow except along that description. The adults therefore protect them by misdirection. They bend stalks in distant thickets, darken moss without touching it, and send false wakes through the water.

As they mature, they undertake long crossings of the plateau. They travel without roads, though the avoiding reeds open corridors for them. They feed rarely. A hand is spread over velvet growth; microscopic layers of the plant pass into the skin, leaving the field apparently untouched. At mineral wells they absorb iron and chalk, hardening internal rings that serve as ballast when down changes direction.

Each carries several smooth stones within its body. These are not tools but companions to thought. When the sea overturns, the stones shift through internal chambers and tell the inhabitant which direction its oldest memories consider downward. On reaching a settlement, one stone may be removed and fitted into a circle. In this way their structures are assembled out of pieces carried inside generations of bodies.

They do not shape stone by striking it. They hold it.

A group may gather around a slab and maintain a minute, continuous pressure for fifty years. Rootlets enter the stone’s microscopic faults. Mineral water is invited into some seams and kept from others. Eventually the slab separates along the desired plane, with no mark of force upon it. Pillars are produced in the same manner, though some are begun by inhabitants who will not live to see them stand. Their descendants inherit the posture needed to finish the work.

The immense shadows over the troughs trouble them.

They do not worship these crossings, but they prepare for them. Forest corridors close. Young ones root temporarily to the underside of stones. Elders lift their limbs from the plateau and make themselves thin. Sometimes the shadow passes without consequence. Sometimes, years before it passes, an entire settlement abandons its circles and migrates. Sometimes it does not pass at all, yet the inhabitants continue behaving for generations as though something enormous had gone overhead.

They call such things by a pressure-sign meaning both predator and missing country.

The pale grazers are treated differently. When one approaches, the inhabitants arrange themselves among the moss fields and allow it to feed across their backs. The grazer removes old growth, parasites, mineral crust, and portions of memory that have become too dense. Afterward the inhabitants are brighter in color and uncertain of their own age. They may greet old companions as strangers. This is considered a kind of renewal, though not everyone submits to it.

There may be dissent among them, but it rarely takes the form of conflict. Their deepest disagreement concerns the edge.

Some believe the suspended sea is gradually forgetting how to remain above the clouds. They study the trembling boundary and construct circles near it, hoping to teach the water the shape of its own continuance. They press their leaf-limbs against the invisible division alongside the horizontal forests and feel rain falling far below.

Others believe the sea is not failing but preparing to overturn completely—to exchange places with the atmosphere, raising the low world through its depths while the plateau enters open sky. These inhabitants spend centuries in air chambers, strengthening their hidden bones and practicing the brief, costly act of breathing.

A few have crossed.

This is not witnessed. Now and then, after a mountain-sized bulge withdraws from the edge, a radial hollow remains among the plants. The reeds avoid it. The moss darkens in the pattern of limbs that are no longer there. Far beneath, shepherds of the low world may later speak of a warm rain falling from an empty sky, carrying black spores and one smooth red stone.

Most remain.

They tend the structures whose purposes have not happened yet. They guide root curtains away from cold wells. They gather in circles when the plateau begins its inaudible tone, arranging their bodies until the stones inside them tremble in sympathy. During inversions they release their holds and drift among uprooted forests, neither swimming nor falling, each keeping contact with another by one long, thinning limb.

When weight settles, they descend—or rise—to the worn plain. They root briefly. Color returns to their skins. Around them the vegetation begins the immense labor of untangling.

Then, across hundreds of kilometers, pressure passes from body to body: not an alarm, not a command, but an acknowledgment.

The sea is still here.

For now, so are they.
