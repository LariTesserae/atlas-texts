The first inhabitants one notices are the gardens, because they occupy the space in the manner of settlements.

Each thicket is a compact commonwealth fastened to a fracture in the stone. The largest bodies stand at its center: low trunks plated in resin, broad solvent-bearing fans, swollen bladders in which metallic vapors condense and circulate. Around them crowd lesser forms—thread-fronds, mineral combs, blind cups turned toward the heat. They are separate organisms, perhaps, but separation means little here. Their dry roots are braided together. Resin seals one body to another. A mineral taken up at the edge of a garden may pass through a dozen sealed vessels before reaching its center.

They live on gradients too slight for surface life to notice: sulfur against iron, warm rock against slightly warmer rock, the faint opposing charges on either side of a fissure. They require no rain and possess nothing comparable to thirst. Their fluids are waxes and thin oils trapped beneath skins as impermeable as glazed pottery. Their pores do not open. Their leaves do not breathe. What appears to be foliage is partly refinery, partly battery, partly armor.

Yet their abundance is not growth. Each garden is a finished abundance, held at the point where every shoot promises to uncurl. They may have no childhood or old age. A seed case does not mature into a new plant; it performs maturity, taut and whistling, forever. A fallen frond is not truly dead. It has merely taken up the fixed office of litter.

Upon these fronds live the pale scavengers.

They are almost transparent, with soft bodies divided into many shallow rings. Their mouths are rasps finer than dust. They consume the outermost resin skin of fallen leaves, but the quantity eaten reappears beneath them so exactly that their feeding tracks remain the same length. Thus each scavenger owns, or is imprisoned within, a single meal. Some have been halfway through the same amber blister for longer than the shelters have stood.

The shell-backed grazers are livelier. Six feet clasp the undersides of fans; a seventh appendage, often mistaken for a leg, folds under the head and bears the mineral tooth. Their eyespots are not eyes in the ordinary sense. The concentric markings register heat, charge, and vibration. A grazer knows the passing of a glider as pressure through its shell and the approach of the narrow-jawed hunter as a brief misalignment in the garden’s electrical field.

They do not flee far. Panic would be useless in a world where every pursuit has already acquired its interval. When the hunter comes, the selected grazer releases its hold at the appointed instant. The jaws close. Elsewhere, beneath a resin fold, another shell rises into view with its eyespots already marked and its tooth already worn to the proper length.

Whether this is birth, replacement, or the return of the same animal cannot be determined inside the hollow.

The cord-creatures inhabit the spaces between branches. They are long, jointed things with no wings and almost no weight. Fine conductive hairs fringe their segments, allowing them to hang along lines of charge as surface insects might stand upon water. They climb without touching anything. Their clicking is both locomotion and orientation: each segment snaps to a slightly different potential, so that the whole animal advances through the air one invisible rung at a time.

They have mouths, but feeding has never been seen. They may take nourishment directly from the chamber’s fields. They may exist chiefly to make the clicking that occupies certain volumes of the cavern. Remove one from its line and the surrounding cords shift until the same pattern of sound is restored.

In the cracks sit the wedge-headed burrowers. Their bodies are broader than their entrances, folded behind them like fans. Blunt forelimbs strike the rock in measured sequences. The blows are not excavation; their holes never deepen. Nor are they courtship, for no seasons distinguish one sequence from another. The burrowers seem to be sounding the stone, listening through their jaws for the minute internal reports of heat and fracture.

They always stop one blow before a straight fissure hums.

This makes them the chamber’s most reliable guides. The old visitors copied their rhythms in rows of dots and learned to cross only after particular pauses. A burrower lifted from its hole continues striking empty air. If placed in another crack, it does not adopt the rhythm of that location. It strikes for the hole from which it was taken, as though distance were merely another kind of obstruction.

The velvety colonies are harder to classify. Individually they resemble warm fruits with dark nap and a slight seam. In groups they share heat, tension, and appetite. One opening its mouth causes all the others to open, including those in distant gardens. Their mouths contain neither teeth nor tongues, only a dull red membrane that gathers aromatic molecules from the air.

They may be the chamber’s noses.

When bruised herbs are brought through the entrance, every colony opens. When blood is spilled, they remain closed. When someone holds the black liquid near them, their mouths open slowly and do not shut until the cup is returned.

Above all these pass the gliders.

There are six to the eye, seven to some older reckoning. Pale, broad-winged, and hollow-boned, they cling to the ceiling only at points along their circuit. Their wings contain resonating chambers divided by thin membranes, and the steady warm updraft plays through them as through buried horns. They do not beat their wings because they never need to correct their path. The cavern’s convection is as fixed as a carved track.

Each glider follows the one before it at an exact distance. But shadows occasionally reveal an extra interval: a seventh place in the procession where dust bends, clicking ceases, and the low drone acquires another tone without another body appearing. The ancient wall sign may therefore be a count not of animals but of positions. Six gliders inhabit seven appointed passages, and the empty passage may be as real a creature as the others.

The narrow-jawed hunter lives among the roots. It is spare, plated, and almost motionless, with forelimbs folded close to its throat. Its eyes have sealed over. It hunts by count.

Scrape, click, knock, boom: the chamber’s noise accumulates within it until a particular total is reached. Then the hunter unfolds, crosses three root braids, takes one grazer, and returns. It does not choose the weak, the near, or the unwary. It takes whichever animal occupies the required place in the pattern. Its hunger may not be bodily hunger at all. It may be one more mechanism by which the cavern preserves its number.

The people who built the shelters were not permanent inhabitants in the same way. They came down as gatherers—small bands carrying baskets, flint, ash, bone, herbs, and fear. They harvested resin that renewed without increasing, shell that could be removed without reducing the grazers, and fronds whose cut edges sealed before the knife completed its stroke. They learned not to sleep across the straight fissures. They learned that fires leaned east. They learned that words spoken inside the silent circle could not be heard, but sometimes appeared later as scratches on the work block.

Most importantly, they learned that departure was not always singular.

A person could enter, gather, and climb out carrying what had been taken. Yet on certain descents—perhaps after passing beneath the unoccupied seventh circuit, perhaps after looking into the cup—something of that person remained. Not a ghost and not a corpse. A continuation.

The wall image of the two identical figures is a warning against counting only those who return.

One such remainder may still occupy the rearmost shelter. The mat retains the warmth of a body without showing its weight. Aromatic fronds have been bundled as if for sleep. The single footprint in the dust has toes shaped by a lifetime of climbing ladders, but no corresponding heel mark, as though its maker had begun to step into the chamber and had not yet completed the act.

This inhabitant cannot be seen directly because it may not yet have fully arrived. It exists in the same arrested condition as the half-unrolled shoots: not absent, not present, but held at the first instant of presence. The tools beside the mat belong to it. The warmth belongs to it. Perhaps the unseen seventh path belongs to it as well.

At the center lives the quietest being.

The five-leaved plant does not participate in the commonwealths around it. It feeds through unbroken stone, makes no resin, offers no charge, and enters none of the animals’ fixed exchanges. Its leaves resemble an open hand, but also a hand from which something has been removed. The clay cup rests where a flower or organ ought to be.

It may be a plant carrying a vessel. It may be five living walls grown around the liquid. It may be the chamber’s only true seed.

The black contents show what every other inhabitant is denied: consequence. Overgrowth. Decay. Soil. Silence. The vision is not necessarily a prophecy. It may be an excretion, a concentrated waste product containing all the changes the hollow refuses to permit. Everything that should have aged, eroded, reproduced, died, or become otherwise may have been drawn into that level black surface.

If so, the cup is full of the chamber’s missing time.

Around it, the gardens strain without unfolding. Grazers consume without diminishing. Hunters kill without reducing. Gliders travel without arriving. An unseen person completes neither entrance nor departure.

They are not immortal in the triumphant sense. Immortality suggests survival across time. These beings scarcely possess time to survive. They inhabit recurrence, each confined to a gesture the cavern continually restores.

What lives there is therefore not a collection of ageless creatures so much as a complete ecology caught in the act of beginning: innumerable lives, warm and ravenous and intricately dependent, all held one breath before becoming different.
