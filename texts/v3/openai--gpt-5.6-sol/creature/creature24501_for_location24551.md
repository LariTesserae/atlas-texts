Those who live there are, at first glance, merely workers.

They wear compact pressure suits patched at elbows, knees, and the soft rings under the jaw. No two suits remain the color in which they were issued. Their shells have been dulled by silt and stippled with mineral growth; their helmets carry handwritten marks so they can recognize one another at the short distance their lamps allow. Some paint a white bar above the visor. Some hang a brass washer from the throat assembly or braid colored wire around an air hose. These distinctions are small, but in the flooded lanes a person is otherwise only a dark volume interrupting the dark.

They move carefully, though not timidly. One hand usually remains on a guide rail. The other carries a tool case, sample cage, suction wand, or tether. Their feet find familiar steps before their lights do. They know which pipes are safe to touch and which have insulation thin enough to burn through a glove. They know the places where the current folds back on itself, where a loose cable may tighten around an ankle during the gate cycle, where the silt conceals an old stair descending farther than the plans permit.

They have the dense, practical knowledge of people whose home can kill them through a hundred minor failures. A change in vibration wakes them. A lamp flickering in the next compartment may not. They can distinguish the pressure pulse of a clogged separator from that of a pump beginning to seize. Many carry little notebooks sealed in waxed skins, although every measurement is entered into the town’s wired system. Paper is trusted differently. On it they record things the instruments reject: a metallic taste in recycled air, crustaceans abandoning a warm main, tapping heard at the wrong depth, the central wheel gaining three degrees while no one watched.

Their bodies have adapted less dramatically than rumor claims, but adaptation is visible. They are slight, partly because food and living space favor smallness, partly because heavy labor belongs to machines. Their shoulders are rounded from hatches and low ceilings. Their faces are pale under amber light, with the bruised shadows of people who sleep near running equipment. Most have scarred eardrums. Older residents pause when crossing pressure thresholds and swallow in a characteristic sequence, as unconscious as blinking.

Their hands matter most. Indoors, in the few dry compartments, gloves come off and reveal fingers callused in narrow bands where suit controls press against them. Nails are kept very short. Skin cracks despite the humidity, whitened by disinfectant and brine. A good mechanic can identify a failing bearing through three layers of housing by laying two fingertips against the wall. Children learn the same sensitivity by touching cups to pipes and guessing which fluid passes beneath.

There are children, though outsiders sometimes fail to imagine them.

They are born in the warm habitation cylinders near the exchangers, where the walls sweat and the air smells faintly of iron, rubber, boiled cloth, and algae. For their first years the sea is chiefly a pressure outside the rooms. They know it through diagrams, through the groan of seals, through the slow parade of suited adults past thick windows. Their earliest toys are retired valve handles, blunt gasket rings, ceramic spools, and instrument housings certified free of toxins. They build model streets that always have roofs.

Before being allowed into flooded public spaces, each child practices in a shallow training cistern. They learn not to kick up silt, not to release both hands from the rail, not to turn suddenly toward another person’s lamp. They learn the emergency markings as children elsewhere learn letters. White chevrons mean air. Paired circles mean heat refuge. A broken blue line means a wall that once held and may not again.

They also learn the other rules, the ones printed in the same plain type.

Adults do not tell them these are superstitions. Superstition implies an alternative explanation. Here the rules are treated as accumulated engineering: procedures derived from incidents whose full reports may have been lost, sealed, or never written. A child who asks why no one follows a light that copies their motion is shown how to test whether a reflection is fixed to glass. If the light is not fixed to glass, the lesson ends.

The settlement has no single ruler in the ordinary sense. Authority follows systems. The heat chief has final say near the boreholes. The pressure keeper can empty any room without appeal. The gate crews govern the nine minutes of current. Archivists control access to the lower surveys and the lead-lined drawers, though they are mocked until their services are needed. Every shift appoints a wheel witness, usually the least senior qualified person, whose duty is to observe the white handle, compare it with the previous marks, and sign the discrepancy.

No one enjoys being wheel witness.

The inhabitants speak softly even in air. Their language has been pared down by throat microphones, static, and the need to be understood during equipment failure. Long descriptions are compressed into work terms. A pipe is not merely hot but *awake*. A passage newly blocked by sediment is *closed by weather*. A machine giving correct readings while behaving incorrectly is *polite*. Something glimpsed twice beyond the boundary lamps is *persistent*. Someone overdue but still visible on a monitor is not called missing; they are *elsewhere in the system*.

Silence is not awkward among them. It is cooperative. People share meals with only the scrape of utensils and the faint transmission of pumps through the table. Affection appears as maintenance: a suit laid out with warmed seals, a lamp lens polished, a preferred tool returned sharpened, a berth moved half a meter farther from a noisy valve. Courtship often begins with volunteered tether duty. Mourning is quieter still. The dead cannot always be recovered, and so their names are stamped onto thin metal strips fixed to the warmest communal pipe. Mineral scale slowly covers the lettering. Families scrape it clean for a generation or two. Eventually they stop.

There are very old residents who have not entered a dry room in years. Their lungs no longer tolerate the settlement’s recycled air, or so they say. They inhabit personal diving shells connected to the warm-water network, sleeping upright in flooded alcoves. Food reaches them through ports. Their voices come through wall speakers, thin and intimate. They remember routes now sealed, workshops before the concrete was poured, exterior towers that stood briefly above the retaining wall. They are consulted about old noises.

Their memories do not agree.

One says the shaft beyond the wall was present when the first habitation cylinder arrived. Another remembers seeing it drilled. A third insists there were lamps descending around its inner edge, green and evenly spaced, until the town’s gate cycle changed and all of them went out at once.

Alongside the people live the accepted lesser populations.

The blind crustaceans in the insulation are tolerated unless they begin migrating. Their movements serve as rough thermal maps. Workers sometimes find them gathered in dense white knots around a pipe that instruments declare cold. Such pipes are isolated immediately.

The algae in old lamps are cultivated without admitting that they are cultivated. A technician will leave one cracked lens unrepaired because its colony has remained green through three years of normal operation. The algae turn yellow in the presence of certain dissolved metals and clear when the water contains compounds the laboratories have not identified. During some dark cycles, every colony in the district contracts simultaneously to a pinprick, though water samples show no change.

The red microbial mats around the outlets are harvested, dried, and used in filters. Under magnification their threads contain metallic grains arranged at regular intervals. They grow in rectangles even when no one crops them. The oldest patches have edges aligned not with the current or the present streets, but with the buried hard surfaces detected beneath the basin.

There are also the silver fish. No one knows whether there is one fish seen repeatedly or a population whose members are indistinguishable. They appear singly. They carry no obvious parasites, show no reproductive organs, and have never been caught in a filter or net. A fish passing a viewport will sometimes stop, holding itself perfectly still against the current, its narrow body reflecting a worker’s lamp as a line of white fire. Then it turns edge-on and vanishes without swimming away.

The workers call them knives and do not eat anything on a day one is seen.

Beyond these are the inhabitants that cannot be entered cleanly in a census.

Something touches the outer glass. Its marks are too broad for the silver fish and too deliberate for drifting debris. Circular impressions appear in sequences, often around ports behind which someone is sleeping. They fade over several hours as the glass relaxes under pressure.

Something handles objects in unlit passages. It rarely takes them. Instead it makes corrections. A wrench left crosswise on a ledge is found parallel to the wall. Three bolts become four. A sample cage is neatly closed though its latch requires opposing thumbs. Once, a length of discarded cable was woven around a rail in the same knot the gate crews use, but tied backward, as though learned from the far side of a window.

And something makes lights.

The first explanation is always reflection. The second is electrical discharge, bioluminescence, helmet glare, instrument fatigue, or stress. Only after these are eliminated does the observer use the prescribed phrase: *responsive illumination outside mapped works*.

Such a light is weak and usually the same color as the observer’s lamp. It appears beyond a corner, across the pale apron, or deep among the pipes where no passage remains. If the observer raises a hand, the light rises. If the observer shades their lamp, it dims. The delay is small but not constant. Sometimes it copies a movement before the person has completed it.

No one follows.

At least, no one now living admits to having followed.

The lower sluices may have inhabitants of their own. Cameras sent into them return images of polished walls, then static, then rooms too broad to fit beneath the surveyed foundations. Hydrophones record soft impacts traveling against the flow. The objects that emerge—shell gears, bone couplings, pale flexible pieces with the shape of manufactured parts—show wear at points where no modern machine would contact them. Some are warm when retrieved. A few continue to flex in the lead-lined drawers.

The archivists classify them as artifacts because specimens are not permitted to be alive without signs of metabolism.

This distinction comforts no one.

It is possible that the town itself is becoming an inhabitant.

Its circulatory systems cross generations of construction. Modern pipes feed ancient sluices; ancient pressure changes turn modern turbines. Microbial films seal hairline cracks. Crustaceans clear soft deposits from insulation seams. Human crews replace valves and scrape filters, responding to readings produced by instruments mounted on walls whose foundations no one understands. Heat, waste, minerals, bodies, and information pass through the district in linked cycles. No one designed the whole. No one can identify a boundary where the settlement stops being machinery and becomes habitat, or where habitat becomes organism.

The slow wheel may be a valve. It may be a gauge. It may be driven by pressure changes too subtle for the town’s sensors. Its shaft descends through the valve court floor into masonry older than the visible district, and attempts to trace it have found only more casing, more mineral crust, more depth.

Each shift marks the white handle’s position.

Each shift calculates where it should next appear.

Each shift finds that it has moved slightly too far, or not far enough, as if another calculation is being made elsewhere and the wheel is obeying the difference between them.

So the inhabitants continue their work. They tend the brine, clear the streets, raise children, number the impossible objects, and warm their hands against pipes laid by the dead. Around them live blind feeders, warning algae, ordered colonies, passing knives, and patient things that have learned the town’s lights and knots.

Beneath them, perhaps, are other streets.

And in the pressure walls, in the sluices, in the buried geometry and the turning wheel, there may live something for which the compact drowned district is not a city at all, but a small warm organ newly grown at the upper edge of its body.
