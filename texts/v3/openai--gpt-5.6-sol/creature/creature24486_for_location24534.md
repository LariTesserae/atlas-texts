The first inhabitants are the ones that require almost nothing except water and an interval.

Mosquitoes hatch in the cupped impressions of tires, in the rain collected on folded tarpaulins, in the collars of survey pipes. Their nurseries may be no broader than a boot sole and may vanish under the next wheel, but until then they are complete worlds. The larvae hang beneath the surface like dark commas, descending together when a shadow passes. Midges rise from the settling ponds in quantities too large to be understood as separate bodies. Around the lamps they become wavering fabric, drawn tight by wind and loosened again.

The frogs live wherever the water has paused. There are tiny ones the color of drying clay, yellowish ones among the reeds, green ones bright as exposed wire, and heavy dark frogs that sit half buried at culvert mouths. Their skins take on the basin’s mineral palette. Rust-red mud dries across their backs; blue-black silt fills the folds of their legs. By daylight they are almost invisible until they jump, when a whole margin may suddenly break apart and throw itself into the water.

Their abundance gives them an air of permanence they do not possess. One week a warm rut is crowded with eggs and trembling tadpoles; the next it has been filled with aggregate. Yet enough pools survive. Enough drainage channels remain connected for a night. The frogs move through hoses, beneath pontoons, along the wet undersides of concrete slabs. They breed faster than the maps can be revised. At dusk their calls overlap until no single throat can be distinguished. The sound seems to issue from the mud itself.

Dragonflies arrive after them. They use survey poles and warning stakes as perches, turning their heads toward every passing gnat. Their transparent wings catch colors absent from the rest of the basin—brief greens, coppers, and petrol blues. Below them, water beetles carry trapped points of silver under their bodies. Skaters tremble on films streaked with fuel. Predatory larvae inhabit sumps beside lost washers, cigarette filters, and curls of orange paint. They grow armored and patient in water that is never quite still.

Fish occupy the deeper excavations, though no one can say with certainty how all of them entered. Some came down canals during floods. Some were swept from farm ponds. Some were released by workers unwilling to let a bucket’s catch die on the road. They are small, pale-sided, tolerant fish, shaped by murk and low oxygen. They gather where pump outflows stir the surface. When a floodlight passes over a sump, their turns flash briefly beneath the brown water, revealing a population where there seemed to be only sediment.

At culvert mouths live crayfish, crabs, and things mistaken for both. Their burrows weaken already soft banks. Their chimneys rise overnight beside cable trays and are crushed by morning traffic. They feed on drowned insects, algae, scraps from lunch containers, and one another. When channels are pumped low, they walk across the exposed bottom with their claws lifted, searching for the next connected darkness.

The birds read the excavation differently. To them the berms are islands, the ponds an archipelago, the floodlights a weather system.

Gulls occupy the highest spoil ridges and face into the warm drafts from machinery. They pry at frogs, fish, food wrappers, and pale grubs exposed by fresh digging. Terns hover above the clearer sumps. Sandpipers and black-winged waders follow new shorelines created by each change in pumping, their fine beaks entering mud laid down only hours before. Swallows stitch the open air beneath the cables. They skim so close to the ponds that their reflections seem to pursue them.

A few birds attempt nests. They choose gravel pads, pipe stacks, the sheltered angles behind barriers. Their eggs resemble stones and are often noticed only after traffic has been diverted around them. Sometimes workers drive stakes and tape a square of ground where a bird sits stubbornly amid engines and alarms. The protected patch remains for weeks: a tiny jurisdiction of feathers in the middle of the works.

Rats inhabit the dry underside of the basin. They know which pallets will not be moved, which workshops still contain food, which concrete rings connect to warm voids. Their paths run beneath hoses and along the backs of retaining walls. They feed at night around the control cabin, sleek with moisture, their whiskers jeweled by condensation. Pump vibrations pass continually through their nests. Young rats are born accustomed to the tremor.

The feral dogs rarely descend far. They keep to the upper ramps and fenced service roads, appearing as silhouettes in mist. Some are lean and mud-streaked; others wear old collars. They watch the birds and the distant workers but distrust the soft ground. Their territories follow the rim, where warehouses provide shelter and discarded meals. When they bark, the sound enters the excavation and returns from several directions, provoking other dogs that may be miles away—or only echoes.

Human beings live there too, though the basin tends to conceal them. They are fewer than their lights suggest.

Pump attendants sleep in prefabricated cabins with damp clothing hung above heaters. Mechanics work in pairs beneath temporary awnings, passing tools with gloved hands slick from rain and grease. Surveyors appear on the berms carrying instruments in bright cases, then stand motionless while the ground shifts invisibly under their tripods. Dredge operators sit behind clouded glass, moving enormous arms by minute controls. Night crews travel between isolated pools of light in vehicles whose wheels are taller than the frogs’ entire world.

They acquire the basin’s habits. They speak in raised voices even after entering quiet rooms. They test every railing before leaning on it. They keep spare socks sealed in plastic and learn which apparent roads are crusts over slurry. Mud establishes itself in their vehicles, lockers, beds, and kitchens. It dries in layers on boot soles, each layer belonging to a different depth and color of earth.

No one calls the place home without qualification. People say they are on shift, on contract, stationed there, waiting for the next phase. Some have waited for years. They know individual pumps by sound and can wake from sleep when one changes pitch. They recognize ponds by smell, gulls by their preferred roofs, and drainage seams by how quickly beads gather after rain. Their knowledge is intimate but provisional. A route used every day may disappear under spoil by Monday.

There are also organisms too small to announce themselves. Algae bloom in warm runoff. Pale fungi eat buried wood and wet cardboard. Bacteria inhabit sulfurous sumps and orange iron seeps, laying down slicks that resemble spilled oil. Liverwort spreads beneath pipe racks where boots cannot reach. Willow roots enter cracks in drains and follow water against its engineered direction. These quiet populations alter the place grain by grain, clogging filters, binding silt, softening timber, opening seams.

Nothing here lives independently. Insects depend on lamps and standing water. Swallows depend on insects. Rats depend on workers. Gulls follow machines that expose the mud. Frogs breed in the failed intentions of drainage. Sedges root where traffic has been redirected. Even the people depend on the pumps, and the pumps depend on people listening through the roar for the first wrong note.

Together they form a community without equilibrium. Its habitats are not forests, fields, or enduring shores, but delays: the month before a pipe is buried, the season before a berm is moved, the night before a trench is drained. Every inhabitant lives in borrowed time, yet borrowed time is the one resource the basin produces without end.

So life does not wait for the excavation to become a finished place. It crowds into incompletion. It lays eggs beside warning flags, roots under temporary bridges, nests in stacked materials, feeds in the wake of dredges, and sleeps above pipes carrying away the water that sustains it. The land is continually made unready, and its inhabitants continually make use of that unready state.
