At first it is difficult to tell which inhabitants are individuals.

The pillar-plants seem singular when seen from a distance: one trunk, one crown, one root mass vanishing into the architecture above and below. Closer inspection confuses this. A pillar is a congress of tissues that do not share quite the same ancestry. Its ceramic bark is cultivated by threadlike colonies living in the seams between plates. Its load-bearing core is braided from generations of downward-growing shoots. The luminous new bark belongs to the plant; the hardened outer plates may be secreted by symbiotic animals no larger than grains. Even the oils moving through its vessels are manufactured partly by root-dwelling organisms that never enter the light.

A pillar may therefore be wounded in one place, diseased in another, fertile in a third, and dead through the middle, while continuing to stand for centuries.

The oldest are called **worldstems** by the upright inhabitants. The name does not mean that they believe the plants hold up the world. It means that each one contains enough country to be a world.

A mature worldstem may support blade meadows, nesting shelves, resin bogs, shell plazas, and entire migratory routes. Its cavities have their own climates. Warm gases rise through the central chambers; cooler vapors descend along the bark. Animals breed in the crossing currents. Some never emerge. Pale herds move through the internal fibers, cropping soft vascular tissue and leaving behind tunnels lined with glossy dung-wax. Needle-faced hunters follow them by resting their jaws against the tunnel walls and listening for chewing.

Worldstems grow slowly by local standards, but they think with pressure. When burdened on one side, they stiffen there. When gnawed repeatedly, they poison the affected layer or abandon it. A bridge bough that receives regular traffic may broaden beneath generations of feet. One crossed only rarely may curl away and become inaccessible. Thus the forest appears to remember use, although no single part of it possesses the memory.

Above the worldstems hang the light membranes.

They are not leaves. Nor are they one creature, despite their apparent continuity. Each pale expanse consists of millions of translucent bodies fused edge to edge, their mineral skeletons thinner than shell and more flexible than skin. They feed from the thermal gradient between the cavern roof and the living country below. During bright periods they lie flat, passing excitation from body to body in long, slow waves. During dim periods they pleat themselves into cream-colored folds and digest the spores that have collected on their upper surfaces.

Occasionally a section detaches.

It does not fall. It folds into a loose bell and swims through the dense air by contracting its edges. For several days it drifts among the crowns, glowing more brightly as stored minerals are consumed. Swarms gather beneath it. Seeds open in its wake. Predators follow the swarms. At last the bell becomes thin and transparent, fastens itself to a high branch, and divides into a colony of small crawling disks. Most of these are eaten before they can reach the ceiling. A few climb, fuse, and begin another acre of afternoon.

The most numerous flying animals are the **sailbacks**. Each has six gripping limbs held close to a narrow, felted body and two broad pinions stiffened with hollow veins. They do not truly fly so much as surrender themselves expertly to the cavern’s rising gases. Their migrations begin when the light membranes brighten. Whole populations release their holds at once, turning the distances gray with wings.

Sailbacks graze by passing close over foliage and combing it with rows of solvent-wet bristles beneath the thorax. They remove only the leaf’s luminous skin. A single animal takes almost nothing; several million can strip a canopy from violet to bone-white in an hour.

Their wing roar masks nearly every other sound. In that cover, smaller creatures mate, hatch, migrate, and kill. When a sailback sheet passes, the forest briefly changes its rules.

Many animals exploit the noise, but one appears to feed on it.

The inhabitants call it a **listener’s widow**. It is flat, black-green, and nearly invisible when wrapped around a trunk. Along its flanks are chambers divided by taut membranes. Low vibrations make the chambers flex, driving chemical reactions in the lining. The louder the surroundings, the warmer and more alert the widow becomes.

It hunts by adding precise false notes to the general clamor. A tap that means *safe branch*. A tremor that resembles *feeding herd moving away*. The courtship rhythm of an armored drummer, repeated almost correctly.

Animals approach the signal. The widow opens lengthwise.

Its mouth is not at either end but runs down the entire underside, a pale seam lined with heated hooks. After feeding, it becomes noisy itself. Digestion makes its chambers knock and murmur for hours, accidentally warning the forest of its location. During that time fist-sized drummers gather around it and strike its distended body, feeding on the small organisms loosened from its hide. Some widows tolerate this cleaning. Others imitate a predator’s arrival and empty the branch in sudden silence.

The most feared hunters create no false noise at all.

These are the **hushes**, gliding animals that live among the highest crowns. A hush has a long, lightly jointed body bordered by reflective scales. Its mouth is a rigid piercing cone. Before it moves, it releases an odor nearly undetectable to the upright inhabitants but instantly recognized by much of the fauna.

The warning travels as bodies cease making sound.

Drummers lower their striking limbs. Browsers close their flank sacs. Sailbacks cling wherever they are. Even some plants retract rattling seed cups. Silence opens around the hush in a widening circle, revealing its position by absence.

It waits at the center, reading the smallest involuntary vibration. An egg turning in its capsule. A burrower swallowing. The tightening fibers of a frightened animal’s limb.

Then it glides.

A large hush can clear half a terrace without killing more than one creature. Its true effect is ecological: while the grazers hide, leaves expand; while the drummers are still, their parasites emerge; while flight stops, drifting spores settle. Certain flowers open only in a hush-circle. Their petals are so delicate that the normal noise would tear them.

Lower down live the animals that become terrain.

A young **vaultback** is mobile, though reluctantly so. It resembles a many-legged bowl covered in dark interlocking armor. It crawls into the gap between two trunks, braces itself there, and begins filtering the air. Over decades its legs lengthen into buttresses. Its back spreads. Its organs multiply into connected chambers, each keeping a different chemical environment. Mosses coat the shell; shrubs take root in the seams; burrowers excavate the less sensitive margins.

Eventually the vaultback cannot move without destroying itself. It may live another two hundred years.

The oldest bear whole settlements of lesser creatures. Their breathing vents are warm pits around which seed-eaters gather. Their mating season comes only a few times in a century. Then neighboring vaultbacks release clouds of metallic-smelling cells, and every exposed surface becomes slick with bronze dust. Small flying animals transport the cells between them. For several days the ancient plazas are, obscurely, courting.

When a vaultback dies, its interior heat fades first. The plants rooted on it flower in response. Its shell remains, spanning the trunks like a floor, while the soft internal partitions are eaten away. Many seemingly mineral terraces are vaultback remains from ages before the people’s oldest braces.

Inside the vegetation move the **rasp herds**.

Each animal is shorter than a forearm, wedge-headed and wrapped in flexible scales. But they travel in coordinated masses numbering hundreds of thousands. Their foreparts cut tunnels; those behind widen them; others press the walls smooth with resinous secretions. Specialized herd members carry eggs in hot chambers beneath their armor. Others do not eat at all but produce directional vibrations by snapping plates along their backs.

A herd inside a pillar sounds like distant grinding machinery. It can change the weight distribution of a worldstem, hollowing one side and packing another with hardened waste. Some pillars direct them by altering the taste of their tissues. Some kill them with volatile sap. Others seem to cultivate them, allowing the herd to remove dying wood and stimulate new vascular growth.

The relationship changes from season to season. Parasite, grazer, surgeon, structural catastrophe: the rasp herds are all of these, sometimes in the same week.

The regular pounding beneath the root vaults comes from something larger.

No upright inhabitant claims to have seen an entire one. Sections of it have been encountered: a pale armored surface under a collapsed terrace; a row of heated spiracles opening and shutting in the floor; a chisel structure as tall as a shelter, withdrawing into compacted salt. The people call these fragments **the Underbeat**, treating them as one animal for grammatical purposes, though they may belong to many.

The pounding is excavation. A deep body drives mineral teeth into the salt and basalt, then consumes the fractured rock for trace metals and reactive compounds. Its tunnels fill behind it with exhaled salts and discarded armor, so it cannot be followed. Worldstem roots invade the softened seams. Furnace-moss follows the roots. New chambers open where its feeding destabilizes the crust.

Copper is found most often in the Underbeat’s shed teeth, carried upward by smaller mineral feeders. Almost every copper tool in the inhabited country is therefore made from a part of an animal no one has seen alive.

The upright tool-users are small because the country punishes mass.

They stand roughly as high as the span of a sailback’s wing. Their bodies are narrow through the trunk, with two strong legs and two longer arms that can lock around branches during storms. A balancing limb folds against the spine when not in use and spreads into a reflective fan when they overheat. Their hides are covered in minute, overlapping scales, pale on the shoulders and dark along the belly. By lifting or flattening these scales, they can shed heat, preserve it, signal alarm, or break up their outlines against ceramic bark.

They have broad, lidless eyes protected by sliding translucent plates. Their nostrils are layered chemical filters, though not sufficient against every spore. Around the jaw grow stiff sensory filaments that detect air movement and low vibrations. Their mouths contain grinding pads rather than teeth. Food is shaved, powdered, mixed with resin-softening saliva, and pressed into dry pellets before swallowing.

They do not name themselves after their shape. Their word for themselves is closest to **those-who-answer**.

To answer is the foundation of their behavior. A branch flexes; they shift their weight. A pillar changes rhythm; they alter the brace. A plant closes a route; they seek another rather than cutting through unless necessity is severe. A herd announces itself through the wood; they strike back with warnings. Even conversation is partly conducted by touch. Two people speaking privately may sit several body lengths apart with their hands on the same taut cord.

Their spoken voices are quiet and percussive. In the surrounding roar, air-carried speech is unreliable. Personal names are short rhythms tapped on bark, bone, or another person’s forearm. Family histories are longer patterns. Important knowledge is encoded in work songs hammered into braces, ladders, and platforms. Children learn geography as a sequence of pulses: hollow trunk, live root, vaultback shell, unstable braid, safe wedge.

The best listeners press forked sounding sticks into the bark and become still enough that tiny ballooning animals settle on them. They can distinguish a feeding swarm from a structural split miles away along a connected scaffold. They hear storms gathering as pressure tremors. They detect the Underbeat in their bones.

Such listeners guide settlements, but they are not rulers. No one can command a route to remain open or a worldstem to stand. Authority belongs to whoever correctly understands what is about to move.

Their settlements are temporary even when ancient.

A cluster of six woven chambers may occupy the same terrace for generations, yet every basket is designed to be cut loose within moments. Seed stores are divided among separate roots. Heat bulbs hang in ceramic nets so they cannot roll into dry growth. Children sleep in inward-facing pouches protected by bitter-smelling fibers. Every settlement keeps at least two escape lines and one sacrificial bridge meant to be severed behind fleeing inhabitants.

At its center is often a brace older than any resident.

These braces are revered not because they are unchanging but because they have changed successfully. The best are alive in places. Worldstem tissue has grown around their joints, carrying oil through channels once carved by tools. Leaves emerge from beams made by forgotten hands. Animal nests occupy the hollow pegs. To repair such a structure, a brace-worker must decide which part remains artifact, which part has become plant, and whether the distinction still matters.

Brace-workers test each addition for days. They warm the intended joint, apply resin, tap invitation patterns, and wait. If the trunk swells against the beam, it has refused. If it opens soft seams around the contact point, it may accept. Some workers maintain that plants recognize skill. Others say only that skilled people notice the answer sooner.

The dead are seldom buried. There is little stable ground, and an untreated body would become hot dust within hours. Instead, the dead are dried in seed smoke, wrapped in fired bark, and placed within a chosen brace. Their fibrous flesh slowly cures into the structure. Thus ancestors literally support paths, dwellings, and listening platforms.

This is not considered a return to nature. Nothing here has ever left nature far enough to return.

Around all these larger lives swarm the uncounted ones: cup-mouths that drink resin vapor; salt lickers with heated tongues; translucent young that hatch inside blue fire; mites that graze reflective scales; ribbon animals living in pollen clouds; seed mimics that wait to be carried and then bite; flowers that borrow the alarm scent of the hush; blind grazers that spend their entire lives walking around the rim of one breathing vent.

Every inhabitant is habitat to another.

A sailback wing bears parasites, and those parasites carry spores, and the spores unfold into traps for smaller parasites. A sounding stick acquires bark-mites that alter its tone. A filter mask develops a living rind that may improve its protection or germinate through the wearer’s face. An abandoned heat bulb becomes an incubator. A copper blade draws mineral-eating larvae from across a settlement.

Nothing is merely owned. Nothing is merely discarded.

And no creature, not even the answering people, can be understood alone. The true inhabitant may be the exchange itself: heat entering flesh, flesh becoming vapor, vapor becoming leaf, leaf becoming wing, wing becoming shell, shell becoming road.

The buried country does not contain an ecosystem in the way a cavern contains air.

It is a multitude continually making the conditions for the next multitude. The worldstems brace the fracture; the Underbeat widens it. The membranes make light; wings distribute it as shadow and movement. Grazers carve passages; people mark them; creepers erase the marks. Fire hardens the ruins into new foundations. Death opens surfaces. Disaster rearranges access to warmth.

Even the rare empty space is inhabited in anticipation. Feelers turn toward it. Seeds drift across it. Hunters watch its edges. Roots apply pressure from beyond the walls.

The people know this. When they discover a new chamber, they do not say that it is vacant.

They say it has not chosen its lives yet.
