It is difficult to say where an inhabitant of the basin begins.

One might select a single animal from the press: one of the pale, broad-backed bodies advancing with its belly close to the shale. Seen during the rare instant when the crowd opens around it, it appears simple enough. It is roughly oval, longer than several human strides, with no obvious head and no fixed front. Six muscular limbs emerge from folds along its underside. Any pair may become forelimbs when it turns. Its hide is milk-white where other bodies have rubbed it clean, but most of it is stained gray or blue with silt. Black seams wander across its back. Some are natural pigmentation; others are colonies of shell-creatures packed so tightly that they resemble cracks.

Its mouth is not a mouth but a rotating pavement beneath the body. Hundreds of mineral-hard plates descend in overlapping rows, touch the stone, and draw backward. The animal feeds as it walks, rasping away the invisible film in cracks and swallowing limestone powder, bacterial tissue, salts, eggs, and whatever small runners fail to escape. Behind it, the rock is left pale and faintly grooved. Nothing edible appears to have been taken, yet generations of such feeding can lower a shelf by the height of a body.

It has no eyes. Along its flanks lie dark pits covered by membranes finer than wet skin. These register changes in pressure: footsteps through rock, wingbeats through air, the compression of nearby flesh. Warmth is sensed by loose combs around the limb joints. The approach of a living body arrives as a shape of heat and disturbance long before contact. A dead body is perceived as a place where the pressure has ceased.

The broad-back does not breathe through a face. It draws air through thousands of pores under the ridges of its hide. These open and close in traveling bands, so that respiration passes over it like wind through an unseen field. During rain the same pores admit water. The animal swells noticeably in a storm, storing moisture in bladders that lace its body. For days afterward, smaller creatures follow it and drink the faint secretion that gathers where its limbs fold.

By itself it seems dull, nearly geological. It feeds, presses forward, pauses at seepage, and gives no sign that it distinguishes one neighbor from another.

But it is almost never by itself.

When several broad-backs touch, the breathing ridges along their hides begin to align. Their bodies exchange low pulses through the stone and through the flesh pressed between them. At first the rhythms are irregular. Then one pattern prevails. Dozens begin to breathe together; then hundreds. Their feeding plates strike the shale in sequence. A vibration passes outward until a whole district of animals is beating in one slow measure.

The measure changes what they do.

A descending note turns the mass toward water. Three blunt impacts announce unstable ground. A tremor held just below audibility means that something is moving beneath the mud. The signal for danger is not a cry but a sudden absence: one animal suppresses its breath, its neighbors feel the missing pulse, and silence expands through the herd faster than any individual could run. A moment later the entire surface convulses.

No single broad-back appears to know the basin. A separated one circles helplessly, returning to its own trail until hunger or a predator takes it. In company, however, they remember routes older than any living body. The memory resides in timing—long sequences of pressure and pause transmitted from flank to flank. A particular rhythm carries the shape of the stone-fin country. Another reproduces the delayed booming of the gas flat. Young animals learn these patterns by being wedged among adults and compelled to move when they move.

Thus a herd can retain knowledge without any member holding it entire. If too many die at once, parts of a route vanish. The survivors may reach a familiar fissure and fail to recognize it. They circle, pulse, hesitate, and sometimes begin a new migration merely because the old rhythm has lost one necessary interval.

Their young do not resemble them. They begin as soft black ribbons, scarcely thicker than fingers, hatched in the warmth of carcasses or beneath overlapping shells. For their first cycle they live in knots, feeding on decay and following moisture. During storms the knots loosen. Each ribbon seeks the underside of a large animal and presses itself into a fold of skin.

There it changes.

The ribbon divides lengthwise, though it does not separate. Limbs bud from one half, feeding plates from the other. Its internal organs turn slowly around until no original front remains. Several juveniles may fuse during this period. A mature broad-back may therefore be descended from one ribbon, or five, or twenty. Some bear uneven arrangements of limbs or several competing breathing rhythms, traces of bodies that never completely agreed on becoming one.

The basin’s lesser animals live upon them so thoroughly that the broad-backs are habitats as much as beasts. Shell layers protect their soft dorsal skin from biting flyers. Pale membrane-drifters lay gas-filled eggs in the warm channels over their lungs. Hand-sized runners nest between their mouthplates and dart out to seize scraps. Thin red scavengers inhabit their water bladders, usually without harm. Even predators may spend part of their lives disguised among these passengers.

An old broad-back can carry more living weight than the weight of its own flesh. Its true outline disappears under shells, eggs, feeding combs, pupal sacs, fungal veils, and the limbs of animals merely crossing over. When it moves, the colony moves. When it stops, nearby bodies climb onto it until it becomes indistinguishable from a low rise in the crowd.

Some live long enough that their backs are worn into recognizable topographies. Other creatures know them as warm landmarks. Migrations bend around them. Flyers descend according to the eddies above their breathing pores. Generations of shell animals build pale ridges along their scars. If such an elder changes direction, thousands may follow, not because it commands them but because a piece of the familiar world has begun to travel.

The oldest give the deepest pulses. Their hides have thickened, and their hollow storage organs resonate when struck by the movements of the heart. These notes can pass through packed bodies for miles. Around them, the herd’s many local rhythms resolve into larger, slower patterns. Predators retreat from some of these patterns. Flyers descend for others. Burrowing forms surface as if called.

Whether this is communication in any inward sense is uncertain. The animals may possess no thought beyond pressure, thirst, and the relief of correct vibration. Yet the assembled herd behaves with something like attention. It tests unfamiliar ground. It falls silent around new cracks. It repeats strange thunder until the sound has been absorbed into its own repertoire. After a storm alters the basin, the broad-backs spend days pulsing against the changed echoes, as though handling the event from many sides.

Their dead are not mourned by a gathering; a gathering is already present. When one weakens, neighbors press more closely around it. This may support the failing animal, or merely occupy the space it is about to surrender. Its breathing rhythm persists in the surrounding bodies even after its own pores stop. For a while the corpse continues to move, borne along by the herd, its limbs dragging beneath it.

Then scavengers open the hide.

The stored water goes first. Runners enter the emptied bladders. Ribbon-young mass in the warmth around the organs. Feeding plates are torn out for their salts. The shell colonies on the back remain in place and may bridge directly onto neighboring animals, carrying with them parasites, eggs, and microscopic films.

By the time the bones are exposed, the dead individual has become several thousand living ones. Soon even those bones are gone. What remains is its rhythm, still traveling through bodies that never knew its source.

The broad-backs are not the only inhabitants, nor the largest, nor the most dangerous. But they form the nearest thing the basin has to a ground of its own. Everything touches them. Things feed beneath them, breed inside them, hunt between them, and cross the landscape upon their backs. They carry water, memory, disease, shelter, and sound.

Perhaps each is an animal. Perhaps each is already a crowd. Perhaps the crowd itself is the inhabitant, using these pale bodies as temporary organs—growing them from ribbons, filling them with rain, teaching them a fragment of its measure, and taking them apart when their pulse can no longer keep time.

In the darkness, the distinction has little use.
