At first, what lives in the Green Nave appears to be nothing.

The goats are absent. The birds have passed beyond the rim. The cushions lie so close to the earth that they resemble the earth itself, and the only movement is the failure of a drop of water, slipping from one silver-haired leaf to the next.

Then the sun touches the basin.

Warmth travels first through stone. Pale grasshoppers ease themselves from fissures, hind legs unfolding one joint at a time. Their bodies bear the colors of fresh fractures: ash, quartz, faint green, a stripe like rust in slate. They do not leap unless forced. A leap here might end beyond the mountain. Instead they sidle into shadow or flatten so completely that their legs disappear.

Beetles emerge beneath them. They are blunt, black, and heavily sealed against cold, more like animated seeds than insects. Their wing cases have fused shut over generations; there is nowhere useful to fly, and too much wind in which to attempt it. Fine grooves along their backs carry fungal spores between moss beds. Thus every beetle leaves an invisible garden in its wake.

Mites graze among forests of moss filaments. Flies rise in columns no higher than the ankle, each swarm confined to a pocket of warmed air. Tiny spiders string their webs under leaves rather than between them. Almost all the basin’s smallest creatures live beneath a ceiling a finger’s width above the ground. To them, the cushions are wooded hills, the root tunnels caverns, the beads of dawn water deep transparent lakes.

The mountain voles occupy the layer below.

They are long-tailed, soot-bellied animals with small ears furred over against frost. Their passages run through peat and under stone, threading nearly the whole basin. Some tunnels are gnawed open; others are simply spaces left between root and rock. In these they build chambers lined with sedge, lichen, hare wool, and the shed down of ground birds. A vole nest is one of the warmest places in the Green Nave. Beetles gather outside its walls. Seeds germinate in its refuse. Fungal threads thicken around it, fed by scraps and breath.

The voles rarely expose themselves for long. They emerge under cover of vegetation, clip a stem, and draw it underground. On still mornings one may see a whole patch of heath trembling as stored food passes from mouth to mouth below.

Their predators know these roads without entering them.

The snow fox listens with its jaw near the ground. It is a small, deep-chested fox with broad feet and a tail heavy enough to cover its face while sleeping. Its summer coat is not truly gray but a confusion of colors—white at the tips, charcoal beneath, yellowed along the flanks—so that it resembles lichen-covered stone. In winter it becomes almost wholly white except for its eyes and the black line of its mouth.

It can wait beside a burrow for most of a morning. When it strikes, it does not pounce high, as lowland foxes do. It drives its forefeet into the mat and tears sideways, opening the vole’s roof. Often the roots resist it. The plants close around its legs; the woven ground defeats tooth and claw. Many hunts end with the fox sitting amid loosened moss, watching the hole mend in the wind.

The cliff hares are quieter still. They move on padded feet and browse in a crouch, their bellies nearly brushing the plants. Their incisors are stained dark by mineral-rich roots. Older hares have scarred lips from feeding among sharp stone, and ragged ears from winters spent pressed into slate hollows. They chew the offerings left at the pillar as readily as heath: hide packets, braided cords, feather shafts, any trace of salt.

Goats arrive by paths no other hoofed animal could recognize. They are lean, narrow-horned, and dark along the spine, with greenish stains on their knees from kneeling to crop the cushions. They enter one at a time, climbing over the lip of the world as though ascending from the cloud. Their kids are born below, in caves and broken gullies. Only when they can place all four feet on a ledge no wider than a hand are they brought into the Nave.

The goats do not remain through afternoon. They seem to know when the high wind will turn, sometimes leaving while the basin is still calm. They also know which green terraces will bear weight. A traveler following their exact steps may nevertheless break through into a vole chamber or send an entire mat sliding over scree. The path belongs to the goats only while they are walking it.

Above them live the birds, though “above” is uncertain here. Hawks hunt beneath the rim. Swifts cross at eye level. Ravens may hang motionless below a standing person and then rise without a wingbeat.

The white-backed ravens are the most conspicuous residents and the least trustworthy sign of life. One may watch the basin for an hour without seeing any, only to discover that a pair has been sitting on the pillar all along, black heads aligned with the rock. Their pale mantle feathers reflect the sky. When they open their wings, they seem briefly to tear holes in the brightness.

They inspect everything. Copper wedges vanish into their caches. Bone needles are carried to nest ledges. Feathers left beneath the upright stone are pulled out and reordered. The ravens drink from the pillar’s cup marks at dawn, but never empty all of them. Whether by habit or accident, they leave the smallest cups untouched, preserving part of the old pattern by which people read the drying water.

The ground birds are brown, round, and almost without song. Thin air reduces their calls to clicks and dry trills. They nest beneath slabs, where they lay speckled eggs among vole fur. Their chicks hatch with strong feet and weak wings. Within hours they can run through the moss passages; flight comes later, and never becomes their preferred escape. At danger they vanish under the living floor.

Danger sometimes comes as the short-winged hawk, beating low between hummocks. Less often, it comes as the mountain cat.

The cat does not properly inhabit the Nave. It includes the Nave in a territory too large for any animal to know completely. It may visit twice in one season and then not for years. It is long-bodied, smoke-pale, with paws furred over the pads and a tail marked by indistinct rings. The first sign of it is not a track but a cessation. Flies lower themselves. Ground birds fold into cracks. Hares become stones. Even the ravens rise from the rim.

Then the cat appears where no approach was visible.

It walks as though the steepness does not concern it. Its weight presses water from the cushions, leaving dark prints that persist after the animal has gone. These fill with springtails and mites. Spores collect in the damp fur between its toes and are carried to other summits. In this way, the rarest visitor may spread more of the Green Nave than any creature born there.

People are also visitors, though the oldest stories call them former residents.

Those who still climb to the basin do not build new walls. They repair a fallen course, clear frost rubble from a chamber, relight an old hearth with fuel carried for days. They sleep badly in the thin air and wake with blood dried beneath their noses. At dawn they kneel by the pillar and watch the water vanish from its cups.

No one now agrees on everything the sequence means. One cup predicts the turning of the wind. Another is said to mark the passage of goats on the southern cliffs. A third warns of thunder rising through the cloud plain. The oldest visitors do not name the signs aloud. They touch each hollow after it dries, then place their damp fingertips to the tongue.

They leave before the basin can become familiar.

The true resident is harder to separate into bodies.

Beneath the cushions, heath, sedge, and creeping juniper lies a web of fungal threads finer than hair. It enters roots without quite becoming root. It surrounds stones, lines vole chambers, receives the spores carried by beetles, and reaches across old cracks in the paving. No single strand spans the Nave, yet strand touches root, root touches peat, peat touches another strand, until almost every green patch participates.

At night the web takes up condensation. Water enters wherever a leaf cools enough to bead. It passes inward and, in tiny quantities, eastward and uphill, drawn toward tissues that will warm first at dawn. Minerals move with it. Sugars travel back. A cushion in shade feeds one exposed to wind; a mat torn by a goat receives moisture along its ragged upper edge. The exchange is slow, partial, and without intention anyone can prove.

Yet over centuries it has made its preference visible.

Green rises.

It climbs against scree, gathers soil above stones, and builds terraces where loose earth should have washed away. Moss seals cracks that frost has opened. Juniper roots brace slate until the slate becomes too large to hold. The whole basin seems engaged in a patient upward migration, not toward the summit exactly, but toward morning.

This life has no heart. If it has memory, the memory is stored as thickness: peat under an ancient cushion, richer growth around a vanished nest, a ring of moss where a shelter wall once stood. The old hearths remain warm places long after their ashes have dispersed. The pillar’s chipped base supports orange lichen found nowhere else in the basin, perhaps brought on the hands or tools of its makers.

Stone continually breaks the pattern. A slab shifts and severs a hundred threads. Frost turns a green terrace inside out. Whole communities fall from the open lip, still rooted together, and disappear into cloud. The web does not recoil. Its broken ends bead with water. Beetles cross the gap. Roots enter the new fracture.

So what lives in the Green Nave is not one hidden guardian, nor any sovereign beast adapted to rule the heights. It is a crowded accord among small things: fur carrying seeds, claws opening soil, droppings feeding moss, moss sheltering eggs, fungus lifting water, ravens rearranging the relics of human hands.

By noon much of it has withdrawn. The flies sink. Beetles turn under leaves. Voles sleep in their warm dark. Goats descend. Water disappears from the stone cups. The basin looks empty again.

But emptiness there is only life holding close.
