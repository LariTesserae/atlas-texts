The largest thing living in the court has no single body.

It occupies the working grille.

From a distance it appears to be another thickness of corrosion: a gray-black rind gathered around the bars, heavier near the bottom and thinning where the current is strongest. Only when the turbine changes pitch does it reveal itself. A shudder passes across the intake. Thousands of shells close almost together. Anemones draw in. Worm crowns vanish into their tubes. The whole grille seems to contract, becoming briefly narrower and harder, like a pupil in bright light.

Then it opens again.

The oldest mussels form its foundation. They grow in the lee of the bars, generation on generation, their shells wedged into the rusted iron and cemented over by younger animals. Most are dead at the center. Their chambers have filled with silt, worm castings, fish bones, and grains of limestone until the colony possesses something like a skeleton—dense, irregular, and stronger in places than the metal it covers.

Barnacles occupy the exposed ridges. Pale anemones stand in the channels between them, each bent toward the flow. Hydroids spread over both. Tube worms bind loose surfaces with parchment-colored coils. Small sponges inhabit the interior, where water seeps through cracks too narrow for anything with claws.

No part of this assemblage directs another, but all answer to the same machinery. The turbine gives them their day and night.

When its draw is low, the grille blossoms. Tentacles lengthen. Worms extend their red and white fans. Mussels part their shells. Shrimp emerge to clean the feeding surfaces, picking away sediment with rapid, fastidious hands. At full draw, everything flattens. Soft bodies retreat into hard ones. The outermost animals have grown short and muscular, their stalks thick, their attachments broad. Those that cannot learn the pressure are removed.

The change in the turbine’s voice arrives through the steel before the water quickens. This gives the colony several seconds of warning. Across centuries, several seconds have been enough to determine nearly everything that lives there.

Among the shells are small, colorless fish found nowhere else in the harbor. They are descended from fish that once sheltered behind the intake bars, but their bodies have shortened and their fins have widened into gripping pads. They do not swim unless forced. They climb.

They move over the grille in little sideways starts, pressing themselves against the current, their translucent bellies pulsing above visible hearts. Their eyes are reduced but not blind. They see the amber maintenance lamps, the green columns of daylight, and the sudden silver violence of a scale torn from some larger fish. More important are the pale lines along their flanks, which read vibration with exquisite precision. They can distinguish the turbine’s ordinary turn from a failing bearing, an opening valve, an approaching seal.

Before a surge, they vanish into the mussel bed. Before a predator arrives, they do not.

The seals cannot reach them through the bars, and the pollock find them difficult to dislodge. Their principal enemy is the grille itself. A moment of poor purchase sends one backward into the dark between the bars. There is no struggle visible from the yard, only a small body turning once in the current and then becoming a white line drawn into the throat.

They lay their eggs in empty barnacles. Each clutch is guarded by a male whose skin gradually takes on the color of the surrounding shell: soot-black among mussels, chalky near the limestone grit, orange-brown on exposed seams of iron. He fans the eggs with his pectoral fins until the turbine rises, then seals himself over them. Many males die in that posture. Their bodies remain pressed across the barnacle mouths while the larvae hatch beneath them.

Crabs live at the colony’s lower edge. They are not unusual in shape, but their behavior has become peculiar. They harvest.

Each time the turbine slackens, they climb onto the grille and tear away the outer fringe: young mussels, worm tubes, soft sponge, anything that threatens to narrow the openings too far. They eat some and drop the rest. The fallen matter supports the animals on the floor, and the cleared channels protect the colony from being ripped off in sheets when the current strengthens.

The crabs do not know they are tending it. The colony does not know it requires tending. Nevertheless, without them it grows too thick, catches too much drag, and is torn away; without it the crabs lose both food and shelter. Their paths have become fixed grooves in the sediment between the grille and the cable trenches.

One crab is almost always larger than the others. It lives inside the broken housing of a pressure gauge near the eastern intake collar. Its shell is dark with manganese and scratched bare at the joints. Around the housing it arranges objects: valve tags, bits of green bottle glass, white stones from the sacrificial blocks, the circular lids of dead barnacles. The arrangement changes after every reversal of the side currents. What rolls away is returned. What proves too light is discarded.

It is tempting to see intention in this, especially beneath the lamp, where the glass pieces glow like small windows. But the crab is only making a wall that admits water and excludes conger jaws. Beauty is a consequence of available material.

Farther inside the grille colony, where the bars disappear into the conduit collar, lives something less easily seen.

It announces itself by absences. A row of mussels is cleaned from the steel. Crab shells appear opened without being crushed. Fish avoid one particular hollow even during a pressure surge. Sometimes an arm, narrow and nearly white, reaches from between the bars and tests the current with its tip.

The octopuses of the working intake are small. Large ones cannot hold against the pull, and those inclined to wander do not survive. These remain within a system of cavities created where old grout has washed from behind the riveted collar. They are born there, feed there, mate there, and seldom enter the open court.

Their skin does not imitate rock. There is little rock to imitate. It makes the patterns of industry: stippled aggregate, parallel rust streaks, the dull green of old bronze, the black-and-cream tessellation of mussels. When alarmed, they show bands resembling the shadows cast by grille bars. Under amber light they become the brown of lamp housings.

They know the turbine’s cycle more intimately than any other animal. They hunt during the brief decline between loads, emerging far enough to gather crabs or seize climbing fish. As the pitch begins to rise, they anchor arm by arm and pour themselves backward into the collar. Their dens contain no shells that could roll into the intake. Everything is tucked, wedged, or tied down with sucker and stone.

Females attach their eggs to old copper wiring exposed in the wall. The wires tremble continually, keeping silt from settling on the strands. A brooding female may remain there for months, growing translucent in the amber spill, touching each egg in turn while the turbine’s vibration passes through her arms.

She dies before they hatch or soon after. The young emerge into the same mechanical pulse that shaped their mother’s life. Most are swept away. A few catch in the inner mussel chambers and begin again.

There are other residents, less permanent: pollock, seals, harbor squid, shoals driven in by weather. The congers in the sealed mouths may live for decades, thickening until they can reverse only in the gate recesses. Shrimp inhabit switch boxes. Amphipods graze the felt of algae on lamp glass. Bacteria work deepest of all, in black seams beneath shell and rust, feeding on iron, sulfur, and what little organic matter filters down to them.

But the grille colony is the court’s enduring inhabitant. Individuals are born and consumed upon it. Species arrive, become useful, and remain. Others vanish. Its dead support its living; its living alter the flow that feeds them. It has edges, growth rings, internal chambers, reflexes, parasites, and a kind of memory held not in nerves but in structure.

It remembers every strong current as a missing piece.

It remembers every quiet decade as another layer of shell.

If the turbine stopped, the colony would not die at once. For a time it would expand. Anemones would straighten. Mussels would open without caution. Fish would leave their gripping places and swim uncertainly through the bars. Sediment would settle over the lower tiers.

Then the food carried by the current would diminish. Silt would choke the interior. Predators would enter the conduit. The carefully maintained channels would close, and the great living rind would soften and collapse from its own accumulated weight.

So it lives by the machine that kills portions of it every day.

The turbine draws. The colony opens.

The turbine deepens its note. The colony closes.

Between those two motions, repeated through generations, the intake has acquired something almost like an organ of its own: black-shelled, many-mouthed, rooted in iron, feeding on the harbor as the harbor passes through.
