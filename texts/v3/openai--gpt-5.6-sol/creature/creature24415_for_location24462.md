At first, what lives there appears to be waiting.

The dust waits in the channels. The thorn trees wait at bends in rivers that do not exist most of the year. Eggs wait beneath mineral films; seeds wait inside cases hard enough to blunt a knife. Even the larger animals, standing in the small shade of noon, have the composed look of creatures paused between instructions.

But the plateau is not dormant. It is attentive.

Its smallest inhabitants live at the boundary between water and stone. They occupy sealed cracks as translucent knots no larger than grains of salt, without visible mouths or limbs. Moisture loosens them. Within minutes of rain they spread into soft, many-lobed grazers that pass over the rock, taking up algae, metals, and light. They reproduce with extravagant speed and leave behind the clear husks that mat the riverbeds. When the pools shrink, some fold themselves into new knots. Others dissolve entirely, binding their bodies into a varnish that slows the loss of the remaining water.

The silver fish arrive after them.

They are simple only at a glance. Their bodies are narrow and nearly featureless, their eyes black points under the skin. Dissection reveals no eggs and, in the youngest specimens, no complete digestive tract. They grow those organs after feeding. At the end of a flood season, the adults swim against the last current into gravel bars and press themselves between the stones. There they become still and gradually lose their outlines. Whether they are animals entering a resistant phase or temporary creatures composed by the riverbed is unresolved. The burrowing colonies collect their scales and carry them underground.

Those colonies are everywhere.

Each chimney belongs not to a single burrower but to a communal body spread through the porous upper rock. Its visible members are small, six-legged, and blind, with transparent abdomens in which mineral grains can be seen moving. No individual survives long outside the colony’s electrical field. Together they circulate air, sort sediment, cultivate dark fungal films, and open or close subterranean passages in response to pressure changes. Their tunnel maps closely resemble the drainage patterns above them, including channels that have not yet formed.

Before storms, the colonies climb their chimneys and stand with their forelimbs raised. The low tone felt in the teeth begins then. It may be their warning, or their contribution.

The long-legged herds are the most conspicuous residents and the easiest to misunderstand.

From a distance they resemble pale browsing animals: narrow heads, sloping shoulders, four tall limbs, a line of reflective scales over the spine. Closer observation shows greater variation. Some have paired grasping digits folded beneath the throat. Some carry water in pleated sacs along the ribs. Older individuals may support dense gardens of mites, lichen, and black threadlike plants in the seams between their scales. The smallest creatures beneath them are not merely scavengers but attendants, cleaning the scales and adjusting their angle one by one.

The herds call themselves nothing that can be translated as a name. Their nearest equivalent is a sequence of color passing along several backs: *those presently crossing together*.

They do not consider the individual body a permanent unit. A herd separates in drought, joins another at a flood, entrusts its young to groups traveling toward better shade. Memory belongs partly to individuals, partly to routes. A particular sequence of turns through a ravine may preserve the account of an old death. A migration performed at the wrong season can be a question. An answer may come years later when another herd walks the same path in reverse.

Their scales are their most visible language. White announces heat rejection, but flashes within the white carry finer meanings: water found, hunter below, unstable stone, a shelter willing to open. At dusk the scales darken and become electrically active. Herd members stand flank to flank and exchange faint pulses through skin, dust, and the small animals moving among their feet. During these contacts, learned routes pass between them. So do fragments of disposition. A timid herd may become bolder after meeting travelers from the eastern scarps; an old animal may die, yet its aversion to a certain hoop persists in descendants that never saw it.

They possess tools, though few objects that an outsider would recognize as tools. A polished pebble wedged into a condensation groove can redirect a night’s water. A pattern of shed scales laid around a chimney tells the burrowing colonies to close passages before flood. Certain animals carry thin rods of floodwood tucked beneath a shoulder fold, used to test shade that may have become detached from its source.

They rarely build. Building, to them, is an act performed with weather over generations.

At intervals one herd member leaves the others and approaches a stone shelter. It does so slowly, usually at dawn, lowering its scales until its outline becomes dull. The recess admits it. Such individuals may remain inside for hours or for an entire season. When they emerge, the skin around their feet is dark with moisture and their gait has changed. They then walk a specific watershed, pausing at cisterns, pylons, and glass fields. Outsiders have called them wardens, priests, surveyors, or repair animals. None of these is accurate.

They do not understand the ancient systems as machines. They understand them as obligations.

One knows that a certain lightning pylon must be circled after the first hot rain. Another knows to press its brow against a shelter wall when condensation begins too early. A third carries a colony of conductive mites whose migrations keep a buried junction from sealing under mineral growth. These practices are inherited through touch, color, and route. Their performers cannot explain what failure they prevent. They can recognize, however, when the plateau has accepted the work. The stone cools beneath their feet. A shadow aligns. Somewhere underground, water changes direction.

The plateau’s hunters share this patience.

They are lower than the browsing animals and longer than they first appear, with bodies divided by flexible joints that can settle into cracks. Their skin does not merely imitate the color of rock. It slowly acquires the thermal behavior of the surface beneath it. After lying still for several hours, a hunter becomes difficult to distinguish even by infrared sense or electrical perception. Dust collects on it naturally. Small insects cross its back. Sometimes a seed germinates in the crease above an eye.

A hunter may occupy the same ambush place for years, feeding only a few times each season. It selects the sick, but not unfailingly. It also takes animals whose scale patterns have become disordered—individuals that repeat warnings without cause, forget migration sequences, or approach water in ways that endanger a herd. Whether the hunters detect illness or whether the herds expose such members to them is unclear.

There is evidence of exchange. After a kill, hunters leave certain reflective scales untouched and arrange the bones so runoff passes through the rib cage. Herds later visit these sites without panic. They take the scales and leave salt-rich dung. No truce is implied. The relation is older and less sentimental than that.

Above them live the pale fliers.

They scarcely flap. Their wings are thin mineral membranes stretched between fingerlike struts, and their undersides bear rows of sensitive pits. They read rising heat, airborne charge, and perhaps the buried lines that brighten after sunset. They hunt the plateau’s smaller animals, but they also transport life. Dormant knots adhere to their feet. Seeds lodge under their wing joints. Fish tissue has been found in their mouths hundreds of kilometers from the nearest recent flood.

When rain begins, the fliers descend toward the black hoops. They pass through in spirals, always in the same direction. The storm bends slightly after them.

Whether they learned this behavior from the structures or the structures learned from them is one of the plateau’s unanswerable questions.

There are other residents that appear only under particular conditions.

In overlapping shadows after sunset, narrow figures have been reported standing where no animal stood before. They are described inconsistently: sometimes two-legged, sometimes many-jointed, sometimes no more than a vertical absence in the evening dust. They do not pursue travelers. They imitate departures. If watched, they remain still; if ignored, they move toward the nearest cliff dwelling and diminish against its wall.

These may be optical aftereffects made durable by the old technology. They may be records of former inhabitants expressed through stored shade. Herd animals avoid them without displaying fear. Hunters do not react at all.

The structures themselves host another order of life. Seamless shelter walls contain branching dark inclusions that grow by fractions of a millimeter each year. The inclusions respond to breath and withdraw from fire. Around active conduits, roots form knots resembling ganglia. Glass pegs repair shallow fractures by drawing silica from dust. None of this can be cleanly classified as organism, mechanism, or mineral process.

The same uncertainty extends to the makers.

No intact body, portrait, inscription, or tool cache has been found. There are no tombs unless the cisterns are tombs, no roads unless the riverbeds are roads, no language unless the watershed is still speaking it. The narrow houses suggest occupants smaller than the herd animals, but their interiors alter subtly around whoever enters. The hoops admit nearly anything. The pylons address storms.

Perhaps the makers disappeared. Perhaps they divided their functions among the plateau’s inhabitants. Their hydrologists may survive as the instincts of fish. Their surveyors may persist in migration routes. Their archives may open only as temporary pools, read by soft grazers during the three days in which they have mouths.

Or perhaps nothing was divided because the makers were never a people in the singular sense.

The clearest sign of a larger resident comes at night. Herds settle around watering hollows. Burrowers rise in their chimneys. Dark plants unfold their dishes. Hunters press deeper into cooling stone. The fliers circle lower, following currents invisible in the clear air. Then the pale seams begin to travel beneath the riverbeds.

Every creature responds, but no two respond alike. Scales tilt. Roots contract. Sleeping fish turn beneath dry gravel. A shelter releases one measured breath of damp air. Far away, a hoop modifies the descent of dew.

Taken separately, these are reflexes. Taken together, they resemble thought.

It is slow thought, dispersed across distances, unable or unwilling to distinguish between a moving animal and running water. Its memory is stored in sediment bands, old paths, scar tissue, seed distribution, and the differing salinity of hidden cisterns. Rain supplies it with temporary connections. Drought edits it. Migration carries signals from one watershed to another. Predation removes errors. Erosion is not damage to it but metabolism.

The plateau may therefore be its own principal inhabitant—not as a spirit presiding over the country, but as a composite life that has recruited stone, weather, animals, and failing machines into one long act of continuance.

The herds are not its servants. The pylons are not its bones. The floods are not its blood. Such comparisons make the whole too simple.

They are participants in something that becomes coherent only occasionally: after localized rain, at the convergence of migrations, during the few violet minutes when buried light moves beneath the channels. At those times the scattered lives of the high country appear to recognize one another. Water is released where it will be needed. A broken structure accepts a root. A herd changes course for no reason it can individually know.

By morning the coherence has passed. The plateau returns to dust, distance, and separate creatures waiting in their separate forms.

But the next day’s tracks are already angled toward rain that has not yet fallen.
