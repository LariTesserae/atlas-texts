The people who live here are called islanders, though most of them sleep below the waterline.

A few remain on the surviving peaks: lighthouse keepers, weather crews, bird wardens, and the families who maintain the radio towers. Their houses are squat and thick-walled, sunk into hollows wherever the rock permits. Roofs are curved into the wind and crossed by steel straps. Windows resemble the ports of ships. Nothing stands loose in a yard. Rain barrels, antenna masts, fuel tanks, even children’s play frames are bolted into basalt.

These surface dwellers develop the forward lean of people accustomed to walking into weather. They know one another at a distance by posture and by the color of their storm clothing. Faces are often hidden inside hoods, voices taken by the wind. Greetings are made with an arm raised low, never overhead, as though signaling across a deck.

But they are only the visible edge of the population.

Most settlements occupy the drowned saddles and the lee sides of the ridges, where chambers have been drilled into stone and sealed behind pressure doors. Some began as turbine stations or emergency shelters and grew by increments: one sleeping module, then a machine room, then a nursery, a clinic, a communal galley. Their outer portions are ugly and durable—concrete collars, steel shutters, cable trunks, docking clamps—but the rooms within extend deep into the old mountains, where the sea can be heard only as a faint transmission through rock.

The inhabitants call these places holds. A hold may contain twenty people or several hundred. No two are planned alike. Corridors follow faults, abandoned quarry cuts, or the easiest route through slate. Floors tilt. Stairs turn without symmetry. Older sections have ceilings blackened by decades of cooking and generator smoke from before their ventilation was improved. Newer galleries are bright with ceramic panels and pipes painted in strict maintenance colors.

There are no unnecessary windows. Where a hold opens onto the sea, it does so through thick observation ports set in deep recesses. Kelp sweeps past the glass. Fish gather around the exterior lamps, and pale anemones grow on the frames despite the brushes that clear them. Children learn the names of common species before they learn the names of distant cities. They can distinguish a seal from a drifting frond by the interruption it makes in green light.

The people themselves are not transformed enough to belong wholly to the sea. They remain air-breathing, warm-blooded, vulnerable to cold and pressure. This vulnerability governs almost everything they do.

They are compact in their movements. They do not fling open doors, leave hatches unsecured, or step over a loose cable without stooping to fix it. In a place where neglect becomes disaster, tidiness is less a virtue than an instinct. Tools are tethered. Knives have blunt tips. Clothing fastens close to the body so that nothing can catch in machinery or current. Nearly everyone carries a small emergency lamp, a line cutter, and two clocks: one showing civil time, the other the tide.

Their most characteristic garment is the tide suit, a layered skin of dark rubberized cloth reinforced at the shoulders, knees, and spine. On platforms it is worn open at the throat. For short work in flooded passages, seals are closed and a compact breathing unit is fitted behind the ribs. Deep crews add rigid helmets, heated underlayers, and articulated shells that protect them from pressure. The oldest suits are patched until their original material can hardly be found.

From a distance, workers moving outside a hold resemble slow black insects clinging to the rock. Up close, their helmets are scratched, their gloves furred with tiny hydroids, their shoulder lights clouded by plankton. They work with deliberate economy. A hand placed wrongly in the strongest current may be pulled into a rotor or slammed against stone before pain can register.

No one dives alone.

The smallest working unit is a pair, and in dangerous water each pair is watched by another. Partners check one another’s seals, gauges, lines, and pupils. They know the rhythm of each other’s breathing through the comms. This intimacy is practical before it becomes emotional, but it often becomes emotional. Friendships are measured in completed watches and shared emergencies. Marriage vows in some holds include the promise to answer a recall signal, though no law requires it.

Their occupations overlap. A turbine mechanic may also fish, pilot a service submersible, and assist with whale surveys. A schoolteacher may spend part of the spring tending mussel racks. Children are taught first aid, weather reading, and hull procedure as ordinary subjects. By adolescence most can steer a boat through marked channels, though they are forbidden the narrow passes until they have seen a full year of tides.

Status does not come primarily from ownership. There is little room to store wealth, and anything exposed to salt eventually becomes scrap. Esteem belongs to people who can keep complicated systems alive: machinists who hear a failing bearing through a bulkhead, pilots who understand crosscurrents, medics who can treat pressure injury, divers who can splice a power line in darkness. The most respected residents are often older maintenance chiefs with damaged hearing and hands thickened by cold.

They speak softly indoors. Outside, speech is compressed into signals, numbers, and confirmations. Their dialect has an unusual abundance of words for motion: water shearing across water, a current curling backward under an overhang, a wave made steep by an opposing tide, a line that looks slack but is already taking strain. They rarely say the sea is angry. Anger implies choice. They describe what it is doing.

They eat what the ridges provide. Mussels, cod, pollock, crab, kelp, and preserved herring are common. Gardens exist under lamps in the warmer machine galleries, but soil is scarce and treated with near ceremonial care. Potatoes grow in stacked beds. Herbs occupy narrow troughs above pipes. Fresh fruit arrives by supply vessel and disappears quickly, especially among children.

Kelp enters nearly every meal in some form: dried into sheets, fermented, ground into flour, boiled into broth, or used to wrap fish for baking. Each hold claims its own way of curing herring, and arguments over salt, smoke, and fermentation are conducted with a seriousness outsiders find comic.

Their rooms are warmer and softer than the exterior suggests. Walls are hung with woven mats to reduce condensation and mechanical echo. Furniture has rounded corners and locking feet. Shelves have lips. Cups are broad-bottomed. Nothing fragile is placed where a distant impact might shake it loose.

Decoration comes largely from the sea, though rarely from killing large animals. Polished slate, shell mosaics, pieces of old green glass, discarded ceramic insulators, and copper recovered from wrecks are common. Kelp is pressed into translucent panels. Children collect turbine-smooth fragments of metal until an adult identifies them as dangerous.

Whale bones are not owned. When one washes onto a skerry or is found in a deep basin, its location is marked and left undisturbed except for scientific work. The inhabitants regard whales with the wary familiarity reserved elsewhere for neighboring nations. They listen to them through the hydrophones. Individual animals return for decades and acquire names based on scars, routes, or habits: Split Wake, Lantern Jaw, the Old Singer Below Three.

The dead are treated differently according to where they belonged. Surface families maintain small cemeteries in cracks high above storm reach, anchoring the stones with iron pins. In the holds, cremation is more common. Ashes may be sealed into the concrete of a repaired wall or released into a chosen current. To say that someone has “gone down-valley” means both that their ashes were carried into deep water and that their obligations have passed to others.

There are few absolute superstitions, but many practices that have survived because no one wishes to test them. A vessel departing in fog taps its hull three times. The first catch of the herring run is returned alive. No one whistles in a pressure lock. A new section of cable is given a spoken name before it is energized, usually the name of a bird, a current, or a dead technician. Engineers insist this is merely useful shorthand. Their careful choice of names suggests otherwise.

The holds are never isolated from the other life of the range. Seals sleep on access platforms and steal fish from pens. Gulls nest on warning beacons. Octopuses enter drainage grilles and must be coaxed from pump housings. Conger eels occupy warm-water outlets. Young crabs ride supply baskets into airlocks and are carried back out in buckets.

Around every settlement forms a dense, unintended reef. Mussels cover anchor points. Tube worms line cable guards. Corals begin on abandoned frames. Maintenance crews clear only what threatens a mechanism; everything else is allowed to remain. Thus the machinery appears older than it is, upholstered in life.

The inhabitants do not imagine themselves masters of this abundance. At most, they are another encrusting species—more troublesome than barnacles, more ingenious than mussels, forever fastening their brief structures to rock.

They know the mountain is what endures.

During the strongest storms, when surface stations close their shutters and all boats are recalled, the holds withdraw behind their pressure doors. Work stops except for monitoring. Families gather in the interior galleries. The turbine hum changes as the tide rises. Now and then a boulder strikes the outer works with a blow felt through floors and benches.

People pause, listen, and then continue eating, mending, teaching, arguing, or singing.

Their songs are slow because fast songs vanish in mechanical noise. Many begin on a single held note, low enough to vibrate in the chest. Other voices enter gradually, as though coming from neighboring valleys through water. The oldest have no clear ending. They circle through verses about departure, weather, failed engines, returning whales, drowned roads, and the shape of land beneath the sea.

Above them, kelp streams flat in the current. Herring turn as one body. Rotors revolve between their pylons. Four kilometers below, silt settles grain by grain onto the finished mountains.

Inside the stone, the people keep watch.
