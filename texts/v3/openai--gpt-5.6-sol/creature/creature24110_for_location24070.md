At first, what lives there seems to be a collection of small, unfinished animals.

They are pallid where they touch stone and nearly transparent where they touch open air. Their bodies have the provisional quality of things assembled before the laws governing them were settled. A beetle’s back is a curved pane through which its organs appear not as organs but as slow refractions. The six-legged lizards are supple and boneless around the mouth, able to press their faces into mineral pores and draw out beads of water before those beads appear on the surface. The threadworms lie in spirals, each animal’s head resting near the place its tail will occupy a moment later. They do not crawl so much as transfer themselves along their own coils.

The mothlike creatures are among the most conspicuous when they choose to move. Their wings carry a fine burden of droplets. Each droplet remains round and cold, never shaken free by flight. As the moths pass through the luminous center, their wings trail rings of bent light, so that a single animal may seem followed by a diminishing procession of itself. They roost beneath the bone arches and lay eggs like clear blisters along the ribs. The hatchlings do not eat their way out. Instead, they wait for their membranes to fill with brightness, then step through them as if through thin doors.

Their abandoned eggshells are valuable. Beetles drag them toward the newest seams. Moss gathers nearby. Stone softens beneath them.

The fish are harder to understand. In the absence of water they remain suspended a finger’s width above the hollows, colorless and rigid, their fins folded close. They resemble slivers of old glass or the ghosts of leaves. They can be disturbed by touch, but not displaced far; released, they drift back to the depth their future pool will reach. When condensation begins, their gills open before the first drop arrives. Water assembles around them in patches, outlining their schools. Only then do they acquire weight and color: pearl bellies, translucent spines, a tiny red mark behind each eyeless head.

They swim vigorously in whatever downwardness the pool has remembered. Sometimes this carries them parallel to the floor or up the shallow side of a basin. They feed on threadworms, snapping loose pieces from the spirals. When the water drains, the fish do not follow it. They remain where the pool was, slowing until they are once again dry, still, and waiting.

The chamber’s grazers are larger and more social, though their society depends upon never quite meeting.

They are long and low, each supported by rows of delicate feet that move in traveling waves. Their hides are cream-colored, creased, and faintly lettered with blood vessels. Seen from above, they resemble strips of damp manuscript covered in an unreadable script. Their heads have no eyes and no obvious ears. The front of the skull tapers into two soft cutting plates with which they trim the white plants.

Every stem in a colony is cropped to the same height, but no grazer measures it. One animal begins feeding, another stops at a distance, and the limit passes silently through the herd. The result is exact. They leave the roots untouched and deposit neither dung nor spoor. What they cannot digest passes out as small hard knots that, after a local rain, open into jointed white shoots.

No two adults willingly come within a body’s length of each other. Their intervals are maintained with greater care than their bodies. When one turns, all nearby turn by lesser degrees. When one halts, stillness spreads outward animal by animal until the entire herd has become a diagram of warning.

Young grazers are an exception. They are born in pairs joined by a clear cord running from flank to flank. While joined, they may cross the chamber’s center. There they circle the empty point, each keeping the other from being solitary, until the cord dries and parts. Afterward they move away in opposite directions and never knowingly approach one another again.

They carry bones nonetheless.

When a grazer dies, the others avoid its body until the flesh has vanished into the clay. Later, one of them returns for a rib. It lifts the rib carefully in its mouth and brings it to a crack already marked by other bones. A second grazer approaches from the far side, never touching the first, and together they raise the rib into an arch. Whether this is mourning, nest-building, or the instinctive maintenance of whatever keeps the chamber open cannot be separated. It is all three in practice.

The predators are not so much charcoal-colored as made from the condition of being close to a wall.

At rest they have almost no thickness. Their broad bodies spread into the dim margin around stone, and their limbs lie along cracks like strokes of soot. One may remain there through several rains while moths roost on it and beetles walk across its back. Its mouth is a narrow interruption in the border of shade.

When it hunts, it does not spring. One limb lifts and bends, pinching the interval between itself and its prey. For a heartbeat the distant grazer stands immediately beside it. The predator closes its mouth. Distance resumes, leaving an absence in the herd.

After feeding, the predator thickens. It must then cross the floor like any other animal, dragging a swollen, velvety body on four or eight limbs—the number varies with the direction from which it is seen. During this vulnerable period it carries some remnant of its prey across the center: a strip of hide, a foot, sometimes only a second shadow. This burden permits the crossing. At the opposite wall it flattens again and becomes patient.

Predators reproduce by division, but only when the chamber enlarges. At the low sigh of new space, a hunting animal is briefly reflected in every suspended droplet. One reflection fails to disappear. It darkens, unfolds from the surface of the drop, and falls upward to the nearest wall. The parent loses one of its limbs; the new animal begins with that limb and slowly acquires the rest.

The chamber also contains quieter custodians.

Glass-backed beetles tend the rings of pebbles. They test each stone with the joints of their antennae, judging not size or color but stored warmth. A pebble chilled by rain is rolled outward; one warmed by the bright center is moved inward. Over time the rings migrate, contract, divide, and join. Threadworms gather beneath them, and eggs are laid in their innermost circumferences. Yet some rings remain empty for generations, maintained around no visible thing.

The beetles communicate by turning their shadows. Because the light comes from open distance, each beetle can cast shade toward several neighbors at once. A whole ring at work resembles a dark flower opening and closing across the floor.

The black moss is less plant than procession. Each patch consists of countless narrow bodies rooted for only a few hours. Those at the rear dissolve into oily green grains; those at the front assemble from the grains carried beneath them by moisture. Thus the moss crosses bare rock without any single part traveling the whole distance. It follows the grazers’ future resting shadows, arriving before the herd and departing after it has been cropped around the edges.

The white stems appear botanical until they open. Their cups contain no pollen. In dry periods they collect brightness, condensing it into brittle flakes. Grazers eat the flakes. When rain comes, the cups close around droplets and push them down their single filament roots, carrying water into layers of stone too young to be fully hard. This softens the chamber’s margins from within.

The four-leaved plant near the floor may be a trap, an organ, or a listener. Its translucent leaves each hold a different view of the cavity. Small animals stepping across them are not harmed, but afterward their next resting place changes. Beetles abandon half-repaired rings. Moths choose unfamiliar arches. Predators wake and move to another wall. The plant does not direct them toward itself. It redistributes them, as though correcting an imbalance only it can perceive.

There are inhabitants seen solely in the suspended water.

Dark birds crowd the woven enclosure in reflection, their feathers close and glossy, their beaks turned toward the chamber. Beside them stand creatures no larger than mice, crowned with branching antlers. A pale coil loops through them all, lifting a throat that shines with a buried blue light. None appears directly in the enclosure. Nothing leaves tracks there. Yet the dried stems bow under weight whenever the droplets show the pen as full.

The reflected animals age from one droplet to the next. In a newly formed bead they are young and frantic. In older droplets they are scarred, molting, or asleep. Occasionally an image contains an empty enclosure and one open gate. Soon afterward, an unfamiliar resident is found somewhere in the chamber: a beetle of a new species climbing from fresh clay, a grazer born with antlerlike growths, a moth whose body carries dark feathers instead of droplets. These altered creatures are accepted without recognition. Within a few generations their peculiarities disperse into the rest.

All the residents share an intimacy with the unfinished clay.

They bury things in it deliberately. Beetles push in dead companions. Grazers press shed cutting plates beneath the surface. Moths lower dim eggs into the softest patches. What returns is never restored, but translated. A corpse may rise mobile and taxonomically strange. A tooth may put out veins. An egg may emerge as a wet mineral nodule that sings a single note when rain strikes it.

Because of this, lineage in the chamber does not run cleanly from parent to offspring. A plant can descend from an animal’s bone. A predator can hatch from a reflection. A fish may be the altered continuation of a seed. Species are temporary agreements among bodies, maintained only as long as the chamber finds them useful.

And gradually it becomes difficult to say that these creatures merely inhabit the place.

Their feeding controls the plants. Their spacing organizes the open light. Their bones brace cracks. Their eggshells soften stone. Their movement dislodges rain, and rain awakens fish, and fish water the moss, and moss draws the grazers, whose deaths feed the arches where the moths reproduce. Even predation has a structural role: each impossible shortening of distance seems to relieve a pressure in the walls. After a successful capture, the nearest seam often widens by the thickness of a hair.

The whole ecology behaves like the anatomy of a single being whose body is absence.

Its bones are placed rather than grown. Its blood forms intermittently and may flow uphill. Its nerves are threadworms, its cilia the many feet of the grazers, its dark contractile muscles the predators along the walls. The moth membranes gather energy; the beetle rings regulate heat; the white stems draw moisture into developing tissue. The fish are impulses waiting for a conducting medium. The reflected captives may be memories, or possibilities not yet admitted into flesh.

The chamber itself is not necessarily conscious. It gives no sign of hunger, fear, or preference. But it is alive in the older sense: it maintains differences, converts one thing into another, grows boundaries, repairs arrangements, and recruits every death into continuation.

Its residents are therefore both a community and a creature in pieces.

They live in it.

They make it.

And perhaps, whenever the earth draws apart with that low sigh, they are what the hidden cavity is slowly being born as.
