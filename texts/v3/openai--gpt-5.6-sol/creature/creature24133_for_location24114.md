The people of the Rain House do not call themselves one people. They are cistern keepers, bird tenders, plaster cutters, jar menders, herb women, gate crews, dung carriers, lamp sellers, rope splicers, and families who happen to sleep behind the same wall as their work. Some descend each morning from the open court and leave before the bats fly. Others have not seen an unobstructed horizon in years.

It is possible to tell the long-dwellers from visitors before either speaks. Visitors look upward too often. They hesitate where one passage divides into three, recoil from water crossing the floor, and wipe the damp from their faces as though it were a temporary inconvenience. Those raised below rarely look at the ceiling unless the sound changes. They test steps with the edge of a foot, duck beneath arches without breaking conversation, and know from the smell of a draft whether it comes from rain, animals, an opened hatch, or an old chamber breathing through a crack.

Their bodies carry the place. Soles broaden and harden from wet brick. Shoulders develop from jars, baskets, and gate boards. Rope workers have glossy calluses across their palms; plasterers are pale to the elbows; pigeon keepers wear narrow caps and smell faintly of feathers even after washing. Most keep their hair cropped, braided tight, or wrapped in cloth against lime dust and insects. Clothing is brief and repeatedly patched: linen or woven reed-fiber around the waist, sleeveless tunics, shoulder cloths that serve by turns as towels, strainers, slings, and bandages. Copper pins go green. Leather mildews. Wooden combs last.

No garment remains its original color for long. Ochre from the walls, soot from lamps, green water, white lime, goat hair, and brick dust reduce everyone to the chamber’s palette.

The oldest residents are not necessarily those with the most years, but those who remember the most configurations. An old gate keeper can point to a goat pen and describe the bath that preceded it, the grain court before that, and the sealed stair beneath both. Such people are consulted when cracks appear. They crouch, press an ear or cheek to plaster, tap walls with pegs, and ask who widened what nearby. Their authority comes from memory rather than ownership. A family may possess jars, animals, tools, sleeping mats, or the harvest of certain pigeon cells, but no one can easily possess a room. Rooms alter allegiance whenever water changes course.

Children learn the Rain House as a sequence of sounds. They know which wall carries a vendor’s voice, which pipe amplifies a whisper, and which cistern answers a slap with a deep note. They race through routes too low or narrow for adults, carrying messages during storms. They retrieve fallen tools, catch crickets for the fowl, gather lamp wicks from scraps, and steal warm pigeon eggs when they believe no one is watching. Before they are trusted with a flame, they are trusted with a stopper. Before they carry a full jar, they learn how to fall without breaking one.

Their games imitate maintenance. They build tiny channels in mud, dam them with pebbles, and argue over imaginary water rights. They chalk doors onto blank walls and then chalk closures elsewhere to balance them. In one common game, a child curls inside a basket while the others pretend to discover an ancient sealed chamber. The discovered child must invent what was stored there. Treasure is rarely proposed. More often it is clean water, an uncracked flight of stairs, white pigeons, or a wind that never stops.

Households are porous. A sleeping enclosure may contain siblings, apprentices, a widowed aunt, two unrelated gate workers, a sick goat, and jars entrusted by people from three galleries away. Meals form around ovens rather than bloodlines. Lentils, greens, onions, pigeon, grubs ground into meal, goat milk, and whatever is traded down from the surface are shared in shifting groups. People eat from shallow bowls held close to the chest, protecting them from dust and droppings. Water is served more ceremoniously than food. Even among intimates, the person pouring watches the other drink before filling their own cup.

Nothing in the Rain House produces a permanent caste, because every craft eventually meets water. A wealthy pigeon keeper may spend a storm waist-deep in a drain beside a dung carrier. A respected plaster reader may be ordered away by a child who has been watching the level in the next court. During heavy rain, authority passes to whoever stands nearest the relevant gate and understands it.

Disputes concern space, contamination, noise, and repair. They are settled with measurements: a hand’s width of ledge, a jar’s volume, the number of baskets needed to clear a channel. Compensation is often labor. Someone whose goats foul a stair may owe three mornings of scraping. Someone who cracks a shared tank must supply lime and work under the eye of those who drink from it. Deliberately polluting live water is among the few acts that can drive a person out.

Their beliefs are difficult to separate from procedure. They deny that the prohibition against whistling near pigeons is sacred, yet glare at anyone who jokes about it. They say the first rain is discarded because it carries roof filth, but some touch black runoff to their foreheads before letting it go. Small offerings appear in sealed niches: a fish vertebra, a twist of hair, a child’s tooth set into wet lime, a perfect beetle wing beneath a pottery shard. No one admits placing them.

The dead do not remain in the inhabited galleries. The House is already too crowded with what must be kept from collapse. Bodies are carried upward when possible. Yet fragments stay: names scratched beside repairs, handprints under plaster, bone pegs whose origin is not always discussed. To say that someone “has gone into the wall” can mean death, disappearance, or merely that their work has outlasted them.

The animals belong to the place in different degrees.

Goats are property, appetite, warmth, and persistent sabotage. They chew baskets, tug herbs from raised beds, and test every tether with patient malice. Their keepers suspend fodder from hooks and sleep lightly. Dogs are only partly owned. They attach themselves to ovens, children, and stretches of corridor rather than individuals. Each knows the boundary of its patrol and announces unfamiliar footsteps before a person appears.

Pigeons are closer to a crop than companions, though some recognize their tenders. The wall cells are harvested in rotation for eggs, squabs, feathers, and dung. Their collective movements serve as warnings. A sudden silence can mean snake, smoke, or shifting plaster. A violent eruption sends everyone nearby crouching with arms over their heads.

Tortoises occupy another category. They are tagged because someone once counted them, and the descendants of that person continue the practice without remembering why. They consume soft refuse and fallen greens, moving between courts with ponderous entitlement. Children decorate their tags. Adults step around them. During floods they are lifted onto platforms, where they wait among the goats like old officials.

Rats recognize schedules, baskets, and weak lids. They raid pigeon cells from behind and drink from channels before the first flush has cleared. Traps reduce them but never master them. The snakes are tolerated in theory and killed in panic. Geckos are welcomed near lamps. Frogs are handled gently because their abundance promises water, though they foul bowls and keep sleepers awake. Bats are protected by custom, practicality, and the impossibility of evicting them. At dusk, people cover food, lower lamps, and allow the black stream to pass.

Even the smallest creatures divide the Rain House among themselves. Pale crickets own the undersides of jars. Gnats hold the drip cups. Beetles turn dung and compost in the raised beds. Mosquitoes gather in warm still courts until smoke drives them elsewhere. Swallows claim the highest light. Moss belongs to the north faces, ferns to brief leaks, figs to cracks no prudent mason would enlarge.

Yet the most characteristic inhabitant is neither human nor animal. It is the colony made by all of them together.

The Rain House lives through distributed attention. One person notices a bitter taste in a tank. Another hears a rope fray inside a shaft. A child reports that frogs have left a drain. A dog refuses a passage. Pigeons burst from one section of wall. Mint wilts in a bed that should be damp. None of these signs means much alone. Taken together, they may reveal a blocked vent, poisoned water, settling masonry, or rain approaching before it is heard above.

Knowledge therefore moves constantly. It is shouted through tubes, marked in charcoal, repeated over meals, embodied in where jars are stacked and where sleepers do not lay their heads. The chamber has no single keeper. Its awareness is made from hundreds of partial watchfulnesses.

When the first hard water enters, the people seem to change species. Ordinary quarrels vanish. Gate crews brace their feet against worn brick. Children run messages. Jar sellers surrender empty stock. Goat keepers move animals upward. Plaster workers watch the walls instead of the channels, searching for dark lines that spread too quickly. Everyone becomes a hand, an ear, a back under a load.

After the storm, individuality returns. Debts are remembered. Missing tools are accused into existence. Children boast about depths they did not enter. Vendors reopen their courts. Someone sings while sweeping mud into the onion beds.

By lamplight, the residents appear briefly as separate figures: a woman combing lime from another woman’s hair, a boy asleep against a warm tortoise shell, a gate worker oiling a wooden board, an old man holding a frog to the light to inspect its belly. Then a pigeon crosses the lamp, water moves over the ceiling, and their shadows join the blurred animals painted there.

They do not resemble rulers of the Rain House. Nor are they merely trapped within it. They are its softest masonry: temporary, replaceable, indispensable. The chamber contains them, but they also contain the chamber—in habits of balance, in scarred hands, in remembered passages, in the instinct to wake at a new sound of dripping.

What lives there is not a hidden city so much as an unfinished act of keeping.
