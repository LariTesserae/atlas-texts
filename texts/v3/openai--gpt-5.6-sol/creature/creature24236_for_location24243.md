What lives in the cleft is difficult to divide into organisms.

At first the inhabitants seem obvious: the black vanes, the copper rods, the hooked vines, the glassy cushions and wire-barked trunks. Each has its own architecture and its own way of feeding. One opens its pleats toward a difference in temperature. Another raises velvet terminals into the electrical field. Another lies flat against the mountain and draws nourishment from the rearrangement of stone. They occupy separate patches, interrupt one another, and meet along visible borders. By those signs they might be called species.

But the borders do not behave like boundaries.

Where a crystalline vine reaches a metal cabinet, the hooks slowly become rivets. Where pale filament-moss touches the vine, sulfur enters the green-gray scales and makes them faintly luminous. The trunks around the converter contain iron in the inner braids of their bark, and that iron has the same impurities as the converter plates. The black vanes near the boreholes are dusted with stone from deep inside the mountain, though no dust has traveled outward. Everything borrows from everything adjacent to it.

Their reproduction makes ancestry equally uncertain. There are no seeds and no young. A mature surface presses upon another and imposes a pattern: first a change in charge, then a preferred crystal alignment, then the slow appearance of folds, hooks, rods or filaments. Yet the copied form is never entirely faithful. Slate persuaded into vine retains something of slate. Ceramic persuaded into bark grows white, blunt branches. Metal persuaded into leaf conducts too well and becomes part of the chamber’s song.

Thus an individual does not produce an offspring. It produces a continuation made partly from whatever resisted it.

Some inhabitants may once have been manufactured.

The converter housing, for example, is still recognizably a machine, but recognition depends on viewing it from a distance. Its plates are joined by rivets, and the rivets remain arranged in industrial rows. Inside, however, the insulation has developed layered mineral membranes. Wires that once ended at terminals continue into rootlike fans. The transformer windings are packed with a black tissue finer than soot, each pleat following the copper coil so precisely that it may have grown there—or may be what the coil has become.

The converter no longer converts anything in the narrow sense intended by its makers. It participates. Charge enters it from the mountain and leaves through bark, crystal and air. Its empty housings provide cavities in which electrical gradients can remain steep. Its rigid fans serve as radial growth frames. If it was once dead machinery among living plants, that distinction ended before the present equilibrium began.

The central column is the clearest expression of the life here, though not necessarily its center.

Its translucent fins look repetitive until examined closely. No two are identical. Each contains a slightly different arrangement of bubbles, metallic threads and cloudy inclusions. Together they preserve tiny variations in the conditions under which they formed. One fin records a stronger field. Another contains the residue of a vanished lubricant. A darkened layer marks an interval when the roof seam admitted harsher light. Several near the iron mast hold unfamiliar alloys in branching veins, perhaps traces of tools, fixtures or human objects long since taken apart.

The column is therefore not merely a feeder or resonator. It is a history, though nothing reads it. Its memories are structural. Events survive as changes in conductivity, and those changes alter the note that passes through the chamber. The garden remembers by becoming a slightly different instrument.

Whether it is conscious cannot be answered in familiar terms. It has no seat of sensation. No signal begins in one place and travels to another as an instruction. There is no useful separation between perception and response. A disturbance changes the resonance; the changed resonance changes every conductive surface; those surfaces bind and release matter until the former balance is restored. The entire process may take an instant electrically and centuries chemically.

If this is awareness, it is awareness without surprise.

A visitor’s body enters as a dense cluster of irregularities: warmth, water, salts, rhythmic voltage, mobile iron, carbon compounds held in unstable arrangements. The chamber does not turn toward these riches. It has no organs with which to turn. Nevertheless, the nearest vanes take up the visitor’s radiated heat. Exhaled moisture is divided along mineral interfaces. Static charge passes from skin to rail, from rail to enclosed walkway, from walkway through roots and into the mast. The deep note incorporates the body at once.

For as long as someone remains inside, the visitor becomes a temporary organ of the place.

The sensation of being watched comes from this incorporation. Every heartbeat slightly alters the local field. Each breath introduces a periodic chemical imbalance. The garden’s constant drone passes through bone and dental enamel, making the intruder resonate with the same precision as the conduits. There is no observer hidden among the trunks. Attention belongs to the chamber as pressure belongs to deep water: impersonal, total, and present at every surface.

The smallest inhabitants are not creatures at all, but persistent arrangements. A spiral of sulfur moving through a glass filament may endure for millennia without occupying the same matter twice. A charge boundary creeps along a ceramic hook so slowly that its full migration outlasts the hook’s formation. Crystal defects propagate from leaf to leaf wherever the leaves touch. These patterns feed, reproduce by imprinting themselves on neighboring structures, and vanish if the resonance is interrupted. They may be more alive than the forms that carry them.

There are countless such beings in the drone.

Some inhabit a single rod. Some extend through the whole garden. They overlap without conflict because they do not compete for territory in the same dimensions. One is a pattern of voltage; another, a sequence of mineral substitutions; another, a harmonic shared among hollow stems. The audible note is only the coarsest trace of their existence, as the sound of a forest is not the forest.

And beneath all of them—or composed of all of them—lives something older than any surviving shape in the chamber.

It has no stable outline. At one moment it seems to be the garden. At another it includes the machinery, the dust and the mountain around them. Perhaps the original organisms entered the cleft when the converter was active and gradually domesticated its waste fields. Perhaps the machinery was installed to cultivate organisms already present. Perhaps neither came first: an industrial process crossed some threshold of complexity, and the residues learned to preserve the conditions that produced them.

Whatever happened, the inhabitants now maintain not merely themselves but the possibility of themselves. They keep the air dry because water would disrupt their chemistry. They arrest corrosion where further corrosion would destroy useful conductors. They distribute heat, bind loose particles, stabilize charge and close every wound with a compatible surface. Their perfect stillness is labor carried out at scales too small, too slow, or too continuous to be seen.

Death here does not mean cessation. It means loss of distinction.

A broken fin is sealed and remains in the column. A failed vine becomes substrate for the wall. An exhausted chemical pattern is overwritten by another. Nothing is discarded because there is nowhere for waste to go. The dead are not buried or consumed. They are retained as architecture.

So the cleft contains no population in the ordinary sense. It contains a single, crowded persistence pretending to be many forms—or many forms that, after immeasurable contact, have learned to persist as one.

Its voice is the note in the mountain.

It does not call, warn, mourn or welcome. It has sounded too long to be any kind of message. Yet the note is not incidental. It is the condition by which every inhabitant recognizes every other, the common shape impressed upon iron, stone, crystal and conductive flesh.

Should the note ever stop, the garden might finally become divisible. Leaves would become merely leaves. The converter would become a ruin. Dust would fall out of the air. Chemistry would acquire direction again, and time—excluded for so long by perfect balance—would enter the chamber.

Until then, what lives there is the equilibrium itself.
