What lives in the hollow cannot be listed by species. Species are only the shapes its inhabitants happen to be wearing.

At first, there seem to be merely innumerable small lives: condensation mites transparent as dew; orange colonies sunk so deeply into the stone that they color it from within; threadworms coiled in the water stored by the silver trees. Midges hang beneath the overhang in narrow, glittering pillars. Each pillar keeps its place despite drafts, rain, or the passage of larger bodies. When disturbed, it bends and closes again, as though the insects share a single elastic spine.

The ants are more distinct. Six peoples inhabit the chamber, each faithful to its own road.

Red ants carry spores from fungus to fungus but never eat them. Black ants tend scale insects beneath leaves and bury their dead in dry cracks around the smallest shrine. Amber ants travel empty-jawed toward one aperture and return from another bearing white grains that dissolve when exposed to air. A nearly colorless species cleans the eyes and nostrils of sleeping lizards. The two remaining roads belong to ants so tiny that their traffic resembles pigment flowing through hairline fractures.

None crosses another road. Where routes intersect, each column pauses in turn according to an order older than any living worker. If one colony fails, another begins producing ants of the missing color. These are not perfect imitations. They may have the wrong number of joints, or eyes where the original species had none. Nevertheless, they take up the vacant road and perform its work.

The beetles are gardeners and surveyors. Broad, matte-backed ones patrol the borders between plant islands, clipping seedlings at the root and dragging them to the moss. Smaller beetles live within the dark shrines. Their shells bear fine concentric markings resembling the mineral bands of the walls. They emerge during the seventeen-minute silences, touch the stone with their antennae, and return before the noise resumes.

No one has seen them feed. Sometimes one disappears into a seam too thin to admit even a leg. Sometimes the shrine clicks afterward, and a pool elsewhere in the hollow rises by the width of a fingernail.

The loudest residents are the cicadas, though they are seldom visible. They occupy cavities in the warm wall and maintain the grinding note that binds the chamber’s other sounds together. Their generations overlap strangely. Fresh adults emerge beside dry husks that have not yet been shed. A cicada may crawl halfway out of its old skin and remain there for months, singing from both bodies.

The frogs answer them. Most are smaller than seeds, little more than mouths, toes, and translucent sacs of sound. They breed wherever water becomes briefly possible: in leaf folds, crab claws, snail tracks, the hollow knots of trees. Their eggs develop with unreasonable speed. Some hatch as tadpoles; others hatch as small finned insects or as limbless, gilled things that enter the moss and are not seen again.

Yet there is always a frog-call at the water’s turning.

This is more important than the frog.

The lizards enforce the clear ground. Long, narrow, and lustrous, they appear in coordinated floods across the pavement. They crop vines, lick up new moss, and carry seeds back to the planted islands in folds beneath their tongues. At rest they flatten themselves into the mineral bands and vanish. When one is caught, the others do not scatter. They halt together, heads lifted, until the predator has taken exactly one.

The predators understand—or are made to understand—the same arithmetic.

The mantises are green among leaves, violet against the wall, and black when they wait beside an aperture. Each kills once after feeding, once after mating, and once before death. Snakes may spend whole seasons without striking. Their bodies grow coats of moss and tiny mushrooms until they resemble fallen branches, though no branch has ever fallen from the three trees.

The spotted cat is the largest hunter allowed to remain for more than a few breaths. It arrives without approaching. One moment the central pool is unguarded; the next, the cat is beside it, forepaws together, looking down.

It is lean but never starved. Its spots are not quite fixed. They drift slowly over the hide as reflections drift over deep water, gathering around the shoulders when monkeys are present and fading when the chamber is empty of them. It drinks without lowering its eyes. It watches the oval absences more attentively than it watches prey.

The cat does not hunt there. Perhaps it once did. A few of the oldest wall-scratches show a spotted animal carrying something small in its mouth. In later carvings it carries nothing. Now its role is not to kill but to represent killing: a pressure held in reserve, enough to keep every grazer alert and every path from becoming careless.

When it leaves, it places its head against a crack high in the wall. Its whiskers enter first. The rest of the animal follows with the soft collapse of cloth being pulled through a ring.

The monkeys are visitors in a different sense. They descend at dawn, black-faced and pollen-yellow, carrying the smell of a distant wet forest. They know the hollow well but do not belong wholly to it. Some have six fingers. Some have tails tipped with small white flowers that close when touched. The oldest females inspect the three silver trees, tapping each trunk and listening to its stored water. The young eat moths, fruit, and the tender margins of fungi, but never enough to strip a plant.

Before they depart, each troop leaves something behind: a seed, a parasite, a milk tooth, a dead infant, a clump of pollen worked carefully into a crack. They depart with equal ceremony, carrying moss, lichen, beetle eggs, or clear water cupped in leaves.

It may be that the monkeys travel among many such hollows, on mountains and under lakes and inside places that are not mountains at all. It may be that they are the chamber’s hands, exchanging small portions of one hidden world with another.

The pale crabs live highest, where no crab ought to live. They hold their claws upward to catch droplets forming on the ceiling. One claw is always larger and cupped; the other is thin and comb-shaped. They strain dust, spores, and minute eggs from condensation, passing water from claw to mouth and then into crevices inhabited by their young.

When the hollow falls silent, every crab turns toward the same shrine.

The three trees are inhabitants too, though “tree” is only their current compromise. Their silver trunks are reservoirs divided into many chambers. In one chamber is rainwater; in another, condensed vapor; in another, liquid so cold it aches in the teeth. Their roots enter the ovals and return altered. Some come back as roots. Some emerge as pale fans that breathe like gills. One tree has a root that returns through its own hollow trunk and ends among the leaves, where it flowers underground-colored blossoms.

The trees keep apart because each carries a different season.

Around the first, insects breed. Around the second, fungi fruit. Around the third, old animals go to become still. If one tree dies, its wood does not decay. It softens, slumps, and is divided among the other two until a sapling appears in one of the empty spaces. For several years the sapling may bear leaves unlike any previously seen there. Eventually it becomes silver and hollow, assuming the season that was lost.

Beneath all these visible lives is the greater population: fungal agreements, microbial wars, roots exchanging poisons, cells carried from body to body. They decide where the islands end. They prevent any one plant from spreading too far. They dismantle excess into portable pieces.

The chamber does not punish abundance with hunger. It exports abundance.

When too many moths crowd the leaf undersides, they begin sleeping nearer the apertures. On the next water-turning, some are drawn through. When frogs become numerous, their calls alter until one oval answers in the same rhythm. The frogs enter. When a moss threatens to bridge two green islands, its advancing edge pales into filaments and threads itself into the dark.

What returns is seldom identical.

After the departure of moths, winged pollinators may emerge with mothlike scales and the hinged bodies of beetles. After frogs vanish, small moist creatures appear that sing with their legs. A surplus of ants may return as seed-carrying crustaceans. Features persist because they are useful; ancestry is treated as negotiable.

Thus the true inhabitants are the offices of the hollow.

There is always something that trims.

Something that opens compacted moss.

Something that carries pollen between isolated crowns.

Something that waits in warm stone with a predator’s patience.

Something that sings before water reverses.

Something that eats the newly dead.

Something that conveys the surplus elsewhere.

Bodies inherit these duties as water inherits a channel. No body is allowed to own them.

The dark shrines may also be alive, though not in any manner recognized by the other inhabitants. Their surfaces heal scratches slowly. Their apertures dilate during storms. They respond to touch not with motion but with added interior, becoming, for an instant, farther away than their exteriors permit. The beetles may be their blood cells. The roots may be their senses. The pools may be organs briefly expressed in stone.

Or the shrines may be only the durable parts of a much larger creature, most of whose body exists elsewhere. The hollow would then be a mouth, nursery, lung, or memory: one exposed chamber in an organism distributed across distances that cannot ordinarily meet.

And there are the counted ones.

These are beings that entered as visitors and remained long enough for the chamber to learn them.

Little is left of them in recognizable form. A patch of warmth persists near the low walls even on cool mornings, roughly the size of a sleeping human. The corroded bowl collects water when no condensation falls. One mineral band bears five pale projections like the bones of a hand, though the stone around them is unbroken. During the great silences, a faint exhalation sometimes leaves the unfinished row of carvings.

Such residents no longer eat or move. They have become conditions.

One may be the warmth at night. One may be the tendency of travelers to look away from the entrance. One may live as the booming voice of the overhang. Perhaps the final counter, who scratched entrances and exits until the work stopped, did not die or depart. Perhaps the hollow assigned that person an empty role and fitted them to it.

A watcher was needed.

An obscurer.

A keeper of the interval between one census and the next.

This is what awaits anything that remains too long. The hollow first makes room in its rhythms. Its insects pause around the newcomer’s heartbeat. Its moisture begins using the newcomer’s breath. Predators account for the newcomer without attacking. Ant roads bend around sleeping feet and, eventually, pass over them.

Then the place ceases to distinguish presence from terrain.

A new aperture forms in one of the shrines, upright and approximately human. It is not a door made for the visitor. It is the visitor’s role, opening.

Afterward, the hollow contains one more way for living things to enter and leave—and one fewer creature that can be said to do either.
