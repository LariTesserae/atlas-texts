At first the chamber appears uninhabited.

The people know how to become part of a wall. They flatten themselves behind the limestone ribs, knees and elbows fitted into familiar hollows, fingers locked around buried cords. Mineral paste dulls the color of their skin. Their garments trail very little: narrow wrappings of softened hide, close nets of fiber, belts crowded with loops. Hair is cropped to a dark nap or bound beneath fitted caps. Anything loose would become a handle for the current.

Only their eyes move.

They are broad through the shoulders and narrow through the hips, built less for swimming than for holding on. Their hands are large, with thickened joints and callused palms that the growing stone continually abrades. Toes spread almost like a second set of fingers. Old people have crooked wrists, healed dislocations, and pale scars where crystal edges have opened them. Almost everyone is missing part of a nail. A child’s first serious injury is usually not a cut but a limb wrenched by water.

They breathe the sea. Along the sides of the neck, beneath protective folds of skin, lie dark combs of tissue that open and close with the pulse. In calm water the movement is scarcely visible. During exertion the throat fans flutter rapidly, flushing red. The chamber’s mineral burden irritates them; keepers often develop a dry white crust around the gill folds and must have it picked away by another person’s careful fingers. No one can properly tend their own throat. This necessity is among the first lessons of kinship.

Their faces remain recognizably human, though pressure and water have altered the habits of expression. The nostrils are small and usually sealed. The mouth is used for eating, tasting currents, and intimate gestures, but hardly ever for speech. They can force sound through it—a grunt felt more than heard—but the Bell Chamber destroys such noises at once.

Instead, they speak on one another’s bodies.

A greeting is two taps below the wrist followed by the laying of palm to palm. Questions are traced across the knuckles. Urgency is delivered to bone: a hard knock at the shoulder or sternum. Longer messages pass through cords, each pattern shaped by direction, number, and pause. The language has many distinctions for tension: a cord made taut by a person, by a steady current, by a sudden current, by something caught, by an opening wall. These are as different to them as colors.

Names are rhythms. Each person possesses a short hand-name for close use and a longer pattern that can be knocked into stone. The long name contains, in part, the chamber where the person was born and the sequence of passages by which their family reached it. As passages close, old names become maps of places that no longer exist.

No single family owns the Bell Chamber. It is too dangerous, too useful, and too short-lived to be owned. It is kept.

Usually three or four keepers occupy the platforms: enough to rescue one another, not enough to exhaust the stored food or crowd the guide-lines. They remain for spans measured not in days but in changes of light and in the chamber’s great tolls. One tends the resonant stone. One watches the vents. One scrapes young deposits from the essential cords. One sleeps, tied inside a hide sling that flexes with the water.

Sleep here is never complete. The body learns the chamber’s ordinary violence and wakes only when that violence changes. A keeper may rest through an hour of hammering water, then open their eyes at a nearly inaudible thinning of one shriek. Silence is more alarming than noise. Silence means a throat has sealed, a partition has cracked, or pressure is gathering where it should not.

The oldest keepers are recognizable by their stillness. They do not fight a current. They wait inside it, feeling its cycle through their teeth, and move during the fraction when opposing forces cancel. A young person crosses the room by strength and arrives shaking. An elder seems to release one hand and reappear at the next hold, carried there by the chamber itself.

Their work is not to prevent change. No one believes that possible. They witness it, signal it, and salvage what can be removed before the stone takes it.

They test openings with weighted cords. They press soft clay into cracks and later read the deformation. They fasten little cages of bone splinters near growing surfaces; the order in which the splinters disappear shows how quickly a wall is thickening. They memorize the flavor of each inflow. Warm iron water tastes of blood. Silica leaves the teeth strangely smooth. A bitter current may indicate a newly opened route into the deeper summit.

Every few lulls, the keeper of the resonant stone unhooks the mallet.

The answering strokes are not elaborate. There is a call for open passage, one for injury, one for fresh water-flow, one for imminent closure. Yet the keepers listen to more than the intended message. The delay of an answer reveals distance. Its pitch reveals new stone. A broken rhythm may mean the striker was interrupted; a faint double note may mean a wall has grown between one chamber and another. Much of their map is made from echoes.

When no answer comes, they strike again.

After the third unanswered sequence, the missing chamber is not immediately mourned. Its people may have fled by another route. They may be waiting out turbulence. The wall between may simply have become too thick. Uncertainty can endure for seasons. Then a new hollow opens, and people thought dead arrive through it carrying children, tools, and cords furred with pale crystal. Reunions are fierce and nearly silent: foreheads pressed together, hands moving rapidly over faces and shoulders, each confirming that the other is materially there.

Their children are carried against the abdomen in close hide cradles until they learn to grip. Before they can travel independently, they are taught to release.

This seems contradictory, but gripping forever is fatal. A closing fissure can entomb the hand that refuses to let go; a guide-line can drag its holder into a throat. Children practice on a training cord stretched across the quietest platform. At a touch from the adult, they must open both hands and trust the loop at their waist. Some scream, producing only a cloud of wasted breath and vibration. Some become rigid. Eventually each learns that survival consists not in holding fast, but in knowing which attachment to abandon.

They eat little in the chamber. Food arrives sealed in clay jars: dense cakes of ground shell-creatures, rendered fat, bitter strands cultivated in warmer hollows, and meat cut from larger animals taken in the drowned valleys below. Wood is precious because it comes from those valleys—from ancient trunks preserved in cold silt or from sparse groves rooted in shallower water far down the mountain’s flanks. A straight piece of wood may pass through ten generations of tools, becoming lever, peg, mallet handle, splint, and finally fuel for no fire at all, but mulch for the mineral-feeding streamers.

The red-tipped streamers are tended more carefully than their poor appearance suggests. They are not merely food. Pressed beneath translucent shell, they give off a weak, steady radiance after feeding on certain crystal powders. This is the source of the keepers’ lamps. Each lamp is a living smear between thin plates, wrapped at the edges with gut and carried in a bone frame. To extinguish it, the keeper covers it with an opaque flap or starves it of flowing water.

The streamers also reveal poison. Their red tips curl black before a harmful deep current arrives. A keeper who sees this does not waste time testing the water.

The little blind shell-creatures are tolerated as neighbors and read as omens, though not supernatural ones. Their emergence means pressure has changed somewhere beyond the walls. Children collect their empty shells and string them onto message cords, but living ones are left alone near the resonant stone. Their threadlike legs are exquisitely sensitive. Sometimes they withdraw moments before a shift that no human has yet felt.

The long ribbon animals are another matter. The people do not know whether each sighting is a new creature or whether one enormous body passes repeatedly through the mountain. A ribbon may enter no thicker than a wrist, then unfold in the central whirl until translucent sheets fill half the room. Within it run dim threads of blue light. It has no face to meet, no apparent front or back. Touching it gives a sensation like numbness followed by remembered pain.

Young keepers are warned not to rescue one.

Nevertheless, some try. The creatures’ helpless folding resembles a person caught in a current, and instinct is difficult to silence. Most ribbon animals are torn apart. Their flesh dissolves into clear strands, leaving behind small hard knots that ring faintly when struck. Several such knots are tied above the platforms. No two produce the same vibration.

The people suspect the ribbons navigate by the mountain’s tolling. They may enter the chambers deliberately, mistaking them for organs of some larger body.

The keepers themselves sometimes imagine the mountain that way. Not as a living being with desires, but as something assembling the conditions of life without ever intending to. The vents are mouths. The new walls are scar tissue. The resonant chambers are ears that hear one another through stone. Human beings, in this conception, are neither masters nor parasites. They are brief impulses moving through an immense mineral nervous system.

This is not their only belief. People scattered among the summit’s hollows disagree about almost everything that cannot be tested with a cord. Some say the mountain is growing toward the bright surface and will one day breach it. Some say the upper ocean is only another translucent wall, with more water beyond. Some preserve hand-patterns describing a world where humans lived in emptiness and water lay below them. In the Bell Chamber, this last idea seems absurd and frightening. Emptiness, to them, is what stone fills. An endless emptiness would be exposure without current, pressure, or handhold.

Their dead are not buried. Burial happens by itself.

A body may be fastened in a quiet fissure with the long name tapped once into the surrounding wall. Mineral water coats skin, hair, and bindings. In time the person becomes a pale shape beneath calcite, then a distortion, then a seam. Sometimes later growth splits the seam open and releases a finger bone or tooth into a new chamber. Such remains are not treated as relics of one complete individual. They are carried to other rooms and fixed there, allowing the dead to continue along routes the living may never travel.

The Bell Chamber contains at least three of them. One is beneath the lowest platform, visible only as the curve of a skull behind cloudy stone. Another hand protrudes from a rib near the central guide-line. Its fingers have been polished smooth by those who touch it before crossing.

The third is still audible.

Long ago, a keeper was enclosed with a bone cage and a slab of resonant rock when a partition formed too quickly to break. The wall over them is now thicker than a person’s body. Yet during certain changes in pressure, a small answering tremor follows the main toll. Whether this comes from the old stone, the bone cage, or some flaw in the wall is unknown. The keepers include it in their signals. Thus a dead person, or merely the chamber shaped around them, still forms part of the network.

The great silhouettes beyond the outer wall are feared because they make every scale of life uncertain.

The keepers have found scars on exposed stone larger than their platforms. Once, after a shadow passed, an entire outer rib vanished and strips of translucent skin were found caught in the vents. Another time the silhouette remained for several changes of daylight, motionless except for what might have been a row of limbs—or loose fins, or mouths—opening along its underside. No one approached the wall to study it.

When such darkness arrives, the people extinguish their lamps not because the creature would otherwise fail to see them. Daylight already reveals the chamber. The act is older and more practical than that. In darkness, every keeper knows the others have stopped all ordinary work. All hands are free. All attention turns to the cords.

They flatten themselves, becoming knots among knots.

The streamers contract. The shell-creatures vanish. The great water-notes shift as the thing outside displaces the sea. Through stone, the keepers feel a pressure unlike any produced by the mountain: slow, deliberate, and traveling against the current.

Then it passes.

No one celebrates immediately. They remain still until the chamber’s familiar violence returns. One person tests the nearest line. Another uncovers a lamp. The red-tipped light spreads weakly across faces and newly deposited stone.

Soon afterward, something always needs doing. A vent has narrowed. A jar has broken loose. A message waits unanswered. The hand of a dead keeper has emerged another finger’s width from its wall.

The inhabitants resume their work before the room can finish becoming whatever it will be next.
