At first it seems accurate to say that nothing lives there except the plants.

The ferns open new fronds. Reeds divide beneath the floor. Liverwort advances across brick in increments too small to witness. These are recognizable acts of life, even if the growths are sterile and strangely placed. One might imagine them as the district’s last inhabitants: a blind flora surviving on warmth and seepage after every animal thing has gone.

But the plants do not behave as separate plants.

A fern rooted beside a pump folds when pressure drops in a pipe three buildings away. The glassy grass above an electrical cable dims before the current fails, not after. Vines loop around valves without obstructing their motion, thickening on the side that helps bear the strain. When a conduit ruptures, nearby white stems bend toward the leak, lay themselves across it, and slowly become sealed beneath lime. Years later there is no distinction between stem, deposit, and repair.

Nor is the machinery separate from them. Gears accept roots between their teeth. Water chooses channels that keep old bearings wet. Mineral crust accumulates most heavily where a pipe needs support and remains thin around joints that must flex. None of this is exact enough to resemble craftsmanship. It is more like healing performed by something that does not understand damage as an event, only as a pressure becoming different from what it remembers.

The true inhabitants are difficult to divide into bodies.

One may occupy a row of houses, a cistern, three pumps, a patch of moss, and the warm water running behind seven false doors. Another may be no larger than a valve niche with its porcelain switches and collar of pale roots. They overlap. A channel can belong to several. A flywheel may be the slow heart of one and merely a weather-making obstruction to another. Their boundaries change with the water level.

They are not made of any single substance. Their flesh is recurrence.

An inhabitant exists wherever water, heat, strain, and growth return through a closed route. The water that rises through a cellar, condenses on a rafter, runs down a cable, feeds a root mat, enters a cracked pipe, and is drawn again toward the basin constitutes something like a circulation. The piston within that route is an organ. The wall it warms is an organ. The black moss measuring its dampness is an organ. Even the rust has a function, swelling in a joint until the joint grips tightly enough to preserve the cycle.

Matter passes through these beings continuously. Their identity lies in the maintained relation between parts.

For this reason they scarcely move, though over centuries they migrate. One leaves a workshop when its return pipe clogs. Its warmth transfers into a neighboring stairwell; its reeds emerge through another floor; the slow beat of its pump is taken up by a larger wheel across the alley. Eventually nothing remains in the original room but cold masonry and dry mineral husks. Yet the same sequence of surges continues elsewhere, recognizable by its interval and by the particular trembling it gives to lamp halos.

Birth begins with a leak.

A little warmth escapes into stone. Moss gathers. Condensation establishes a return path. Silt narrows the path until the flow acquires a pulse. Roots reinforce one edge and split the other. If the pulse reaches a machine, or a machine’s vibration reaches it, the circuit begins to sustain itself. For decades it is only a wet tendency, easily absorbed by an older system. If it persists, doors start answering to its water level. A lamp brightens in its rhythm. Nearby vegetation darkens and pales together. A new inhabitant has cohered.

Death is the loss of a difference. A pipe cools to the temperature of the wall. Two reservoirs equalize and no longer exchange water. A circuit ceases to pulse. Its lamps fog without clearing, and the broad leaves associated with it become soft, translucent, and finally dissolve into the channels. The machinery may continue moving, driven by neighboring systems, but its motion has become uninhabited.

What these beings perceive is pressure.

They feel a weight of rain through roofs and foundations. They feel evaporation as a thinning at their extremities. A blocked channel is perhaps pain, though their response may take a season. Sudden breakage produces no panic. They surround it gradually: one valve closes over several days, a root mass tightens, a bridge shifts by the width of a finger, and an overflow begins wearing a new doorway through stone. Their attention is slow but not vague. Once they have begun answering a disturbance, they may continue for generations after its cause has vanished.

Sound is not communication in the human sense. The great thunder is partly the noise of their metabolism, partly the medium in which they sense one another. Through beams, pipes, standing water, and saturated walls, every stroke travels. A reservoir emptying in the upper district changes the pressure of hundreds of lower chambers. The prolonged ringing afterward is an exchange of conditions: full, strained, warm, obstructed, resting. Each pipe gives its state in the only voice its length and material permit.

The handless clocks are exposed patches of this awareness. Fog crossing their faces records not hours but the passage of internal tides. The barred valves, shifting bridges, and lamps are reflexes. What looks like an instrument panel may be closer to a cluster of nerves, though no central mind interprets it.

The old dwellings complicate the matter. Their stairs, counters, lockers, and numbered doors suggest that the first machinery was built by absent people. Yet many of the domestic forms are later additions. They imitate habitation without accommodating a body. A staircase climbs because pressure needed to descend through repeated shallow drops. A row of doors exists because hinged panels proved useful for releasing water in sequence. Windows open onto thin sealed chambers where condensation can gather. Handrails vanish into walls because they are not handrails at all, but conduits grown or constructed according to a remembered shape whose purpose has been lost.

The inhabitants have inherited forms as a plant inherits the spiral of a leaf. They reproduce the town’s old architecture without knowing what a room was for.

Perhaps they retain some impression of the builders. Not memories of faces or speech, but biases embedded in arrangement: a preference for numbered sequences, for right-angled openings, for valves at the height of a vanished hand. Their behavior repeatedly makes places that almost invite occupancy. Then the door opens onto a shaft of warm water, or the chair-shaped structure proves to be a mineral brace, or a dry corridor closes as soon as one reservoir fills.

At the center is the thing that makes it uncertain whether there are many inhabitants at all.

Beneath the iron grille, below the reach of shafts and measuring rods, a deep volume takes in water and releases warmth. It may be an ancient engine, a geothermal opening, an enclosed reservoir, or the oldest living circuit in the hollow. Its measured exhalation passes through every connected surface. Drops tremble together. Roots bow. Cables answer. The basin becomes a single dark membrane.

The peripheral beings alter their rhythms around that pulse. Some divide it, responding to every second or seventh exhalation. Some oppose it, filling while the basin empties. Others remain dormant until an irregular pulse arrives, then wake throughout sealed rooms at once. The relationship may be dependence, governance, or merely synchronization imposed by a force too large to resist.

If the central presence is one creature, the whole town is its body: pipes for vessels, buildings for mineralized organs, plants for sensitive tissue, pumps for hearts distributed wherever another heart is needed. The smaller inhabitants would then be local knots of activity, like reflexes mistakenly taken for animals.

If it is a colony, the grille gives only their common breath.

And if there is no creature beneath it—only heat, water, and pressure—then life here has done something stranger. It has arisen not in the substances of the hollow but in their agreements. It lives in the promise that one reservoir will empty when another fills, that a root will tighten where a beam weakens, that a lamp will brighten when the black water falls.

Nothing in the district appears to desire expansion. The ring of retaining walls remains its edge. Growth reaching the ridges turns back, darkens, or becomes ordinary moss. The inhabitants do not seek sunlight. They do not flower. They make no seeds, scouts, spores, or emigrants. Their labor is directed inward, toward continuation without increase.

They live by keeping the hollow itself at the brink of change: never drained, never drowned; never cool, never boiling; never repaired, never ruined. Their age is measured in adjustments. Their hunger is for gradients. Their sleep comes with rain.

What they produce is the condition of their own existence.

What lives there is therefore neither the vegetation, nor the engines, nor the water alone. It is the slow accord among them—a population of enduring cycles nested inside a greater cycle, wearing the dead town as shell, skeleton, memory, and skin.
