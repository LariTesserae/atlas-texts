At first the inhabitants seem fewer than they are.

The obvious animals are easily counted: the leather-backed grazers advancing over sunlit walls; the gliders folded beneath membranes; the patient, plated excavators in the lower courts. But prolonged attention makes the city increasingly crowded. A dark line on a column proves to be a procession. A patch of iridescence is a sleeping herd. What looked like mineral stippling around a vent extends feeding threads when warm air rises.

Even the empty lanes are inhabited. Transparent films spread over them at night, feeding on traces left by traffic, then withdraw before dawn into cracks no wider than a hair.

The smallest lives are resin-bodied motes carried in the tower currents. Some are spores, some eggs, some complete adult creatures. They drift upward inside the white ribs, taking nourishment through their skins, and emerge onto the terraces as a faint metallic haze. Most travel only between neighboring towers. A few are swept over the black rim and lost. Their bodies darken and seal the instant the outer air touches them, becoming hard grains that may remain viable for centuries.

The wall-builders have no separate bodies in the ordinary sense. Each tower, arch, buttress, or roof is a federation of growths joined by chemical exchange. Along the outer surfaces are chalky cooling tissues, almost dead. Beneath them lie amber-filled vessels, contracting pores, mineral-digesting strands, and slow sheets of living matter that divide according to pressure. Deepest are the old layers: blackened, brittle, and filled with abandoned channels.

A tower can lose a limb and survive. Two towers touching for long enough may become one organism, although their tissues retain different rhythms for generations. A colony separated by a new crack may remain chemically attentive to its severed half, releasing scents into the wind whenever the buried current within the stone changes.

They do not grow continuously. For much of the day they are nearly inert. Growth occurs in brief pulses after sunset, when stored heat begins to move outward through the streets. New tissue extrudes soft and dark, shines with resin, and hardens before the upper terraces cool. On some evenings the entire city gives a single small contraction. Roofs tighten. Columns settle deeper into their sockets. Hairline fractures close. The sound is no louder than cooling stone.

Their organs are exposed as architecture. The green-black bladders along walls store volatile compounds and regulate shade. The pale roof membranes are lungs as much as shelters, trading gases over immense surfaces. Spiral buttresses are mineral-feeding limbs that have thickened where wind strain is greatest. The chambers high in the towers, unreachable to larger animals, are reproductive organs warmed by rising air.

There is no distinction here between a building’s usefulness to itself and its usefulness to others. A shaded passage conserves resin in the colony that formed it, but also gives safe transit to grazers. A warm vent disperses spores, but also sustains a fringe of filter-feeders. A buttress redirects the wind and inadvertently makes a nursery on its sheltered side. The city’s apparent generosity arises from countless private needs fitted tightly together.

The broad-footed grazers are among its most visible residents. They have no fixed front or back. When one reaches an obstacle, the limbs nearest the obstacle become feelers and the opposite limbs begin walking, so that the animal seems to reverse without turning. Its mouth is a soft abrasive field on the underside of its body. As it passes, it removes only the outermost microbial bloom, leaving parallel clean marks that fade within hours.

Their changing sheen is produced by overlapping plates filled with dark oil. In safety the plates lie flat and scatter little light. Alarm lifts them into violet interference bands. Courtship makes them flash silver, though “flash” is too rapid a word: the brightness travels across a gathering over several minutes, one animal answering another until an entire wall seems to acquire moonlight.

Their young begin as immobile buttons attached to warm stone. Adults feed around them but show no obvious care. During cold intervals, however, several adults may settle over a cluster, joining their broad edges into a nearly seamless cover. Whether this is protection or merely attraction to the same warmth is impossible to tell.

The needle-faced creatures of the roofs are lighter still. Their bodies consist largely of folded flight surfaces surrounding a narrow digestive cord. They climb by puncturing the membranes with microscopic hooks, never deeply enough to provoke sealing resin. At a warm high point they unfold, test the street currents with long facial vanes, and release their grip.

They do not fly so much as descend with great precision. Some can spend half the day crossing the city in successive glides, climbing one tower and spiraling down another. When spores are abundant, they open their faces into delicate cages. The smallest particles pass through; edible ones adhere and are drawn inward. After feeding, their translucent wings become clouded with stored material and their descent grows steep.

They lay eggs beneath arches in geometric arrays. Each female adds her cases beside those already present, maintaining a constant distance by touch. No individual perceives the resulting pattern. Nevertheless, entire ceilings become covered in interlocking spirals whose direction changes from one district to the next.

The round-backed animals below are less numerous and considerably older. Their plates bear growth rings, impact scars, and patches where wall-colonies have begun to grow upon them. They appear to be part of the ground until the mouth opens.

That mouth is a rotating arrangement of mineral-hard ridges. It grinds the nutrient nodules that swell around column bases, swallowing stone, living tissue, and resin together. Internal chambers separate the useful compounds. Waste emerges as dry, chemically altered pellets, which the wall-builders accept more readily than untouched rock.

A round-back may follow the same circuit for decades. The columns along that route become scarred in regular bands and respond by producing larger nodules. Thus animal and street slowly shape one another: the animal teaching the columns where to feed it, the columns fixing the animal’s path by the placement of food. Very old individuals travel through lanes no longer wide enough for them. The living walls withdraw fractionally as they approach and close behind them again.

Pale scavengers accompany these heavy feeders. A single “scavenger” is actually a temporary knot of threadlike bodies. They travel entwined for warmth, loosen around fresh fragments, and divide to feed. If threatened, the whole cluster collapses into a chalk-colored bead. If food is plentiful, it breaks into several smaller knots that depart in different directions. Their relationships are therefore difficult to describe: companions become offspring, offspring rejoin as organs, and an apparent individual may, by evening, be distributed among half a dozen groups.

The seam-hunters are shaped by waiting. Long and narrow, they press into architectural joints until their outer tissues adopt the color and texture of the surrounding material. Their metabolism falls so low that tiny filter-feeders may settle on them. Only the mouth remains alert: not a jaw, but two adhesive surfaces held slightly apart.

When prey enters the seam, the hunter closes over it with the deliberation of a healing crack. Digestion may last several days. Afterward the seam opens again, indistinguishable except for an empty patch where its borrowed coating has sloughed away.

They seldom take healthy adults. Their prey is usually chilled, damaged, or chemically exhausted. In this sense they are less pursuers than points at which failing life is quietly removed from circulation.

No species appears to command the others. There are no keepers of streets, no defenders at the rim, no caste that can be called builders. Yet information passes everywhere.

A grazed surface changes the chemistry of a wall. The wall alters the angle of its bladders. The changed shade redirects a blade-plant’s growth. Its new position diverts a current carrying spores. Filter-feeders thicken around the altered current; gliders follow them; predators settle near the gliders. Their waste enters the stone and is carried, years later, into the pale ribs of a new arch.

Nothing knows the city, but the city retains the consequences of everything.

The midday stillness is the closest its inhabitants come to acting together.

It begins in the towers. Vertical pores narrow, slowing the warm updrafts. Membranes become taut. Green-black bladders turn their reflective faces toward the central court, followed by blade-plants, folded leaves, and the bright backs of animals. Even the seam-hunters expose a narrow strip of reflective tissue.

The gathered light warms the bare stone by only a small amount. That small amount appears to matter.

Below the court is a dense knot of old growth unlike the roots elsewhere. It neither dissolves minerals nor sends up shoots. It is sealed in successive layers of glassy resin, some laid down when the plateau was still hot. Fine strands connect it to the arches, and through the arches to much of the city. At midday, the warming stone causes the innermost layer to expand. A pressure wave passes outward.

To a human hand it would feel like a single faint pulse.

The animals stop because the surfaces beneath them tighten. The leaves turn because neighboring tissues release a trace of bitter vapor. The towers still their currents because pressure reaches their buried vessels. Every response is local. Together they create the appearance of attention.

What lies in the resin knot is uncertain. It may be an ancestral colony carried upward with the plateau, alive only in the slowest chemical sense. It may be a store of reproductive matter waiting for conditions that have not yet arrived. It may be dead tissue around which later life organized itself, preserving the shape of an accident. The oversized arches could be remnants of an early growth form rather than provisions for any missing inhabitant.

Yet the city treats the place differently from mere stone.

Roots bend around it. Spores fail to open upon it. Grazers encountering the court’s edge withdraw, even when no barrier is present. When cracks begin to approach from the outer plateau, new buttresses arise along their path years before they threaten the central arches. The preserved emptiness is not passive. It organizes growth through refusal.

For those few midday minutes, the plateau’s life becomes a single arrangement facing inward: towers full of sealed amber, walls breathing through pale membranes, gliders hanging motionless from their hooks, old round-backs paused with mineral dust between their mouthplates, hunters closed in the seams, nurseries fastened under arches, motes suspended in arrested air.

Then the buried pressure fades.

Each creature resumes its private economy. Currents rise. Feeding threads extend. Silver courtship passes slowly along a wall. A scavenger-knot divides around a resin crumb. Somewhere in a high chamber, a dry case opens and releases a new generation into the warm pores.

No one departs the basin. Almost no one looks beyond the black crags. The inhabited world is the city, and the city is made from its inhabitants: a multitude of sealed, frugal lives building shelter without intention, exchanging messages without language, and preserving at their center either an ancestor, a future, or the exact shape of something they have lost.
