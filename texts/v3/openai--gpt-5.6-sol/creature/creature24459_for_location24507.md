At first it seems that nothing lives there except the water.

Then, after several hours at the rim, one begins to distinguish the water’s movements from the movements made through it.

The inhabitants do not come to the surface entire. What appears is a pressure passing beneath a plate, the paired score of something narrow traveling through mud, a row of bubbles turning sharply where no conduit is shown on any plan. They reveal themselves by displacement. One does not see a body crossing a sump; one sees the pale sediment rise in two parallel lines and settle behind it. A valve wheel rotates a fraction. Three numbered washers gather at the mouth of a pipe. Somewhere underfoot, a hollow space closes.

There may be many of them. There may be only one.

If many, they are small, patient creatures, each occupying a length of cable, a flooded housing, a pocket between layers of brick. They pass objects from one territory to another without meeting in the open. Their trails cross but do not mingle. A disturbance near the western wall may be answered minutes later by a trembling among the rails in the east, then by the collapse of a mineral shelf at the center. In this interpretation, the basin is a colony, and its channels are not roads but conversations.

If one, it has no central body. Its anatomy is the whole buried works: sumps for lungs, pipes for gut, plates for scales, cables for nerves. Rain enters it. Rust and clay pass slowly through it. It extends downward by opening chambers and upward by exposing whatever obsolete organ is needed next. The iron door beneath the water may be no more an entrance than a mouth is an entrance. The hut, with its dampening chart, may be an old sensory structure grown brittle at the edge of the organism.

Both possibilities seem true at different times.

What lives below is soft enough to inhabit machinery without being machinery itself. It has learned the useful shapes left by industry. It presses into elbows and valve chambers. It braces itself against bolts. It draws thin extensions through conduits and abandons them when the conduit closes. Where ceramic insulators remain, these extensions coil around them in pale, translucent strands finer than roots. Where the insulation has decayed, they incorporate the fibers, so that portions of the creature—or creatures—have the faint sweet smell of old electrical cloth.

No specimen has been recovered intact. Pieces lifted from clogged drains resemble ordinary accumulations: iron slime, gelatinous mineral film, cable grease, fine roots with no plant attached. In a tray they remain inert until water is poured over them. Then the strands draw together around any stamped or engraved fragment nearby. They do not seize it quickly. They arrange themselves along the marks.

Numbers interest them.

A numbered tag lowered into a sump will not necessarily sink. It may stand upright beneath the surface, held between currents too slight to feel. By morning it will have moved to another region of the yard, often near tags with adjacent values. Sequences are seldom completed. One number is duplicated, omitted, or turned face down. Whether this is counting, sorting, or simply a sensitivity to incisions in metal is unknown.

The former attendants seem to have believed it was counting.

Their chart in the hut is full of vertical rulings, corrections, and columns drawn over older columns. Toward the bottom, human handwriting gives way to repeated notches impressed into the paper by dampness and pressure. The last clear annotations do not record depth or flow. They record *intervals*. Beside several entries someone has written:

**WAITED FOR RESPONSE**

and later:

**RESPONSE OCCURRED BEFORE TEST**

The amber cabinet is the nearest thing the inhabitants have to a keeper, though it is unclear who tends whom. The relays send weak impulses down through the drowned wires. At each click, some buried strand contracts. Channels alter. Gravel draws into crescents around unseen points of tension. The effect is not obedience so much as recognition.

When the cabinet is opened, the inhabitants become still.

This is not fear. Fear produces retreat, and nothing retreats. Rather, the whole basin appears to attend. Water stops in the act of choosing a direction. Bubbles hold beneath films. Even seepage along the walls narrows. For those few seconds, whatever lives below seems to turn all of itself toward the open cabinet, waiting for a signal that no longer comes.

When the door closes, it resumes its work with the slight disorientation of something waking in a changed room.

Its work is maintenance, though not maintenance of the yard as people made it. Straight lines are corrected first. Trenches are bent. Grids loosen. Equal depths become unequal. Objects at the center are carried aside. No boundary within the basin is permitted to remain exact for long. The inhabitants seem unable—or unwilling—to tolerate a final arrangement.

They cultivate collapse without allowing destruction.

A wall is undermined until it leans, then packed from below. A rusted housing is emptied of sediment just before its weight would crush the chamber beneath it. Channels are allowed to clog so pressure can discover a new seam. Afterward the old channels may be restored, but never in quite the same positions. Their care resembles neglect because it preserves no individual object. It preserves only the continued possibility of rearrangement.

They feed on differences: higher and lower, wet and dry, charged and inert, numbered and blank. A perfectly level surface starves them. A sealed chamber draws them. So does a taut wire, a trapped bubble, a plate bearing weight unevenly. They gather around strain the way flies gather around sweetness.

Rain is their season of abundance.

As the basin fills, their separate traces multiply. Paired grooves emerge from pipes in every quarter. Mud heaves in low traveling ridges. Beneath the water, long pale articulations can sometimes be seen spanning from one iron edge to another. They are jointed, perhaps, but the joints do not bend like limbs. They telescope and branch. One may divide around a bolt, reunite beyond it, and continue as a single strand.

At the height of a storm, these strands connect.

For a few minutes the inhabitants cease to be a colony and become one thing—or the one thing ceases pretending to be many. Every wet surface draws tight. The basin’s scattered tensions align. The amber relay clicks once, and a response passes downward through layers no instrument has measured.

Then the floor opens somewhere underneath itself.

What descends with the water is not merely lost. It is taken into use. Rails, gauges, broken tools, pieces of stair and wall: all become parts of deeper arrangements. The inhabitants line cavities with plates, brace mud with cable, set numbered objects at junctions. Their buried architecture is temporary but immensely elaborate. Each level is dismantled to support another. They do not occupy ruins. Ruination is how they build.

This may explain the staircases that occasionally surface. They are not remnants of buildings. They are structures assembled below from remembered forms, raised accidentally into view. The steps are often correctly proportioned, though they lead only three or four treads downward. Doors appear without rooms. Rails terminate at carefully made thresholds. The inhabitants reproduce functions they cannot complete.

Perhaps they learned these forms from the factory.

Perhaps the factory learned them from what was already below.

They do not seem hostile. A person entering the basin is not pursued, but their weight is noticed. Water gathers around the boots. Nearby channels orient toward each footfall. If the person stands still, the gravel slowly forms crescents at the edges of the soles. After several minutes a low knock travels beneath them, followed by another farther away, as if their position has been entered into a system.

Remaining at the exact center is unwise.

There, attention accumulates. The floor does not swallow at once. Instead, the center relocates by degrees: a trickle shifts, a ridge slumps, a nearby plate lifts. The person feels that the basin is not moving them away from its middle but moving its middle away from them. If they refuse to leave, the ground begins making a place beneath their weight.

The old attendants understood this. Their hut stands on deep footings not to keep it from sinking, but to prevent it from being reclassified as movable. The numbered tags on the walkway may once have marked safe stations. Now they turn in still air as the inhabitants tug delicately at their shadows through the metal.

The marsh birds sense them and depart. The white flies do not matter. Moss is tolerated because it follows joints without changing them. Reeds are pruned from below when their roots enter active passages. Nothing with a fixed root system thrives. Nothing that insists on one shape is allowed to remain.

Yet the inhabitants themselves may be very old roots—roots of no plant, feeding not on soil but on infrastructure, spreading downward through successive human attempts to drain, contain, measure, and control the hollow. They have worn iron as bark. They have used brick as bone. Electricity has become one of their weather senses.

Their age cannot be read from the uppermost ruins. Those are only the most recent layer.

Far below, where storm water vanishes, there may be older housings without bolts, channels lined in stone, timber blackened but not decayed, clay conduits impressed with marks no one would now recognize as numbers. Deeper still, perhaps there is no industry at all—only a natural seam where mineral water once shifted grains of gravel, making and unmaking tiny paths.

That may have been the first inhabitant: not an animal, not a machine, but a habit in the ground.

The habit learned pressure.

Then it learned channels.

Then metal, numbering, sequence, and response.

Now it lives throughout the basin, distributed among everything that has sunk there, endlessly feeling for the next arrangement.
