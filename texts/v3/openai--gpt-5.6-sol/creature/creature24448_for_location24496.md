At first it is difficult to tell which shapes in the Needle Ward are inhabitants and which are parts of the machinery.

A figure crouched over a junction box may remain motionless for an hour, black-backed and narrow, while current crawls blue around its hands. Another hangs beneath a gantry with its limbs folded among the braces, indistinguishable from a bundle of tools until it turns its head. In the sleeping galleries, hundreds lie against the heated walls in nested rows. Their breathing is too slight to see. Only the occasional shifting of anchor hooks gives them away.

Fully extended, the people of the Ward are taller than they first appear. They usually go bent, keeping their weight close to whatever supports them. Their bodies have six limbs arranged in three pairs, though they make little distinction between hands and feet. Each extremity ends in two strong opposing digits and a smaller hooked one, useful for cable, ladder and stone. A Warder can stand across the corner of a room, braced against three surfaces, and continue working while the floor moves beneath them.

Their outer surfaces are dry and finely plated. The plates overlap like flakes of iron scale, charcoal-black in some families and gray, umber or faintly metallic in others. Around the joints, the plating gives way to flexible folds sealed with waxes and conductive salts. Age shows first in these folds. They whiten, then develop branching stains of green or violet where harmless mineral colonies take hold.

Most adults allow these colonies to remain. A healthy skin film regulates charge, discourages parasites and changes color before certain industrial gases become dangerous. Young workers sometimes scour themselves clean for fashion, revealing the matte darkness beneath, but old fault hands regard a spotless body with suspicion. It means either inexperience or illness.

The head is a low wedge protected by a crown of movable plates. There are four eyes: two broad, dark ones for dim light and two small upper eyes that narrow against furnace glare and electrical discharge. They have no external ears. Sound reaches them mainly through the jaw and the pads of the limbs. When two Warders speak privately, they often touch brow plates or rest their jaws against the same railing. From a distance, conversation appears to be a gathering of silent figures with their hands laid upon the city.

Their spoken language is soft and close-range, full of clicks transmitted through bone. Their more useful language is percussive. A coded knock on an iron brace can travel through several districts, altered but recognizable at each junction. There are taps for a jammed lift, a fouled atmosphere, a missing child, an approaching load, an animal surge and the first uncertain suspicion that the mountain is moving.

Names, when transmitted through the railings, are not words but short rhythms. Personal marks imitate these rhythms as arrangements of dots and bars. A worker’s harness, tools and breathing hood all carry the mark. Doors do not. Rooms belong to their present attachments, not to the people inside them.

The bodies of the Warders contain almost no free liquid. Their chemistry is carried in dense, dampless pastes: organic salts pressed through fibrous channels by muscular valves. A cut does not bleed. It opens in glittering grains and dark conductive smears that harden quickly in the air. Small wounds are packed with hot resin. Larger ones are bridged with ceramic staples while the injured person remains hooked to a red rail, jaw clenched against it so the medic can feel whether their internal pulse is failing.

They breathe, but slowly. Their lungs are rigid chambers lined with mineral membranes, emptied and filled by muscles along the back. Thin air does not trouble them under ordinary conditions; abrupt changes do. Every harness therefore carries a folded breathing hood and a cartridge of compressed atmosphere. A Warder caught by evacuation without one becomes sluggish, then loses grip, then curls tightly around whatever limb remains anchored.

Powdered electrolyte is both ration and medicine. They press it beneath the tongue plates or work it into the soft seams at the wrists. It tastes, by their account, of heat, metal and distance. Children receive sweetened varieties mixed with ground film cultures. Laborers favor bitter compounds that sharpen muscle response but stain the mouth green.

Food is similarly dry. Mineral films are scraped from cultivation panels, compressed with mite meal and baked on waste-heat pipes into thin black wafers. Rivetback flesh is eaten on rare occasions, usually after a population cull, though many districts consider this distasteful; rivetbacks work alongside them and sleep under the same engines. Cable moths are ground whole into pale flour. Certain furnace colonies produce branching red growths prized for their electrical tang. A rich household is not one with much food stored, but one with rights to a reliable warm wall and a clean current.

A household is called a fastening.

It may include mates, siblings, former mates, apprentices, adopted young, elderly route-keepers and unrelated workers whose schedules bring them to the same heat conduit. Fastenings have no expectation of remaining in one room. Their true possessions are packed into narrow chests with hinged corners: bedding membranes, ancestral tools, culture plates, electrolyte tins and pieces of old anchor bolts marked with the tapping-names of the dead.

When a district moves, the fastening moves. When it divides, its tool chests are divided. Disputes over inheritance concern not land but access: a lift interval, an anchor socket, a furnace’s night heat, a safe line through three factories. The Warders have elaborate law concerning the right to pass through a place that may not exist tomorrow.

Their young hatch from layered salt cases kept in warm cabinets. Newly emerged children are soft, pale and almost translucent, their plates taking years to darken. They cling instinctively. For the first months they are carried beneath an adult’s harness in mesh slings, gripping the bearer’s body with all six limbs. Later they are moved to communal creches built inside inactive counterweight housings, among low rails and deliberately shifting floors.

A child learns anchoring before speech. Creche keepers release the floor without warning, tilting it a few degrees while the young scramble for colored holds. They learn never to trust a level surface, never to place all limbs on the same moving structure and never to follow another person through a door without looking at the hinges. Their earliest games involve building bridges of rods and cutting them loose just before they buckle.

They are not a humorless people, though outsiders often mistake their quiet for severity. They make jokes by sending misleading rhythms through furniture. They decorate one another’s back plates with reflective dust, especially where the wearer cannot see the design. Catch crews compete to swing across the central shaft in the least dignified posture possible. During long stable periods, workers suspend useless objects—spoons, broken gauges, ceramic animal shapes—from tension lines merely to watch them indicate movements too slight for the body to feel.

Music exists almost entirely as vibration. It is performed on occupied structures.

A musician fastens resonators to bridges, ducts and braces, then strikes a sequence that draws hidden tones from the city’s frame. The listeners recline with jaw plates against the floor. Because the mountain is always changing, no composition can be played twice in exactly the same way. A celebrated performer is one who recognizes a new crack or shifted load in the middle of a piece and incorporates it before the engineers interrupt.

Their most respected workers are not the foundry owners or factory coordinators but the anchor crews.

Anchor crews work where the city is least certain. They drill fresh sockets, sever structures under strain and catch released bridges as they swing across the fault. They wear heavy ceramic mantles and carry several lines wound around their torsos. Their plates are scored by grit. Many are missing digits. They speak little even by Ward standards, since years of tool vibration leave their jaw-sense blunted.

Close behind them in esteem are the seam-readers. These are patient people who travel the fault walls, tasting gas leaks with culture strips and pressing their bodies to bare rock. A good seam-reader can distinguish the mountain’s ordinary settling from the beginning of a district-killing shear. They work with animals whenever possible.

The animals are not pets in the usual sense. They are colleagues of uncertain loyalty.

Glass mites are cultivated in warm electrical cabinets, their processions guided with lines of edible mineral paste. Cable moth colonies are marked with tiny reflective flecks so a keeper can tell whether another district’s insects have entered the ventilation. Rivetbacks are given sleeping collars around particular machines; shift workers count the rise and fall of their plated bodies before trusting a turbine room. Sail-skates are tracked by lamp from gantries high in the shaft. The angle of their membranes reveals crosscurrents too faint for instruments.

Needle hounds come closest to companionship. They choose individual Warders, often without invitation, and follow them through the pipe chases. A hound sleeps hooked to the underside of its chosen person’s bed shelf and hunts whatever comes near the fastening’s food stores. Their heat-sensing pits make them attentive to illness. More than one worker has been ordered to a medic because a hound began staring persistently at a particular joint.

The Warders insist that needle hounds cannot be trained. They then spend years teaching them elaborate hand signs.

Wire-borers alone are granted no tolerance. Children earn their first tool by killing one cleanly and presenting the insulated ovipositor to a creche keeper. There is no ceremony attached to this, but most adults remember where they found theirs.

Death in the Ward is understood as a failure of attachment, though not always a fall.

The dead are stripped of hooks, lines and conductive metal. Their bodies are placed in warm fracture niches seeded with the foundational films. Over months, the plates dull and collapse while copper-green hexagons spread across them. The resulting salts are collected and used to start new cultivation panels. Nothing personal is made from this material, but every fastening keeps some culture descended from its dead.

Those who fall outside cannot be recovered. Their names are tapped once into the outermost wall, followed by a long interval of silence. Warders say the mountain keeps such people only briefly; after that, they belong to the distance.

They do not call their city the Needle Ward among themselves. Their own name for it is a rhythm difficult to translate because it changes depending on which surface carries it. On iron it means something like **held under strain**. On basalt, **the place that has not yet come apart**. Tapped lightly through a living body, it means **here, for now**.

Near the summit slit, the people become quieter still.

No one builds directly around it. No fastening claims its heat or access. A few observers maintain hooks, gauges and shutter mechanisms nearby, but even they withdraw when the animals gather. There are arguments about what draws the creatures there. Charge in the outer dust. Pressure changes. A frequency carried through the mountain. Some seam-readers believe the animals are not sensing the coming shift at all, but listening to something beyond the summit that the Warders’ bodies cannot receive.

Whatever the cause, the people trust the gathering.

They trust more deeply the instant when it ends.

When moths, hounds, rivetbacks and sail-skates suddenly pour inward, every Warder reaches for a line. Adults seize children and tool chests. Furnaces close. Workers in transit hook themselves wherever they can, sometimes beside strangers, sometimes upside down, sometimes across a structure already beginning to twist. In that moment a fastening may consist of whoever shares the same red rail.

Then the mountain moves.

Afterward, the animals emerge first, because they have no maps to betray them.

The Warders follow with lamps, chalk and cutting tools. They inspect the altered stairs, rename the routes and tap along the railings until distant answers return. Before they search for their own rooms, they free anyone trapped in the new shape. Before they mourn a vanished district, they secure the warm conduits and mark the fresh fractures. Before they sleep, they teach the children which way home has become.

By the next working interval, the Needle Ward is inhabited again—not because its people have remained where they were, but because remaining has never been what living there means.
