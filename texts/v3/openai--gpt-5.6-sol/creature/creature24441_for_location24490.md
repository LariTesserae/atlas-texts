At first, the inhabitants of Orra are difficult to distinguish from its architecture.

A figure waiting beside a freight line may appear to be a narrow service column: a dark stalk with folded braces, a crown of stiff sensory leaves, and four or five roots sunk neatly into the road-felt. It may remain so for hours. Then an approaching crawler sends a tremor through the avenue. The column loosens itself from the floor, gathers its roots into jointed walking limbs, and steps aside.

They are called Orrans only by convenience. Among themselves, no single term corresponds exactly to inhabitant, citizen, or person. The nearest is **a growth that can answer**.

An Orran is not born with one permanent shape. The part considered the individual is a dense, many-layered knot of conductive tissue called the **keeping core**. It is usually carried behind the upper sternum, protected by overlapping ceramic scales and a web of sacrificial salt glands. Around this core, a body is cultivated for a particular interval of life.

Common bodies stand between one and three times the height of a human, though height has little social meaning. They are radially organized, with three to seven load-bearing limbs arranged around a central trunk. Their joints bend by differential pressure in nested fiber bundles. Their feet divide into brushes of black anchoring filaments, which spread over the living floor and draw together when lifted. On polished factory surfaces they walk with a soft sequence of dry adhesions, like strips of paper being peeled from glass.

Their outer tissues vary with occupation. Refinery workers grow thick white armor that clouds and flakes in heat. Surveyors are narrow, pale, and many-limbed, able to insert an arm into a fracture and divide it repeatedly until its tips are finer than thread. Freight tenders have low, broad bodies with horizontal ribs from which tools and cargo may be hung. Those who work among the ceiling lamps cultivate gliding membranes and hooked extremities; seen crossing between luminous crowns, they can be mistaken for large sailbacks until they unfold a hand.

None of these forms is wholly fixed. An Orran may prune an arm, seal the cut, and bud a more useful one. Damaged locomotor trunks are replaced. Sensory fans are seasonal equipment. Even a head, where present, is not necessarily the seat of identity.

Most Orrans place their principal visual organs high on the body in a ring of transparent bark. Beneath it lie sheets of metallic pigment that buckle and flatten to focus light. They see color well, including narrow bands beyond those emitted by the ceiling crowns, and they are acutely sensitive to polarization and electrical corona. A working induction mill is surrounded in their sight by a shifting architecture of dim halos. Living roots shine faintly through the floor. Another Orran’s tension, illness, or concealed excitement may be visible as a crawling discoloration under the skin.

Their hearing is poor by comparison. Thin air carries too little information, and Orra is too absorbent a city for voices to be reliable. Orrans can produce sound by drawing serrated plates across one another or forcing atmosphere through dry reed chambers. These utterances are used at close range, around nonliving machinery, and in emergencies when contact with the floor has been lost. Their voices are papery, layered, and full of clicks that seem at first mechanical.

Their richer speech passes through touch and ground.

When rooted into the road-felt, an Orran can send patterned changes of charge into the city. Nearby inhabitants receive these through their feet and answer almost without visible movement. A plaza that appears empty may therefore be full of conversation. Figures stand hundreds of meters apart beneath the white lamps, each motionless, while arguments, work orders, jokes, technical diagrams, and private recollections travel through the charcoal fibers between them.

The floor does not merely carry these exchanges. It retains traces of them. Frequently repeated patterns deepen into conductive habits, so a proverb spoken for generations becomes easier for the city to transmit than an unfamiliar thought. Old decisions linger around intersections. Certain factory aisles feel impatient. Nesting districts are layered with half-erased comforts and cautions. Newly grown floor has a disturbing mental silence, and survey crews entering a fresh chamber often remain physically connected by braided cords until the road-felt has learned the shapes of ordinary company.

For secrecy, Orrans uproot themselves.

Private conversation is conducted on dead ceramic platforms or while suspended from bridges, with two or more participants linked by a direct filament. To refuse the floor is a meaningful act. Lovers do it. Conspirators do it. So do judges when they must consider whether the remembered inclinations of Orra are evidence or merely pressure.

Individuality in Orra is consequently neither simple nor absolute. An Orran’s keeping core preserves continuity: formative memories, characteristic electrical rhythms, practiced responses, and the slow chemical biases that amount to temperament. But each time an inhabitant roots into the city, some experience passes both ways. The individual changes Orra, and Orra alters the individual.

Young Orrans are not produced as miniature adults. They begin as **question buds**, usually raised in communal nurseries high on habitation trunks. A bud is supplied with carefully selected memory tissues from several sponsors, none of whom alone is regarded as a parent. For years it remains attached to a teaching branch. It grows senses before limbs and spends its earliest life watching, listening through the trunk, and receiving controlled fragments of the civic network.

Its first body is small and deliberately awkward. The young are expected to discover weight, distance, breakage, and delay for themselves rather than inherit these only as information. They fall often. Their limbs split along prepared seams instead of shattering, and adults quietly collect the shed sections for reclamation.

A young Orran becomes a person in the civic sense when it first sends a pattern into the floor that neither its sponsors nor the teaching branch can account for. This is called an **unborrowed answer**. It may be a new idea, a refusal, an error sustained against correction, or an unexpected interpretation of an old plan. The content matters less than the evidence of independent growth.

Their households are known as knots. A knot may contain three inhabitants or several hundred, and its members need not occupy the same building. What defines it is the maintenance of a shared reserve of sap, stored memories, dormant body patterns, and mutual obligations. Some knots specialize for centuries. There are fracture-reading knots, lamp-tending knots, pollinator-breeding knots, and knots whose sole work is to inspect old civic instructions for dangerous literalism.

Others are temporary. Workers assembling a bridge may form a knot for the bridge’s lifetime, exchange pieces of memory tissue to coordinate their skills, then separate when the structure is mature. Years later they may experience one another’s recollections as if recalling a task performed by someone adjacent to themselves.

Orran habitation therefore contains little permanent personal furniture. A dwelling bulb is lined with absorbent resting tissue and fitted with racks for unattached limbs, sensory crowns, tool symbionts, and sealed memory plaques. In the center is usually a rooting basin where the resident can enter deep communion with the trunk. Some inhabitants sleep upright in these basins. Others dismantle their bodies almost completely, leaving only the keeping core nested in cool conductive fibers while maintenance organisms clean and rebuild the surrounding tissues.

They do sleep, though not all at once. Rest comes as a progressive stilling. Limbs cease, peripheral senses fold, recent experiences are sorted into chemical layers, and finally the keeping core releases its hold on the immediate body. During this deepest interval, the city may borrow the sleeper’s unused vascular capacity. Sap moves through them toward another chamber. Messages pass beneath awareness. An Orran wakes sometimes with a taste of distant basalt or the residual geometry of a district it has never visited.

Food is not eaten in meals. Minerals are taken through the feet while rooted. Carbon-rich wafers, metallic salts, and catalytic resins are inserted into processing pockets along the trunk. Many inhabitants maintain small internal gardens of specialized cells that manufacture compounds unavailable from the floor. Hospitality consists of offering access to a rare nutrient line, a carefully balanced electrical source, or a cultivated strain of symbiont.

There are pleasures in this. Orrans distinguish thousands of mineral compositions by contact. They gather around newly exposed rock not only for survey work but to taste geological layers sealed since before the salt plain formed. A particularly complex seam may draw visitors for decades. They stand along the wall with fine roots pressed into it, exchanging impressions of pressure, iron, buried heat, and ancient chemical change.

They also cultivate fragrance, though their perfumes would seem harsh to creatures of wetter worlds: hot resin, powdered metal, ozone, sulfur complexes, and the bitter volatile compounds released by freshly cut fiber. Personal scent is produced in tiny sealed scales and opened only among chosen company.

Their art is often mistaken for infrastructure.

A bridge may be tensioned to produce changing electrical harmonics as traffic crosses it. A habitation trunk may contain galleries whose branching pattern encodes a historical dispute. Some artists train transparent bark into layers that split the ceiling light into slow-moving geometric colors. Others work directly in civic growth, persuading the floor to produce useless but beautiful conductivities—thought paths that curve, echo, and return altered.

The most private art is the cultivated scar. Orrans do not conceal every healed wound. They guide some into branching silver ridges, embedding fragments of memory in the repair tissue. To touch such a scar with permission is to receive an abbreviated sensory account of the injury: the pressure, the alarm flash, the taste of severed minerals, the approach of helpers through the floor. Without permission, touching one is an extreme violation.

Despite their regenerative abilities, Orrans are not indifferent to bodily harm. A replaceable limb may contain decades of trained reflex and local memory. Losing it is closer to losing a craft than losing a tool. Replacement organs begin naïve. An experienced bridge worker with a newly grown gripping arm may require years before it understands the tiny yielding motions that keep a living cable from tearing under load.

Death occurs when the keeping core loses the ability to maintain a distinct answer. Sometimes it is destroyed suddenly by crushing, electrical overload, predation, or chemical contamination. More often its layers gradually cease to separate present experience from inherited and civic memory. The individual roots more deeply, moves less, and eventually cannot be induced to withdraw from the floor.

Such a person is not removed. Their outer body is reclaimed, but the core is allowed to open into Orra. For a time, recognizable habits radiate from that place. Traffic may slow without instruction. Nearby doors may open for members of the dead one’s knot. A factory bed may repeat an obsolete but beloved production rhythm. These echoes fade as the core is absorbed, though some enter the city’s permanent reflexes.

Thus there are locations in Orra where one can be greeted by someone who has been dead for a century, not as a speaking ghost but as a sequence of small accommodations.

Water is the one substance for which Orrans possess both terror and elaborate discipline. They detect it at concentrations other beings would consider negligible. Sensory leaves clamp shut. Vascular gates isolate exposed tissue. Limbs detach at prepared joints before contamination can reach the core. Entire buildings may seal themselves around a single damp object.

Specialists called **dry wardens** respond to such threats. Their bodies are compact and disposable, divided into dozens of isolated compartments. They carry reactive powders, heat blades, and glassy containment pods. Dry wardens are respected, but other inhabitants often avoid touching them; however careful the decontamination, the idea of water clings to them.

Their oldest stories describe water inconsistently. In some it is a universal solvent that erases all boundaries, reducing citizen, city, memory, and stone to one poisonous confusion. In others it is presented as an impossible abundance: a substance that falls freely from above, gathers in open depressions, and moves without pipes or roots. Many Orrans regard this second version as more disturbing.

No verified inhabitant has seen the surface. The ascent shafts end far below it, sealed by sacrificial mineral forests. Knowledge of the salt plain comes from deep instruments and ancient pressure records. “Sky” exists in Orran thought as a technical abstraction—an unroofed volume associated with radiation, violent temperature change, and the possibility of water.

They do not long for it.

The direction of wonder is inward.

The most honored citizens are often the fracture readers who stand at the limits of existing light with their hands divided into faults. They interpret stresses too gradual for machines: the minute electrical complaints of salt under pressure, the silent adjustment of basalt, the shape of voids waiting beyond stone. Their reports may determine the path of an avenue that will not be lit for two hundred years.

Behind them come plan interpreters. Orra’s projected structures are encoded throughout the roots, but the instructions are not always complete, and they are not always comprehensible. A floor may insist on placing a transit hub beneath an unbreakable basalt mass. A trunk may prepare habitation bulbs for a body form no living Orran uses. Seed foundries sometimes produce unlabeled buds whose tissues do nothing known.

There are factions over what this means.

Some believe Orra was designed in full before the first chamber opened, and that present inhabitants are merely the mobile organs by which the design realizes itself. Others hold that the city predicts rather than remembers: it grows possibilities ahead of need, most of which will never be used. A smaller and more unsettling school argues that Orra is germinating toward an adult form in which neither streets nor citizens will remain recognizable.

The city gives no direct answer. It has no central brain, no chamber where its intentions can be questioned. Its awareness, if awareness is the right term, is distributed among root pressures, inherited growth rules, accumulated civic memory, and the million small corrections made by those who live within it.

Yet Orrans sometimes receive instructions no one recalls composing.

A crawler is redirected toward unopened stone. A nursery trunk begins producing unfamiliar cores. Lamps brighten over vacant ground. Hundreds of citizens wake from deep rest carrying the same simple spatial impression: not an image, but a certainty of distance and direction.

Then work gathers at the newest edge.

Surveyors arrive first, tall and thread-limbed. Behind them come dry wardens, structural cultivators, machine tenders, mineral tasters, and young question buds brought to witness their first wall-birth. They stand well back while the roots press into the fault. Their bodies are still, but the floor beneath them is alive with anticipation.

When the stone opens, they do not cheer. Sound would be too small, and the event is not considered a conquest.

Across the road-felt passes a single civic pulse: recognition, caution, welcome.

The inhabitants step forward into the dust, carrying bodies grown for rooms that have never before existed.
