The smallest inhabitants are the easiest to mistake for ornament.

They occur in seams and under lips of stone: pearl-backed newts no longer than a grain of rice, insects spread into translucent fans, worms clear enough that their interiors look like threads of colored glass. Some possess eyes, though not always on their heads. Some have mouths shaped less for eating than for fitting exactly over the mouth, wound, root, or pore beside them. A few seem to have no outer surface of their own. Their membranes continue into the moisture coating the rock, and the moisture continues into other bodies.

None can be said to rest. Rest implies an interval between efforts. They are simply held in postures that resemble crouching, swimming, feeding, hatching, molting, mating, fleeing. Their muscles are taut but never tire. Their gills stand open in water that never passes through them. Wings sustain the sound of flight without completing a stroke.

Among them are animals that appear to be young, but there are no adults of their kind; animals swollen with eggs, but no empty-bodied forms; animals caught in the extravagance of courtship beside partners that seem to be entirely different species. A blind fish presses its open mouth to the luminous stone. Inside its throat, visible through its flesh, is a chain of tiny silver crustaceans. Each crustacean clasps the tail of the next. The last one holds a violet root between its jaws. Whether this is feeding, pollination, parasitism, or construction cannot be decided. In the Held Chamber, those may be the same act viewed from different bodies.

The larger inhabitants are harder to count because they are not wholly local.

The curled six-legged creature at the chamber’s center might be one animal. Its shell-plated belly rises between its limbs like a seedpod, and beneath the plates are crowded chambers full of milky fluid. Hair-fine tubes lead from these chambers into the surrounding stone. The tiny red beings seated in pits along its back are not merely riding it: their lower halves are continuous with its nerves. Each has one glossy eye, and each eye faces a different portion of the chamber. Taken together, they may serve as the hidden creature’s vision. Taken separately, they look watchful and afraid.

Its concealed face cannot be uncovered. Slender forelegs wrap around it, and between those legs is a membrane like the sealed door of a pupa. From behind the membrane comes one of the chamber’s deeper notes, an endless sound midway between a groan and an inhalation. Perhaps the creature is about to emerge from itself. Perhaps it is hiding. Perhaps “face” is only the name an observer gives to the part that has been refused to view.

The antlered limbs in the wall also resist belonging. Each is jointed like a crab’s leg, furred like a winter mammal, and tipped with a small cluster of soft feeding mouths. One mouth touches a mat of silver velvet. Another cups a droplet. The third closes around the tail of a bead-sized amphibian without crushing it. The limbs may meet somewhere beyond the seam, but no common body can be found. They may instead be three complete animals whose bodies consist only of reaching.

So too with the gray flank. Up close, its smoothness resolves into countless overlapping plates, each plate bearing a minute ear. The chamber’s fixed chorus enters these ears, but nothing suggests that the enormous animal hears in sequence. It receives the noise entire. If it has a mind, its thought may be like the chamber’s sound: every syllable simultaneous, no conclusion approached, no memory left behind.

There are builders here, though no inhabitant can be isolated and named as one.

The shell arcades appear to be maintained by pale thread-bodied things stretched between their columns. Their bodies harden where they touch shell and remain soft where they cross open space. The tendon bridges are similarly alive. Minute mouths line the braided strands, passing nourishment from one end of a crack to the other without swallowing. Wax-sealed cells bulge with creatures shaped to occupy them precisely; whether they secreted the cells or were secreted by them is uncertain.

The spiral wall around the amber eggs is attended by flat insects whose wings match the pebbles in color and grain. Each insect grips one stone. None lifts or places it. Yet the wall is unmistakably their work—not the remnant of labor, but labor itself made permanent. They are builders because they are forever at the exact point where building and having built touch.

They have no society in any familiar sense. There is no exchange followed by obligation, no signal answered, no elder teaching a young one. Yet order passes through the crush. It passes as contact. A pressure in one shell is already the posture of the animal beside it. The shape of a jaw determines the curve of a root; the curve of the root determines the cavity in the stone; the cavity cradles a pool whose convex surface supports a sheet of eggs. Nothing issues a command. Nothing obeys. The arrangement itself is their only law.

For this reason, it may be wrong to think of them as a multitude.

The chamber contains thousands of apparent creatures, but almost none possesses a complete boundary. Fur enters crystal. Crystal continues into bone. Intestines narrow into mineral channels. One animal’s excretion is already another’s blood. The white radiance in the rock can be traced through the violet plants, through the clear worms, through the blind fish, into the eyes on the curled creature’s back. What looks like ecology may be anatomy distributed among many shapes.

If so, the true inhabitant is not any one of the packed bodies. It is the fitting-together.

It has no central organ unless the chamber itself is its organ. Its skin is every surface of contact. Its voice is the impossible chord held in the stone. The tiny shell architectures may be parts of its skeleton; the pools, parts of its circulation; the predators and prey, opposing muscles locked into a single tension. The raw ridges in the walls resemble the beginnings of teeth, gills, vessels, and sensory pits because the pale stone may not be a container at all. It may be the least specialized tissue of the being, still capable of resembling geology.

Yet even this does not fully account for them. A single organism, however strange, usually persists by process. It takes in, transforms, expels. The inhabitant of the Held Chamber does none of these. It does not persist from one moment to the next. It possesses persistence as a feature, the way another creature possesses scales.

Its life is not long. Its life has no length.

The sound made by the animals is therefore neither speech nor noise. It is their name, pronounced without duration. Every click, shriek, croak, scrape, and wing-tremor is a component of that name. No one in the chamber utters it. All of them are it.

Were a visitor somehow inserted among them, the inhabitants would not turn to look. Turning is unavailable. They would not bite, retreat, welcome, or investigate. Instead, the visitor would discover that every exposed part of the body had acquired an exact counterpart: a mouth fitted to a fingertip, a wet eye aligned with each eye, a breathing membrane pressed over the lips, a luminous crease shaped for the spine. The arrangement would not make room. It would reveal that the visitor’s room had always been present as a set of complementary surfaces.

Then the visitor might join the chamber’s life—not by dying, and not by remaining alive, but by losing the difference between arrival and having always been there.

This is what lives beneath the ground: animals, certainly; plants, perhaps; structures that are also organs; a colony that may be one body; a single instant crowded enough to resemble a world.

And beneath all those descriptions, something less like a creature than a condition:

life held so completely that it can never become death, and cannot quite continue being life.
