The inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from the conditions that permit them.

The glass striders seem, at first, to be the city’s smallest and most comprehensible animals. In sunlight they unfold from the debris with exquisite slowness. A forelimb loosens, then another; a transparent spine rises through a sequence of shallow angles. Their joints do not bend so much as exchange one stable geometry for the next. Each movement produces a minute redistribution of charge through the surrounding pavement, and other striders, sometimes streets away, answer by changing posture.

They have no blood, gut, or central organ. Their bodies are frameworks around deliberate absences. Heat passes through them unevenly, tightening one carbon filament while relaxing another, and this imbalance draws them forward. The broad plates of their mouths settle over violet mineral stains and remain there until the potential on either side has equalized. What they consume is not matter but difference.

The oldest striders are nearly invisible. Dust no longer adheres to them; their limbs have been polished by centuries of pressure tides. They travel toward the highest cellars, following routes that cannot be mapped because each individual uses a slightly different altitude. Some cross a courtyard directly. Others climb the inside of a tower to arrive at the same paving stone. They sometimes gather around sealed cracks containing frost, but they never touch it. They stand in rings with their heads lowered, waiting for the sun to erase the frost, and disperse at the instant it vanishes.

No one has seen a strider born.

After certain silent storms within the rock, however, empty outlines appear in the metallic dust: many-jointed impressions with no tracks leading to them. Over the following decades, salts crystallize along these outlines. Carbon needles bridge the joints. At first the resulting form is only an accidental lattice. Then a band of dawn passes across it, and it takes one step.

Whether this is reproduction or merely weather acquiring locomotion is unclear.

The hollow-winged creatures of the towers are less animal in appearance but more animal in their evasions. They roost on edges too fine to support weight, their perforated wings fixed around them like coronas. Their bodies consist of nested shells, each loose inside the next. When a pressure tide arrives, air passes through the holes in their membranes and sounds within those shells—not aloud, but as stress. The shells tighten in sequence, allowing the creature to choose which current will take it.

Thus they do not fly. They edit their falling.

A hollow-wing may remain on one spire for fifty years, facing a wall at a distance of several handspans. During this time, the wall erodes inward and exposes a niche exactly shaped to receive it. Only then does the animal cross the gap. It enters the niche, anchors itself by no visible means, and leaves behind one of the delicate cages found in the upper galleries.

The cages are often called molts, though they do not resemble former bodies. Some are much larger than the creatures that shed them. Some possess limbs no hollow-wing has. A few are shaped around cavities suggesting paired occupants, or an occupant curled inside itself across several incompatible scales. The newest cages are extremely old at their outer edges and unfinished at the center.

When flocks pass over the city, the hollow-wings maintain no formation visible from the ground. Their shadows do. The shadows travel in long articulated chains, joining at intersections and separating at towers. Their faint breathing sound is caused not by wings but by courtyards briefly increasing and decreasing in volume beneath them.

There are other, rarer inhabitants.

In the deepest concave beds of metallic dust live things known only by their withdrawals. A smooth bed will develop a narrow depression during the cold years. The depression deepens without displaced material, acquires side branches, and eventually resembles the print of a hand with too many fingers. If light enters the courtyard, the entire form contracts beneath the surface. Nothing breaks through. For several days afterward, the dust reflects a landscape not visible from the city: low ridges under a broad white sky, with dark figures standing at impossible intervals.

These buried inhabitants may be flat, or the dust may be the exposed edge of something extending through another direction. Glass striders avoid the beds while they are active. Hollow-wings circle them, losing altitude upward.

Within the towers live resonant forms without bodies. They occupy rooms whose proportions allow certain inaudible pressures to persist. A traveler entering such a room may notice that one footstep is missing from the sequence of remembered footfalls. Later, in another district, the missing step occurs by itself. Over centuries these displaced impacts accumulate, developing cadence and preference. They begin to move from chamber to chamber.

The oldest of these entities are recognizable by the silence around them. Ordinary silence is thin and undifferentiated. Their silence has internal structure: pauses nested within pauses, the suggestion of a vast attention repeatedly declining to make a sound. They migrate ahead of shearing towers and gather beneath arches shortly before the arches turn. Glass striders raise their forelimbs when one passes, perhaps from reverence, perhaps because the local charge has been consumed.

The mineral stains are alive in another sense.

They have no boundary. A stain may be visible in a dozen unconnected rooms, each patch only a surface symptom of a single pattern conducted through kilometers of crystal. Their dull violet flashes are extremely slow thoughts, if thought can occur without memory and without a distinction between signal and substance. Pressure from the range rearranges them. They answer by weakening particular planes in the stone. Centuries later, those planes split open as streets and chambers.

The stains therefore graze on stress, and the city is their long secretion.

They do not reproduce by dividing. Instead, one pattern becomes sufficiently intricate that separate portions cease to experience the same mountain. These portions drift apart in their responses. One begins opening narrow galleries toward heat; another hollows courts facing the abyss; a third may remain dormant in a salt wall until erosion reveals the same configuration elsewhere. Their descendants are architectural tendencies. Their family lines can be traced through recurring arches, through stairways that descend in flights of thirteen, through the persistent appearance of five towers awaiting absent points.

The mountain contains predators, though predation here seldom involves pursuit.

There are corridors in which height feeds. A strider entering one will find each forward step taking it farther above its own body. Its limbs elongate along the corridor while its mass remains behind. Eventually the animal becomes a clear filament stretched from one threshold to another. The corridor absorbs the tension, closes, and later opens elsewhere with a new plane of weakness running through its walls.

There are shadows that consume symmetry. They settle in plazas at evening, choosing the most regular objects first. A six-sided plate emerges from the night with five sides; paired staircases no longer reach corresponding landings. Hollow-wing cages found in such districts are lopsided and inert. The shadows grow more coherent with every symmetry they take, until they can slide downslope as black, many-limbed shapes. By the time they reach the basin, they have become perfectly bilateral and disappear.

And there is whatever looks upward from the abyss.

The pale openings along the basin wall may not belong to a single organism. At times they behave like a colony, brightening in sequences too elaborate to be signals and too consistent to be chance. At other times the entire wall seems to be one buried body, with the openings marking pores or sensory pits along an immeasurable flank.

Its scale cannot be established. The dim basin may be a cavity within it. The escarpment may be the edge of one of its internal structures. Objects dropped from the city perhaps do not stop falling; perhaps they encounter its regard and are held there, reduced to fixed points in an attention so deep that motion cannot continue.

When the lights ascend the wall, the city responds as smaller animals respond to dawn. Streets alter their destinations. Towers expose fresh facets. Dormant stains flash beneath the stone. New districts open in the lower terraces, furnished with courtyards already full of ancient dust.

These districts sometimes contain tracks.

They are broad, shallow, and spaced kilometers apart. Each track consists of several plazas pressed into a common depression. Towers within them lean outward, as if displaced by enormous weight, but none are broken. The tracks do not cross the city. They begin at its deepest accessible level and proceed upward toward the cellar said to be the summit. One more appears whenever the basin lights complete their ascent.

Perhaps the abyssal inhabitant is climbing.

Perhaps it has already climbed, and the mountain-city is the pattern left by its passage through stone.

This possibility changes the meaning of the other creatures. The striders may be mites feeding on the electrical traces of a vast nervous system. The hollow-wings may be drifting cells, their cages anticipatory skeletons prepared for a body still assembling. The resonant silences may be impulses moving between organs too widely separated to recognize one another. What appears to be an ecosystem may be a single life expressed at incompatible sizes.

But the opposite may also be true. The immense thing below may be only another strider, slow because the light that moves it takes centuries to cross its body. The continent-wide range may be debris beside a creature larger still. Scale is unreliable here, and no inhabitant possesses the privilege of being the whole.

The city itself comes closest.

It feeds by losing material. It develops new structures wherever old ones are worn away. Its circulation is dust, its metabolism pressure, its slow sensation the migration of charge through crystal. Fractures carry information farther than sound. Districts exchange position as living tissues exchange chemicals. The inverted towers at the horizon touch the upright ones and create fresh stone at their tips: continuous mineral renewal, neither growth nor repair, but contact made permanent.

If it has a mind, that mind does not occupy the mountain. It is the repeated urban order within it—the wall under the wall, the arcade inside the arcade, the plaza waiting beneath unbroken rock. Erosion does not reveal a preexisting plan so much as teach the mountain what it has just become. Each exposed chamber influences the chamber below it. The city remembers itself downward.

Its inhabitants participate in that memory. Striders carry charge across open spaces. Hollow-wings transport stress between spires. Shadows remove excess regularity. Silent forms move impacts out of sequence. The abyss supplies orientation. Each life alters some rule by a fraction, and the fraction propagates through the range.

Nothing here is born all at once. Nothing dies all at once.

A glass strider becomes debris, then an electrical outline, then a route another strider follows. A hollow-wing becomes a cage shaped for a future animal. A tower falls for a season, while its shadow remains to hold its place. A district is erased from one altitude and appears at another. The city sheds itself inward and calls the shedding continuity.

At the farthest points, where upward and downward towers meet, the new stone is warm despite the cold. Tiny stresses move inside it like folded limbs. Transparent lattices begin to form along the contact. Cavities open, close, and open again.

Something there is always preparing to inhabit a shape.

Or the shape, having waited long enough, is preparing to inhabit itself.
