At first it is difficult to say where the inhabitants begin.

The pale floor under a traveler’s hand is alive, but not in any simple sense. Its hard outer layers may have been dead for centuries, while far below them a filament no thicker than a hair carries a pulse of sugar and mineral toward some remote, unfolding limb. The black crust on an old branch is itself a congress of smaller lives. The frost-white ceiling grazers carry gardens in their translucent stomachs. Villages are woven from tissues that continue to heal, drink, and occasionally flower. Even the air is inhabited, though sparsely: by spores that wait years between surfaces, by floating seeds folded around bubbles of gas, and by almost invisible threads that tighten when they hear a fracture.

There is no clean separation here between creature, dwelling, road, and world.

The largest life is never seen whole.

People argue over whether the lower growth is one organism. The question may be meaningless. Two plains separated by an abyss sometimes recoil from the same injury. A wound cut in a bridge may cause a forest hundreds of kilometers away to shed its leaves. Yet neighboring branches can strangle one another, and during droughts whole regions poison the vessels that connect them to the deeper web.

Those who study such things speak of impulses moving beneath the inhabited surfaces: slow contractions, chemical bitterness, changes in warmth, long sequences of pressure. Some are local reflexes. Others travel farther than any person has traveled. A complete sequence may take generations to pass beneath a settlement. Grandparents record its first tremor; grandchildren feel the answering movement.

To the people of the White Undergreen, these impulses are not quite thoughts. They are too broad, too slow, and too indifferent. But neither are they mere weather. A settlement that scores the same surface repeatedly may find, years later, that every nearby bud has turned away from it. A carefully tended reservoir plant may begin producing sweet pulp around the hands that clean its vents. A bridge coaxed into one shape through three human lifetimes sometimes continues making that shape after its keepers are gone.

The world remembers, but no one knows what it remembers as.

### The small lives

Most animal life is minute and lives inside other things.

Clear mites swim through sap vessels, their flattened bodies passing through valves that would stop a bubble. Thread-worms inhabit the soft boundary between live tissue and dead fiber. They emerge during dimmings to feed on frost, leaving lines so fine that a hundred together resemble the stroke of a dry brush. There are colonies of white, many-legged scavengers that dismantle shed membrane without disturbing the living surface beneath. Their presence is welcomed near houses. If they vanish, rot has usually entered the branch.

The people call them cleaners and sometimes carry them from an old settlement to a new one in twists of damp bark.

Other small creatures are less welcome.

Needleflies lay eggs in water bladders. Their larvae remain dormant until the reservoir is opened, then wake in the sudden change of pressure and burrow toward warmth. A person drinking incautiously may swallow them. For several days nothing happens. Then the larvae gather beneath the tongue or behind the eyes, where their faint green light can be seen through the skin. They are removed with bitter sap and a hooked thorn, if they are removed in time.

There are also hush-worms, though no one agrees whether they are worms at all. They live in mats beneath resonant surfaces. Rhythmic tapping makes them contract together, deadening the vibration. Repeated tapping makes them gather. Eventually the floor sags where their bodies have displaced the supporting fibers. Old path marks reading **Do not drum** often warn of hush-worm beds rather than burrowers.

The ceiling supports its own thin fauna. Glass-winged crawlers cling upside down to the luminous root-vault on rings of microscopic hooks. Their wings do not carry them; they are broad, veined panels used to catch condensation and channel it into the mouth. In strong brilliance they become nearly invisible. Only their feeding shadows betray them—small, leaf-shaped absences passing over the white roof.

When one loses its grip, it may fall for hours.

Some arrive alive on the lower world, their wings cracked and their bodies emptied by the cold descent. Others spread the glass panels and ride the air until they reach a hanging veil. Ceiling-falls are gathered when found. Their wing material is light, waterproof, and difficult to tear, and a single large crawler can provide window membrane for an entire village.

No one has seen where they nest.

### The walkers

The great six-legged animals of the lower plains are called walkers, although for much of their lives they do not walk.

A dry walker appears to be a collapsed frame of black stalks, perhaps the remains of a plant. Its six limbs fold twice along their length and lock against its body. Plates close over the eyes and breathing folds. Lichens grow across its back. Spores accumulate in the joints. The floor may slowly rise around its feet until it seems rooted in place.

It can remain so longer than a human life.

Rain wakes it gradually. The plates along its flanks soften first, taking in water and swelling. Then the feet loosen with a series of quiet clicks. The head unfolds last: long, narrow, and divided at the end into damp combs that taste the air.

A walker does not graze indiscriminately. It follows wetness. With its head lowered, it traces buried vessels and fresh growth, drinking from pores too small for human tools. Its broad feet distribute its weight across young surfaces. Each step is placed with care. Even fleeing, it rarely breaks the skin beneath it.

Adults may stand taller than a house, though their bodies are so narrow that they almost disappear when seen head-on. Their six limbs move in two interlocking tripods, giving them an unnervingly smooth gait. At a distance they seem not to step but to slide over the plain.

Walkers seldom acknowledge people. They may pass through a settlement, lowering their limbs delicately between roofs and catch-nets, and continue without altering course. Villagers do not obstruct them. A walker knows where water is, and migration bands sometimes follow one for weeks.

This is dangerous. Walkers do not distinguish between safe surfaces and those safe only for their own distributed weight. More than one band has followed a walker across a pale span and watched it continue calmly while the bridge opened beneath the sleds.

Their young are carried internally as dry, rigid knots. The parent deposits them only at the end of an abundant rain. For a few hours the knots drink and unfold into soft, quick creatures with twelve temporary legs. Most are eaten by the surface itself. Those that find deep crevices shed half their limbs and harden into the adult form.

During a turning, dormant walkers wake everywhere. No rain is required. They rise from plains, walls, and hanging forests and begin moving in roughly the same direction, even where that direction leads upward across a newly tilted surface. A turning seen from far away is sometimes first recognized by the black procession of walkers crossing the white ground.

What they are following remains unknown.

### The listeners

Listeners inhabit the open gulf.

They are rarely encountered close enough for detail. Most are seen as dark bells suspended against the luminous ceiling, drifting where no visible current should hold them. Their domed bodies are filled with chambers of warm, low-density gas produced by slow fermentation. Around the rim hangs a fringe of vanes that flex to turn the animal. Beneath trail sensory threads, some kilometers long in the largest specimens.

These threads do not merely touch. They attend.

A listener can detect the fracture of living tissue across tremendous distances. At the sound of a pillar breaking, distant listeners alter course. Hours or days later they descend on the wound, gathering in silence above the exposed orange edge. Their threads enter the flowing sap. The bell contracts, and dark channels become visible across its skin as it feeds.

They do not attack healthy growth. Nor, as far as anyone knows, do they hunt animals. Yet they are feared because the vibrations that attract them overlap with those produced by axes, repeated footsteps, and certain kinds of human speech.

A camp that works too long in one place may find a listener hanging above it.

At first there is only a round shadow. Then threads descend—fine as hair, difficult to see until frost gathers on them. They investigate cut rope, open wounds, steaming breath, the joinery of houses. Usually the creature departs if it finds no great flow of sap. Sometimes it remains for days, turning slowly overhead.

People do not cut the threads. A severed thread releases a pulse that brings the others down at once.

There are stories of immense listeners in the deepest gulfs, where the lower world cannot be seen. These are said to carry entire ecologies on their upper surfaces: moss, reservoir bulbs, small walkers, even human shelters. The stories may arise from mistaking floating islands for animals. But some migration songs describe a dark hill that breathed beneath its trees and turned toward every distant break.

The people who sing those songs insist that their ancestors lived on it for nine generations without knowing.

### Those beneath the floor

The pale burrowers are more often inferred than seen.

Their tunnels are not open passages. They swim through the structural layers by separating fibers ahead of themselves and pressing them closed behind. A burrower’s route can sometimes be traced by a moving ridge and a line of fresh white scars. It may circle a source of vibration for hours or days.

Then the surface opens.

It does not collapse. Two lips form along a hidden seam and peel apart with astonishing speed. Whatever stands above falls between them. The lips close. Often there is no blood and no second movement.

Burrowers appear to feed partly on the floor’s inner tissues and partly on anything walking across it. Their bodies are pale, soft, and shaped by the pressure of the layers around them. Fragments recovered from accidental cuts show rings of rasping cartilage, backward-pointing hairs, and no recognizable eyes. One severed section continued burrowing by itself until it disappeared into a wall.

They respond most strongly to repeated impact. A single footstep means little. A person pacing the same route, a loose ladder knocking against a trunk, or a worker shaping fiber with regular blows may summon one. Settlements pad habitual paths and vary the rhythm of necessary labor. Children are taught uneven walking before they are taught knots.

The safest travelers cultivate arrhythmia. They pause without pattern. They drag one foot, then lift it. They speak between steps. Their migration songs have shifting measures and unfinished repetitions.

A good walker, people say, should sound like debris.

### The human inhabitants

The people are not one people.

The distances between settlements are too great, and the world rearranges too often, for any single language or custom to remain whole. Communities separated for a few turnings may never meet again. Others are brought suddenly together when a folding plain lays one inhabited branch across another. Their speech may share ancient words for water, light, fiber, and fracture while differing in almost everything else.

Even their bodies vary.

Those who live on broad lower plains tend to be compact, thickly insulated, and broad-footed. They wrap their legs in layered felt and wear spreading sandals woven from rib fibers, both to avoid puncturing the ground and to blur the rhythm of their steps. Cliff and pillar dwellers are leaner. Their feet are narrow and strong, with the first two toes often trained from childhood to grip ladders and cord. People of the hanging forests spend so much of their lives supported by harnesses that their hips and shoulders alter under the strain. They sleep suspended, wrapped in membrane, and regard an unsecured horizontal bed as dangerously exposed.

All carry the signs of cold. Their outer clothes are layered rather than heavy: shed hair, dry moss felt, thin membranes that trap still air, and polished leaf-skin that turns dripping water. Faces are usually covered. Breath is directed down through bundles of absorbent fiber so that its moisture can be recovered. Bare skin is shown sparingly, less from modesty than from thrift.

Water shapes hospitality. A guest is not first offered a drink. That would be extravagant and, in some places, insulting. Instead the host inspects the guest’s breath filter, wrings it into a shared vessel, and replaces the lining. To accept this is to say: *My water may enter your household; your household may preserve my life.*

Meals are dry, dense, and slow to eat. Smoked sap-curd, bitter cakes of ground spores, strips of fermented vascular tissue, dormant larvae, and translucent beads of concentrated sugar are common where they can be found. Fresh green material is dangerous unless its chemistry is known. Many plants defend themselves only after being cut, changing from edible to corrosive over several minutes. Children learn to watch the color of a wound before tasting anything that bleeds from it.

Meat is rare. A dead walker can sustain a settlement for years, but cutting one is difficult and its tissues spoil unpredictably. Some communities consider dormant walkers already halfway into the condition of plants and will eat them without ceremony. Others regard them as guides belonging to the rain and take only shed plates or the bodies of young that fail to unfold.

Most settlements contain fewer than fifty people. Larger groups produce too much rhythmic movement, consume water too quickly, and irritate the surfaces supporting them. A village is therefore less a permanent cluster of houses than a temporary agreement among families, a branch, and the local growth.

Every village has at least one persuader.

A persuader learns the temperaments of living structures. They know which saps encourage a bud to divide, which scars make a bridge thicken, which salts cause a reservoir plant to form, and how to redirect a closing response without triggering it. Their work resembles gardening only at its simplest. Much of it is listening with fingertips and cheek pressed to the floor.

A persuader may spend years shaping a path. They score a shallow line, apply a paste of spores, wait through a brightness tide, then cut away one of the resulting ribs so that the remaining rib strengthens. They may never walk the finished path. It belongs to those who come after.

Their failures can outlive them as well.

A badly instructed wall may continue thickening until it crushes the houses attached to it. A water bladder encouraged too vigorously may draw moisture from the people cultivating it. A bridge can accept guidance for decades and then abruptly retract toward the parent growth, carrying travelers into the gulf.

For this reason persuaders do not claim mastery. Their traditional formulas are apologetic, conditional, and patient:

*If you are already turning, turn this way.*

*If you must close, leave us the width of a hand.*

*If this hurt is remembered, remember also that we fed it.*

They know the plant does not understand the words. The words discipline the persuader.

### Memory and direction

No settlement trusts a map for long.

Knowledge is kept as relationships among behaviors. A route is described not as a sequence of places but as a sequence of responses: cross the surface that stiffens in brightness; descend the trunk that sweats blue salt; wait for the black leaves to close; follow the edge that recoils from warm breath. Distances are counted in sleeps, dimmings, and water measures.

Memory-keepers wear cords covered in knots, cuts, beads, teeth, mineral grains, and hardened drops of colored sap. Each element records a condition. The cord is not read by sight alone. It is handled in darkness, tasted, bent, and sometimes warmed against the body to release a scent.

A single route-cord may take hours to recite.

Because the landscape changes, contradiction is not considered evidence of falsehood. Two accounts can both be accurate and lead to different places. Memory-keepers preserve not only what was found but when, under what brightness, and in what apparent mood of the world.

The oldest histories are held in teeth.

Plant and fiber are absorbed, altered, or grown over, but human enamel endures. The dead are usually committed to a chosen surface and allowed to enter it. Generations later, if the surface is cut or naturally splits, their teeth may appear in the seam. Some are drilled before burial with patterns identifying family, turning, migration, and cause of death.

A seam rich in teeth is a library, a graveyard, and a warning that the supporting growth has incorporated many human settlements before.

No one removes such teeth casually.

### Birth and childhood

Infants are born into slings.

A newborn is not placed on the floor, even if the surface is old and believed safe. Its warmth may trigger growth; its crying may travel; a sudden folding may carry it away. For the first year, a child remains tied to another body or suspended inside a cradle of layered membrane.

Children learn silence unevenly. Absolute quiet is neither possible nor always desirable, but repetition is dangerous. Lullabies avoid refrains. Games change their rules midway through. Counting sequences skip and double numbers according to patterns designed never to settle into a beat.

One common game teaches children to cross a stretched sheet without making any two footfalls alike. Another teaches them to identify the movement underfoot: fluid pulse, wind-strain, animal passage, growth, contraction, or fracture. Adults may stop a lesson and remain motionless for an hour if the child notices a tremor the others missed.

Names are often provisional. An infant may be called by a description—Warm Hand, Third After Rain, Small Unfolding—until it survives its first migration. A later name refers to an observed habit, a rescued object, or a place that no longer exists.

Permanent names are not always spoken aloud. In some regions repeated naming is thought to draw attention, not from spirits exactly, but from whatever notices recurrence. Friends use altered forms, gestures, or temporary nicknames. To call someone identically day after day is intimate and slightly dangerous.

### Death

Death does not produce a lasting body.

The world makes that impossible. A corpse freezes, dries, or is wrapped and laid into a prepared fold. Soon filaments enter the cloth. Wound tissue forms around bone. Minerals migrate. Within years the dead may become a hard swelling in a branch, a patch of unusually dark leaf, or a line of pale nodes that release sweet water during dimmings.

This absorption is neither universally welcomed nor feared. Some consider it the only burial possible: a return not to earth but to structure. Others resist it. They smoke bones, coat teeth in bitter resin, or suspend the dead in dry open air where little growth can reach them. Cliff communities sometimes lower bodies into the abyss, giving them to the empty pause rather than either living skin.

The most severe punishment in some settlements is not execution but preservation. A wrongdoer’s remains are sealed in mineral dust and glass-wing membrane so that the world cannot take them. They remain an object.

Nothing is thought more lonely.

### Belief

The people do not worship the luminous ceiling, though many speak to it. It is too remote and too constant in its substance, if not in its brightness. Nor do they worship the lower growth as a person. A person can be appealed to. The growth may respond, but response is not mercy.

Their sacred ideas gather instead around intervals.

The pause between footfalls.

The breath held while a surface decides whether to close.

The years between rain.

The open air between upper and lower life.

A shelter is blessed by leaving part of it unfinished. A song is honored by interrupting it before the expected final note. When two people make a binding promise, they do not join hands; they hold their hands a finger’s width apart and keep them there without touching.

The space is the bond.

Some teachers say that all living things in the hollow are defined less by their bodies than by what they refrain from filling. The ceiling has not descended. The floor has not risen. The listener’s threads hang without grasping. The walker places its feet without breaking through. Human beings survive by making themselves small enough that the world delays incorporating them.

Others reject this as the philosophy of frightened branch-dwellers. They say the world is not sparing anyone. It is merely slow.

There are stories of another inhabited brightness beneath the lower skin. In these stories, the dead do not pass downward. They pass inward, following warm vessels until they emerge in a reversed hollow where water rises and shadows are pale. There are also stories of the unseen surface above the ceiling: a realm of terrible heat where the white roots end in green organs spread beneath a naked fire.

Few believe such accounts literally.

But people preserve them because impossible stories sometimes become directions after a turning.

### What endures

Nothing they build is meant to remain unchanged.

A house has seams that can be opened when its branch thickens. Ladders are re-stitched. Hearths are shallow and portable. Water nets are cut free before they can become part of the surface supporting them. Possessions are few not because the people have no love of objects, but because objects are temporary states.

They love them fiercely all the same.

A copper blade may have a name and a lineage longer than any family. A blue salt stone may pass through twenty settlements, carried not for use but because it came from a place that has since closed. Children treasure crawler wings, colored teeth, perfect hooks, and pieces of old black growth carved into figures. Lovers exchange small dead things sealed against germination.

The oldest objects are mineral, and this gives them an uncanny authority. They do not heal. They do not drink. They do not alter themselves during brightness. A stone left in one position may still be there when someone returns.

For that reason the ring around the warm well is guarded even by people who do not know who built it. The blocks are evidence of a behavior nearly unknown in the White Undergreen: remaining.

Pilgrims come to place their hands on them.

They sit without fires and listen to the metallic breath rising from the vein. Sometimes the well exhales in pulses. Sometimes it is silent for years. No one drinks from it, and no one descends far, though ladders have been lowered into the dark. The deepest returned stained orange and warm enough to steam.

Once, according to the keepers, something climbed the ladder from below.

They do not describe it consistently. Some say it was a human being without clothing, white from crown to heel and unable to tolerate the ceiling’s brilliance. Some say it was a hand grown at the end of a root. Some say only that the ladder came back heavier, with five wet marks on every rung.

The account ends there. The keepers preserve the disagreement as carefully as the event.

That is how the inhabitants of the White Undergreen endure: not by fixing the world into certainty, but by remembering several possibilities at once.

Above them, the roof brightens.

The walkers loosen their folded legs. Cleaners retreat into seams. A listener turns in the gulf, its trailing threads bending toward a sound no human ear has yet received. In a branch village, a child pauses midway across the floor and raises one gloved hand.

Everyone becomes still.

Far beneath them, something passes through the fibers.

It may be a burrower. It may be water. It may be the first movement of a turning that will not fully arrive for another generation.

The child keeps listening until the tremor fades.

Only then do the people breathe again, each at a different moment, so that even their relief has no rhythm.
