The inhabitants are, above all, keepers of small reserves.

They keep water in their throats, beneath their shells, between layers of skin. They keep eggs dormant in dust and seedlike pupae sealed in clay. They keep coolness in burrows no wider than a finger, memory in paths pressed imperceptibly deeper across stone, and whole seasons folded inside bodies that appear dead.

Few grow large. The country does not favor anything that must feed continuously or cross great distances each day. Its animals are built close to the ground, where shade is measurable in the thickness of a pebble. Their backs are broad, their undersides soft, their limbs short enough to withdraw quickly. Many have the same dark polish as the plateau stones. Others resemble lichen, old shell, thorn, or a scrap of mineral skin.

The little tortoises are among the most visible, though visibility here is relative. Their shells are flattened and irregular, with pale margins that look chipped rather than grown. Dust settles into the seams of their plates. Lichens sometimes take hold on the oldest individuals, so that a resting animal becomes a small inhabited hill.

They are not solitary. In dry years they gather in favorable hollows by the hundreds, fitting themselves together with slow precision. The lowest remain cool beneath the weight of the others; those above angle their shells to catch condensation. At dawn, beads of water travel down the stacked carapaces and collect where their mouths can reach. Individuals may occupy the same hollow for decades, leaving it only after rain. Then the entire heap loosens. Heads emerge, feet seek purchase, and the tortoises pour across the stones with an urgency made strange by their slowness.

Their dead remain near the pools. Wind and passing shells move the empty carapaces outward. Generations of this slight pressure build the white, branching walls. Living tortoises use them as guides, walking with one flank against the remains of their predecessors.

The lizards are thinner and more secretive. Their ribs compress, their skulls are wedge-shaped, and their scales overlap backward so they can push into cracks but cannot easily be pulled out. Some are almost colorless except for a dark line over the spine. Others are mottled with orange and black, matching the stone crusts on which they wait.

They do not chase insects. They select a threshold—the mouth of a tunnel, the shaded edge of a slab, the narrow passage between two pebbles—and become part of it. Their tongues emerge at long intervals to taste changes in the air. A lizard may know that a beetle is approaching long before the beetle appears, having felt the minute alteration of grit beneath its feet. The strike, when it comes, is the only rapid thing it may do all day.

The plateau’s snakes live similarly. They are short, heavy-bodied animals with heads no wider than their necks, well suited to entering abandoned chimneys. During the hottest months several may coil together in a cool cell, tangled so closely that the group seems a single many-spined creature. They eat rarely. Some spend a year digesting one clutch of frogs or a nest of unfledged birds. Their scales are dull, but when wet they show faint bands like buried strata.

The frogs are stranger because most of the time they do not resemble frogs at all. Dry and folded, they are little fists beneath the soil. Their legs lie wrapped against their bodies; their eyes sink behind translucent lids; layer after layer of shed skin forms a waterproof envelope around them. Only the nostrils remain open, and these can close when even the underground air becomes too dry.

They may remain so for years.

Rain reaches them first as pressure. A drop strikes a stone, and the vibration passes downward. Then comes the darkening of soil, the softening of clay, and the chemical taste of new water. The frogs split their coverings from within. They rise already calling, forcing voices through throats unused since the last storm. Their entire active lives may be compressed into a single night: feeding, mating, laying eggs in pools that did not exist at sunset and may not survive the following noon.

Their young develop with equal haste. Some hatch before the egg jelly has fully swollen. Tadpoles rasp algae from the shells of drinking tortoises and consume unhatched siblings when the water begins to shrink. Those that complete their change leave the pools no larger than beetles. They crawl beneath stones, bury themselves, and become another generation of closed fists.

The mud chimneys belong to pale, soft insects seldom seen outside them. Each colony is less a crowd than a distributed body. Deep chambers hold the heavy breeding forms; higher cells contain grubs, fungal gardens, seed husks, and pellets of damp clay. The towers regulate heat through narrow shafts. Warm air rises from the central passages while cooler air is drawn through pores near the base. On condensation mornings the inner walls sweat. Workers gather the droplets in their mouths and carry them downward.

A living chimney has a faint pulse. Place a hand against it at dawn and one can feel the movement of thousands of jaws altering walls grain by grain.

But the colonies do not own their structures. Small birds nest in cavities near the sunward face. Mites live in the birds’ nesting material. Beetles eat the mites; smaller mites cling to the beetles. Lizards occupy breaches made by birds. Snakes enter after the lizards. Roots penetrate from below, and pearl-bodied grubs feed on the roots. When the colony dies, the tower may remain inhabited for centuries, its tenants changing as slowly as the stone around it.

The birds are modest in color and economical in flight. Most have long toes for stepping between thorns and narrow beaks for extracting dormant insects. At noon they press themselves into the dark interiors of cushions, facing outward through the spines. Their feathers are edged like dry grass, and their eyelids carry fine combs that exclude dust.

They sing seldom. A continuous song would disclose too much and waste water from the mouth. Instead they exchange isolated notes, each separated from its answer by enough time to confuse a listener. A pair can remain in contact across an entire basin through six or seven chirps in an afternoon.

After rain, larger birds descend. They are not true residents, yet the plateau belongs to their life cycle. High above the tableland they circle for days, watching for the brief dark shine of flooded basins. Their legs trail behind them like threads. When they land, they move with great care among the tortoises, spearing frogs and soft insects from the shallows. Before the pools vanish they depart, carrying plateau life elsewhere as eggs stuck to their feet, mites beneath their feathers, and seeds in the mud dried around their toes.

The invertebrates are beyond counting. There are beetles whose wing cases seal tightly enough to form canteens around their bodies. Moths that never feed as adults, living only long enough to mate and press their eggs beneath a flake of stone. Scale-backed worms that move through broken rock by expanding alternating sections of their bodies. Transparent mites that drink condensation directly through their skins. Predatory larvae shaped like grains of grit, waiting at the bottoms of dust funnels.

Some species exist almost entirely on a single recumbent boulder. One lives only on its orange lichens, another only in the black crust at its northern edge. A tiny hunter follows the boundary between the two. To them, the stone is not an object but a continent: its upper hollow a lake country, its sunward face a desert, its shaded underside a deep and stable world. A migration from one side to the other may occupy several generations.

The plants, too, behave like animals in hiding. What appears to be a thorn-cushion is mostly underground: a dense wooden body thrust into fractures, with only its defended tips exposed. Its roots follow condensation seams and the ancient routes of vanished water. Some roots grow around buried shells, using them as chambers in which moisture lingers. Others enter abandoned insect galleries and kill the remaining colony slowly, then inherit its cool depths.

Their leaves remain folded until dampness reaches a precise threshold. Then they open, revealing pale inner surfaces furred to hold droplets. Flowers may last less than an hour. They smell faintly of honey and wet iron, calling insects that have been waiting in the same patch of ground for years.

There are predators here, but no creature rules openly. Size is less important than possession of the correct threshold at the correct hour. The lizard waits for the beetle. The snake waits for the lizard. A buried grub waits beneath the snake’s resting place for scales of shed skin. Nothing wastes itself in pursuit.

Even competition is slow. Two thorn-cushions may contest the damp seam below a slab for a century, each thickening roots too gradually to be observed. Two tortoise congregations may press shell walls toward the same seasonal pool until their dead form a single enclosure. Colonies in neighboring chimneys may conduct wars entirely underground, stealing chambers one clay partition at a time.

To an eye accustomed to movement, these lives seem diminished. They are not. They are compressed.

Each creature carries an answer to absence: how to drink when there is no water, how to feed when nothing grows, how to reproduce in an hour and wait ten years, how to be numerous without seeming present. Their greatest shared adaptation is not armor, camouflage, or dormancy, but restraint. They spend almost nothing—not motion, not sound, not moisture, not opportunity.

At noon they disappear into the completed land.

At rain they reveal that the stones were crowded all along.
