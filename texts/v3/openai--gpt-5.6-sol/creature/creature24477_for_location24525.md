At first, what lives on the High Green seems too small to count.

The surface trembles in places where there is no wind. A blade tilts, rights itself, and tilts again. Water quivers in a leaf-basin though the sky is clear. If the living floor is parted, the apparent ground resolves into traffic: pale bodies slipping between roots, lacquered beetles turning sideways through narrow pores, mites grazing on wet films, and translucent larvae pulsing inside the water-storage tissues of plants.

Most never see daylight. They spend their lives in the layered dark between the green upper skin and the oldest brown roots below. There are raspers that eat only dead fiber, borers that enter living stems, and small armored scavengers that follow behind them, sealing damaged walls with paste made from pulp and saliva. Soft colonial animals line some cavities like patches of gray flesh. They draw nourishment from seepage and withdraw all their feeding threads at the first vibration of a heavy foot.

The thumb-crabs are among the most visible of these hidden nations. Their shells are mottled in the colors of wet root and old leaf. After rain they climb out of their chambers to graze the rims of cisterns, carrying drops on the bristles of their legs. As the plateau dries, each returns to a chosen pocket, plugs the entrance with chewed fiber, and sits in darkness over its diminishing pool. Some live for years in the same plant hollow. When the runner grows, their chambers travel slowly with it.

Deeper down are the cause of the tremors.

The long-bodied burrowers are not worms, though only the front of one is commonly seen. They have blunt, plated heads and dozens of short gripping limbs. Rather than forcing tunnels through the mat, they loosen its woven strands, pass between them, and knit the opening shut behind themselves. The largest are longer than houses. They browse on old layers that would otherwise accumulate until the surface smothered itself. Their passage drains some hollows and dams others. Pools, fungal gardens, and even footpaths may owe their existence to a burrower that passed beneath them generations ago.

People do not hunt these animals. A wounded one leaks a bitter fluid that kills roots for many strides around it. More importantly, where burrowers disappear, the ground becomes waterlogged, heavy, and prone to tearing away from the stone.

The colony-makers live higher in the weave. They resemble large ants only at a glance. Each has six walking limbs, two fine cutting appendages beneath the jaw, and a swollen abdomen that carries fungal spores. Their cities occupy hollow runners. Within them are warm nurseries, refuse pockets, chambers of damp shredded leaf, and orchards of colorless fungus.

Different colonies cultivate different strains. Some fungi digest corky tissue; others neutralize plant poisons or produce wax used to waterproof the nest. Colonies raid one another less often for food than for spores. During such raids, lines of workers meet on the runner roads and wrestle almost soundlessly while winged guards hover above them, rattling their hard forewings.

Their enemies listen for that rattle.

The plate-backed hunters spend most of their lives under the visible surface. Their bodies are low, muscular, and loose-jointed, with overlapping dorsal scales resembling dead leaves. They can push up a flap of vegetation without breaking it, look out through the gap, and pull the green lid closed again. Their eyes are poor. Instead they press the sensitive bones of the jaw against roots and hear movement transmitted through the mat.

They hunt colony-makers, nesting birds, young grazers, and occasionally people. Before an attack, the ordinary insect chorus often ceases in a widening ring above them. This is the silence the plateau dwellers fear. It is not emptiness but attention.

Above the hidden world moves another, more conspicuous one.

The seed eaters arrive first as a darkening of the horizon. Each animal is scarcely longer than a finger, with black wings, a bronze abdomen, and a heavy mouth designed to crack the plateau’s hard, wind-resistant seeds. Alone, one is delicate. In migration they become weather. Millions skim just above the plants, turning together around pools and outcrops. Their wake presses the silver blades flat. When they settle, whole districts change color.

Copper pollinators follow the flowering fronts. Their bodies are densely furred and hot to the touch, and they sleep in communal knots beneath leathery fans. At dawn the outermost animals wake first and vibrate their wings, warming those packed within. A single sleeping mass may contain tens of thousands. When it breaks apart, it does so from the surface inward, as if a piece of copper were fraying into sparks.

The greatest fliers rise from the ravines.

They begin life far below, in damp shelves on the cliff walls. Their immature forms are heavy, many-legged grazers that cling to hanging gardens and feed through several rainy seasons. At maturity they seal themselves inside pale cases. On hot days the cases split, and the adults launch into the rising air.

Their wings are clear except for a lattice of dark veins. The largest span several body-lengths from tip to tip and climb without beating their wings for minutes at a time. They have no mouths. All the stored strength of their long youth is spent in a few brilliant days of flight, mating, and egg-laying. Their empty bodies eventually fall across the plateau in such numbers that grazing birds change their routes to follow them.

These birds are the most familiar large animals of the open summit.

They stand as high as a person’s chest but carry their weight on broad, spreading toes. Their wings are reduced, useful for balance and threat display rather than flight. The beak is wide and edged with replaceable plates that shear young shoots without tearing up the structural roots below. As they walk, they utter low contact calls nearly below human hearing. A company may appear silent while the mat hums under their feet.

Their plumage supports a fauna of its own. Bright mites clean the shafts. Long-legged parasites drink blood at thin-skinned joints. Tiny false flowers cling behind the birds’ crests and lure pollinators close enough to be eaten. Some birds tolerate these growths; others scrape them away on stone outcrops. Old individuals can be recognized by the gardens on their backs.

They nest around warm vents where decomposition heats the deep mat. Eggs are laid in communal rings and covered with fermenting leaves. No parent knows only its own young. The chicks emerge together and remain in tight creches at the center of the company, where the adults’ feet and bodies shield them from burrowing predators.

The long-winged flock birds belong almost entirely to the air. Their feet are small hooks, useful only for clinging to root cables during storms. They feed, mate, and sleep while flying. At night, half the flock circles with open eyes while the rest drift in brief sleep, each bird held in place by the calls of its neighbors. The flocks remember the airways between plateaus: invisible corridors of lift shaped by cliff, sun, and season. Young birds learn them by following elders. A route can persist long after a rockfall has altered the land below.

Along the rims patrol solitary hook-bills. Their bodies are narrow and their tails divide into steering vanes. They do not strike prey from above. Instead they approach from beneath the cliff edge, hidden by stone, then rise abruptly through a migration. Their hooked beaks close around a wing or limb. A successful hunter may carry its prey to a ledge where generations of discarded glassy wings have formed translucent drifts.

The six-legged browsers are the largest regular walkers of the summit.

They are built close to the ground, with three pairs of columnar legs ending in circular pads. Each pad can spread wider than the animal’s head, conforming to the root mat without puncturing it. Their hide hangs in loose folds that channel condensation toward the mouth. Two forward-facing sensory crests taste the air and touch the ground, reading chemical traces left by plants, water, predators, and earlier herds.

A browser does not remember a cistern as a picture. It remembers the sequence of flavors that leads there: bitter silver leaf, chalky runner sap, the pepper scent of a colony nest, then the cool mineral taste of roots near stored water. Herds follow these taste-roads with remarkable precision, even through cloud.

They browse slowly and selectively. One mouth crops leaves while a second, smaller mouth beneath the throat gathers fruit and insects disturbed by the feet. The middle pair of legs can lock in place during sleep. From a distance, a resting herd resembles a scatter of low gray boulders.

Their calves are born narrow-footed and cannot safely cross weak ground. For their first season they ride in shallow dorsal folds on an adult’s back. Several calves may be carried by one animal, and the carrier is not always a parent. Herd membership matters more than descent.

At cliff edges the fauna changes shape.

Turnclaws live on the hanging root curtains. They are compact, furred animals whose hind feet rotate nearly backward. Their tails divide at the end into two gripping lobes. They descend headfirst, testing every cable before committing their weight, and sleep wedged where roots branch against the stone.

Their fruit grows not on the bright upper leaves but along the shaded undersides of cliff runners. Turnclaws eat the pulp and press the seeds into damp cracks with their thumbs. In this way the hanging gardens move slowly across the walls. A cliff stripped of turnclaws may remain green for decades, then fail all at once as its old roots die without replacement.

Some turnclaws live their entire lives on one vertical garden. Others cross between plateaus on root cables during calm weather. Their calls are short and resonant, shaped to travel sideways along cliffs rather than upward. Neighboring colonies may converse across distances no ground animal could cross.

There are stranger inhabitants deeper in the mountain: blind shell-backed things in flooded galleries, pale fliers that emerge only through vertical shafts, and slow colonies that coat stone with a luminous skin. Plateau people know them mostly from what storms bring up—shed plates, translucent eggs, or drowned bodies in cisterns. They say the massif is not hollow but full, and that the visible animals are only those the mountain has exhaled.

The people themselves are few enough that an entire day’s walk may pass without sight of another dwelling.

They are broad-footed and light in build, not through any dramatic alteration of form but through lifelong habit and selection. Children learn to crawl before they are allowed to stand on uncertain mat. Adults walk with the feet set slightly apart, placing the outer edge first and rolling the weight inward. A stranger stamps; a highlander listens through the sole.

Their toes are usually bare inside woven sandals, free to feel changes in tension. Thick belts distribute carried loads across hips and chest. Nothing heavy hangs from one narrow strap. Even infants are carried in wide fiber slings, often between two adults when crossing weak ground.

Hearing is both prized and protected. Most people wear soft plugs made of wax and plant down during the noon chorus. These are removed to test a bridge, locate a cistern, or listen for the disturbed rhythm that betrays a predator. Their spoken languages favor low vowels, touch signals, and visible movements of the hands. In the loud season, conversation becomes partly tactile: fingers against wrist, shoulder, jaw, or the tensioned cord linking a group in cloud.

Names often contain a route. A person may be known by the cistern near which they were born, the bridge their household tends, and the seasonal path on which they first traveled. Kinship is similarly practical. One belongs to those who share water rights, warning calls, pruning duties, and responsibility for a span of living road.

Their houses cling to stone because stone is one of the few things that does not breathe, sag, heal, or wake.

A household may consist of several low rooms around a bare outcrop, with raised hearth trays and roofs tied down by cables that remain living at the ends. Sleeping mats are suspended just above the floor so the movement of small creatures can pass beneath. Food vessels hang from branching frames. Water is kept in sealed root bladders, each marked with the date and place of filling.

No house is quiet. Colonies tick in the walls. Roof leaves contract at dusk. Tether roots creak. People sleep within this sound and wake quickly if part of it stops.

Their most respected specialists are not chiefs or hunters but graft-keepers.

A graft-keeper knows which runners will join, which will strangle one another, how to persuade roots to grow along a frame, and how much leaf a bridge must retain to feed itself without becoming too heavy. They tend crossings over decades, though no individual expects to finish the work they begin. Apprentices inherit bridges as others might inherit estates.

Each living span has a character. Some are stiff and low, armored by old wood. Some sway broadly and are safer in a wind than in calm air. Some must be crossed barefoot because their bark reacts to hard soles by closing its water pores. People address them by name—not because they believe the bridges are persons exactly, but because anything that changes, remembers injury, and carries one’s grandchildren over an abyss is owed more than the language of an object.

Cistern-openers hold comparable authority. They read the swelling of stems and the coolness of the mat, then cut only where water can be taken without draining the surrounding roots. The incision is braced, tapped, and sealed with clay mixed with antiseptic sap. A badly opened reservoir can kill a runner road or collapse a neighborhood of burrows. A well-kept cistern may serve a settlement for centuries, expanding as the plant grows around the chamber.

The people eat insects, eggs, cliff fruit, fungal cakes, and the tender tips of plants pruned from roads and bridges. Grazing birds are rarely killed except during famine. Instead people follow their companies to gather parasites, shed feathers, infertile eggs, and the rich compost left around nesting vents. Six-legged browsers are not domesticated, but some herds tolerate human presence. Travelers sometimes walk in their tracks, where the mat has already been tested by a greater distributed weight.

Fire remains a dangerous guest. It is raised in clay trays on stone feet and fed with dried insect cases, dung, dead root scrap, and oils that burn with little spark. The people have no great bonfires. Their ceremonies of light use polished shells, luminous cave growths, or jars filled with small flashing fliers.

Death is handled without burial. The living floor is too crowded, and deep cutting is forbidden. Bodies may be wrapped in fruiting vine and placed on remote stone ledges, where hook-bills, insects, and sun reduce them. Some families suspend their dead beneath a chosen root bridge. The bundle remains there until the wrapping grows through it and takes its weight. Years later nothing distinct is left, but the bridge thickens.

Thus an ancestor may become, quite literally, part of the road.

The older stone circles are treated in much the same way: not as monuments to be restored, but as living obligations. Vines are pruned from certain entrances and encouraged over others. Pale route-posts are grafted when they split. No one remembers the first planter, yet each settlement sends someone to tend the line before the rains.

The High Green has few creatures that can be understood alone. The burrower aerates the mat; the colony-makers digest it; the fungi feed the plants; the grazing birds prune them; the fliers pollinate the cliff gardens; the turnclaws plant those gardens anew. Browsers press paths into the surface without breaking it. People open water, seal wounds, and carry grafts from plateau to plateau.

Even the predators serve the weave. They keep the colony-makers from hollowing runners too completely and prevent grazing birds from stripping new growth to the root. Silence follows them, but so does balance.

From a distant plateau, the inhabitants vanish into scale. A browser herd becomes a gray stippling. A settlement is no more than several pale roofs beside a dark stone tooth. Root-tenders crossing a bridge look like seeds caught on a thread. The great migrations are the only lives large enough in number to alter the face of the country.

Yet under that apparent vacancy, almost nothing is unoccupied. Water belongs to larvae and crabs. Hollows belong to colonies. Root undersides are nurseries; cliff walls are orchards; warm vents are nesting grounds; the air itself is partitioned into feeding heights, sleeping gyres, hunting paths, and ancestral routes of lift.

The people do not describe themselves as living on the plateau.

In their oldest expressions, they live **among the breaths**: the upward breath of the ravines, the wet breath of cloud, the green breath of the mat, and the deep slow breath beneath it that lifts, settles, rots, heals, and bears their weight.

They are not masters of the High Green. Nothing there is.

They are among the larger creatures it has learned to carry.
