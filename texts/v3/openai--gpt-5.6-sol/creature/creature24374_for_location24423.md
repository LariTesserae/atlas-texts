The inhabitants are easiest to mistake for travelers.

Seen at a distance, they are narrow figures crossing spaces built for giants: one dark mark on a pale avenue, three passengers in a tram long enough to carry hundreds, a maintenance crew standing like loose nails at the base of a tower. They wear layered coats of pressed fiber, stiff at the shoulders and weighted along the hems so the wind cannot turn them inside out. Hoods extend into short cowls over the face. Their goggles are gray or amber, their gloves ribbed with conductive thread, and nearly everyone carries a coil of line.

They do not hurry. Haste is useful only where distance can be trusted.

Up close, they are human enough to make their differences seem at first like consequences of climate. They are tall, though generations raised beneath immense ceilings may account for that. They tend to be lean and broad-footed. Their skin has a dry, faintly grained surface, often marked at the temples and wrists by branching lines darker than veins. Their eyes close against dust with two movements: an ordinary lid followed by a translucent inner film. Older people may remain motionless for long intervals in sunlight, facing it with an attention that is difficult to distinguish from rest.

Their resemblance to humanity becomes less certain under medical examination.

They have warm blood, lungs, bones, and the familiar arrangement of organs, but threaded among these are pale vascular structures that resemble rootlets. Fine green-black bodies collect around the ribs and shoulder blades. In bright seasons these tissues produce sugars and medicinal compounds; in winter they retreat into dormancy. No one survives by light alone, yet a plateau resident deprived of daylight becomes ill more quickly than one deprived of food.

Their bones contain mineral laminations rather than marrow in some places. Broken limbs are splinted with living fiber that eventually grafts to the patient and must be pruned away—or not. Teeth are replaced by ceramic growths cultured in mouth-shaped molds. Scar tissue sometimes buds.

Whether these traits are inherited, acquired, or imposed by the city is a question with political implications. The inhabitants rarely answer it directly.

They call themselves, in the most common translation, **the Kept**.

The word can mean those who are preserved, those who are maintained, or those assigned to maintenance. It also carries the sense of something retained in the memory of a living structure. An old building may be said to keep a staircase. A reservoir keeps last year’s rain. A person is kept by a household, a district, and the plateau itself.

Each child is grafted ceremonially to a municipal plant soon after birth. The contact is brief: a shallow incision in the palm pressed to a receptive bud. The resulting scar forms a small branching emblem unique to the district. Officially, this establishes citizenship and gives the city a biochemical record by which to recognize the child. Doors open more readily to a recognized hand. Domestic plants moderate their toxins. Transit vines accept the passenger’s electrical signature.

In practice, the bond goes deeper. People dream of places they have never visited. They wake with the taste of rain in districts still dry. A child may become feverish when an old civic tree is felled kilometers away. At death, some bodies are buried in root chambers, and years later their habitual gestures appear in the architecture: a window repeatedly unlatches at the hour its former keeper rose; a stair develops the slight unevenness of a remembered limp.

The Kept do not consider this survival. Neither do they consider it entirely metaphorical.

Their population is low partly by necessity. Children require water, warmth, and spatial consistency, all expensive resources. Births are often planned by district councils according to reservoir forecasts and the availability of stable rooms. Pregnancy is monitored not only by physicians but by surveyors. A child carried through repeated spatial distortions may develop contradictory anatomical patterns: doubled fingerprints, organs displaced into improbable cavities, memories of siblings never born.

For this reason, nurseries cluster around locality stones. They are among the few crowded places in the city.

There, children play winding games with colored cords. They learn to test corners before they can read. They are taught that a building has several kinds of truth: the shape it shows, the shape recorded in civic plans, the shape through which water passes, and the shape it remembers. A competent adult must know which truth matters at a given moment.

Their earliest lessons are practical:

Count doors when entering.

Touch the floor before trusting the view from a window.

If your echo returns in another person’s voice, leave by the route you used.

Never answer a familiar call from inside an uncut wall.

They learn these rules without solemnity. Children make jokes about old rooms and dare one another to step over harmless seams. They collect shed signal petals, polish glass mites into tiny ornaments, and race along root grooves while the adults shout about unstable gradients. Adolescents are notorious for entering buildings during leaf-turn simply to see where the rooms go.

Most eventually become more cautious. Some become route readers.

Route readers are recognizable by the spools worn across their backs and the small mineral weights fastened to their belts. They walk ahead of work crews, funerals, freight convoys, and wedding parties, paying out marked fiber behind them. The line is measured for length, tension, twist, elapsed time, and changes in sap odor. A reader does not merely find a path. They negotiate with the city’s present geometry.

The best readers seem eccentric even to their neighbors. They pause to watch shadows on blank walls. They knock on roots and listen through their teeth. They may refuse a broad, familiar boulevard and instead lead a convoy through kitchens, service hollows, and the crown of a sleeping transit vine. Their instructions are obeyed because bad readers disappear and good ones grow old.

Surveyors are more formal. They work in synchronized pairs or groups of three, never alone in the oldest districts. Their clocks are sealed, mechanical, and mounted over the sternum. They carry rigid measuring rods grown around mineral cores, because flexible instruments can be persuaded by the city. Surveyors map contradictions and submit pruning orders when structures have become logically unsound.

This gives them unusual authority. They may condemn half a household because its upstairs has begun to precede its downstairs. They may order the removal of a beloved ancestral room. Disputes between surveyors and residents can last for generations, particularly when a dangerous chamber contains family memories, stored water, or an elderly relative who insists nothing is wrong.

Pruners execute such decisions.

They are arborists, engineers, surgeons, and sometimes executioners. Their tools include saws, cautery hooks, electrical lances, growth suppressants, and wedges of inert ceramic. Before cutting, they feed the structure a sequence of chemical signals intended to distinguish injury from revision. A cleanly pruned building heals around the contradiction. A frightened one may seal doors, flood passages with bitter sap, or shed an occupied floor.

Pruners therefore speak while they work. Some recite the official plan of the building. Some sing old industrial rhythms. Some simply explain each cut in a calm voice, as though reassuring an animal.

Factories support another class of inhabitants: pump crews, vat tenders, fiber pressers, fermentation keepers, electricians, and deep-shaft mechanics. These workers are heavier than most plateau people, with reinforced boots and coats waxed against sap. Their hearing is often damaged. They communicate across machinery through hand signs and flashes from throat-mounted lamps.

Industrial households live by shifts rather than daylight. Their kitchens smell of yeast, hot resin, mineral salts, and the sweet metallic odor of cut vascular tissue. They eat dense cakes of fungal protein, translucent root starch, fermented leaf curd, and occasionally reservoir worms reserved for illness, childbirth, or civic feasts. Water is served in small black cups and consumed completely. Leaving a cup unfinished is not merely rude; it is difficult for plateau residents to comprehend.

They are not humorless. Much of their humor concerns the city’s appetite for tools.

A wrench set down on a warm floor may be engulfed by morning. Cables are incorporated into branches. Service hatches heal shut around ladders. Workers mark equipment with irritant dyes and give especially vulnerable machines insulting names, on the theory that a living structure is less likely to absorb something addressed as an individual.

Households are rarely defined only by blood. A household may consist of siblings, apprentices, former spouses, a route reader who cannot find their original district, and one person who has technically been dead for years but continues to occupy a room accessible every third afternoon. Membership depends on shared water rights, maintenance obligations, and recognition by the dwelling.

The dwelling’s judgment matters. Domestic structures can be temperamental. They adjust humidity, grow sleeping niches, warm benches, and produce edible membranes, but they also remember conflicts. A house repeatedly exposed to anger may thicken its internal doors. One treated with excessive tenderness may become overprotective, sealing residents inside during bad weather or extending corridors to prevent departures.

Courtship therefore includes the architecture. A suitor may be accepted by a person and rejected by their rooms. Couples exchange grafts rather than rings: small cuttings from their household plants, trained together around a mineral bead. If the graft survives a full dry season without one stem consuming the other, the union is considered promising.

Funerals are held in open wind whenever possible.

The dead are wrapped in absorbent cloth and carried along a route verified that morning. At each locality stone the procession stops, allowing the deceased’s official position to be recorded one final time. Some are cremated in fermentation furnaces, their mineral residue added to new anchor stones. Others are lowered into civic root chambers.

Names are not carved. They are encoded in growth instructions.

This has produced an archive that no one completely understands. Memorial gardens send up narrow leaves patterned with biographies, but mutations accumulate. A respected machinist may be remembered after four centuries as a sequence for pressure-resistant bark. A singer may survive as the pitch of a ventilation trunk. A criminal’s name may become an immune response, causing certain doors to close whenever descendants with similar chemistry approach.

The Kept accept that remembrance alters what it preserves. Their histories are maintained as multiple incompatible records: written fiber sheets, mechanical recordings, oral recitations, and living tissues. Agreement among all four is viewed with suspicion. It usually means one medium has begun rewriting the others.

Beyond the human inhabitants are beings whose status remains unsettled.

The monumental infrastructural trees react to names, schedules, and labor disputes. Signal flowers have been observed transmitting warnings before their control systems detect danger. Unentered factories continue to make useful decisions about production. Detached roots gather at reservoirs and retreat when watched. The colorless worms of the deep cisterns arrange themselves in repeating patterns that resemble old street plans.

Some residents insist these are only complex responses. Others say the city is populated primarily by minds too slow, too large, or too distributed to recognize themselves as singular beings.

The oldest civic theologians go further. They suggest the approximately human population is not the city’s builder or partner but one of its temporary organs: mobile sensory tissue grown because roots cannot cross dry plazas, hands produced because branches cannot repair turbines, short-lived memories sent walking where the plateau’s deeper memory has become confused.

The Kept tend to dislike this idea, especially when voiced by outsiders. Yet their language quietly supports it. They do not say they live **in** the city. They say they live **among its thoughts**, or sometimes, more simply, that they are **where the city can reach**.

When the buried contour lines begin to darken and the plateau attempts to recover an ancient mountain, the inhabitants gather around the locality stones. They attach their coils to the mineral mass and to one another. Factory workers arrive beside children, surveyors beside reservoir keepers, solitary residents from half-empty towers beside households who have not spoken to them in years.

As the ground rises, each person recites their name, district, work, ancestry, and current address. The recitation is practical: a chorus of fixed facts against geological memory. The city groans beneath them, full of former valleys and erased summits. Roots pull toward shapes older than streets. Buildings lean as if listening.

The people continue until the motion stops.

Afterward they untie themselves, compare clocks, account for anyone lost, and return to work. They reopen valves. They reset slipped belts. They cut new doors where old ones no longer lead outside. A few remain with their palms against the locality stone, feeling for vibrations.

At that distance they seem small enough to be incidental.

But the plateau has forgotten mountains and retained them. The city has lost rivers and still prepares for rain. It may be that the Kept endure for the same reason: not because this immense living place needs inhabitants in any ordinary sense, but because, among all its methods of remembering what shape it occupies, they are the only memories capable of walking back.
