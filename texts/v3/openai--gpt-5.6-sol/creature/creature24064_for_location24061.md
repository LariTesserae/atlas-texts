At first it seems that nothing lives there except water.

It is everywhere without having a single body. Rain arrives thinly from above, but much of the water is older than the weather that reveals it. It waits beneath pavements, inside walls, under collapsed floors. It gathers in cisterns cut for hands that no longer draw from them. It follows the remembered inclinations of gutters and avenues, slipping through the city as though the stone were a vast, damaged vessel.

In upper courts it is bright, shallow, and restless. Farther down it becomes cold and nearly still. In buried districts it has occupied whole rooms, filling them to the spring line and holding their stairs, thresholds, and pillars in a dim suspension. It passes from one form of habitation to another: fog in a doorway, beads beneath an arch, a black mirror in a reservoir, a thread running down worn steps. Frost arrests it briefly in cracks. Sun lifts it invisibly from plazas. Then the clouds return it.

It has no intention, yet it explores. A fallen block closes one route and sends it elsewhere. A floor gives way, and water descends into chambers it may not have entered for centuries. It discovers old conduits by pressure, abandoned houses by seepage, lower courts by gravity. Wherever it remains, other inhabitants gather around it.

The smallest of these are nearly transparent.

In cisterns that never empty live pale newts, their skin the color of submerged stone. They spend long intervals motionless beneath projecting ledges or among drowned leaves. Their limbs are slight, their eyes dark and unwinking. When disturbed, they cross the water with a few undulations and disappear into cracks too narrow to suggest shelter. Generations may be born, hunt, breed, and die within a single rectangular basin, enclosed by walls whose purpose they cannot know.

Below them, in the water moving through the city’s foundations, are smaller creatures still: colorless crustaceans with attenuated legs, soft bodies, and feelers extended into perpetual dark. They inhabit no plaza or district. Their city is a network of wet fissures, mineral films, flooded fill, and spaces beneath slabs. Some are carried slowly from one buried chamber to another. Some may spend their entire lives within the volume of a cupped hand. To them, a change in seepage is a migration of climate; a grain of organic matter descending from the surface is an event.

Beetles live under loose stones where dampness meets air. They occupy the underside of architecture: the cool plane beneath a paving slab, the narrow black seam behind a flake of wall, the sheltered grit at the foot of a stair. Many are dull and hard-shelled, easily mistaken for seeds or fragments of lichen. They emerge after rain to feed on softened growths and windblown remains. Before the stone dries, they have withdrawn again.

There are mites among the moss filaments, minute worms in silt, larvae in pools that last only a season. Spiders stretch sparse webs across protected corners, choosing doorways too enclosed for wind and too open for dripping water. Their silk is visible chiefly when fog beads upon it. Then an empty passage may reveal dozens of delicate territories, each one shining for an hour before becoming invisible again.

The plants live slowly and mostly sideways.

Black moss occupies the city’s permanent shadow. It grows beneath north-facing ledges, around spring mouths, and along the lower courses of walls where moisture persists. At a distance it resembles staining. Close by, it resolves into tiny upright forms packed together: stalks, cups, and leaves finer than hair. Its colonies keep to their own climates. A ledge may support a dark band for centuries while the wall an arm’s length away remains bare.

Other mosses wake only after rain. Dry, they are almost indistinguishable from dust. Wet, they open into sudden green fields across paving stones and roof slabs. For a few hours the city acquires a living surface. Then the sun passes over, the leaves close, and the color retreats without the plants dying. Their life is not continuous display but repeated suspension.

Lichens are less hurried. Pale gray, yellow-white, rust, and faint green, they spread across the volcanic stone in crusts so thin they seem to be alterations in the rock itself. They occupy years rather than spaces. Their margins advance by increments too small to watch. They soften carved marks, bridge mineral grains, and draw sustenance from rain, dust, light, and the barest chemistry of the wall. A single patch may outlast a roof, persist on the block after it falls, and continue growing when that block becomes part of a rubble slope.

Silver grasses prefer the lines made for water. Their roots settle in grit accumulated inside drains and joints. Their narrow blades bend under droplets, then rise again. In exposed places they remain low, forming scattered combs along the pavement. Where a conduit has filled with soil, they trace it exactly, turning buried engineering into a pale stripe visible across an entire district.

Red sedge grows where water stands and withdraws. A single clump can possess a broad plaza simply because so little else rises there. Its stems darken in wet weather and hold their color after the surrounding growth has gone gray. Seeds travel in runoff, lodging against steps and basin rims. Most fail. A few establish new red points far downslope, as if the water were slowly mapping its own connections in vegetation.

The shrubs are the city’s largest permanent residents, though few grow higher than a person’s waist. They inhabit sheltered courtyards and the lee of retaining walls. Wind has bent them into dense, twisted forms. Their leaves are small, waxed, and blue-green; their bark lies close to the wood. Much of each plant is hidden. Roots descend through mortarless joints, branch into old fill, follow damp seams, and sometimes emerge many courses below as pale fibers before finding darkness again.

A shrub may appear to grow from a wall when in fact the wall has grown around its ancestry. Its roots hold some blocks together and prize others apart. It makes no distinction between shelter and obstacle. Fallen leaves collect in the same crevices that admitted its first roots, creating a little soil from which beetles, fungi, and seedlings derive their brief abundance.

Fungi are present even when no fruiting body shows. They live in the dark remains of roof beams, in buried wood softened nearly to peat, in leaf litter pressed between stones. After prolonged rain, small gray caps may appear in a cellar or under an overhang, stand for a day, and collapse into translucence. Threads of fungus pass through material the city has almost finished forgetting: lintels, pegs, doors, baskets, perhaps the compacted residue of interiors whose shapes are gone.

Higher in the ruins, the swifts arrive with warmth.

They are small, gray, and so quick that they lend the empty air a temporary population. They nest in crevices near the tops of broken towers and beneath surviving cornices. The city’s scale means little to them except as a profusion of cavities, ledges, updrafts, and rain-sheltered recesses. They feed over the marshes and reservoirs, turning sharply above open water where insects rise.

Their calls are among the few sounds that seem to address the city rather than merely occur within it. For part of the year they stitch the high courts together with flight. They vanish into cracks, emerge elsewhere, circle towers, and gather in the pale evening air. Then the season changes, the insects diminish, and the swifts leave. Their nests remain: a little dried grass, feathers, husks, droppings whitening a ledge. Winter closes over their absence without drama.

Other birds visit but do not establish themselves in number. A solitary hunter may rest on a tower stump, watching the marsh margins. Small ground-feeding birds descend after storms to pick among silt lines. They remain wary in the open plazas, where every movement is exposed. The city offers shelter in excess but food only in scattered intervals.

The mountain deer enters as though crossing a place that belongs to weather.

It comes at dawn or during fog, lean-bodied, long-legged, its coat close in color to dry stone. It follows broad terraces where grass grows between paving slabs and drinks from basins whose edges have been rounded by rain. Its hooves leave two dark marks in wet silt, sometimes continuing along an avenue before turning abruptly through a collapsed wall.

The deer does not need the city, but it has learned its gradients. It knows which stair fields can be descended, which courts trap movement, which causeways remain firm beneath shallow water. It pauses often, head raised, listening into rooms where nothing moves. In fog, its antlers or ears may appear above a low wall before the rest of it becomes visible. Then it passes into another district and is gone, leaving tracks as the only proof that an animal of any size crossed the stone.

A fox ranges more widely and is seen less.

Its territory may contain reservoirs, roof terraces, marsh saddles, and miles of narrow lanes. It sleeps in a dry chamber reached through a break in a foundation, or among rocks warmed briefly by afternoon sun. It hunts beetles, nesting birds, small mammals where they occur, and whatever the water strands. Its paths are not the old streets, though they sometimes coincide. It slips through wall gaps, crosses parapets, travels beneath arches, and uses buried rooms as passages.

The fox knows the city intimately without possessing any image of it as a whole. It knows odor, shelter, distance, and recurrence: the cistern where birds drink, the court where rodents shelter, the stair that floods after rain, the dry ledge above the marsh. Its knowledge is precise and local. A traveler might never see it, only find paw prints laid over the concentric silt of a drained pool, or feathers gathered in a corner where no wind could have carried them.

There may be other mammals in the interstices—small, nocturnal, and few. They live on seeds collected from grasses, insects, roots, and the sparse fruits of shrubs. Their burrows enter rubble rather than soil. Walls protect them from weather but also divide each pocket of nourishment from the next. Their populations expand in generous seasons and contract almost to disappearance in hard ones. The city does not support abundance. It supports continuance.

Nothing here forms a crowd.

Each life occupies a narrow allowance: a certain depth of water, a certain duration of shade, one warm season, one wall face, one chain of cisterns. Habitats that appear adjacent may be separated by dryness, exposure, mineral composition, or the simple impossibility of crossing a paved court. Yet the water connects them indirectly. It carries spores downward, seeds through channels, fragments of leaves into underground pools, insects into the feeding arcs of swifts. It leaves silt for sedges and keeps roots cool within the masonry.

Death also travels by water. A drowned beetle descends through a drain. A feather adheres to the edge of a basin. A small bone, bleached and polished, moves during a storm and comes to rest against a gate groove cut before any living creature now present was born. These remains are not omens. They are nourishment, mineral, obstruction, and eventually part of the route itself.

The greatest inhabitant may be neither water nor any individual organism, but the thin, distributed life that appears wherever conditions briefly agree.

It has no center. It is a darkening under a ledge, a green film after rain, eggs fixed beneath a submerged stone, roots inside a wall, a bird turning over a reservoir. It contracts during dry weeks into cracks, spores, sealed bodies, deep pools, and patient tissue. When the weather changes, it expands—not triumphantly, not enough to conceal the ruins, but with exactness. Every suitable hollow answers in its own way.

Even the stone participates at the edge of life. Lichen acids loosen its grains. Roots enter its joints. Mineral-bearing water coats moss stems, clogs channels, and builds pale lips around springs. Shells, bones, spores, grit, and calcite settle together. The distinction between inhabitant and habitation becomes difficult to maintain. A moss bed alters the moisture of the wall that supports it. A shrub makes soil from the debris that confines its roots. Newts shelter in cisterns while their bodies return small quantities of substance to the water. The city shapes its residents, and they revise it by minute degrees.

None of them mourn the vanished builders.

The swifts do not know towers from cliffs. The newts do not know cisterns from ponds. The fox does not distinguish an avenue from a ravine unless one offers easier passage. Moss accepts a carved threshold and a natural ledge on the same terms. Roots encounter a wall as a pattern of pressures, darkness, minerals, and openings.

Only the forms of habitation remember people, and they remember without awareness. A doorway becomes a windbreak. A market court becomes a seasonal pool. A stair becomes a cascade. A buried chamber becomes an aquifer cave. Human intention persists chiefly as opportunity: shade held here, water detained there, a crevice kept dry, a basin made deep enough not to freeze solid.

Thus what lives in the city is not a successor civilization, nor a wilderness waiting to erase the old one. It is a sparse confederacy of dependent things.

Water moves among them all, older than any generation and younger than each rain. Moss opens. Lichen widens. Grasses seed the drains. Newts rise at dusk through clear cistern water. Translucent creatures feel their way under drowned streets. Swifts inhabit the air for a season. A deer crosses a roof that has become a terrace. A fox drinks from a pool reflecting clouds.

Then fog enters, and most of them vanish from sight.

They remain under stones, inside walls, beneath water, beyond the next ridge—separate, quiet, and sufficient to keep the empty city from ever being empty.
