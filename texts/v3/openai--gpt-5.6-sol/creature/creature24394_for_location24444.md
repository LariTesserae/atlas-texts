What lives here has learned not merely to endure the mountain, but to enter its processes.

There are few clean boundaries between animal, plant, colony, and terrain. Life uses the same methods as the high country: conduction, pressure, brine, mineral deposition, long storage, sudden release. Flesh alone would be too vulnerable. Most organisms reinforce themselves with substances drawn from the stone. Shells are ceramic, bones threaded with iron, leaves varnished in graphite, eggs sealed in frost-resistant glass. Even the softest bodies carry some small architecture of crystal.

The most widespread inhabitants are almost invisible.

They live in the wet interface between moss and rock: films of black microbial growth, electrical fungi, and branching colonies no thicker than smoke stains. Their cells form conductive chains along ore grains. A discharge does not kill them. It drives their metabolism. For weeks they may remain dormant, digesting mineral traces too slowly to measure; then a current passes, and whole colonies complete a season of growth in seconds.

Their fruiting bodies appear afterward as violet dust beneath stones. The dust is not easily airborne in the thin atmosphere, so it waits for feet.

The small ceramic-backed grazers carry it.

These creatures are low and broad, with six or eight limbs tucked beneath overlapping shells. The youngest have translucent coverings through which their organs can be seen as pale knots. With age they plate themselves in silicates and metal oxides until they resemble chips of glazed pottery moving through the moss. No two colonies produce exactly the same finish. Some are dull black, some white as old bone, some patterned with blue lines that brighten when the ground is charged.

They have no eyes because little useful light reaches beneath the vegetation. Instead, they read the mountain through their feet. Each leg ends in a fan of fine conductive hairs. From tiny differences in current and vibration they distinguish root contractions, approaching browsers, shifting ice, and the pulse preceding a vent rupture.

When strong charge enters the ground, every grazer in a colony turns along the same axis. Plates inside their shells rotate and lock with a single click. For the duration of the current they become part of the circuit, bleeding excess energy into their shells and into one another. Older individuals are often found fused to mineral crusts where repeated discharges have plated them in place. They are not necessarily dead. Some remain embedded for years, feeding without movement, until erosion frees them.

Among the mosses also live soft, ribbon-shaped things that never expose their whole bodies. A single animal may thread through several square meters of vegetation, sending up temporary feeding mouths wherever condensation falls. If one mouth is disturbed, the others withdraw. The buried body tightens like a net. During drought it divides into sealed loops, each surrounding a droplet of brine; when moisture returns, the loops rejoin by touch.

Above this hidden abundance, the plants occupy the wet hollows in crowded, severe societies.

There are soot-dark cushions that grow only on the warm side of cracks. There are translucent reeds filled with saline gel, their stems bending without freezing. There are ferns whose fronds remain rolled for most of the year and open only during the long electrical release. Their undersides are silvered with receptive tissue. Charge runs over them in branching violet patterns, and spores are fired into the air by sudden contraction.

Many apparent flowers are not invitations to pollinators in the ordinary sense. They are discharge organs. They accumulate charge until the difference between plant and atmosphere becomes great enough to produce a cold corona. The glow attracts the seed-sized fliers, which feed by touching the luminous structures with metallic mouthparts. In doing so, they drain the flower and leave behind grains from every other plant they have visited.

These fliers hatch without wings.

Their larvae live inside brine stems, eating neither plant nor sap but the crystal deposits that would otherwise clog the stem’s conductive channels. When mature, they cut their way out during charged weather. The first current hardens the metallic dust in their bodies into wing veins. Until then they are soft, pale, and helpless. Afterward they rise in clouds.

Each has four broad wings made from nearly weightless membranes scattered with magnetic crystals. They steer less by muscular effort than by altering the alignment of those crystals. A swarm can pivot as one body when the field shifts, collapsing into a thread, widening into a veil, or circling a column in stacked rings. Their motion resembles intention, but no individual leads. The pattern belongs to the field passing through them.

They live for only a few releases. When their wings wear thin, they settle on plants and are absorbed. The metal in one generation becomes the conductive tissue of the next.

The greatest plant life is seldom visible as a whole.

Across some valleys, what appears to be a population of isolated shrubs is one ancient root-being. Its surface crowns are dark, compact, and separated by kilometers. Beneath them, woody conduits descend through warm fractures, joining in chambers where geothermal vapor condenses. The oldest cables are thicker than the hanging trees and plated externally with mineral layers. New tissue grows inside them while the outer rings become pipe, insulation, and eventually stone.

This organism does not think as an animal thinks. Nevertheless, it remembers.

A damaged basin receives less water for many seasons afterward. A slope repeatedly grazed is supplied with bitter salts. Where vents have become dangerous, the root seals adjacent cracks with dense growth and withdraws its crowns. These responses travel so slowly that cause and effect may be separated by years. To a short-lived creature, they are simply properties of the land.

Its reproductive bodies are mistaken for pools. They form in sheltered hollows as dark lenses of regulated brine. A pool may remain unfrozen through the longest night because root filaments beneath it pulse minute quantities of heat and antifreeze into the water. Within, pale seed-knots grow around grains of ore. When mature, they are drunk by the long-bodied browsers and carried to distant fractures.

The browsers are the most conspicuous animals on the terraces, though even they seem slight against the distances.

They stand taller than the low vegetation but keep their bodies close to the ground. Six legs support a narrow trunk, each foot spreading into long flexible toes that rest on snow without breaking the crust. Their outer skin is gray-black and matte. Frost collects along it in fine lines, giving resting animals the appearance of cracked stone.

Along the back rise vascular sails: thin, warm, densely nerved structures that can flatten beneath protective folds or stand upright to gather heat. The sails register atmospheric charge before any visible illumination. Their edges twitch toward conductive veins beneath the snow. Herds use them to locate safe routes, fertile gardens, and one another.

The sails also disclose mood. Alarm sends a hard ripple from tail to neck. Courtship produces slower waves, pairs answering across a terrace without sound. A sick animal’s sail loses tension and twists in the wind; gliders can recognize that irregular silhouette from far above.

The browsers have small heads and broad scraping mouths. They feed with restraint not because they are gentle but because the plants punish deeper injury. Beneath the upper tissues lie crystals, toxins, or sudden stored charges. A practiced browser removes only the renewable skin. Juveniles learn by watching the herd’s feeding lines. Those that bite too deeply may be stunned, poisoned, or marked by luminous sap that makes them visible from the ravines.

Their migrations are not commanded by a leader. Old individuals press their feet to the ground when the hollow columns pulse. One by one, the herd aligns. The most experienced stand at different places along the formation, comparing the vibration through snow, stone, and root. Direction emerges from their collective unease.

They sleep in fissures, folding their sails and interlocking legs so that several bodies form a stable lattice above warmer ground. Calves occupy the center. During dormancy, mineral-eating organisms clean their feet and sails. A sleeping herd may become furred with moss and frost until the reversal of the column sequence wakes it.

Death rarely leaves a body intact for long. The herd gathers around a fallen member, not in ceremony but in retrieval. Each animal licks a little mineral-rich tissue from the sails. Ceramic grazers enter from below. Roots rise into the carcass. Within a season, little remains except six shallow foot hollows and a dark patch of newly fertile growth.

The predators do not often walk upon the terraces.

They belong to the cliffs and the rising breath of the mountain. Their bodies are long, pale, and compacted into muscular cords. In repose they knot themselves around projections of stone, wings folded so closely that only a wet sheen reveals them. Some spend days hanging head-down beside a vent, kept from freezing by the warm exhalation.

Their wings are almost transparent and enormously wide when opened. Fine black struts spread through the membrane, changing shape with pressure. In geothermal lift, the gliders need scarcely move. They loosen their grip and become suspended, steering with minute contractions along the body.

They hunt edges.

A glider does not usually strike a browser with enough force to kill it. Instead, several descend from different angles, each passing close, scraping a flank or flexing a wing to create a local pressure change. They drive the chosen animal toward unstable snow or an unseen drop. The mountain delivers the fatal blow.

This has shaped the herds’ deepest instinct: not fear of a visible predator, but suspicion of open space behind them.

Gliders nest in the downward forests, far below the root crowns. Their eggs are laid in clusters among hanging branches and disguised with mineral paste. Hatchlings emerge wingless and climb downward, away from the roots, following warmth into the mist. Only after months of clinging among the lowest twigs do their membranes unfold. Their first flight is not a leap into air but a deliberate release into darkness.

The hanging forests support other lives unseen from above. Pale hook-footed birds move along the descending trunks as though the cliff were a level plain. Their feathers are hair-fine, oily, and almost soundless. They build enclosed nests around warm droplets, maintaining a bead of liquid water at the center. Their young drink from it and feed on insects drawn to the tree’s faint charge.

Some birds never visit the terraces. They orient vertically throughout life: roots are “above,” vapor is “below,” and the opposite cliff is a distant sky. If blown onto level ground, they stagger and cling to stones until they can reach an edge.

Deeper in the ravines live heavy-bodied scavengers that cannot fly. They climb the cliff faces with rings of muscular gripping limbs, following whatever falls. They may take a month to reach a carcass lodged on a ledge. Their metabolism is so slow that fresh meat freezes before they finish feeding. They secrete warm brine onto it, soften one mouthful, eat, then seal themselves against the rock while digestion proceeds.

The hollow columns have their own inhabitants.

Inside the breathing shafts, colonies of membrane-creatures stretch across narrow passages. They resemble dark skins or immense eardrums. Vapor passes through pores in their bodies, leaving mineral nutrients behind. Pressure bows them up and down. Their slow movement contributes to the impacts felt along the line of columns, though they do not create the rhythm by themselves.

When a membrane grows old, it stiffens with deposits and tears loose. It may fall hundreds of meters down the chimney, striking walls and shedding thousands of curled offspring. These adhere wherever temperature, pressure, and mineral content are suitable. Most fail. A few spread across a crack and begin filtering the mountain’s breath.

Larger animals sometimes shelter in the mouths of the columns, but none remain when the sequence reverses. That vibration announces pressure moving the wrong way—an imminent exhalation of gas, black water, or shattered ice. Every native creature recognizes some part of the warning. Moss contracts. Grazers bury themselves. Browsers leave. Gliders rise. The warning does not pass from species to species; it enters all of them through the stone.

There may be no single creature that knows the whole mountain.

Yet the mountain’s life behaves at times as though knowledge were distributed through it. Roots store water where herds will later pass. Herds carry spores to newly warmed fissures. Flowers discharge into swarms that redistribute metal. Gliders remove the weak and drop bodies into ravines, feeding the hanging forests. Column membranes alter pressure pulses. Buried fungi alter conductivity. Each participant changes the signal received by the next.

During the great release, these separate lives become briefly legible as one system.

Ceramic grazers lock into shining rows beneath the moss. The immense roots contract. Dark pools lift as if breathing. Browsers stand with every sail raised, blue current branching through their veins. Seed-swarms spiral above the gardens in magnetic vortices. The hanging forests ignite in long violet threads, revealing nests, gliders, cliff-crawlers, and countless smaller bodies at depths usually mistaken for emptiness.

For that hour, the inhabitants do not hide from the light.

They are the light.

Then the charge drains away, and each returns to its own scale of existence: the beetle beneath a leaf, the herd between distant gardens, the pale hunter in the updraft, the root tightening once in half a day, the membrane bowed across a shaft.

None rules the place. None built it. None could survive apart from it for long.

They live there by becoming functions of the darkness—small, patient workings inside a greater work that has no worker.
