What lives there is not one people but an agreement among thousands of creatures that have never agreed to anything.

The nearest thing to founders are the beavers. They occupy the descending waters in extended families, though the boundaries between families shift whenever a dam breaks or a pond changes shape. The oldest are broad-bodied and dark, their guard hairs silvered by mineral water. Their incisors carry an orange stain like rust. By dusk they emerge from lodges built partly of wood and partly of living roots, and begin the work that governs much of the basin without ruling it.

They know each current separately. A trickle escaping through a dam brings one response, a submerged breach another. They push mud with their forepaws, carry stones under their chins, and drag willow lengths through channels kept clear for that purpose. Some dams have been maintained so long that no animal now alive could distinguish their original branches. Alders grow from them. Sedges knit their downstream faces. Water voles burrow into the banks, and nesting birds harvest fibers from the crowns. When the beavers repair such a structure, they are working on something half lodge, half woodland.

Their ponds contain other citizens in greater numbers than themselves. Muskrats raise smaller houses among the reeds and quarrel at close quarters. Water voles cut runways through floating plants. Otters pass through with the smooth entitlement of creatures that build almost nothing and use everything. They sleep in abandoned bank chambers, feed beneath old dams, and enter the upper ponds by slides worn through moss. The beavers dislike them but cannot exclude them. At night an otter may surface beside a lodge, whiskered face shining, while warning tails strike the water one terrace after another.

The shallows belong to smaller lives. Frogs bury themselves in mud where springs prevent a hard freeze. Newts shelter beneath drowned bark. Larvae cling to the undersides of stones in water cold enough to numb a paw. Caddisflies build cases from pale limestone grains, fragments of reed, and minute slivers gnawed from beaver wood. Their constructions resemble the basin in miniature: whatever the current provides, joined without plan into something protective.

Above the ponds, the root-bound slopes are filled with rabbits. They are compact animals with thick, gray-brown coats and ears shorter than those of their open-country kin. Damp has darkened the fur around their feet. Their warrens began as separate refuges under alders and tilted slabs, but years of digging have joined many of them. No rabbit knows more than a fraction of the whole. Passages collapse, are abandoned, and become drains; drains dry and are enlarged into chambers. In some places warm air from their breathing rises through the earth and keeps a patch of moss green beneath snow.

They come out cautiously onto the mud aprons, never far from cover. They crop fern shoots, sedges, bark, and the small flowers that appear after rockfalls. Their paths converge at gravel shelves where the ground is dry enough to sit. There they groom in loose groups, alert to every change in the basin’s ordinary silence. One rabbit stamps, and the warning passes underground through feet and packed soil before the danger is visible.

Voles live around and beneath them, using the city at a different scale. A beaver dam is a continent to a vole. A rabbit path is an exposed highway crossed at a run. They inhabit woven nests under sedge tussocks, cavities in moss-covered stone, and the dry spaces inside old bird nests fallen from trees. They gather seed, cut grass into short lengths, and maintain narrow covered ways whose roofs are living vegetation. Shrews enter these ways to hunt them, moving almost continuously, driven by a hunger too urgent for caution.

The cliffs hold the basin’s most numerous inhabitants.

Swallows and martins return when insects first rise from the ponds. Their clay cups crowd every protected face. New nests are fixed to old ones; old nests become foundations, windbreaks, or sealed chambers full of mites and husks. From a distance the colonies look like patches of rough stone. Close by, each opening contains a pale gape, a dark eye, or the barred edge of a folded wing.

They do not sing much. Their communication is a quick fabric of clicks, throat notes, wing sounds, and recognition calls exchanged at the nest mouth. Hundreds may be airborne at once without filling the basin with clamor. They skim the water, turn sharply between leaning slabs, and drink by touching only the lower half of the beak to a pond’s surface.

Wrens occupy cracks too narrow for the swallows. Dippers nest behind the thin falls descending the cliff, entering through moving water. Kingfishers use the firmer banks where a tunnel can remain above flood level. Small owls roost in cavities under the surviving roof and hunt by waiting over the vole runs. Their feathers are so soft that the basin seems to notice them only after they have struck.

The alders support rookeries of a different kind. Herons and cormorants place broad stick platforms in the upper branches, adding to them year after year until some trees bend toward the water under the load. Smaller birds nest within the bases of these platforms, protected by structures built for bodies much larger than their own. Finches take fibers from the edges. Beetles live in the packed droppings and fish remains below. When a platform falls, it may bridge a channel and become a crossing before the next rain.

No creature uses all levels of the city.

Hoofed animals come down from the upper woodland by a few reliable routes. Small deer browse the willow crowns and stand belly-deep among sedges, black legs hidden in mist. They move delicately over the angular stone, placing each hoof where generations have already found purchase. In winter they gather beneath the surviving shelves, where snow is thin and ferns remain reachable. They do not sleep there often. The enclosed valley dulls scent and sound in confusing ways, and they are never entirely at ease.

Wild goats sometimes appear along the highest fractures. They descend for mineral water and young cliff plants, using ledges that seem unable to support them. The inhabitants below recognize the scrape of their hooves. Birds lift from the wall in a slow wave as grit showers down.

The hunters inhabit routes rather than districts.

Foxes keep dens above the usual flood line, often enlarging abandoned rabbit chambers. Their coats become dark and heavy in the wet season. They patrol dams because everything must cross them eventually: rabbits seeking fresh growth, voles displaced by rising water, fledglings that have fallen, frogs moving between pools. A fox may sit for an hour at a bridge junction, appearing idle while listening to the separate movements beneath water, moss, and roots.

Stoats use narrower ways and enter the warrens themselves. Otters hunt the water. Owls take the air between. A lynx occasionally descends from the rim and alters the behavior of the entire basin for several days. Deer remain under branches. Rabbits feed only at tunnel mouths. Foxes vanish into stone. Even the beavers shorten their time ashore. The lynx leaves little but wide paw prints filling slowly with clear seep-water.

Most deaths belong to smaller hunters: spiders under leaves, diving beetles in pools, dragonfly larvae in submerged stems, leeches in the black margins. Bats occupy the deepest dry cracks and emerge into evening mist, flying low above the ponds where insects collect. Their colonies are known more by warmth and smell than sound. Moths rest around them in pale clusters, and cave crickets gather where roots penetrate the ceiling.

Farther beneath the surviving roof, life loses much of its color.

The fish there are not entirely blind, for daylight still reaches the chamber, but their eyes are small and their bodies nearly transparent. They hold themselves above flooded blocks where spring water brings minute crustaceans from deeper passages. Some move seasonally between the dark chamber and the reed ponds; others never cross the band of brightness at the entrance.

White amphipods browse films on submerged stone. Flatworms cling where the current narrows. Snails with thin, colorless shells feed on mineral-soft growths. The moths on the walls are joined by creatures that appear to be drifting lint until they unfold legs. Roots descending from above support their own minute population: mites, springtails, fungal threads, and larvae feeding on whatever the upper woodland sends down through the cracks.

At the low cleft where the basin’s water disappears, eels and young fish sometimes gather against the current. Most larger animals avoid it. They feel the pull in the water and the close stone overhead. But salamanders enter, and insects pass freely, and now and then an otter pushes its head and shoulders into the opening before backing out with flattened whiskers.

There are also inhabitants that cannot be seen as individuals.

Fungi bind the soaked city together. Their threads occupy dams, droppings, nests, drowned wood, antler, feather, and fur. They soften what teeth and claws cannot use. Bacteria stain spring mouths orange or cover still pools with an iridescent skin. Algae take the first hold on new rock. Moss comes after, then roots, then the animals that eat roots and the animals that eat those animals.

Thus the true resident may be the basin itself—not as a single mind, but as a body assembled from repeated acts. Beavers raise the water; drowned trees make nesting sites; nesting birds enrich the mud; enriched mud thickens the sedges; sedges shelter voles; voles feed foxes and owls; abandoned burrows divert springs; springs undermine dams; broken dams expose silt where willow takes root. Nothing understands the sequence. Everything contributes to it.

At night, no lights reveal the city. It is shown instead by absences and movements: a black wake dividing reflected sky, a pale owl crossing a cliff face, reeds bowing where something passes beneath them. The beavers’ teeth continue their faint cutting. Rabbits breathe underground. Fish turn among blocks that were ceiling within the memory of the oldest trees.

And under all of it, through the narrow cleft, the water carries away the city’s smallest leavings—pollen, scales, fragments of moss, milk teeth, eggshell, spores—while above, fresh material falls in. The inhabitants sleep, feed, breed, hunt, repair, and flee inside that exchange. None possesses the place. Together, they make it crowded enough to seem possessed.
