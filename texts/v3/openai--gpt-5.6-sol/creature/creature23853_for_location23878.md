What lives there has learned not to depend on permanence.

Much of it survives by becoming absent.

Under the clay are eggs finer than sand, spores sealed in dust, seeds with coats thick enough to resist years of heat, and small animals folded into waxy sleep. Lungfish knot themselves inside chambers of hardened mud. Frogs retreat into cracks and wrap their bodies against evaporation, leaving only the faintest passage for breath. Crustaceans vanish entirely except as dormant specks. To a dry-season eye the ground may seem nearly vacant, but it is crowded with withheld beginnings.

Rain releases them.

The first pools cloud with bacteria and algae before they have acquired any color of their own. Filaments spread across submerged stone. Minute grazers hatch and begin feeding almost at once; transparent bodies fill with green and gold. Fairy shrimp and tadpole shrimp paddle through puddles only hours old, carrying forms older than many of the rocks exposed around them. Beetle larvae hang below the surface. Mosquito wrigglers gather where still water holds a skin of pollen and dust. Tiny fish appear in basins that seemed isolated, arriving through temporary threads of water or waking from eggs laid in an earlier season.

Everything proceeds urgently. The shallow pools are nurseries, but they are also clocks.

Frogs govern the wet nights. Some are small enough to shelter beneath a loosened flake of stone. Some are broad, mud-colored animals with mouths built to take anything that fits: insects, hatchling frogs, fish stranded in shrinking water, even smaller members of their own kind. Their voices seem too large for them because distance offers no resistance. A throat inflates; a note rolls out over the flats; another answers from beyond the visible pools. The chorus is not conversation in any human sense. It is location, readiness, rivalry, rainfall made audible through living bodies.

Their tadpoles crowd the warm margins. They rasp algae from rock and clay, dark commas flicking through reflected sky. Where a pool is failing, they change quickly or die together. Egrets learn which basins are nearest exhaustion.

The birds belong less to the plain than to its recurring abundance. They arrive from elsewhere, following rains whose fall they may never have seen. Plovers take insects from the bare rims. Ibises drive curved bills into softened ground, feeling for worms and crustaceans. Egrets hunt by stillness. Storks and herons seek deeper pockets where fish have concentrated. Their legs are long because the boundary between earth and water is everywhere and nowhere; their toes spread because firmness is never guaranteed.

They nest on the low islands, trusting elevation measured in inches. Eggs lie in shallow scrapes among chips of ancient stone. Chicks hatch already colored like clay, crouching when shadows cross them. Many are taken by snakes, monitor lizards, raptors, sudden water, or simple heat. Those that survive run early and fly as soon as their growing bodies permit. No nest is intended to outlast the season.

The insects are more numerous than the birds can reduce. Cicadas spend most of their lives under the plain, drinking from roots in darkness, then emerge together into one brief, metallic summer. Dragonflies patrol the pools, their larvae hunting below while the adults hunt above. Midges rise in columns so dense that they become visible from far away, wavering like smoke where there is no fire. Ants occupy the driest crowns of the islands and rebuild downward after every inundation. Termites raise no towering monuments here; their works are low, broad, and repeatedly slumped, more like swellings in the earth than architecture.

Among the grasses live mice and other small burrowers, though burrowing is dangerous where water can appear beneath a sleeper. Their tunnels are shallow, with several exits, and their nests are lined above the dampest soil. They feed on seeds during contraction and on the sudden wealth of insects after rain. Snakes follow them. So do lean, long-legged hunters that travel alone at dusk, placing each foot where the crust will hold.

The grazing animals are built for distance rather than speed. They have broad hooves or splayed toes, high joints, heavy bellies, and hides darkened at the legs by mud. Some crop the wiry grasses of the islands; others pull submerged plants from water or chew the soft new growth that appears around receding pools. They move in loose associations because the food is widely scattered. A tight herd would exhaust each patch too quickly and mire the ground beneath itself.

Their days are a slow negotiation with footing. They remember shelves of bedrock, persistent basins, and passages where the clay is thin. Calves learn by following the precise placement of older feet. When danger comes, the animals do not always run. Sometimes they bunch on firm stone, heads lowered, while the mud around them becomes a defense.

Large predators are uncommon and patient. A crocodilian may occupy one of the deepest tea-colored pools, nearly indistinguishable from mineral-stained water and black rock. It feeds heavily when channels connect and endures hunger when they close. Elsewhere, a cat or hyena-like scavenger travels the islands by night, guided by frog noise, nesting birds, and the odor of animals trapped in mire. Vultures need no path. They circle above the whole plain and descend wherever temporary abundance has become temporary death.

The few trees support worlds disproportionate to their number. Hollows in their pale trunks hold geckos, bats, bees, and rainwater darkened by leaves. Birds dispute every durable fork. Caterpillars strip new foliage after the rains, and wasps hunt among the branches. Beneath each crown, dung, feathers, fruit, shed bark, and bodies enrich the soil. Grass grows differently there. So do fungi. A tree’s complete shadow is also a complete refuge, and during the hottest hours animals arrange themselves around it according to rank, caution, and tolerance.

But the most characteristic residents are not the visible ones. They are the reducers.

Bacteria occupy the films between water and stone. Fungi enter every fallen stem and drowned root. Lichens work at the exposed ledges grain by grain, only to be submerged and replaced by algae. Mites, nematodes, worms, and microscopic crustaceans inhabit the dark pores of the clay. They process droppings, feathers, eggshells, carcasses, pollen, and the skins of drying pools. Nothing remains sharply itself for long. Bone stains, softens, separates, and settles. A hoofprint becomes a nursery for larvae, then a cracked cup, then no feature at all.

Even the larger creatures seem shaped by that law. They are rounded, low-slung, mud-colored, difficult to distinguish from where they feed or rest. Brightness appears chiefly in courtship and only briefly: a frog’s yellow throat, a dragonfly’s blue abdomen, the flushed skin of a breeding bird, the wet black shine of a beetle’s wing case. Display is intense because time is short. Camouflage returns afterward.

No single animal owns the plain. Ownership would require boundaries that persist.

Instead there are recurring claims: a male frog calling from one stone until the pool dries; a bird defending an island until its chicks can fly; a crocodilian holding deep water until hunger drives it elsewhere; grazers returning to remembered mineral licks; insects rising from the exact patch beneath which they waited for years. These claims overlap, dissolve, and are made again.

The inhabitants carry the landscape’s calendar inside them. They know rain as pressure, vibration, scent, and temperature. They know drying water by increasing warmth and mineral taste. They know which urgency belongs to a night and which endurance must last through seasons. Some leave. Some sleep. Some lay the future into mud and die.

When the plain appears still, most of its life is waiting.

When it becomes loud, most of its life is hurrying.

And beneath both conditions, in the wet pores of the oldest ground, innumerable small mouths continue the patient work of making every fallen thing level with the earth.
