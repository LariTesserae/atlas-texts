No single creature can be called the inhabitant of the drowned plateau. Nothing possesses it. Nothing knows its extent.

Life survives there by borrowing fragments of the structure: a seam, a current, the shelter behind a rounded molding. The animals are not a community so much as a collection of solutions to pressure, darkness, hunger, and involuntary travel.

The most visible are the **sliver shoals**.

Each sliver is longer than a human arm but scarcely thicker than a rib. Its flesh is almost clear, its organs compressed into a dark thread, its muscles laid in fine opposing bands. Seen broadside, a shoal resembles a flight of pale leaves. When danger or turbulence approaches, every animal turns edge-on at once. Hundreds disappear into a few trembling lines.

They do not swim continuously. They surrender themselves to the plateau’s alternating currents, making only small corrections with transparent fins. In this way they cross enormous distances without spending the energy that ordinary pursuit would require. Their mouths open only in upward-flowing water. Fine combs unfold from their jaws and catch bacteria, shed tissue, reproductive dust, and particles lifted from the buried warmth.

A sliver may spend years circling the same district, carried through a sequence of avenues and shafts as dependable as a migration route. Then a storm changes one archway, and the route ceases to exist. The shoal is scattered. Its members either discover a new circuit or vanish into the levels below.

Closer to the fabricated ground are the **seam mouths**, round, heavy animals that seem poorly suited to a place of violent water. Their bodies are low and armored with overlapping translucent plates. Beneath them, hundreds of gripping feet operate in waves, allowing them to crawl over polished walls without being peeled away.

A seam mouth has no head recognizable from above. Its underside is almost entirely rasp: concentric rings of mineral-hard teeth surrounding a soft intake. It follows joints between slabs, shaving off the pale residue that settles there. Most of what it swallows is useless ceramic powder. The nourishment lies in the minute colonies of heat-fed microbes mixed through it.

Its gut is correspondingly enormous. Digestion can take months.

When the bacterial dust fails, seam mouths enter a state so still that they are easily mistaken for decorative bosses. Their limbs withdraw, their plates seal, and their internal fluids become dense with compounds that resist freezing. Some remain closed through several generations of slivers. When warmth next rises from below, they open and begin crawling as though no interval had passed.

Their young are unlike them. They hatch as tiny, many-armed swimmers and drift upward into the dark water. Only after finding a suitable joint do they settle, dissolve most of their nervous system, and become the slow circular animals of the seams. Thus the mobile young choose the place; the adult spends its life consuming the consequences.

In vertical chambers live the **mourning bells**.

A mature colony may contain tens of thousands. Each bell is soft, colorless, and tethered by a filament far stronger than its body suggests. The filament is rooted deep within a microscopic flaw in the artificial material. It grows black because it incorporates traces of metal and carbon from the water, not because the bell itself makes pigment.

The bells feed only when the architecture exhales.

As the first warm current enters a shaft, they open in succession from bottom to top. Their rims spread into fine ciliated veils, turning the chamber into a living filter. If the pressure shifts, the entire colony contracts. The response moves faster than any signal could pass from animal to animal; each bell is reading the same change in the water. To an observer it appears as a single immense blink.

Detached bells do not die immediately. They collapse into beads and drift until they encounter a new surface. Most are eaten. A few extrude fresh filaments and found new colonies in chambers exposed by collapse. In this way a storm can destroy an ancient population and seed a hundred others.

Among them hunt the **brackets**.

A resting bracket has the shape and color of broken ornament. Its outer limbs lock into shallow sockets or brace across a conduit, while its body flattens against the wall. No heartbeat is visible. Digestion slows almost to nothing. Pearly deposits accumulate on its hide until even seam mouths crawl across it.

What appears to be a single animal is, in fact, a temporary arrangement of limbs around a central feeding sac. As prey enters the conduit, pressure-sensitive hairs trigger the outer arms. The bracket unfolds across the passage, each limb branching into finer hooked threads. It does not chase. It makes the current hunt on its behalf.

Slivers strike the net and are drawn inward. The bracket empties them rapidly, then discards skins and skeletal threads into the flow. After feeding it may remain active for several hours, loosening its grip and crawling with grave, jointed motions to another constriction. There it folds again.

Old brackets sometimes lose the ability to move. Their anchoring limbs fuse permanently into cracks, while their feeding branches continue to open and close. Such animals may live for centuries as dark, carnivorous fixtures. When the supporting wall finally breaks, they fall with it, still grasping.

Farther from the surfaces move the **mantle riders**, the broad-winged shapes occasionally glimpsed over the plateau.

Their wings are not true fins but membranes stretched across flexible cartilaginous fingers. A rider rarely beats them. Instead it bends them to the layered currents, rising on one flow and descending on another. Its body is small compared with its span, little more than a muscular knot surrounding a mouth and reproductive organs.

Mantle riders navigate by pressure. Along the front edge of each wing runs a chain of fluid-filled chambers capable of detecting distant turbulence: an opening conduit, a falling block, the coordinated turn of a sliver shoal. The plateau exists to them not as walls and avenues but as a shifting field of compression and release.

They are scavengers when possible and predators when necessary. A rider drapes itself over carrion, enclosing the body beneath its mantle while digestive fluid reduces it. If food is scarce, it descends over a shoal and curls both wings inward. The trapped slivers are swallowed whole.

Their mating occurs during storms. Adults gather where descending water shears against an upwelling current, then release packets of eggs into the unstable boundary. The eggs are scattered through the architecture. Most are crushed or buried. A few lodge in warm cavities and hatch years later, ensuring that no single collapse can exterminate the species.

The largest animals are the **violet drifters** at the escarpment.

They resemble umbrellas only from a distance. Up close, each is a continent of soft tissue: lobes folded over lobes, with mouths distributed across the inner surface and long vanes trailing behind. Weak light pulses beneath their skin. The glow comes from symbiotic organisms kept in branching chambers, fed with minerals drawn from the drifter’s blood.

A violet drifter does not illuminate prey. It has little perception of light and no need to see. The glow regulates its symbionts and perhaps warns other drifters when two vast bodies approach in the dark. To smaller animals, the slow violet pulses accidentally reveal the ruin around them.

Drifters station themselves behind the violent outflow between the buttresses. Their trailing vanes comb the water for plankton and bacterial floc. Though they appear passive, they can contract with tremendous force. A threatened animal folds its canopy and expels the water inside, launching itself away from the escarpment. The resulting wake can overturn blocks already made unstable by erosion.

Their lives are long enough for the plateau to change around them. Scars on the oldest individuals contain ceramic fragments from walls that no longer exist. Colonies of bells settle on their trailing vanes. Seam-mouth larvae mistake their skin for architecture. Small predators live between their folds and feed on whatever the larger animal captures.

When a drifter dies, it sinks slowly along the outer face. For weeks its glow continues, dimming as the symbionts consume the last reserves. Scavengers gather around the descending light. Eventually the carcass passes beyond the reach of every current known to the plateau.

The Basin of Steps has its own inhabitants: **reverse crabs**, brought up from the constructed depths during exhalations.

They are white, eyeless, and asymmetrical. One side of the body is heavily armored; the other is covered in feathery gills. This suggests a life spent pressed against the inner walls of narrow channels, exposed to abrasive flow on one side and protected on the other. Their legs bend in directions that look impossible until they enter a crack and move through it without turning.

Most reverse crabs expelled into the basin die. The water is too cold, the distances too open, and the predators unfamiliar. They scramble downward during the next inhalation, following chemical traces of warmth.

Some fail to return and adapt to the terraces. Over successive molts their armored side thins, their legs become more balanced, and their gills shorten. They feed on storm-killed animals and fragments of bell colonies. These stranded populations may survive for centuries before an exhalation introduces deep-dwelling relatives that can no longer breed with them.

Thus the basin repeatedly creates new forms and repeatedly erases them.

There are smaller lives beyond counting: threadlike scavengers that enter the bodies of dead slivers; parasites that live inside the pressure-sensing chambers of mantle riders; translucent worms that stitch themselves into cracks with mucus; colonial sacs that digest trapped organic dust one molecule at a time. Some species are known only from remains caught in the bells. Others appear after a storm, flourish in one newly opened hall, and disappear when the passage silts shut.

None repairs the plateau. None meaningfully transforms it. Even the strongest anchors penetrate only existing flaws. The animals cannot bore the pale material, shape it, or add to it. They live upon a world that refuses to become habitat except where age has damaged it.

Their senses are fitted not to objects but to events.

They feel the approach of pressure fronts. They taste warmth from lower levels. They read turbulence curling from an arch and know whether a predator occupies it. They distinguish the vibration of settling grit from the first minute slip of a slab that will soon cleave away. Light, where perceived at all, is secondary.

Memory is likewise geographical only in the loosest sense. A creature may remember a pattern of currents, a sequence of pressures, the flavor of water passing through a particular buried reservoir. When walls collapse, those patterns change. The remembered world becomes unreachable although it remains physically near.

So the plateau’s inhabitants are always being displaced, even when they do not move.

They are pale because color serves little purpose, soft because armor cannot resist the forces that matter, tenacious because every secure surface is temporary. They feed on what rises, falls, or is driven past them. They wait more than they seek. They survive not by mastering the structure but by yielding at the correct moment and holding fast at all others.

Nothing among them wonders who made the drowned highland. Nothing needs the avenues to have been avenues, the windows to have been windows, or the vast concentric basin to have possessed an intended use.

To them, the maker is the current.

The architecture is weather.

And home is whatever hollow remains when the water has finished passing through.
