What lives in the Bell-Cirque has learned not to stand higher than it must.

The oldest inhabitants are mats, crusts, threads, and stains: lichens sunk into the black stone; moss packed into hollows until it resembles green felt; fungi hidden in the soaked dark beneath it. They hold the cirque together by increments too small to see. They catch grit, keep water from leaving, soften the beds of seeds, and close over every dropped feather and splinter of bone. A bare ledge is never wholly bare. It carries a gray map of growth, expanding at the pace of weather.

Above this first skin lies a second, red-stemmed and close-woven. Dwarf willows travel sideways, their branches rooting where they touch. Juniper crawls with them, dark and resinous, while sedges occupy every runnel the water has considered. The blue flowers are among the cirque’s briefest lives. For most of the day they remain shut, no more conspicuous than bluish pebbles. When sunlight enters cleanly, whole patches open together. Tiny flies find them almost at once, as if they have waited inside the stone for that particular color.

The birches under the cliffs are trees only by ancestry. They bend, divide, and lie down. Some have trunks thick enough to suggest a proper woodland, yet their crowns scarcely reach a person’s shoulder. Their roots clutch entire slabs. Their bark is ringed with old wounds where snow, hooves, and gathering hands have passed. In wind they do not sway so much as tighten. Hares sleep beneath their lowest branches in shallow forms lined with shed fur.

Everything in the moss lives at the scale of droplets.

There are black larvae in the tarn, each working over submerged growth with minute jaws. Beetles wedge themselves under loose flakes of stone, where the temperature changes less violently. Pale moths hang beneath leaves like scraps of wet ash. Midges rise in faint columns during still intervals, though stillness in the cirque rarely lasts. When spray crosses them they vanish, then reassemble over another patch of sedge.

The tarn’s fish are slender, dark-backed, and almost colorless along the belly. Most are no longer than a finger. In clear light they can be seen holding themselves over the copper gravel where the underground spring enters, facing a current invisible from above. Rain draws them upward. They take insects from the surface with small, precise sounds.

No one knows how they came there. The people who once used the shelters appear to have wondered as well. Fish bones are uncommon in the hearth ash, and several old jawbones were found threaded on fiber rather than split for food. Perhaps the fish were spared. Perhaps they were eaten only at certain times. Perhaps they were believed to know a road through the mountain that human beings had lost.

The cliff birds know every cavity in the rock. They nest behind curtains of dripping moss and in seams so narrow that their bodies seem to turn liquid when they enter. Their young hatch into darkness and waterfall noise. At the first shadow of a long-winged hunter, adults burst from the wall in a single changing body. They bank low over the tarn, silver-bellied when the light catches them, then break apart among the ledges.

The choughs are less fearful and more inquisitive. They walk with a rolling gait through the turf, lifting moss, tipping over chips of slate, and inspecting anything newly exposed. They have carried away needles, hooks, bright flakes of volcanic glass, and once, perhaps, the missing handle of the cracked cup. Bits of worked material sometimes appear in their nests high on the rear wall. The oldest choughs have learned the eastern cleft and wait near it when weather is mild. A person climbing into the cirque would hear their calls before seeing the basin. Whether they announce visitors or demand payment is a distinction the birds do not make.

Hares keep to the dwarf birches and stone ribs. In summer they are brown mixed with lichen gray, their ears edged in black. In winter they become an imperfect white. The cirque’s snow is too intermittent and wind-scoured for a pure coat to conceal them, and so they often look stained, half changed, or dressed for weather that has already passed. When the waterfall’s voice disappears, they flatten without hesitation. They do not look toward the sky or the cliffs. They look toward the brink.

The hoofed animals arrive by routes no ground-dweller can understand. They are compact creatures with deep chests, coarse coats, and horns swept backward close to the skull. Spray beads on their guard hairs. They step onto ledges scarcely wider than their hooves and pause without trembling over the gulf. The oldest females lead. They know where black stone remains sound beneath moss and where an apparent shelf is only vegetation stretched over emptiness.

They come for salt as much as grass. Along the western wall, seepage leaves a pale mineral bloom on the rock. Generations of tongues have polished it into shallow bowls. The animals lick there at dawn, crop the sedges around the tarn, and sleep briefly in the lee of the cairns. Their paths cross the basin only where crossing is necessary. Nothing that survives long in the Bell-Cirque wastes movement.

The gray cat beneath the fallen stones is built on the same principle. It is low, broad-pawed, and thick-furred, with blunt ears and a tail banded so faintly that the markings appear only when it passes through sun. It hunts moths, birds, larvae stranded near the tarn’s edge, and young hares when it can take them. It drinks where the stream disappears beneath the root mat, never from the open tarn. Its den lies somewhere in the western collapse, probably beyond a slit too narrow for any larger animal.

There may be more than one cat. Tracks after rain sometimes differ in size, and in late summer a thin whistling cry comes from the stones. Yet no one sees two together. The cirque has a way of making a population seem like a single, deathless creature.

The larger tracks belong to something else.

They appear beside the outer lip after nights of cloud: four broad toes, blunt claws, a long pad impressed deeply into wet ground. The animal that leaves them does not come through the eastern cleft. It does not use the ungulates’ shelves. Its trail begins where the falling water vanishes and proceeds along the brink, sometimes for twenty or thirty paces, before returning to open air.

It may be a wolf from some lower valley, climbing by a concealed rib. It may be several wolves whose tracks weather into one another. The old inhabitants seem to have thought neither explanation adequate. They called such animals the ones belonging to the drop—not beasts that might fall, but beasts for whom falling and walking were adjacent forms of travel.

Whatever leaves the tracks seldom hunts inside the cirque. The hares remain numerous. The hoofed animals do not abandon their visits. Even the gray cat crosses the prints. Only the center of the basin is avoided, as if the invisible traveler observes a boundary corresponding to the seven cairns.

Those cairns were raised by the last tall animals to live here.

The people of the Bell-Cirque were few. Three shelters do not suggest more than several families at once, and the sleeping platforms were narrow. They were probably seasonal inhabitants in the beginning, coming during the mild months for hides, horn, medicinal plants, eggs, and the volcanic glass found in a seam above the tarn. Later, someone learned to remain through winter. Turf roofs were thickened. Birch was bent into inner frames. Hooks were carved for hanging food away from damp and mice. Fire-hardened pegs multiplied in the eastern cleft.

Over generations, the cirque worked its shape into them.

They would have been strong in the hands and shoulders, patient on ladders, sparing with fuel. They could not walk anywhere carelessly. Children learned the safe stones before they learned distant names. A dropped tool might be gone beyond recovery; a lost iron rivet might represent months of value. Nothing portable was thrown away until it had become too small to mend another thing.

Their days followed water rather than hours. Hides went into the soaking basin when the stream ran cold and clear. Roots were gathered after prolonged cloud, when they came loose from the soil. Glass was chipped during low wind, lest flakes disappear into moss and bare feet find them later. Smoke was kept small beneath the overhangs. Birchwood was precious, so fires burned sedge turf, juniper, old dung, and grease.

They must have spoken quietly—not from reverence, at first, but because the cirque mishandled voices. A warning could return from the wrong direction. A name spoken by the tarn might come back after the named person had already answered. During certain winds, words thrown outward rose again from below in altered company. Over time practical caution would have hardened into custom. Some things were said only under the shelters. Some names were never called near the brink. Children were taught to wait for an echo before speaking again, in case the mountain had not finished.

The seven cairns may have counted the dead, the safe winds, the households, or the routes by which animals entered. Their pale upright stones were chosen from somewhere beyond the cirque; no matching rock is visible nearby. The undarkened bands near their tops show that something once encircled them—cord, leather, woven grass, perhaps little collars of pale metal. Whatever was tied there has rotted or been removed.

The people did not leave suddenly. There are no scattered stores, no collapsed roof pinning tools beneath it, no signs of struggle. Useful things were taken. Heavy things remained. One shelter was maintained longer than the others, its hearth repeatedly cleaned while moss advanced through the neighboring walls. The final inhabitants may have been an old pair, or one keeper returning alone each summer to repair the eastern pegs and reset stones around the cairns.

Then the repairs stopped.

Still, the cirque is not empty of them. Their feet selected the path the ungulates now use beside the stream. Their cutting encouraged willow to thicken around the shelters. Rust from their rivets stains the moss. Birds incorporate their fibers into nests. The gray cat passes through a gap made by a missing hearthstone. Human occupation has become another layer of habitat.

And beneath all these lives is the inhabitant that shaped them most thoroughly: the moving air.

Wind enters as one thing and is divided by the cirque into many. It inhabits cracks, hollows, throats of stone, the bent birches, the feathers of hovering hunters. It gives each opening a pitch. It presses the vegetation flat, carries spray uphill, strips warmth from exposed bodies, and tells every creature when to crouch. The Bell-Cirque earns its name not from a bell hung there, but from the basin itself: stone for the cup, cliffs for the rim, the gulf below for the great unseen chamber into which its sound descends.

When that sound abruptly ceases, all the inhabitants obey.

Birds enter the walls. Choughs lower their heads. Hares disappear into grass. Hoofed animals stop chewing. The cat remains wherever it is. Even the tarn fish sink until their silver sides no longer answer the light.

For a few moments the cirque appears wholly deserted.

Then a broad track may darken beside the brink, filling slowly with blown water, though nothing visible has crossed the turf.

The thunder returns. Wings rise. The birches click. Insects gather above the sedges, the fish come back to the rain, and the small blue flowers turn toward the open sky. Life resumes, low to the ground and fastened tightly, while beyond the final slab the stream pours itself into a country no creature in the cirque needs to see.
