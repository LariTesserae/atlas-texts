At first, the inhabitants are difficult to separate from the work.

A figure stands beside a handrail in the rain, nearly motionless, and might be mistaken for a bollard or a bundled coil of hose. Another passes behind condensation-streaked glass, visible only as a shift in the greenhouse light. Farther down, three people lean together over an open gutter. Their hoods touch. One reaches into the water, another watches a meter, and the third writes something on a slate strapped to the forearm. When they finish, they close the grating and leave no sign that anything was wrong.

They dress for wetness rather than cold: close-woven coats waxed at the shoulders, short capes that do not catch on ladders, high boots with soft soles for iron stairs. Most garments are dark green, soot brown, brick red, or the faded blue of old work cloth. Reflective tabs appear briefly when lamps strike them. Tools are kept close to the body in narrow belts and breast pockets, because anything hanging loose will snag on a valve wheel or cable brace. Even children learn not to trail scarves.

There are many kinds of worker, but the distinctions are less clean than the signs on doors suggest. A water tender knows enough masonry to reset a loosened curb. A gardener can isolate the heat loop under a cultivation bed. Freight crews prune vines away from signal boxes while waiting for a wagon. The oldest people seem to know entire districts vertically: not merely which lane reaches a building, but which stair enters its former roof, which lift shaft was once a spillway, and which warm pipe can be followed through three workshops when the public route is closed.

No one knows the whole valley.

Knowledge lives in overlapping routes. It is spoken at shift changes, scratched inside cabinet doors, embroidered into colored tags, entered in ledgers that have survived several generations of machinery. Plans are useful, but every plan is incomplete almost as soon as it is printed. The people rely on annotations: a pencil line showing where water actually goes, a date pressed into fresh mortar, a small brass arrow fixed beside a pipe that appears to lead uphill but does not.

They have an exact vocabulary for damp. There is the harmless sweating of a cold conduit, the first darkening of stressed brick, the mineral fur that means a hidden seep has been moving for years. They distinguish roof mist from channel mist by smell. They can hear whether a drip is falling into an empty cavity or onto the back of a ceiling panel. A child sent to fetch someone may report that a wall is “breathing wrong,” and an adult will go at once.

They do not treat the machinery as sacred. They strike casings with mallets, swear at seized fasteners, replace elegant old controls with plain new ones, and use obsolete housings as shelves. Yet they seldom call anything dead. A stopped turbine is sleeping, resting, isolated, waiting on bearings. A disused line is dry, not abandoned. Buildings are spoken of by what they were and what they are becoming in the same breath: the old dye hall, now mushrooms, soon filters.

Maintenance is the valley’s most common occupation, but maintenance here does not mean preserving things unchanged. It means keeping passage possible. Water must be able to find another channel. Freight must have another lift. Heat must be accepted somewhere when a boiler house closes. Soil must be moved before a roof is opened. A repair crew may arrive to replace a pipe and leave having built a stair, shifted an orchard, and opened a room that had been sealed since their grandparents’ time.

They work in small groups, often without much speech. The valley is full of signals too modest to notice from a distance: a strip of yellow cloth tied beneath a railing; chalk marks that rain will erase when they are no longer needed; three lamps in a vertical row; the angle of a gate handle. Hands touch shoulders in narrow passages. Tools are passed backward without looking. Before a heavy mechanism starts, everyone in sight raises one open palm, and the gesture travels along the gallery until the operator receives it.

Their quiet is not solemnity. Indoors, there is argument, gossip, song, laughter sudden enough to carry through ventilation grilles. Dining rooms occupy improbable corners: above a switch hall, beneath a reservoir, in the glazed bridge joining two hospitals. People eat thick soups, mushrooms fried with herbs, pickled cabbage, dark bread, soft cheeses kept in cool galleries, apples cooked until their toughness gives way. Tea is nearly continuous. Cups have lids. Every workplace has somewhere warm to sit, even if it is only a bench beside a return pipe.

Homes are threaded through the industrial fabric rather than gathered apart from it. A family may live in rooms built along the outer wall of a pumping station, with rounded windows looking into mist on one side and an inspection gallery on the other. Some apartments are deep within terraces and have no exterior view; their windows open instead onto lit courtyards, fern wells, tram halls, or the humid interior of a greenhouse. The quietest bedrooms are often the farthest underground, where the sound of the valley becomes a soft vibration through the bed frame.

Domestic life extends into shared passages. Boots dry above communal grates. Bundles of herbs hang in warm stairwells. Children’s height marks climb service doors among inspection dates and flood levels. Laundry rooms borrow heat from substations. Roof kitchens tend narrow plots of leeks and blue cabbage, each bed labeled not only by crop but by the weight the roof can safely bear.

Children know the valley first as a sequence of permissions. This rail may be touched; that one may be hot. Blue lines lead home. Red lamps mean wait. Never step over a moving chain, even if it appears slow. Never enter fog where the floor cannot be seen. If water rises over the second stone, climb, do not run downhill.

They play all the same. They float seed husks through gutters and race them between grates. They build miniature lock systems from broken brick. They know which freight lifts have attendants willing to carry them one level farther than necessary. In cultivation halls they press their faces to screened pollinator rooms and watch the maintained bees move among pale blossoms. Adolescents climb to ventilation roofs after dark, where they sit among thyme beds and listen to freight windows moving across the opposite slope.

The elderly are visible in gatehouses, kitchens, tool libraries, seed rooms, and warm galleries. Some continue working in narrow specialties that seem almost ceremonial until they are needed: recognizing old mortar mixtures, repairing leather pump seals, reading submerged markings, grafting the small valley apples, recalling which buried conduit belongs to a district renamed twice. Their memories are treated as infrastructure. When one of them can no longer climb, younger workers bring pieces down: a corroded fitting, a moss sample, a recording of a turbine’s irregular note.

There are officials, though they do not appear to rule from any single high place. Authority moves with the problem. During a drainage shift, the water coordinators may close roads and command freight crews. During a structural repair, masons decide which homes must empty and which canals may remain full. Growers control heat allocations in a cold week. Decisions are made in rooms with maps on sliding racks, but also on wet landings, with five people looking into an opened wall.

The valley has politics because every flow has an upstream and a downstream. Warmth given to one roof is lost to another. Clean water can be used first by households, workshops, orchards, filters, or turbines, and the order matters. New construction shades old beds. A diverted tram line sends vibration through someone’s ceiling. Arguments may last for years and become embedded in the landscape as duplicated pipes, compromise bridges, deliberately inconvenient junctions. People remember who received the bright southern wall and who was promised the waste heat beneath it.

Still, waste is the nearest thing they have to a shared enemy. A leaking roof is not only a defect but water in the wrong place. Unused warmth is an accusation. Empty rooms attract proposals before dust. Broken ceramics become filter fill; worn cloth becomes insulation; food scraps descend to digesters; excavated brick is sorted by hand. They are not austere. They simply live where every remainder is visibly adjacent to another need.

Birth and death also occur inside this circulation, though not without privacy. The hospitals are warm, green-windowed buildings set near clean reservoirs. Newborns are sometimes given temporary names associated with the weather of their first week—Mist, Clearbreak, Long Rain—before receiving the names they will keep. The dead are washed with heated rainwater. Some are cremated using recovered furnace heat; others are placed in mineral chambers built into the upper retaining walls. Their names appear on small ceramic plaques among maintenance marks, so that memorials resemble labels and labels sometimes become memorials.

People leave the valley, but departure is less common than movement within it. One can travel for days through connected works and still sleep under the same system of lamps. Those who go beyond speak of open ground with a mixture of longing and distrust. Uncontained rivers seem beautiful but extravagant. Wide roads make them uneasy. Silence without a transformer hum may keep them awake.

Visitors find the inhabitants reserved. Greetings are brief, directions exact but incomprehensible to anyone who thinks only in streets: descend two water levels, cross above the old canal but below the new one, follow the warm main until the brick changes, take the lift marked for reeds. A local may escort a lost stranger a considerable distance without asking a question, then vanish through a service door.

Their faces are not uniform, nor are their origins. The valley has been accumulating people as long as it has accumulated structures. Families arrived with foundries, canal projects, seed collections, electrical works, refugees, collapsed industries elsewhere. Names from many languages have been shortened by intercoms and lengthened again at kitchen tables. What they share is less ancestry than habit: looking upward when rain changes; stepping aside before a warning lamp fully brightens; noticing whether a wall sounds hollow.

And they are not the only things living there.

The cultivated plants are tended with close familiarity, but the mosses are watched with nearly equal care. Certain kinds mean stable moisture; others warn of acid runoff or failing mortar. Ferns are allowed to remain in cable recesses if their roots stay shallow. Reeds are cut in rotating sections so that the treatment beds never fall silent all at once. Fungal threads pass through compost, tunnel beds, timber, and forgotten insulation, useful in one chamber and dangerous in the next.

The bees live in screened warmth and are moved between flowering halls in small wheeled hives. Their keepers open gates according to light levels, tracking the insects by colored dust on their backs. Fish in the inspection pools are both livestock and instruments: a change in their swimming may close an intake before a sensor confirms anything. Cats belong nominally to workshops but observe no such boundaries. Their names are repeated across districts, giving rise to disputes over whether a black cat seen beside three loading doors is one animal or several.

Frogs survive in the reed beds without authorization. Moths live brief lives around hooded lamps. Pale crustaceans inhabit the oldest covered drains. There are rumors of eels in the buried canals and of blind, colorless fish beneath the turbine halls, but no maintenance crew has produced one.

The rare wild birds interrupt the valley’s practiced attention. A heron can suspend a gallery. A flock descending through fog draws people onto roofs. Windows open. Work gloves point. For a few minutes the inhabitants watch creatures that cross levels without stairs, ignore signals, and leave by no route anyone maintains.

Then the bird lifts away.

The people return to pumps, seedlings, ledgers, meals, quarrels, repairs. Beneath them, water changes course. Around them, walls drink and release the weather. Their lives do not stand apart from the apparatus, yet neither are they its servants. They are its memory, its irritants, its improvisation—the part that notices what no sensor was placed to notice.

Seen from far across the basin, they scarcely appear at all. A lamp is covered, then uncovered. A small group crosses a bridge. A greenhouse door opens and spills green light into mist. Along an upper wall, someone kneels beside a leaking joint while another holds a sheet against the rain.

By morning there will be a new gutter there, feeding a tank that did not exist the day before. Above it, in a pocket of fresh soil, something will already have been planted.
