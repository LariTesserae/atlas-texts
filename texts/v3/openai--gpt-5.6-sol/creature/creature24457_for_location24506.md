What lives in the basin is difficult to count.

At first, everything appears divisible. A glassleaf fan is one organism, the saffron mat beneath it another. The white figures are animals; the six-winged absences are smaller animals; the mineral flowers belong to some third kingdom that has borrowed equally from plant, lens, and stone. Each has a boundary. Each occupies a place.

Closer observation makes those boundaries less convincing.

Glassleaf is the most conspicuous life, but not necessarily the most abundant. What looks like a grove is a system of black-green blades rising from a common depth beneath the pavement. The blades are not soft enough to be leaves and not inert enough to be machines. Silver ribs divide their surfaces into long cells in which charge circulates without obvious source or destination. At intervals, a muted green pulse passes along one rib and appears immediately in another plant hundreds of meters away. Nothing crosses between.

A blade can be cut free. The detached portion remains warm, continues to hold the sunlight at the same angle, and retains every microscopic sign of having grown where it did. The stump retains an equally complete history in which it was never cut. Both are genuine. If the loose blade is carried toward the basin’s rim, it becomes progressively heavier—not by gaining mass, but by acquiring more possible positions. Most carriers abandon it before reaching the ordinary dust.

The saffron carpets are less passive. Their ceramic filaments lean toward pressure before pressure arrives. Under a walking foot they arrange themselves into narrow supporting arches, neither resisting nor yielding. They seem to know not only where the foot will land but all the places it might have landed. Afterward, several incompatible impressions may briefly be seen among the strands. Then the carpet resolves into its untouched form.

No seedling has ever been found in such a mat. No filament is younger than another. Yet the carpet contains structures resembling seeds: hard orange beads buried among the roots, each enclosing a perfect but unexpressed pattern for the whole plain. If removed and opened beyond the boundary, they contain only gray powder. Inside the basin, they cannot be opened. Every attempt produces a bead with an unbroken shell and, beside it, two halves that have always been empty.

The crown-plants may be organs of perception. Their pale limbs ring an absence that is more consistent than any substance around it. The empty centers do not frame whatever lies behind them; they choose what can be seen through them. This is why their apertures remain aligned over impossible distances. At dawn, faint bands of charge travel inward along every limb and vanish at the central gap. At evening, the process reverses, though nothing visible emerges.

The six-winged creatures inhabit these gaps.

They are usually called veils, although a veil has substance and these may not. Seen against the sky, each is a moving omission in reflected light, roughly the size of two spread hands. Six narrow regions radiate from a central absence. They do not beat like wings. Instead, one region darkens while another becomes clear, and the creature is elsewhere between those changes.

A veil sometimes settles over the central opening of a crown-plant. The plant’s limbs then display reflections of landscapes that cannot be found nearby: a dark sea under stars, a field of red vapor, a white interior crossed by black roots. Whether these are distant places, rejected histories, or merely the veil’s coloration is unknown.

Veils do not feed in the ordinary sense. They pass over suspended pollen, and afterward each grain remains in place. But for several minutes the pollen casts no shadow. The shadows gradually return, first as thin lines pointing in different directions, then as ordinary specks beneath the grains. It may be light, rather than matter, that the veils consume—or one of the unused paths by which light could have arrived.

Their young have never been observed. Occasionally a crown holds a smaller absence among its limbs, folded into a six-pointed shape. It does not grow. Later it is gone, though the crown retains an exact, permanent accommodation for its presence.

The tall white beings are rarer and more troubling because they resemble creatures that ought to have intentions.

They stand higher than the glassleaf understory, on four or sometimes five slender legs. Their bodies are narrow and seamless, with no clear joints except when they move. The surface is neither skin nor shell. It has the matte whiteness of unglazed porcelain, yet bends without cracking and shows no pore, hair, scale, or opening. A vertical dark seam occupies the forward end of the body. Observers call it a face because the seam tends to be oriented toward whatever is watching.

When seen from opposite sides at once, the creature faces both observers.

It has no mouth and leaves no spoor. It neither crushes the saffron filaments nor casts a stable shadow. Dissections have not been possible. A blade passes into the white surface and emerges from another point in the landscape, often several minutes earlier. Attempts to trap one produce enclosures that, on inspection, were built around empty ground.

These beings do move, but movement is not what carries them. A white figure may lift one leg and place it down in the same visible spot. Afterward it is farther away. Its pace is stately, almost ceremonial, each step altering the measure between itself and the observer. It can cross an open field without changing apparent size, then appear among distant crowns as though it had always formed part of their alignment.

They are sometimes found around damaged things: beside a recently snapped mineral needle, near a scorched patch that will not remain scorched, or at the site where a foreign object was dropped. They do nothing visible. The basin corrects itself whether they are present or not. This has led to their being called wardens, but there is no proof they guard anything. They may simply be drawn to contradiction. They may feed on events the basin refuses to keep.

One account describes a white figure bending over a human footprint during the fraction of a second before the print disappeared. The dark seam opened—not outward, but lengthwise through the scene. Inside was the same footprint, repeated at diminishing scales. Then the seam closed, and both figure and print were absent.

The dry clicking heard on warm evenings may belong to them, though none has been seen producing it. The first click passes across the surface. The answer comes from beneath.

Something does live below the crust.

Roots penetrate the pavement, but not into ordinary strata. Drilling reveals dense basalt for a few centimeters, followed by cavities that should collapse and do not. Within them are downward forests: glassleaf fans opening into darkness, crown-plants arranged around vertical shafts, mirror-flowers turned toward a light deeper than the borehole can reach. These structures are never struck directly. The drill proceeds between them regardless of its angle.

Recordings lowered into the cavities capture no ambient sound. If a click is played, the instrument retrieves two answers. One is an exact return. The other is subtly altered, as if the sound had been imitated by something that understood its pattern but not its cause.

The answering presence has no agreed form. It may be a buried animal of continental extent. It may be the lower half of every plant, joined in a single inverted canopy. Some believe the visible basin is only its exposed sensory surface: mineral flowers as eyes, crowns as distance organs, glassleaf as a means of thinking in sunlight. In this interpretation, the white figures are not inhabitants but thoughts—mobile concentrations of attention moving through a mind that has no need to distinguish space from memory.

Yet there are reasons to doubt that the basin is one being.

Different groves correct disturbances in subtly different ways. Some preserve every possible state. Others reject all but the oldest. A few appear to favor the version containing the greatest symmetry. At the borders between groves, leaves interlock in elaborate unmoving contests for light neither side requires. Pale crowns aim their openings at one another. Sounds released from the silver ribs emerge changed, as if translated between incompatible systems.

The place may therefore contain many lives, but lives that overlap too completely to become competitors. Each occupies its own set of physical rules. They share sunlight, stone, and apparent geography without necessarily sharing one world.

Nor is it clear whether any of them are alive in the familiar sense. They do not begin, reproduce, age, or die. They do not maintain themselves against decay, because decay has no authority over them. Their metabolism resembles remembrance more than hunger. They persist not by enduring change but by refusing to let change become exclusive.

What lives there may be every stage of an ecology at once: ancestor, adult, offspring, fossil, and eventual machine, all held in the same warm instant.

Or the only true inhabitant may be the basin itself.

Its plants would then be gestures it has never finished making. The veils would be gaps through which it senses elsewhere. The white walkers would be questions it poses to intruders. The deep clicking would be its one effort at speech—a call sent across its own surface and answered from the immeasurable country within.

It does not appear hostile. Hostility requires preference, and the basin permits no state to replace another. But it is not harmless. A visitor remains foreign only while the place can reconcile their passage with its completed whole.

Those who return sometimes bring contradictory memories. They recall walking alone and in company. They remember meeting a white figure and seeing none. Some carry scars from wounds they never received. Some cast, for a few days, an extra shadow pointing toward the basin.

A few do not return, though their companions can still see them far behind among the glassleaves: motionless, untouched, and always at the same impossible distance.

Perhaps they live there now.

Perhaps, within the basin, they always did.
