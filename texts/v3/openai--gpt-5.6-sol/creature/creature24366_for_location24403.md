The first inhabitants one notices are not necessarily the largest, but the ones whose labor can be felt through the rock.

They are squat, tripodal animals called, for convenience, **knucklers**. Each grips the stone with three shell-clad limbs arranged around a central body like the jaws of a chuck. Their shells vary according to diet: oil-black where they feed on obsidian, dull silver over iron-rich seams, and blue-gray when their tissues have accumulated cobalt. No two limbs are quite alike. One anchors, one tests fractures with a tapping claw, and one carries the feeding apparatus—a stack of mineral teeth that rotates behind a circular lip.

A knuckler may remain attached to the same handspan of rock for most of its life. It rasps only a few times each hour. Between strokes it listens. Pressure-sensitive membranes inside its casing compare the returning vibrations, allowing it to distinguish solid stone from a cavity, an ore inclusion, an egg chamber, or another animal working on the opposite side of the ridge. When several knucklers meet along one seam, they adjust their strokes until they alternate. The resulting pattern resembles coordinated hammer-work, though each animal is merely avoiding interference.

Their young begin as soft, transparent bladders with three dark points where the limbs will form. They drift until vibration draws them to exposed glass. Then they settle, swallow a dusting of stone, and use its silica to make their first teeth. The shell hardens later. A juvenile caught before this happens looks scarcely animal at all: a clear drop with a mechanical tremor inside it.

Deeper in the fractures live the **sourhearts**.

Only their mouths and exhaust siphons are usually visible. Their bodies descend through the rock as chains of muscular chambers, some no wider than thread, others swollen into bulbs. They cultivate acid-producing microbes in warm internal sacs and pump the resulting brine into cracks. Their metabolism is extraordinarily slow. A sourheart may spend a season pressurizing one vein, then release the entire charge in a single contraction. The crack opens with a report like a distant gunshot.

The animal withdraws before the loosened stone shifts. It has no need to collect what it has broken. It feeds on the dissolved material carried back through its siphons, while the flakes become food and shelter for everything around it.

Sourhearts are among the longest-lived creatures in the Crown. The oldest have no single body left. As chambers fail, neighboring chambers seal themselves off and bud replacements. The original mouth may have vanished centuries before while the colony of organs beneath it continues pumping. Some cracks contain generations of one individual connected by scar tissue and inherited plumbing.

The loose flakes are swallowed by **assayers**, barrel-bodied animals that move on rims of tiny hooked feet. Their interiors are divided into bright, chemically distinct stomachs. Through a clear strip along the shell, one can see powdered rock pass from chamber to chamber: black in the first, rust-red in the second, sulfur-yellow in the third, and finally pale as bone.

Assayers are selective only in the arrangement of their guts. They swallow almost anything small enough, including larvae and fragments of other animals. Useful elements are plated onto internal membranes. What remains is pressed into precise pellets and expelled in strings. The pellets differ in conductivity, density, and hardness, and other creatures recognize them by the sounds they make when struck.

An assayer’s waste may therefore attract an entire procession. Jawless gatherers carry away iron pellets. Pale brood animals wall their nurseries with silica. Sulfur-eaters wait at the vent itself and drink what emerges before it hardens. Tiny parasites lodge around the opening and steal trace metals one grain at a time.

Many of the crater’s apparent “parts” are these animals’ leavings.

The green-edged copper blades that protrude from ledges are assayer feces extruded as thin sheets. The silver wool in sheltered hollows is shed filtration mesh. Black terraces around the central vent consist largely of compacted shells, each one emptied of flesh and filled with mineral precipitate. Even the conductive network laid across the rock begins as excretion.

The creatures that make that network are the **wiregutters**.

They are long, low animals with dozens of jointed segments, though only the first and last segments contain substantial organs. Between them extends a living loom. Wiregutters browse on metallic filaments collected from filter feeders. Inside the loom, ciliated channels sort those filaments by thickness, coat them in proteins, twist them into strands, and braid the strands together.

A wiregutter feeds through its front and lays cable from its rear.

The fresh braid is soft and faintly pink, nourished by capillaries that remain connected to the animal for several days. It is pressed into grooves and around shell projections. There it mineralizes, darkens, and becomes electrically active. When the wiregutter finally detaches, it leaves a small portion of living tissue behind. These remnants fuse with the remnants of other individuals, so the network is neither wholly alive nor wholly dead. It heals slowly. It can become infected. It sometimes grows blind loops into useless cracks.

Wiregutters follow electrical gradients but cannot tell whether they are strengthening the colony or merely circling back toward their own work. In old regions of the Crown, miles of microscopic braid are tangled into knots that lead nowhere. Such knots warm under current and become nurseries for heat-loving eggs.

The electricity itself is supplied in part by **spark lice**, small shell-backed creatures that congregate at junctions. Each contains stacks of electrocytes wrapped around a violet digestive core. Alone, a spark louse can produce only a feeble pulse. In dense clusters they discharge sequentially, passing charge from one body to the next until it enters the braid as a sustained current.

Their synchronization is social without being cooperative. A louse shocked by its neighbor contracts and shocks the next. The signal travels through thousands of bodies as a visible blue runnel. When one becomes exhausted, it drops out of the cluster and feeds on mineral slime until its chemical reserves return.

Spark lice are continually eaten by flat predators called **listeners**.

A listener has six eyes but makes little use of them. Its broad underside is packed with vibration receptors. It creeps over shell banks, testing each animal beneath it through the tips of many fine feet. Healthy machinery produces regular cycles, even when those cycles are complicated. A failing chamber skips, shudders, or develops a flutter too faint for the water to carry far. The listener detects these faults.

Its attack is almost ceremonially slow. First it settles over the defective animal. Then six wedge-shaped mouthparts find the seams of the casing. Muscles pressurize over hours. The shell edges separate by fractions. Often the victim continues feeding throughout the process, its grinders turning while the listener’s mouth enters the gap.

Listeners prefer damaged organs because healthy ones resist digestion. In consuming the failing, infected, or rhythmically disordered, they prevent disease from spreading through the crowded banks. But they are not infallible. During mating cycles, when many creatures deliberately alter their rhythms, listeners sometimes open healthy animals and are themselves killed by the moving parts within.

In the narrow water between bodies drift the **bell-globes**: clear spheres from the size of a bead to that of a human head. Their transparency is interrupted only by a red-violet ring of muscle and a knot of mirrored sensory tissue. They pulse weakly, more to maintain position than to travel. Pressure waves flatten them, and the deformation triggers a flash.

A single hammer stroke may kindle a dozen. A deep concussion from the mountain lights thousands at once.

Bell-globes feed on suspended microbes and metal dust. Different metals produce different flashes. Blue-white indicates iron, green indicates copper, and a dim crimson reveals the rare compounds brought up by the turbine pumps. Other animals read these colors. Filter feeders open when the globes burn green. Needle swarms turn toward crimson. During periods of scarcity, the entire crater appears to answer the globes’ chemistry.

Their eggs are almost perfectly invisible. They adhere to shells as thin patches of altered refraction, discovered chiefly when they hatch and release clouds of flickering points.

The **needles** are among the few inhabitants that seem fast, though their speed is an illusion of number. Each is a rigid sliver with a fringe of microscopic fins and a mouth at either end. A single needle advances by minute corrections, but thousands align along an electrical field and hand food backward through the formation. A captured particle may cross the crater quickly even though none of the animals does.

A swarm behaves like iron filings around a magnet. It bends around charged cables, opens lanes for strong discharges, and collapses into dense mats when the field weakens. Needles scavenge dead tissue, exposed microbes, and the soft shells of newly settled larvae. They also clean conductive braids, scraping away insulating films. Spark lice tolerate them until they approach an egg mass, at which point the local cluster fires repeatedly and scatters the swarm.

Some needles carry no functioning mouth. These sterile forms accumulate magnetic minerals and serve as living compasses for their siblings. When they die, their bodies remain aligned in the sediment, preserving the direction of ancient fields in thin metallic strata.

Beneath nearly all visible life lies another, quieter population.

The protected cracks are coated with **tarnish colonies**: microbial communities that oxidize volcanic hydrogen, sulfur compounds, and dissolved metals. Their films are brown, black, dull green, or colorless. They grow in layers only cells thick, and every larger inhabitant depends upon them either directly or at several removes.

The films are not passive pastures. Some alter the charge of the rock to discourage grazers. Some precipitate poisonous crystals. Others exploit feeding animals by surviving passage through the gut and using the animal’s waste to colonize new stone. Vast microbial exchanges occur inside the sourhearts, assayers, turbines, and filter feeders. What appears to be one animal is often a moving enclosure for hundreds of chemical ecologies.

No inhabitant of the Crown is entirely an individual. Shells contain symbionts; guts house mineral gardens; joints are cleaned by scavengers; eggs require electrical stimulation from unrelated bodies. An animal removed from the crater may continue moving for months, yet be unable to molt, digest, breed, or repair its teeth. Its apparent autonomy is temporary.

At the center stand the beings around which much of this dependence turns.

They are known as **rootwheels**, though neither root nor wheel describes them fully. Their outer bodies are upright cylinders armored in overlapping ribs. At the top, broad vanes form an open crown. The vanes do not beat. They submit to the long tidal current and turn with it, slowly enough that encrusting animals live entire lives upon a single moving edge.

Inside, rings of enamel teeth transfer the rotation downward. Each tooth grows from a living socket. Worn teeth loosen and are swallowed by maintenance chambers, dissolved, and regrown farther along the ring. The gears are wet, muscular, and self-replacing. Their misalignments produce much of the crater’s grinding roar.

Below the visible cylinder, a rootwheel branches through rubble and bedrock. Its pumping organs surround warm fissures. With every fraction of a turn, they draw up mineral water and force it into fleshy conduits that spread beneath the shell banks.

Young rootwheels are mobile. They resemble armored stars and creep through the crater testing cracks with soft probing lobes. A suitable fissure must be warm, chemically rich, mechanically stable, and positioned where the tidal current can reach it. Most juveniles never find one. They are eaten, crushed, or settle in poor stone and remain stunted.

Once rooted, the animal changes sex repeatedly over its life. The current, temperature, and electrical condition of the network determine which reproductive tissues mature. Gametes are released into the pumped fluid rather than the open water, so a rootwheel may fertilize another whose visible body stands far away. Some may be connected by buried conduits so thoroughly that it is difficult to decide where one ends.

Their slowness gives them an appearance of permanence they do not possess. Rootwheels suffer gear rot, vane fractures, parasitic blockages, and catastrophic seizure. A seized adult becomes a feeding ground. Listeners arrive first, followed by wiregutters, sourhearts, and dense microbial blooms. Its internal pressure can persist long after death, causing the carcass to turn intermittently for years.

Nothing is wasted quickly, but almost everything is eventually used.

Old shells are occupied by **hushers**, pale, eyeless animals that line cavities with soft tissue. Hushers absorb vibration and feed on the minute heat produced in doing so. They gather most densely around egg chambers, where their bodies protect embryos from damaging resonance. A bank of hushers creates a rare pocket of near-silence. Predators dislike crossing these pockets because their feet become deaf.

Yet hushers do not merely damp sound. By contracting different regions of their bodies, they shape what passes through. Low frequencies may be admitted while high ones vanish, or a diffuse knock may be focused into a neighboring crack. A mature colony turns discarded shells into an acoustic maze. Larvae use these altered sounds as navigational cues, sometimes settling in nurseries prepared unintentionally generations before.

Other animals exploit resonance more aggressively.

The **seam singers** cling beneath overhangs on narrow stalks. Their bodies are hollow chambers crossed by tensioned mineral ribbons. They take in water and expel it across the ribbons, producing sustained tones that rise or fall as the chamber pressure changes. The sound helps them loosen microbial films, but groups near a fracture also tune themselves to the stone. When resonance builds, grains leap from the seam into the water.

A seam singer cannot hear in the usual sense. It measures the vibration of its own shell and adjusts until resistance falls. During mating periods, however, this mineral-seeking reflex becomes entangled with reproduction. Hundreds converge on shared frequencies. Their gonads open only when the crater answers strongly enough.

Thus courtship becomes excavation.

The oldest seams in the Crown bear regular grooves made by singing generations. Some tones have persisted as inherited traits even after the geology changed. Colonies may continue singing to a vanished ridge, producing an unresolved whine that attracts mates but breaks no stone.

Death in the Crown is often the swiftest motion an animal ever makes.

Pressure chambers rupture. Anchors fail. Shells snap shut. A body that has spent a century advancing across one ledge may tumble into the vent in seconds. Such events scatter feeding parts, release stored acids, tear cables, and trigger discharges. The response spreads mechanically before it spreads behaviorally: nearby animals contract from the pressure wave, their contractions shake others, and the entire wall may briefly clatter.

Yet death is not always easy to identify. An empty shell may still contain functioning microbial organs. Severed pump tissue may attach to another creature. A wire braid may remain alive after its maker is gone. Rootwheel gear teeth continue growing in detached rings. The Crown is full of processes outlasting the bodies that began them.

When the great electrical release approaches, these inhabitants change together without understanding the total event.

Spark lice swell with charge. Wiregutters stop laying braid and clamp their limbs around it. Needles descend into aligned mats. Bell-globes empty themselves of water and become limp against the rock. Knucklers withdraw their grinding plates. Listeners freeze over their chosen prey. Rootwheels gradually enter the same phase, their enormous internal teeth meeting in a shared interval.

The final alignment may take days.

Then the living network fires.

For those few white seconds, the Crown contains almost no separate creatures. Shells, water, cables, tissues, and mineral veins become one conducting body. Eggs open along predetermined fault lines. Some animals reverse the polarity of their membranes. Old attachment muscles release. Parasites are stunned loose from gills. Dormant microbes begin dividing in warm knots of cable.

Afterward, individuality returns unevenly.

The bell-globes reinflate. Needles peel away from their mats. A listener resumes the closing of a jaw begun days before. Knucklers extend their tools and test whether the stone still answers as it did. Newly hatched larvae rise in clouds, each carrying only enough structure to seek the vibration, charge, chemistry, or warmth that will complete it.

Taken one by one, the inhabitants are grinders, pumps, filters, scavengers, cables, capacitors, valves, and resonant chambers. None knows the system. Many spend their lives consuming one another’s waste or correcting disturbances they cannot perceive beyond a few body-lengths.

Taken together, they are harder to name.

They are not a colony in the manner of social insects, because they share no ancestry, purpose, or authority. They are not an organism, because their boundaries are open and their parts prey upon one another. They are not a machine, because nothing was assembled and no operation reaches completion.

They are an economy older than intention: a metabolism distributed through thousands of shells, using the mountain as both food and skeleton.

And every creature within it, from the rooted wheel to the invisible tarnish cell, lives by learning one minute portion of the Crown’s unending rhythm—when to grip, when to grind, when to conduct, when to open, and when, at last, to let go.
