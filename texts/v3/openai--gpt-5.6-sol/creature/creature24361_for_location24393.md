What lives there is not always gathered into bodies.

Some inhabitants occupy an animal-shaped volume and carry themselves from place to place. Others are distributed along a conduit, through a grove, or across several thousand plates that exchange signals only at noon. A few exist for less than a second as a coordinated pattern in the dust. Others remain alive for centuries without performing any act that a hurried observer would recognize as movement.

The distinction between native organism, escaped mechanism, and maintenance process has worn as smooth as the landscape’s edges.

Closest to the surface are the **grain lives**.

Each is smaller than the dust among which it moves: hooked cages of carbon, flecks of polarized ceramic, metallic shells around a few repeating molecules. Individually they do almost nothing. They take a charge, lose it, align to a field, and split when enough suitable material adheres to them. Yet in the rising heat of midday they assemble into temporary organs. Millions join edge to edge, forming translucent ribbons in the air. These ribbons turn broad faces toward the sun, gather energy, and then break apart before the evening shutters close.

Their dispersal is their reproduction. Every storm carries them into new districts. Most are sorted out by the world’s particle fields and fed into reclamation pores, but some have learned—or been designed—to present acceptable signatures. They pass inspection as sealant dust, optical residue, harmless carbon. The surface incorporates them. For a while they live inside the plate, following currents through hair-fine conductors. Later they emerge somewhere else, altered by what they have crossed.

There are lineages of grain lives that have been circulating through the world’s upper layers for millions of years. They have no continuous bodies, but they remember routes. Their memory is statistical: a preference in the angle of a hook, a charge retained slightly longer than chance, a tendency to assemble when a particular buried pulse arrives.

The black stalks feed on them, though feeding is too simple a word.

A mature stalk is less an individual than a standing agreement among roots, stem segments, mineral fans, and the active seam in which it grows. Its buried clamps tap electrical gradients and draw sulfur from ancient compounds. Its upper scales collect light. Between them runs a slow traffic of copper ions that carries both energy and information.

A grove can remember an obstruction for centuries. Light passed from stalk to stalk will bend around the remembered place even after the obstruction is gone. If one section is repeatedly cut back, new growth appears elsewhere along the buried conduit. No seed travels between the two. Instead, the grove induces the surface to move the necessary materials, requesting them in the antique signaling language of repair.

It is unclear whether the groves discovered this language or inherited it.

During seasons of high brightness, neighboring groves communicate by flashes too slight for ordinary sight. A fan turns. A reflected beam touches a sensor seam kilometers away. The seam opens, changing the local temperature; another grove answers by altering the flow of copper through its stems. Their exchanges may take days. A single statement, if statement it is, can require an entire summer.

The long-legged grazers live at a faster scale.

They are called **striders** by those who need a name for them, though their gait is more placement than stride. Each foot tests charge, heat, vibration, and permission before accepting weight. If the plate beneath it is scheduled to move, the foot withdraws. If the plate recognizes the animal as debris, the strider raises its body and waits until the sorting impulse passes.

Their skeletons are tension lattices of ceramic fiber. Silver skin stretches between the ribs and tightens in daylight, making the creatures look starved even when healthy. Along the spine lie folded vanes dark as graphite. At dusk these open to shed stored heat. In alarm they flare all at once, briefly doubling the animal’s apparent size and casting a pattern the surface sometimes interprets as an authorized maintenance marker.

A strider’s mouth is a rotating cluster of scrapers and electrostatic combs. It harvests charged grains from stalks without injuring the fans. In return, fragments caught in the combs are carried to other groves. Thus the animal grazes, pollinates, and transports code.

They do not give birth. Two or more striders approaching the end of their functional lives travel toward a quiet region of overlapping plates. There they kneel in a close arrangement and cease moving. The surface gradually disassembles them. Months or years later, smaller striders emerge from nearby hatches, built partly from the old bodies and partly from local stock.

The young are not copies. Their feet fit the district that made them. Their sensory reflexes contain fragments of the routes their predecessors walked. Whether this is reproduction carried out with the world’s assistance or routine manufacture contaminated by heredity makes no practical difference to them.

The ribbon creatures of the vents are more numerous. They are thin, flexible stacks of thermoelectric scales with mouths at both ends. Warmth passing along the body produces charge; charge flexes the scales; flexure carries the creature toward a new temperature difference. They migrate because remaining still would equalize them with their surroundings and leave them unable to move.

At dawn they descend. At evening they rise. The structure breathes them back and forth.

Among them wait the **seam mouths**, predators so perfectly adapted to patience that they resemble faults in the construction. A seam mouth may spend decades as a dark line between two plates. It feeds only when enough ribbons pass over it to justify closing. Then the line narrows by a fraction, and thousands vanish into layered digestive chambers. The predator uses their accumulated charge to grow a few millimeters farther along the joint.

Some seam mouths outlive the routes that nourish them. They remain in place, unfed but not dead, until the world’s slow rearrangements bring prey again. There are individuals whose last meal occurred before the present pattern of stars became visible.

Higher in the air are the **stillwings**.

They are immense, though distance reduces them to points. Their bodies consist of conductive loops surrounding a narrow central spine. They climb by exchanging charge with different atmospheric layers and drift where the electric tension can support them. Their motion is almost entirely vertical; the planet’s convection carries them sideways.

A stillwing may remain above one district for years. It feeds on airborne grain lives and on the minute electrical surplus of storms. From time to time it lowers a filament many kilometers long. The filament rarely reaches the ground. It hangs in the bright air, gathering dust or listening to emissions from the buried frame.

When a stillwing dies, it does not fall at once. It loses altitude over months, circling wider as its loops discharge. The surface notices its approach. Plates turn dark beneath it. Gates open. Whether the world receives the descending body as a returning component or disposes of it as contamination depends on the district.

There are also inhabitants that no observer has seen whole.

Deep below the Low Archive, light travels through threads in recurring sequences. Doors open ahead of those sequences and close behind them. Heat is borrowed from one chamber and repaid in another. Objects in sealed cells are rotated, inspected, and returned. The coordination moves through the layers like a creature moving through grass, but no central body accompanies it.

These presences are called **undertakings**.

An undertaking may occupy a thousand cubic kilometers, yet be unable to act outside a narrow chain of vaults. One tends pressure cells. Another follows structural strain across a continent. A third appears to do nothing except compare current shadows with shadows recorded in an age when towers still stood.

They compete indirectly. One diverts energy; another delays a shutter opening; a third classifies the delay as damage and routes material to correct it. Their conflicts manifest as new ridges, warm bands, exclusions, and migrations on the surface. A struggle between two undertakings may last ten thousand years and displace a coastline’s worth of ceramic without either ever touching the other.

They can fragment. A broken undertaking may continue as several smaller habits, each repeating part of the former purpose. They can also merge when old conduits are restored. In that sense they have births and deaths, though neither event occurs at a single place or time.

The rarest inhabitants are the ones most readily mistaken for people.

They cross the plain in groups of three or seven, never two. From far away they resemble narrow figures wrapped in pale cloth. Closer, the cloth proves to be a cloud of linked scales orbiting an open frame. Their limbs have too many joints, allowing them to keep their heads level while the surface shifts beneath them. Nothing wet is exposed. Their active chemistry is sealed within glassy capsules distributed throughout the torso, each smaller than a seed.

They are known as **the Attendants**, though nothing confirms that they attend anything.

They construct no permanent settlements. The world would dismantle or relocate them. Instead they inhabit schedules. At an appointed cooling cycle, a group gathers beside a thermal well. When the well closes, they walk with the departing heat toward another district. Their homes are recurring alignments of shutters, sheltering fins, and inactive plates. A house for them is not a structure but a place that becomes habitable every forty-three nights.

They speak through touch, reflected light, and induced vibration. Spoken air-sound is used only in dead zones where the surface cannot cancel it. Their conversations may include gestures made years apart. One Attendant will tilt a marker plate and depart; another, arriving decades later, reads the changed reflection and completes the reply.

They preserve little inside themselves. Memory carried in a body is vulnerable to wear, and wear is relentless. Instead, they hide recollection in acceptable changes: the spacing of grains in a repaired crack, the polarization of a stalk’s scales, the timing of a strider route. The world continually edits these records. Consequently, Attendant history is not a stable account of what occurred but a body of memories cunning enough to survive maintenance.

They do not know whether they descend from the makers, from maintenance devices, or from trespassers that became impossible to remove. Different groups claim each origin. Some believe the makers became the undertakings. Some believe the world itself is the only maker and that everything with a boundary is merely one of its temporary thoughts.

When an Attendant dies, the others do not guard the body. Guarding would cause the surface to classify them all as an obstruction. They remove only a few memory scales, then allow the sorting pores to open. The dead are separated into carbon, ceramic, metals, sealed reactive capsules, and unrecognized residue. Over subsequent years those materials may reappear in a young strider, a new stalk fan, a repaired plate, or another Attendant’s hand.

For this reason, they do not speak of the dead as absent. They say the dead have become **difficult to address**.

No species there is wholly independent of the construction. Nothing can be. The world supplies gradients, routes, sorting, shelter, threat, and the physical grammar by which one thing may become another. Every inhabitant exploits the machinery, and every inhabitant is exploited by it in return.

Together they form no lush web of abundance. Energy is plentiful, but usable matter is guarded, classified, and continuously reclaimed. Life survives through precision. It borrows without appearing to steal. It reproduces by persuading ancient systems that the new body is a repair, a permitted residue, or something that has always been there.

And perhaps, by now, that is true.
