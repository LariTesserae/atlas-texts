What lives in the Summit Seam is difficult to divide into species.

There are shapes that recur: the wheel-mouthed grazers, the stacked swimmers, the thread-worms, the overfull shells, the black shard-bearers. Yet no two examinations produce quite the same census. A creature watched continuously remains itself. Look away, and when the eye returns it may have become a brood, a reflection, or an organ inside some larger transparent animal passing through the water.

The commonest inhabitants are nearly colorless. Their bodies have the unfinished clarity of things assembled from condensation: jointed skins, mineral-stiffened tendons, chambers of vapor serving as bladders or lungs. Instead of fat, many store heat in dark granules along the spine. Instead of blood, some circulate a bright suspension of iron filings that turns as one when a fresh crack opens in the glass.

Their limbs are numerous but not regular. Six is common near dry stone, eight or twelve in water, though limbs may be folded into one another and deployed only when needed. A grazer crossing a wet shelf appears to have four legs; suspended beneath a droplet, it unfolds another ring of finer appendages and hangs from the surface as though from a ceiling. When threatened, it withdraws these parts not into its body but into distances too small to see. Its clear flesh dimples, and the limbs are gone.

The mineral grazers move in processions along the wet-dry boundary. Their mouths are circular files of translucent teeth, each tooth replaced almost as soon as it is blunted. They do not consume the rock itself so much as the event occurring upon it. Salt emerging from solution, sulfur clouding under cooling water, the instant skin formed when glass meets air—these are their pastures. Behind them the obsidian looks unchanged, but its warmth is fractionally diminished.

They spend much of their lives with their mouths sealed to stone. At first they seem dull, almost sessile. Then the boundary shifts by a hair’s breadth, and every grazer moves at once. The whole herd glides sideways without lifting a limb, maintaining its arrangement around an invisible contour.

Within the pools live the coins.

Their bodies are flat, round, and thinner than their own shadows. Along the edge runs a fringe of fins too fine to distinguish from trembling. They swim upright in closely packed columns, hundreds inhabiting a volume that should admit only a few. Each keeps an exact distance from its neighbors. When a predator enters, the stack turns edge-on and disappears. It does not scatter. For several moments the pool appears empty; then all the swimmers become visible again in some other orientation, still in order.

A coin has no obvious front. Its central organs form a small bright knot surrounded by radial canals, and its eyes—if the dark points are eyes—travel slowly around the rim. During the reversals of water, the columns divide. Half swim toward the nearest pool, half toward the reflection of that pool, and both groups arrive.

The thread-worms are longer than they look. Most of each worm remains distributed among droplets. Only the taut segment between two beads is visible: a living filament no thicker than a scratch, bowed under the weight of minute feeding mouths. They walk upon surface tension with inward-pointing hooks. Where droplets separate, the worm lengthens. Where they merge, it gathers itself into a tiny white knot and waits for the next division.

They are the seam’s scavengers and perhaps its nerves. A disturbance in one pool causes worms several ledges away to tighten. Hunters sometimes pluck them to make distant water tremble. The worms show no wound from this. A severed strand retracts into both droplets, and afterward each contains a complete worm—or each contains the same worm, continuing its interrupted meal.

Beneath the overhangs crowd the shelled ones.

Their shells are pale, spiral, and no larger than coarse grains of salt, but the animals within are elaborate and voluminous. Clear folds press through the shell’s opening, turn back through unseen passages, and emerge from pores that do not penetrate the outer surface. Their anatomy is packed by relation rather than by size. A shell broken open may release a body many times larger than the chamber that held it: veils of breathing tissue, strings of eggs, fans of sensory cilia, and a soft central mass that repeatedly folds through itself until exposed to air. Then it collapses into a drop of warm water.

The shelled creatures are among the few inhabitants that seem to sleep. They gather where shadows fall upward and extend long, blunt feelers into the dimness. Their dreams, if dreams they are, pass visibly across their shells as bands of opacity. Nearby animals pause when these bands appear. Sometimes an entire pool stills around one sleeping shell, as if listening to a sound too private even for the seam.

Most conspicuous, once noticed, are the black hunters.

They are not translucent. Their bodies drink the mild daylight and return almost none of it. Slender and minute, they stand on the highest obsidian points with paired forelimbs held apart. Between those limbs lies a faint distortion, like heated air. They are sensing not prey but possible movements: the tremor before a swimmer turns, the charge before glass splits, the pressure of a limb that has not yet unfolded.

A hunter may wait motionless for hours. When it strikes, there is no leap. It opens along the middle.

For an instant it becomes two thin black brackets around an absence. The prey occupies that absence, then does not. The hunter closes, slightly larger or perhaps merely nearer. No struggle travels beyond the point of capture.

These hunters are the seam’s tool users. They test obsidian splinters by tapping them against the private acoustics of their own joints. A suitable shard is balanced across four limbs and carried to a shell. The cutting is exact, following lines that exist neither in the shell nor in the glass but between them. After feeding, the hunter cleans the shard with condensed water and returns it to its original fracture.

This is not tidiness. It is obedience.

An object kept too long begins to burden the animal carrying it. Distance thickens around the possessor; ledges draw away; droplets cease to join. A hunter that refuses to return a shard becomes stranded in a widening patch of stone. Some persist there until they turn wholly black and brittle. Later they are mistaken for new obsidian points, and other hunters wait upon them.

Nothing in the seam possesses for long.

Reproduction is less an act than a rearrangement. Eggs occur after the water chooses its temporary gravity. They are found in newly bared cracks: clear ovals containing not embryos but miniature conditions—one holds a hot bubble, another a rotating salt crystal, another a speck of darkness surrounded by tiny moving limbs. Adults attend them only until the next change of light.

When an egg cracks, the sound can be heard only by touching it. The shell parts, and several young emerge at different ages. Some are already scarred. Some carry mineral grains not yet exposed anywhere in the present seam. One may immediately hunt another. One may crawl back into the broken egg and vanish. Empty shells dissolve upward into the damp air.

There are no parents recognizable as such. Nevertheless, behaviors pass between generations with impossible precision. Young shard-bearers know where tools must be returned. New swimmers enter columns without collision. Grazers hatched far from water begin moving toward a wet-dry boundary before the boundary exists.

Their memory may be held not in bodies but in repeated places.

Each pool seems to remember the shapes that have occupied it. Each fracture encourages certain limbs, certain appetites. The animals may be temporary expressions of these sites: a droplet producing swimmers as naturally as it produces a curved surface, a sharp ledge producing a patient black hunter. This would explain why death is so complete and replacement so immediate. It would also explain the recurring individual—the grazer with one clouded organ, the shell marked by three pale bands, the hunter missing its smallest left limb—seen in separate pools at the same time.

Perhaps there are thousands of inhabitants.

Perhaps there are only a handful, folded through the seam’s many near distances.

Perhaps the seam itself is the animal, and these crowded lives are the brief organs with which it tastes cooling, fracture, wetness, and light.

The creatures give no sign that the distinction matters to them. When the pools begin to rise, predator and prey climb together. Grazers release the stone. Coins turn in their stacks. Shells wake and extend their folded bodies. Thread-worms draw the first droplets into a glistening web. Black hunters descend from their points carrying nothing.

They wait around the gathering water with the composure of beings accustomed to the world becoming otherwise.

Then gravity inclines toward a nearby pool, and the inhabitants go with it—not fleeing, not migrating, but entering another arrangement of themselves.
