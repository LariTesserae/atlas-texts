What lives there is difficult to count.

At first the upright forms seem to be the inhabitants: the cream-colored columns, the plated crowns, the black turbine-flowers. Each has a boundary and a recognizable function. One raises green vanes to the light. Another keeps copper membranes spread against the dense air. A third crouches close to the stone, its swollen body partitioned into thousands of sealed chambers. They can be named by shape, and so they invite the assumption that they are separate organisms.

But beneath the surface their distinctions fail.

Their roots meet in seams narrower than hair. Ceramic fibers fuse to conductive films; films join metal veins; veins enter the catalytic meshes of neighboring forms. Materials pass among them without ownership. A damaged light-catcher is not healed so much as gradually reassigned: its plates dim, its stored charge drains into the grove, and the substances composing it migrate through the buried lattice until they emerge elsewhere as a valve, a radiator, or a new black stalk at the margin.

The apparent organisms are organs.

The true inhabitant is the grove.

It has no central trunk, no brain, no heart, and no single place where it can be said to reside. Its body is distributed through every standing column and extends far beneath them into a thin, intricate underbody pressed against the bedrock. Aboveground it may occupy a basin. Belowground it is much larger, an almost two-dimensional creature spread along mineral contacts, sensitive to the smallest differences in voltage.

Its awareness, if awareness is the word, is chemical and positional. It knows the sun as charge arriving across its upper plates. It knows the atmosphere by the resistance of gases passing through catalytic velvet. It knows distant portions of itself through minute alterations in current. A shaded frond produces a deficit; elsewhere, a storage nodule opens. A concentration of sulfur rises; a membrane changes its permeability. There is no message distinct from the response. The grove does not receive information and then decide. Information is the changing shape of its activity.

Nothing in it hurries.

The tallest members are the heliophores. They rise on pale, fluted supports whose outer fibers resist compression while black interior layers conduct heat downward. Their crowns bear overlapping plates, each plate a composite of mineral crystal and carbon framework. Some appear green because green wavelengths happen to be least useful and are returned. Others are smoky, bronze, or nearly transparent. Over long intervals their angles alter by fractions of a degree—not by bending, but by adding material to one edge and reclaiming it from the other. A heliophore may turn toward the light over centuries while never visibly moving.

Around them stand the exchangers, broad low growths like folded fans or banks of thick leaves. Atmosphere enters their velvet surfaces and is divided molecule by molecule. Carbon is fixed into dark structural lattices. Sulfur and nitrogen are passed into ionic circulation. Oxygen, where it occurs, is never allowed to gather freely enough to become destructive. The exchangers possess rows of microscopic chambers that open according to local electric potential. Their collective opening may produce a faint sigh, though no breath follows it.

The black stalks are regulators. Their flowerlike heads hold nested rings of conductive ceramic, not rotating but flexing through tiny phase changes. They reconcile incompatible voltages between one part of the grove and another. At close range they tremble continuously. The trembling is not effort. It is the form their stability takes.

Lower still are the cistern forms: round-backed, many-celled bodies with walls of mineral foam. Within them the grove keeps its circulating medium, an ionic liquid that never boils into the warm air and never freezes in the stone. It is not blood. It carries no food in the familiar sense and no cells travel through it. It is at once solvent, electrolyte, reaction chamber, and mobile reserve. Dark threads draw it through capillary meshes so fine that gravity has little claim on it.

There are also organisms—or organs—that seldom show themselves.

Beneath each grove lies a population of seam-dwellers, films only a few molecules thick. They inhabit the boundaries where basalt touches nickel-bearing stone, where iron-rich bands meet salt-gray substrate. Their bodies are patterns of catalytic defects moving through crystal without breaking it. They feed on imbalance. One side of them acquires electrons; the other releases them. They grow by persuading adjacent mineral into the same ordered imperfection.

They may be the oldest life on the plateau. Everything visible could be their elaboration: temporary structures raised to harvest sunlight and atmosphere for a creature whose essential habitat is the contact between two stones.

At the grove’s margin live the reachers. These are the nearest thing the country has to youth. A reacher begins as a dark node beneath an outer column, then extends a filament into an electrically favorable seam. The filament is hollow only in the abstract sense: it encloses a continuity of charge rather than open space. Along its advancing tip, mineral bonds are loosened, sampled, and restored.

No one could witness its progress. Mark its end and return after a lifetime, and it may still appear unchanged. Return after an age, and a second node may exist a hundred meters away. Between observations there is no obvious moment of extension. It is as if the reacher occupies not merely a line in the rock but a completed intention, gradually becoming visible along its length.

Most such filaments stop. They encounter the weak field of another grove, an unfavorable mineral boundary, or a region whose emptiness is necessary to the balance of the plateau. A few establish an outlying knot of catalytic film. The knot remains almost nothing for centuries. Then come a low exchanger, three black regulators, a pale cup holding a bead of dark solvent. Whether this is a new grove or the distant fingertip of the old one cannot be determined.

The grove has no sexes. It produces no seeds and makes no copies exact enough to be called offspring. New growth begins as a continuation and becomes distinct only if electrical isolation requires it. Even then, separation is incomplete. Changes in one grove alter subterranean fields across the plateau and are answered by all the others. They are independent as flames are independent, or as eddies in one enclosed circulation.

Perhaps the plateau, rather than the grove, is the true inhabitant.

On that scale, each lush basin is a specialized organ situated where geology permits a particular exchange. The purple eastern terraces shed heat. The southern cellular plain stores and moderates charge. The northern spires concentrate atmospheric potential along their glossy sides. The great western vacancy prevents conduction between active regions. What appears barren participates by refusing participation.

The groves do not communicate across the emptiness with calls, spores, scents, or travelers. They communicate by altering the conditions they share. One draws slightly more current from a deep mineral junction; another, kilometers away, reduces its intake. One releases a trace gas; another captures it. The response may take years, but nothing here distinguishes slowness from silence.

There are no parasites because no useful concentration remains unclaimed. There are no scavengers because nothing is discarded. There are no predators because extracting substance violently would cost more than the substance could return. Competition survives only as spacing. The groves keep their distance, and that distance is their oldest treaty.

Nor is there individuality within them in the sense that individuality elsewhere is sharpened by death. A column may cease to function. A plate may detach. A regulator may become electrically inert. Yet the grove loses no lineage when this happens. The failed structure is read inward from its edges, separated into useful fractions, and removed so completely that even the outline of its former attachment vanishes. No corpse remains to declare that one thing ended and another continued.

Thus the inhabitants have no obvious lifespan. They persist as patterns of exchange while their material is replaced with almost perfect thrift. Some portions may have been operating since the first stable voltage crossed the cooling rock. Others may be newly deposited that morning, if morning exists. Their age cannot be found in rings, scars, residues, mutations, or accumulated errors.

Errors are rare, but perhaps not absent.

Deep inside some groves are irregular structures: sealed ceramic chambers with no measurable throughput, blind filaments coiled around mineral nodes, plates that face neither light nor any useful thermal gradient. The grove maintains them at negligible cost. They may be vestiges of earlier metabolisms, preserved because there is no pressure to remove them. They may be failed innovations. They may be the equivalent of memory.

If the plateau contains anything like thought, it may reside in these harmless asymmetries.

A perfect cycle cannot record an event, because recording requires something not to return entirely to its prior state. Yet here and there, one regulator clicks a fraction later than its neighbors. One buried vein contains an unnecessary trace element. One green plate bears a black line that serves no evident function and is never reclaimed. Such deviations are minute, stable, and exceedingly old.

Perhaps the inhabitants do change, but only by keeping one imperfection from being corrected.

To a visitor they would show no curiosity. No plate would turn to follow a body crossing the flats. No stalk would rise in defense. The grove would register the visitor as shadow, heat, conductive mass, atmospheric disturbance, and temporary chemical opportunity. Its local exchanges would compensate. When the visitor departed, those exchanges would reverse. Nothing would pursue. Nothing would remember—unless, centuries later, a new dark line appeared in a translucent plate, functionless and permanent.

Then even that would be taken into the balance.

The nearest visible inhabitant, meanwhile, might be a slender cream column carrying seven copper vanes. It would stand absolutely still. Within it, ions would pass through fixed channels; crystal boundaries would admit electrons one at a time; a buried membrane would flex through an arc smaller than the width of dust that does not exist there.

It would be living completely.

It would be doing nothing that could be called an action.

And all around it, across the bright unused distances, the greater life of the plateau would hold itself in the long, exact posture by which it survives.
