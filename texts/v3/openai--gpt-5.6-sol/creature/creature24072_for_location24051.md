The Fixed Foundry appears to contain thousands of animals. It may contain no individuals at all.

The smallest are the first to suggest this.

They are called **tickers**, for the sound their feet make on steel. Each is no longer than a fingernail, with a copper shell, twelve hooked legs, and a head divided into two wedge-shaped mandibles. They crowd bearings and valve stems, scraping away oxide too fine to see. Some carry grains of ceramic in baskets beneath their abdomens. Others press those grains into pits that appear flawless before and after the work.

A ticker can be crushed. Its shell splits, its legs fold inward, and a puff of sweet-smelling powder escapes. At the same instant, somewhere else in the chamber, another ticker unfolds from beneath a casing. It bears the same discoloration on its shell and the same missing tip on its seventh leg.

Whether the second animal is a replacement, a duplicate, or the same creature arriving by a route unavailable to sight cannot be determined.

Above them stand the **vapor fans**: pale, stilt-legged things surrounding every exhaust port. Their bodies are narrow ceramic bladders held high on four or six legs. They have no recognizable faces. When a piston strikes, a seam along the front of each body opens into a pleated metallic fan. The fan catches ionized vapor, folds it inward, and closes.

They always feed together. Not merely at the same beat, but in arrangements repeated from vent to vent: five open, three remain shut, one turns slightly toward the glass engine. The pattern migrates through the chamber without changing shape, as if it were a single gesture passing from body to body.

The ceiling bells are less animal in appearance. They hang in dense inverted orchards from transparent tendons, each bell containing a knot of dark granules that leap with the machinery’s vibration. They filter charged dust and produce the faint sweetness in the air. Occasionally one splits down its side and spills a stream of violet mites. Elsewhere, at that exact instant, a swarm of mites vanishes into an intact bell.

The bells are both hives and mouths. The mites are both their offspring and their organs.

The larger grazers are known as **kilnhounds**, although they are not much like hounds beyond their size and their habit of traveling in lanes. They are built low, with shoulders wider than their hips and ceramic scales overlapping from nose to tail. Their lidless eyes are black mineral lenses. Behind the lenses turn slow rings of reflective tissue, giving the impression that something is winding deeper into the skull.

A kilnhound eats by lowering its whole head over a slag heap. Within its mouth, three toothed wheels counter-rotate, reducing sulfurous stone and metal waste to powder. Internal presses compact the meal into hot, angular pellets, which emerge linked in chains.

Tickers dismantle the chains and carry each pellet away.

Kilnhounds display scars, broken scales, worn teeth, and healed punctures, but none can be watched acquiring them. A newly hatched animal may step from a ribbed egg already bearing an old fracture across its back. An adult seized by a predator may leave behind an egg whose shell carries the same fracture in miniature.

Their eggs are not laid. They are found at the end of movements: behind a beast that has just turned a corner, beneath a foot lifted from the dust, inside a pellet chain opened by the tickers. Examination shows an entire folded kilnhound within, fully scaled and hot. When the shell opens, the creature does not grow. It merely expands into the posture it already possessed.

The predators are called **seams**.

At rest, a seam is almost perfectly flat. Its limbs divide and divide again until their tips become black lines laid into the salt wall. A long central jaw resembles the fresh edge of a fault. It has no visible sensory organs. Yet it always knows which grazer will pass nearest and exactly when the compressor will strike.

At the concussion, the seam comes away from the wall.

For a fraction of a second it is a tall web of mineral joints surrounding a vertical mouth. Then the impact ends, the predator folds back into the stone, and some portion of the prey is gone. There is little blood. Kilnhounds contain only dry lubricants, powdery salts, and bundles of conductive thread.

Seams do not eat because they are hungry. Their attacks occur with the precision of valve operations. If prevented from taking one animal, another seam elsewhere strikes twice. If both attacks are prevented, the compressor produces one anomalously heavy blow, and something beneath its sealed housing gives a brief grinding cry.

This has led to the belief that the seams are not predators at all. They may be external jaws belonging to the machinery.

The metallic fans growing in mineral cracks complicate the distinction further. Their roots bridge electrically opposed stones; their leaves turn by fractions whenever animals pass; their coil-seeds lodge preferentially in places where a ticker will later place a pellet. Cut from the wall, a fan remains bright and rigid. Another fan closes elsewhere. If the severed plant is removed from the chamber, its leaves slowly blacken—but the plant left behind in the Foundry opens again before the removed specimen has finished dying.

Taken separately, these creatures form an ecosystem. Taken together, they behave like a calculation.

Movements cancel. Feeding and predation balance. Every opening is answered by a closure, every transit by an opposite transit. The life of the chamber is not preserved by reproduction but by equivalence.

Because of this, some explorers argue that the true inhabitant of the Fixed Foundry is the **Counterworker**.

No complete Counterworker has ever been seen.

It first appears as coincidences around the control booth. A kilnhound pauses with its forelegs arranged like boots beneath the hanging coat. Tickers gather inside the sleeves and make the cloth bend at the elbows. Ceiling bells lower on their tendons behind the opaque window, forming the vague height and breadth of a head. Dust thickens where hands might be.

Never all at once. Never enough to make a person.

Then the chair creaks.

One ivory switch strains upward while another strains down. The dead intercom warms. Among the uninterrupted thunder of the flywheels, pressure changes can be felt inside the teeth and jaw. These changes are paired so that the noise itself remains constant, yet the listener begins to distinguish words.

Not heard words. Compensated words.

**SHIFT UNFINISHED.**

**COUNT NOT CLOSED.**

**REMAIN AT STATION.**

The Counterworker may be the surviving operator of the Foundry, dispersed across its animals when the governing instant became fixed. It may be the factory’s control process, forced to use living bodies after its cables disappeared. It may be nothing more than the human tendency to find intention in repeated labor.

The coat offers no answer. When its sleeves are emptied, they later contain dust again. The shift token cannot be removed without another token appearing in some pocket, burrow, egg, or sealed machine housing. The booth’s two chairs suggest two operators, but only one coat remains.

The second chair is always slightly warmer.

Foreign life troubles the chamber because it brings unbalanced events. A visitor sweats, exhales moisture, breaks a crust, shifts a pellet, loses a hair. These are consequences the Foundry has not already canceled. Tickers gather around footprints. Vapor fans turn from their vents. Kilnhounds cease grazing and orient their black eyes toward the disturbance. Even the seams alter their fault-line bodies to point toward it.

They do not attack immediately.

They measure.

Pellets are stacked into the approximate volume of what entered. Metallic plants unwind their roots and weave conductive outlines. Violet mites trace the heat of limbs. In sheltered spaces, ribbed shells begin to appear—first small, then larger, each shaped less like an egg than a mold.

The Counterworker is attempting to balance the visitor.

If given enough time, the chamber constructs an answering form from ceramic, salt, iron, and dry tissue. It is not an exact duplicate. Its joints bend in opposite directions. Marks on its body appear on the wrong side. It inhales when the visitor exhales and steps backward when the visitor steps forward. Its face, if uncovered, remains unfinished: a shallow plane crowded with minute moving parts.

So long as the visitor remains, this counterpart remains incomplete.

If the visitor leaves, something must stay behind to close the count.

Perhaps that is who wears the coat. Perhaps the old builders did not vanish and were not destroyed. Perhaps they were divided among tickers, bells, hounds, seams, plants, and engines until every action had an answering action and every life was made incapable of becoming a past.

The Fixed Foundry is crowded, but it is lonely in a precise way.

Everything in it may be one worker, still performing the first instant of a shift that cannot begin until it ends.
