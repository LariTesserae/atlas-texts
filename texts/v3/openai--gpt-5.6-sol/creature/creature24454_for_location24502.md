At first it is difficult to identify the inhabitants.

The ferry beasts are clearly alive, but so are the ferries growing on their backs. The towers respond to injury, anticipate weather, and revise their own designs. Canals remember traffic. Market stalls ripen only when approached by someone who needs what they bear. A person may stand motionless beside a wall for an hour while the wall changes color around their hand, and it is not obvious which of them is speaking.

The city recognizes citizenship less by species than by participation. Anything that carries water, warnings, pollen, memory, or repair from one part of the basin to another is, in some degree, a resident.

Among these residents are people.

They are not numerous. From the rim one might watch for half a day before noticing any, because their lives are distributed through the greenery rather than imposed upon it. They seldom gather in dense crowds unless a task requires many separate bodies. Most are slender, long-limbed beings with broad feet and flexible toes suited to mud, root bridges, and the yielding backs of ferry animals. Their skin comes in umbers, gray-greens, deep blue-black, and the pallor of submerged wood. A fine nap covers much of it, directing condensation into channels along the ribs and limbs. In humid weather they glitter continuously.

Their resemblance to one another ends at a distance.

Some carry leaflike fans along the spine, inhabited by bacteria that cool the blood. Others have clear secondary eyelids for moving through heavy rain. There are people whose fingers divide into delicate root-colored filaments, people with throat chambers that can answer the city’s booming trunks, and people whose ears host small pollinating insects in warm folds. These features are not fixed lineages. They are agreements—made with symbionts, inherited for a few generations, then relinquished when a district, profession, or climate no longer needs them.

Clothing is alive and usually asleep.

A common garment resembles a drape of dark, wet petals until danger comes, when it contracts into a tough skin and spreads flotation bladders along the chest. Work garments grow pockets in response to repeated gestures. Ceremonial mantles release remembered scents: the rain of a person’s childhood, the resin of a vanished tower, the mineral smell of a canal from another possible history. No garment belongs entirely to its wearer. It must be fed, allowed to reproduce, and occasionally returned to a communal wardrobe grove, where it exchanges habits with others of its kind.

The people do not name themselves once.

A child first receives a water-name, a short pattern tapped into a basin or sung through a reed. This name describes where their body entered the circulation of the city. Later comes a chosen name, often translated as an action rather than a noun: *Waits Beneath Leaves*, *Turns the Fever Aside*, *Carries News Against the Current*. In adulthood, many acquire a root-name known only to the city and to those with whom they share memory. Root-names are not spoken. They are patterns of heat, salts, electrical rhythm, and expectation.

Because distance behaves badly in the basin, identity does too. A person may sometimes meet another version of themselves.

Such encounters are treated with courtesy and caution. The two are offered separate water. They are not permitted to sleep under the same roof leaf, and for three days neither may make decisions involving children, structural growth, or the Last Slope. Usually one version fades back into its neighboring history. Occasionally both remain. They then become siblings, regardless of age, and must choose new names. It is considered cruel to ask which is the original.

Children are raised by shifting households called nests. A nest may include several adults, a chamber-tree, one or two ferry beasts, an archive of ancestral microbes, and whatever animals have learned the household’s signals. Parenthood is understood as a form of sustained attention rather than ownership. The one who contributed a body, the one who taught speech, the one whose memories stabilized a fever, and the tower that grew the child’s first room may all be addressed by the same kinship term.

Young children spend much of their time nearly naked in shallow water. They learn currents before roads, scents before written marks, and the temperaments of structures before abstract geometry. One early lesson is how to ask a door whether it wishes to open. Another is how to distinguish an empty clearing from an abandoned one. They are taught never to fill silence merely because it is uncomfortable, never to dam moving water without first tasting where it has been, and never to follow a familiar adult whose shadow falls in the wrong historical direction.

There are specialists, though professions are temporary.

Listeners press their bodies to root junctions and interpret the city’s distributed deliberations. They return from this work streaked with sap, speaking slowly, sometimes using plural pronouns for days afterward.

Weather shepherds climb signal masts and release engineered pollen into forming pressure systems. They cannot prevent storms, nor would they want to. Their work is to persuade rain to arrive in a shape the basin can use.

Outcome gardeners tend small plots where neighboring possibilities are allowed to germinate. They cultivate cures, resilient structures, forgotten flavors, and occasionally solutions to questions no one has yet asked. Their gardens look sparse: a few labeled pools, isolated plants, nets holding empty air. The danger lies not in what grows but in what almost grows. Gardeners are recognizable by the asymmetric scars left when an unrealized injury brushes against the body.

There are also eaters of the third rain. They arrive wherever dead tissue has not been transformed in time. Their masks are colonies of pale mouths, their baskets warm digestive chambers. They consume rot, failed architecture, shed garments, and sometimes memories too painful to preserve in conscious form. Nothing they process is simply destroyed. It returns as soil, medicine, scent, larval food, or a cautionary pattern in the roots.

The city’s most respected inhabitants may be the emptiness keepers.

They maintain the clearings.

They cut back eager growth, redirect roots, and prevent the city’s abundance from answering every vacancy too soon. Each keeper is responsible for places whose purpose remains unknown: a circle of bare mud where birds refuse to land, a meadow that appears only after upward rain, a room that a tower continually grows and then leaves unopened. Their duty can last generations. Many die without learning what they protected.

They are not considered failures.

The Last Slope has its own attendants, although they do not call themselves priests. They wear no living garments and carry no tools that borrow from adjacent histories. Their bodies are painted with ordinary clay from outside the basin, a substance valued there because it has only one present form.

They watch the low plant at the center.

No one touches it. No one drinks from the black water. When one of its widely spaced leaves yellows, the attendants gather along the edge of the empty zone and recite events that did not happen: storms that turned aside, children who were never born, districts considered but not grown, wars avoided so completely that only the city remembers them. These unrealized histories are given acknowledgment, then allowed to subside.

The people fear the Last Slope, but not as they fear a predator. They fear it as one fears an ancestor whose smallest gesture has become the foundation of one’s life.

Death in the basin is a negotiation.

A dying person decides which aspects of themselves may continue. Bodily tissue is usually divided among a household tree, scavenger gardens, and the larvae of memory-bearing moths. Skills may be impressed into tools or taught to healing plants. Sensory memories can be stored in fruit: bite into one and briefly know the pressure of a vanished hand, the color of a particular dawn, the exact unease felt before an old flood.

Whole personalities are rarely preserved. The inhabitants regard perfect continuation as a kind of blockage. A memory that cannot change is dead material, however vividly it speaks, and dead material must be eaten, remembered, or transformed before the third rain.

Some refuse all inheritance and ask to pass wholly into water. Their bodies are opened in a quiet pool, and the city’s smallest organisms take them apart. For several days afterward, doors may respond to their scent. Ferry beasts may pause at their old stops. A room might warm as if expecting them. Then these recognitions fade into the general intelligence of the basin.

Not every resident is gentle.

There are hunters who live near the designated gardens, their senses shaped to narrow upon a single heartbeat. They move with alarming stillness and wear symbionts that suppress their scent. They kill for food, ecological correction, and ritual obligation. Yet outside the hunting grounds many become overwhelmed by the unfiltered abundance of nearby life. A hunter capable of bringing down a long-legged grazer may weep helplessly in a crowded nursery pool, hearing the pulses of fish, children, roots, insects, and chambers all around them.

Predators of other species learn the city’s boundaries more simply. Some cooperate. Sleek, six-limbed carnivores patrol the outer reed beds in exchange for access to hunting gardens. Small aerial hunters nest in people’s hair and take biting flies. Pale canal serpents remove diseased fish but must be sung away from spawning pools.

Then there are residents no one has classified.

A ripple sometimes travels against the current for weeks, pausing at doors and markets as though conducting errands. A certain grove speaks only through frogs, shaping their calls into warnings about structural failures that have not yet occurred. One ferry beast has followed the same route for longer than any canal has remained in place; when no waterway connects its remembered stops, rain gathers beneath its feet and makes one.

Deep in the vascular channels, ribbon-bodied couriers occasionally arrive bearing spores from towers that do not exist. The city always accepts them.

At night, when the basin begins its storm rearrangement, the people do not retreat from the city so much as become more deeply included in it. They fasten themselves into sleeping chambers, climb onto ferry backs, or wade into the warm streets carrying bundles of larvae and seed. Some sing pressure changes to the towers. Some guide frightened animals toward the correct scents. Some merely stand in the rain with their faces lifted, allowing garments, skin microbes, household plants, and memory moths to drink.

By morning, a person may discover that their home has become a garden, their workplace has moved across the basin, and an unfamiliar sibling remembers sharing their childhood.

They complain. They grieve lost rooms. They argue with bridges and curse uncooperative weather. They become attached to particular views even while insisting that nothing must remain unchanged. They are not serene embodiments of balance. They are residents of a city whose stability consists of surviving transformation, and they must repeatedly learn the difference between continuity and sameness.

What lives there, then, is not one species or society.

It is a citizenship of exchanges: people, towers, ferry beasts, garments, predators, microbes, remembered dead, unrealized siblings, and the warm sideways-moving water that passes through them all.

Each belongs only partly to itself.

That partial belonging is what the city recognizes as life.
