The inhabitants are easiest to see when something needs tending.

In dry weather they scarcely qualify as figures. They are variations in the city’s surfaces: a vertical darkening under an arch, a fold in the moss lining a bed, several slate-colored stems growing too close together. One may pass within arm’s reach of such a presence and notice only that the wall is faintly warm, or that condensation has gathered in the shape of a hand.

Rain gives them depth.

As the first drops strike the plateau, upright forms begin to separate from pillars, garden margins, and shallow carvings. They do not emerge through openings. Rather, a surface that had seemed complete reveals that part of its completeness was a person standing perfectly aligned with it. Shoulders detach from lintels. Long heads lift from among slate fins. Limbs unfold where braided roots had crossed a courtyard wall.

At first they appear robed. The robes are narrow, layered, and stiffened by mineral dust, falling in straight lines that make the wearer resemble a standing crack in stone. Closer inspection never establishes where clothing ends. The outer layers may be woven from silver grass and strips of softened bark, but moss grows through them without seams. Black leaves lie flat along the chest and reverse in response to weather. Pale ceramic plates protect the throat, temples, and joints, though some plates bear buds or rootlets on their inner faces.

Their bodies have no constant proportion. Most are taller than the doors they use, yet pass through without stooping. At a distance their arms seem to reach almost to the ground; nearby, those same arms may be ordinary until extended toward a tool, when an extra length becomes available between one moment and the next. They are extremely thin, less from hunger than from an unwillingness to displace much air. Viewed from the side, some nearly vanish.

Their faces are shallow and calm. Features look pressed rather than grown: a ridge where a nose might be, closed seams for mouths, dark depressions holding neither shine nor visible eye. Moisture gathers in these depressions. When they turn their attention upon something, the droplets tremble or run outward against gravity.

No two witnesses agree on the number of their fingers. Their hands often resemble fans of root or folded fern. The palms open inward, into narrow cavities in the wrists. This is why the city’s cups have handles inside: the hand enters the vessel, closes around the hidden grip, and lifts the cup from within. Tools are held in the same manner. A bronze blade may disappear halfway into a keeper’s palm, leaving only its cutting edge visible.

They move without the small corrections common to animals. There is no shifting of weight, no balancing motion, no breath disturbing the shoulders. A keeper stands, and then stands elsewhere, the intervening posture supplied only if someone watches continuously. Even then, movement is uncertain. The paving may be what passes beneath it. The building may shorten the distance out of courtesy.

They rarely travel alone, but neither do they gather in crowds. During rain, groups appear along aqueducts and cistern courts, spaced at exact intervals. One clears moss from a groove. Another lifts a swollen wooden gate. Others stand knee-deep in the temporary reservoirs, drawing reeds through the water to remove dust. They work with enormous patience and no visible command. A task begun by a figure in one district may be completed by another several miles away, as though the action itself had traveled underground.

It is difficult to say whether these are different inhabitants.

Individuality among them seems to attach not to a body but to a sequence of duties. The one who clears the red cistern may later be found covering a well beyond the quartz road, though the two forms bear no resemblance. Conversely, a single tall figure has been observed working simultaneously in several courtyards, each instance making a different gesture. When the work is finished, all may flatten into walls except one. That survivor carries the accumulated tools away.

They do not speak into the air. Communication passes through materials. A hand laid upon bedrock produces a low note that can be felt hours later in another district. Condensation is tapped through ceramic channels in intricate rhythms. Leaves are turned silver-side upward in ordered patches. Doors are opened not to admit someone, but to make a sentence of light across a plaza.

When they must address a traveler, they arrange circumstances around them. Frost appears on a safe stair. A warm draft issues from the wrong doorway. Three bowls of water are placed along a route, the third covered with a stone lid. Such messages are precise to those who live there and almost impossible to translate. They contain direction, warning, weather, and tense at once.

Their sense of time is practical rather than chronological. A keeper may postpone a gesture under an eave and retrieve it generations later. Some are followed by brief climates: a figure carrying stored morning has pale light beneath its sleeves; one returning from a sealed chamber trails the cold of an extinct winter. The oldest appear not worn but seasonally confused. Frost forms on one side of them while fern spores ripen on the other.

They eat, if the word applies, by kneeling beside the dense gardens and placing their hands beneath the moss. Nothing is plucked. Instead the surrounding plants dim slightly, and the keeper’s robes grow more green. At other times they stand in shallow pools until the water level falls by the width of a thread. A dark residue remains, arranged in delicate branching patterns like the shadow of roots.

They keep no livestock and hunt nothing. The idea of an animal seems absent from their tools and ornament. There are no hooks intended for flesh, no barriers designed against teeth, no images of wings or horns. Their few figurative carvings depict empty garments, vessels, weather fronts, and arrangements of hands. Perhaps they never knew a world in which life ran, cried out, or consumed other life bodily.

Their children, if they have children, are not miniature adults. In sheltered courts one sometimes finds a new relief forming on a blank wall: first a darker oval, then the suggestion of folded limbs, then ceramic fragments placed nearby as if awaiting incorporation. For years the shape remains shallow. Moss is trimmed around it. Condensation is directed across its face. Eventually, during a suitable rain, the wall is found empty and a narrow figure is seen walking away.

Other young forms grow in gardens. Certain mirrored flowers produce not merely shoots but small upright likenesses enclosed in translucent tissue. The gardeners bend neighboring stems over them and leave tools beside their roots. Most harden into plants or pillars. A few disappear between one rainfall and the next. Afterward, unfamiliar hands begin opening the cisterns.

There is no clear death among them. A keeper may slow over centuries, remaining longer after each task and becoming harder to distinguish from the place where it rests. Slate gathers along its back. Its feet braid into the pavement. Black leaves cover its face. Eventually it is called a tree, a column, or a stain beneath an arch, though unseen workers continue to leave bowls of water near it.

Some of the oldest structures may be such inhabitants. Their chambers warm and cool with long rhythms. Their doors close before frost. Roots pass through them in patterns resembling veins, and condensation collects in interior hollows too regular to be accidental. A district may therefore be both dwelling and dweller: an elder spread beyond upright shape, receiving rain across acres of roof and speaking through the settling of slabs.

This possibility changes the appearance of the whole city. The broad streets begin to resemble spaces left between organs. Aqueducts seem less like public works than vessels. The isolated houses, each surrounded by an extravagant emptiness, may not have been built for privacy but for room to extend. The immense plazas might be pauses in a thought too large to occur quickly.

Yet there are moments of recognizable tenderness.

A keeper will turn a frost-split seed so its geometric seam faces the sun. Two figures will stand together beneath a condensation roof, touching wrists while water ticks around them. A smaller presence, still half relief, may be carried from a crumbling wall and pressed carefully into newer stone. Along communal tables they set out bowls for absent companions—some expected tomorrow, some in a century, some already present as the warmth trapped inside a jar.

They are not ghosts. They maintain too much, alter too much, and possess too patient an interest in what comes next. Nor are they simply plants, though they root, bud, and return to stillness. They are what life becomes in a place where location, body, and time have all been weathered smooth: persons only intermittently separate from their surroundings.

When the rain ends, they finish quickly. Gates are lowered. Jars are sealed. Reeds are stacked beside the cisterns. One by one the inhabitants return—not necessarily to homes, but to surfaces. A figure steps against a wall and becomes its discoloration. Another kneels in a garden and closes into a black-leaved shrub. A crew along an aqueduct grows thin, rigid, and wooden until only a row of narrow trees remains.

The last keeper is often seen in a plaza after dusk, placing lids over bowls of fresh water. It appears very far away. Then it appears near enough that frost can be heard forming on its sleeves.

It does not look toward the traveler. It lays one long hand against the pavement and waits until a deep note passes into the stone.

Across the city, doors close.

Whether this is a farewell, a warning, or merely the city acknowledging itself cannot be known.
