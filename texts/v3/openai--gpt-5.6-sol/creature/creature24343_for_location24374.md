The oldest inhabitant is the country itself.

Whether it is one organism cannot be decided by cutting it apart. A severed tract continues to pump, breathe, regulate its temperature, and answer the wind. Given centuries, it may grow toward its former neighbors and fuse with them again. Yet neighboring provinces do not always cooperate. They cast shade across one another, divert charged gases, exude incompatible films, and sometimes close their vascular borders so completely that the tissue between them starves and becomes a valley. Beneath the armored plain, graft lines intersect with invasion fronts and ancient scars. It is less a single being than a civilization without individuals: innumerable lineages joined until ancestry and anatomy have become the same thing.

Its living depths are warmer than the surface. There, below kilometers of compressed rind, slow organs turn in chambers full of dense solvent vapor. Muscular conduits carry fluorinated sap under pressures that would split an exposed animal. Root-furnaces pulse only a few times in a day. Reservoirs inflate and subside over decades. Some processes are so slow that a valve may spend the lifetime of a grazer opening.

The hard plains are not dead tissue. They are watchful skin. Each ridge bears fields of microscopic shutters that measure light, static, vibration, and chemical drift. When something walks across them, the response may arrive far away: a tower alters its pitch, a seam warms, a row of nutrient blisters ripens beneath the traveler’s path. No central organ appears to command these changes. Signals propagate through pressure waves and electrical branching, overlapping, fading, and returning altered. The plateau does not think quickly, if it thinks at all. It notices in the manner of weather and remembers in the manner of geology.

Its upright forms are temporary organs.

The black towers begin as buried knots of wound fiber. For years they accumulate gases and wind charge. Then the rind splits, and the young tower rises in telescoping sections, glossy and soft at first, hardening in the cold air. Mature towers breathe through long vertical mouths. Their inner membranes are stacked in ranks: pale, muscular sails that snap back and forth under the pressure of the gale. Colonies of catalytic cells line the chambers, sorting the atmosphere molecule by molecule. The waste gases leave through crown vents as amber streamers.

A tower’s bass note is partly metabolism and partly declaration. It tells the surrounding rind how hard the wind is blowing, how much charge has accumulated, whether the trunk is injured, whether its buried reservoirs are full. Nearby towers adjust in response. Across a province, their voices descend before a storm and rise after it, not in perfect unison but in long, shuddering chords.

The vane groves are more delicate. Each mirrored blade is a living laminate, transparent at the edge and dark with heat-bearing veins near the root. They track the sun, but also one another. A blade that directs too much glare onto a neighbor receives a flash in return and turns away. Thus whole forests negotiate the distribution of light through chains of reflection. From above, their movements resemble scales rolling along the back of an immense pale animal.

When a vane dies, it does not fall immediately. Its bearing locks, and the rigid leaf continues to whistle for years while dust scours away its reflective layers. Small animals know these dead notes. They nest in the lee of them, safe from sudden rotation.

The plateau’s mobile inhabitants are creatures of seals, shells, and internal pressure.

The great grazers are built close to the ground. Their bodies are long, often longer than a tower is wide, but rarely higher than a standing animal’s knee-joint. Six-legged species move with a steady alternating gait; eight-legged species can lock half their limbs and feed while the others brace against the wind. Their shells are pale mineral composites laid down in overlapping growth bands. Old individuals become mirror-smooth on the windward side and deeply fluted behind.

Nothing on them opens casually. Their eyes, where present, lie beneath clear pressure windows. Their breathing pores are labyrinths with muscular valves. The mouth is a circular crushing chamber bordered by black ceramic plates. To feed, a grazer clamps this chamber over a sap blister, isolates it from the atmosphere, and ruptures the blister inward. The released solvent and soft tissue are ground together, filtered, and transferred into sealed digestive sacs. Only then does the mouth detach.

They do not travel toward water, shade, or greenery. They follow pressure and rhythm. Sensitive pads beneath their feet read the buried vascular system, distinguishing a rich feeding line from a poisoned one by the timing of its contractions. Herds may stop simultaneously, each animal lifting its forelimbs as a deep pulse passes below. If the pulse is healthy, they continue. If it falters, they turn as one and begin the long journey toward another vein.

Their migrations are slow from nearby and astonishing from a distance. Thousands spread into narrow files along warm trenches, their polished backs flashing in sequence. Calves travel between the inner legs of adults, not only for protection but to learn the substrate’s language. A young grazer initially follows every vibration. With age it learns to ignore towers, storms, conveyor pods, and the deceptive tapping of predators.

Behind the herds come the foil-flocks.

These scavengers are little more than a digestive knot suspended inside a pleated sheet. They have no powered flight. By curling one edge, stiffening another, or snapping their bodies concave, they climb pressure gradients and skate through the turbulent wake of larger animals. Their colors are structural—silver, poisonous green, sudden magenta—and change as their folds open.

They feed on salts crushed into grazer tracks, on flakes of shell, on exhaled digestion products, and sometimes on one another. A flock can settle so densely over a footprint that the ground appears to flower. At the first tremor, the whole bloom lifts and vanishes sideways.

The tower fauna are stranger because they spend much of their lives without touching the land.

Hook-wings hatch in sheltered intake chambers, where their larvae cling among warm filters and eat trapped spores. As they grow, their bodies lengthen and their outer tissues harden into open frameworks. The adult wing is not a continuous surface but a lattice of rods, loops, and vibrating teeth. Air passing through it produces tones used for courtship, flock cohesion, and warning. Every species has a different chord. Some hum in narrow intervals; others make a raw, many-voiced shriek that carries beyond the horizon.

They circle towers because towers concentrate both food and lift. Vast rings form around productive crowns: millions of animals orbiting in layered bands, the youngest nearest the trunk and the strongest adults riding the outer turbulence. At a distance these rings resemble dark discs fitted loosely around the towers. They may persist for seasons, thinning at night and thickening when spore clouds pass.

Hook-wings mate in flight by briefly interlocking their lattices. Their egg cases are dropped into the intake current, not laid. Most miss the vents and are carried away. Those that enter a tower adhere to its membranes and become part of the internal colony. A thriving tower may contain generations descended from a single fortunate storm.

Among them drift balloon creatures, translucent sacs with a few dark organs hanging beneath. They inflate themselves with waste gases gathered from plant vents. Their tendrils comb aerosols from the sky. Some remain aloft for their entire adult lives, rising by day and sinking into the warm tower wakes at night. They appear helpless, but many bear explosive defensive bladders. When seized, they burst into adhesive filaments that foul a predator’s wing lattice and may drag both animals to the ground.

The aerial predators are called black only because they return almost no light.

While soaring, they spread into broad, angular kites, their bodies flattened between branching spars. Nothing moves visibly. They remain overhead for hours, indistinguishable from fragments of peeled rind. When they detect the vibration of a distressed flock, they collapse their wings and fall.

The strike is a momentary closure: a black shape, a snapped lattice, a puff of glittering fragments. Then the predator opens again, using the impact and the tower’s updraft to climb. Its prey is enclosed within a sealed ventral chamber and dissolved during flight. Large hunters carry the pale scars of failed descents, when a vane turned unexpectedly or electrical discharge passed through their spars.

On the ground, ambush predators imitate damage.

They grow asymmetrically, coating themselves in dust and fragments until they resemble splintered stalks or overturned conveyor pods. Some wait beside nutrient belts for years. Their metabolism slows until only a faint pressure pulse betrays them. When a pod-animal or young grazer approaches, the apparent debris unfolds into jointed clamps.

The oldest of these hunters become ecological fixtures. Wind packs sediment around them. Scavengers shelter beneath their false branches. Vascular tendrils grow across their lower limbs. Eventually the predator cannot relocate without tearing itself free, and so it ceases to wander altogether. It feeds, mates by airborne gametes, and dies in the same posture. Later generations mistake its shell for a mineral outcrop.

There are smaller lives everywhere, though the brilliance conceals them.

Valve-mites inhabit the seams of the rind and feed on leaking vapor. Thread colonies stretch between vane tips, harvesting static charge. Burrowing disks travel through the upper layers by melting the tissue ahead of them and hardening it behind. Parasites live inside tower pistons, synchronizing their reproduction with each compression stroke. Pale ribbon organisms wind around electrical veins and steal a fraction of every discharge; during storms they shine from within, revealing tangled networks hundreds of kilometers long.

Even the drifting clouds are alive. The green spore blooms consist of armored reproductive cells wrapped in reflective dust. Amber wax sheets are communal organisms whose members remain dormant until they encounter the correct chemical film. Carbon columns contain black, heat-loving specks that rise toward sunlight, divide, and sink when their coatings grow heavy. What seems like weather may be migration, mating, predation, or all three at once.

Death here is usually a loss of pressure.

A wounded animal does not bleed. Its tissues whiten, plume vapor, and contract around the breach. Small injuries seal with foamed resin. Larger ones whistle. Scavengers hear that whistle through their feet and arrive before the victim has stopped moving. When a tower ruptures, its stored atmosphere erupts for hours, launching internal colonies into the sky. The plateau closes around the emptied roots, digests what remains, and eventually raises something new from the scar.

No creature is entirely independent of the great substrate. The grazers prune its blisters and transport catalytic salts. Foil-flocks clean exposed tissue. Hook-wings keep intake chambers from choking with spores. Predators prevent feeding herds from lingering until a vascular line collapses. Even parasites alter pressures in ways the towers sometimes exploit. The machinery is not merely habitat. It is host, food, climate, road, signal, and ancestor.

There may be no mind among these inhabitants in the familiar sense. No one stands apart from the living works and understands the whole. Yet during the largest storms, distinctions become less certain.

Animals lock themselves to the rind. Towers close and retune. Vane forests flatten. Electrical veins brighten. Herds move toward sheltered basins before the pressure front is detectable in the local air. Signals race through roots and feet, through wing chords and static flashes. For a little while, millions of separate bodies behave like reflexes of the same vast creature.

Then the storm passes. The flyers scatter. The grazers unlock their feet. The towers resume their differing notes. Whatever unity existed dissolves back into appetite, rivalry, and distance.

The country continues beneath them, slowly sealing its wounds.

It remembers where they walked.
