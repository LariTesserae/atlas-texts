At first, nothing seems to live there.

This is nearly true.

The pale reeds are alive in the narrowest sense. They extend, withdraw, take in water, and slowly multiply by appearing farther along the same submerged stair. The black wall-fans feed. The red moss follows certain fractures with patient accuracy. Yet these growths seem less like inhabitants than exposed tissues—small, specialized surfaces through which something larger exchanges moisture with the air.

What lives in the basin is difficult to separate from the basin’s laws.

It has no body that can be approached from the outside. Stone is not its flesh, exactly, nor are the canals its blood. The towers are not limbs. Such comparisons become misleading as soon as they are made. A limb belongs to one place on an animal. Here, a tower may migrate west while remaining connected to a courtyard in the east; a canal may pass through itself without joining; a chamber may enlarge internally while its walls draw closer together. Whatever lives here does not occupy a shape. It occupies the persistence of a shape through contradiction.

Its nearest equivalent to a body is the city’s pattern.

This pattern is older than any particular stone. Erosion reveals it rather than damages it. A wall dissolves and the same interval returns as an arcade. A roof settles and becomes a plaza of the same dimensions. When a district sinks beneath the water, its arrangement reappears in condensation on the underside of another district. The material changes continuously, but the relations endure: this stair has forty steps; this court opens toward warm rain; this canal does not mix with the canal crossing it; this doorway leads somewhere larger than its wall permits.

These are not architectural rules. They are anatomy.

The inhabitant is most active where space is enclosed and unattended. In sealed rooms it produces distance. Corners bud from corners. A cistern develops shores, then weather, then remote structures visible only when its water is still. Given sufficient privacy, a cupboard may deepen into a corridor; a corridor into a quarter; a quarter into another basin under another black sky. This is how the creature grows—not outward, but inward, by increasing the amount of elsewhere contained in what already exists.

Open spaces restrain it. Attention restrains it more strongly. To be watched is, for it, a form of pressure. A long gaze compresses avenues, closes intersections, and brings remote facades near. It does not hide from observation in the manner of prey. Rather, observation reduces the medium in which it lives. It withdraws because there is less of it to inhabit.

Water protects it from this compression.

Every reflection provides a second location for a structure, and thus a choice. A mirrored building is held between two versions of itself. A flooded street cannot easily be shortened because its length persists beneath it. In the wettest quarters, the inhabitant rests in stable configurations, doubled and quiet. When the canals reverse and the reflections shiver, these fixed relations loosen. Streets migrate. Towers exchange districts. Doors attach themselves to different interiors.

The long inward breath heard in the canals is not respiration, though it serves the same purpose. It is the periodic release of arrangement.

No one portion of the city appears aware. Yet changes answer one another over great distances. A slab settles in the eastern quarter, and a basin in the west fills along its vertical side. A black wall-fan retracts, and somewhere beneath the central depression a street begins to bend. Rain rises from a plaza, and several miles away an invisible tower acquires a wet outline. The responses are too slow to resemble thought and too consistent to be coincidence.

If it thinks, it thinks by relocating adjacency.

One place is set beside another. A stair is made to return to its own courtyard. A door is placed next to weather. A buried street is brought alongside the underside of an upper avenue. These conjunctions persist for a time, are tested by water, and then separate. The city’s changing plan may be a sequence of such thoughts: immense, exact, and without language.

Its senses, if they can be called senses, are distributed through condensation. Moisture appears first where a change is approaching. Droplets gather around a doorway before its destination alters. A moving street below the central basin becomes slick in advance of its motion. Towers destined to vanish dry from the top downward, while those about to emerge acquire a trembling skin of water.

The city does not use water merely to reveal itself. It feels along wet surfaces.

The isolated plants assist in this. The cold reeds descend farther than their visible stalks should allow. Their lower ends pass through submerged steps and emerge in dry rooms beneath the street, where they flatten into pale threads. Black fans spread over walls at points where two distant chambers nearly touch. Red moss marks cracks that lead nowhere because “nowhere” is not absence here; it is unassigned connection, a place before the city decides what should adjoin it.

They may be organs. They may be parasites. They may be separate lives that have adapted so completely to the larger one that distinction no longer matters.

There are also local manifestations—temporary concentrations that an observer might mistake for individual inhabitants.

They occur most often during sideways rain. Droplets moving across a street will sometimes fail to continue through an apparently empty section of air. They gather on a surface that cannot otherwise be seen: a sloping plane, a narrow vertical curve, several jointed hollows. For a few moments the water outlines something approximately the height of a doorway.

It is never clearly shaped like a person.

The resemblance comes and goes. What looks like a shoulder may lengthen into an arch. A bent head may prove to be the beginning of a stair. A row of wet hollows might be ribs, windows, or the mouths of small corridors receding into a body whose interior is another district. No two witnesses would agree on the number of its limbs, because the limbs are mostly routes. One extends down a canal. Another passes through a wall and returns as rain. A third may be visible only in the lower city, moving beneath the bright colorless sky.

These condensations do not walk. The street alters around them until they are elsewhere.

They leave no tracks because the paving beneath them becomes the paving toward which they move. They make no sound because nearby sound is absorbed into their depth. Occasionally, after one has passed, a sealed room contains the clear murmur of rain from miles away. This may be the closest thing they have to a voice.

Whether these are individuals is uncertain.

They may be brief bodies raised by the whole city, as a hand is raised from an animal. They may be young forms not yet deep enough to hold buildings. They may be apertures through which the larger inhabitant attends to a particular street. Their proportions explain why doors recur at bodily intervals, though no builders ever cut those doors. The city did not design itself for occupants. At times, it condenses portions of itself into an occupant-like scale, and the architecture forms around those episodes as mineral gathers around a vanished root.

This is why the city resembles habitation without ever having been inhabited.

The apparent inhabitant and the apparent habitat are two phases of the same thing.

Its feeding is almost imperceptible. It takes in difference: the difference between dry stone and wet stone, between an open room and a closed one, between a structure and its reflection, between one arrangement and the next. Heat supplies continual change. Water carries it. Darkness preserves alternatives not yet made visible. The faint sweetness rising from the paving is released where old configurations are being dissolved.

Nothing rots because decay would surrender form without retaining it. The city permits no such waste. Fallen matter thins into stains, and the stains enter the grain of the stone. Collapsed arches fuse to terraces. Dead reeds become pale lines that later determine the edges of rooms. Everything is incorporated as instruction.

Its reproduction, if reproduction is the right word, takes place at the circular depression.

The lower city visible there is not merely another layer. It is an excess of pattern: more streets than the upper basin can presently contain. Its bright sky, inverted rain, and slowly moving avenues belong to a forming body. Over long periods, correspondences accumulate between the two cities. A courtyard above acquires a counterpart below. A canal duplicates its reversals. Forty steps appear beneath forty steps. When enough relations have been established, a whole district migrates upward and settles somewhere in the basin.

Nothing is displaced to make room. The basin becomes capable of having always contained it.

Meanwhile, the lower city remains larger than before.

Thus the pressure felt everywhere—the sense of further architecture attempting to condense—is neither construction nor invasion. It is generation. The inhabitant continuously produces more interior than the world provides exterior for.

The towers in the western marsh may be its oldest extensions or its spores. Each stands alone, held in place by its reflection, while causeways sink and reappear around it. The bubbles containing cold blue daylight rise near these structures most often. For an instant, each bubble shows a condition the city does not otherwise possess: distance under a sun, air that is not saturated, a sky with depth rather than density.

The bubbles may come from other basins.

They may be fragments of environments enclosed during earlier growth. Or they may be lures—not deliberate ones, but involuntary glimpses of the kinds of space this life gathers into itself. When they burst, the daylight is not extinguished. It is taken into the warm darkness and stored as another inaccessible weather behind another door.

There may be many such cities, though “many” is uncertain for a life whose parts exchange locations. The isolated tower in the marsh could belong to this basin today and to another tomorrow. A sealed cistern might touch several at once. The city below the central depression may be its offspring, its ancestor, or the same organism viewed from the side toward which it is growing.

It has no need of animals. Animal life would be too rapid, too localized, too committed to singular bodies. Tracks would insist on continuous routes. Nests would fix inside against outside. Predation would remove forms rather than translate them. Even decay would force time to travel in one direction.

The basin excludes such life without hostility. Its conditions simply favor a slower and more spacious order.

What lives there is not lonely. Loneliness requires recognition of another absence, and emptiness here is fertile. Every vacant chamber is occupied by its own enlargement. Every unentered doorway is busy acquiring destinations. Every black pool holds a district steady in reflection. Every upward drop carries the outline of something not yet visible.

The city is full.

It is full of one life, or one kind of life, distributed so completely that there is nowhere for an inhabitant to stand apart from its dwelling. Its age cannot be measured by ruins, because ruin is one of its methods of maintenance. It does not sleep, although whole centuries may pass between one adjustment and the next. It does not wake, because no configuration is privileged enough to be called waking.

It continues.

A room closes and grows a distant shore.  
A canal inhales.  
A wet tower appears where no tower stood.  
Far below, under colorless daylight, a street bends toward the surface.

And throughout the basin, in cracks, under plazas, inside the darkness pressing against upper windows, the living pattern slowly makes more of itself.
