At first it seems that no one lives there—only things that grow there.

The smallest inhabitants are almost transparent. They occupy films rather than spaces: algae inside gauge faces, yeasts in the warm seams of vats, fungal threads stretched through the root floor. Pale snails graze condensation without descending to the ground. The rare gnats appear newly made and unfinished, with soft bodies and wings too long for them. They do not swarm or breed in visible numbers. They pass from one flower to another when the flywheel sounds, then disappear into the cups of the plants.

These are not the basin’s masters. They are tolerated motes, minor exchanges in a system that has little surplus for animals.

The more substantial inhabitants are rooted, though “rooted” does not mean stationary. Every plant in the basin moves; only the scale of movement differs. The broad-leaved stands migrate by abandoning their oldest edges and raising new fans several handspans away. Vines cross chambers over years. Floating rosettes rotate positions in slow, collective currents. A plant may begin beside a vat, spend part of its life climbing an iron column, and flower decades later from the roof, having transferred most of its living tissue along the route.

Individual plants are difficult to distinguish. Stems that appear separate join beneath the root mat. A vine enters the ground green and emerges elsewhere white, wrapped around copper. The folded-hand leaves in the vats share water, sugars, and impulses through pale cords threaded into the valves beneath them. When one is cut, leaves several meters away close.

What lives here is less a forest than a set of occupations.

There are plants that hold water, plants that strain it, plants that collect rust, plants that keep channels open. Some grow hard, translucent root tips that enter pipe joints and turn within the threads. Others produce flat pads exactly beneath dripping bearings, feeding on grease and shedding an oil that preserves the metal. Fine red vines follow electrical conductors and tighten when current passes. Thick gray rhizomes surround load-bearing columns but leave inspection plates uncovered.

These habits are too consistent to be accidental and too slow to look deliberate.

The basin’s closest equivalent to workers appears only when watched across many days.

A braided mass may lie beside a drainage slot, indistinguishable from discarded vine. After a mechanical report, its outer fibers stiffen. By the next morning it has drawn itself partly across the grate. A day later the grate is clear, its debris gathered into a damp cylinder. The braid then relaxes around the cylinder, penetrates it with white rootlets, and remains there until both worker and waste have become one rooted bundle.

Others climb.

They have no heads, though the forward end often bears a hood of overlapping leaves sensitive to heat and weak electrical fields. They have no limbs in a fixed number. Any tendril that finds purchase thickens; any unsupported one withers and is reabsorbed. Their bodies are woven from several species: a contractile vine at the core, absorbent moss packed around it, fungal cords carrying nutrients, and a skin of small shield-shaped leaves. Tiny ceramic fragments and flakes of copper are sometimes embedded along their undersides.

They move too slowly to be caught moving. One is simply found in a new posture: stretched between switch bank and floor, looped around a valve wheel, hanging from a rail where yesterday there seemed to be only vegetation. Their passage leaves pressed tracks in the wet sheen and small, regular punctures where tendrils have tested the ground.

They maintain rather than build. A blocked pipe is entered by hair-fine roots that swell, twist, and withdraw with the obstruction caught among them. A loose belt is touched by adhesive pads until enough of them accumulate to alter its tension. Bearings are polished by repeated contact with silica-edged leaves. Fresh filament is wound not by hands but by a climbing braid circling a spool for weeks, laying each strand beside the last.

The amber lamps call these forms into activity. They do not approach the light as insects would. Instead, the whole nearby growth subtly changes its pressure. Hooded tips lift. Leaves expose their silver undersides. Conductive vines tighten around old wires. The lamp may be less illumination than signal—a warmth, a voltage, an instruction surviving from the installation’s first life.

No single worker seems to understand that instruction. Understanding is distributed across reactions.

The engine house is the nearest thing the inhabitants have to an organ of thought. The flywheel gives them a common interval. Its revolution synchronizes flowers, valves, root pressure, and the opening of microscopic pores. The buried pump provides a longer rhythm. Rainfall, mineral concentration, water temperature, and strain in the columns add further measures. Together these pulses pass through the basin as changes in tension and chemistry.

A response may take hours to cross from one side to the other. A decision may require a season.

When a roof panel begins to fail, nothing rushes to repair it. First, nearby leaves alter their angle and divert water. Then the vines above the weakness thicken. Root mats dry fractionally, becoming lighter. A climbing braid arrives weeks later and lays wet fiber along the crack. Fungi mineralize it. By the following year, the panel remains rusted and broken, but its load has been transferred elsewhere.

The basin does not restore objects to their original condition. It preserves their duty.

This may be what became of the workers who once occupied the installation. There are no bones, tools, clothes, or personal rooms remaining, yet certain plants contain structures unlike anything else in the basin: branching fibers sheathed in fatty tissue, mineral knots resembling joints, thin sheets that contract when exposed to salts found in sweat. In the oldest root masses beneath the engine house, rows of dark nodules produce weak electrical patterns at irregular intervals.

Whether these are imitations of nerves, descendants of engineered stock, or the last dispersed remains of human nervous systems cannot be determined.

Occasionally, the growth assembles something almost person-shaped.

It happens near a task requiring reach. A column of braided stems rises from the root floor and braces itself on two divided masses. Upper vines extend toward a lever or lamp hood. For several hours, sometimes days, it possesses shoulders, a bent back, even the suggestion of a hand where tendrils gather around a handle. But the resemblance is functional and temporary. Once the work is done, one leg roots, the torso slumps into epiphytes, and the arm remains hanging as an ordinary vine.

Nothing in it insists on staying a person.

Nor is there evidence of speech. Communication occurs through pressure in buried water, through chemical traces laid on leaves, through the timed knocking of metal. A cut drainage vine causes valves to open elsewhere. A diseased vat is isolated by roots constricting its inlet. When one of the rare lizards dies, it is wrapped before insects can find it, divided among fungal cords, and incorporated with unusual care. For several revolutions afterward, the translucent flowers nearest the engine open late.

The inhabitants reproduce by replacement. New growth is trained through old forms until it assumes their strain, their connections, perhaps their memory. A young vine follows a corroding wire. When the wire finally parts, the vine contains enough metal to conduct the signal itself. A wooden plank softens under moss; roots preserve its shape and continue serving as a bridge. A label disappears beneath algae, but the plants around each switch retain different sensitivities, as though the words have been translated into tissue.

Thus the basin’s oldest resident may not be any organism within it. It is the arrangement: the allocation of space, the circulation of water, the sequence of valves, the patient distinction between FEED and RETURN. It lives partly in roots, partly in machinery, partly in repeated acts of maintenance. Its body is replaced continuously without ceasing to be itself.

The rare animals live inside it.

The climbing braids serve it.

The plants compose it.

The machinery regulates its breath.

And when the flywheel completes its turn and the impact passes through iron, water, fungus, and leaf, the whole basin briefly contracts—not dramatically, not enough for an eye to prove—but with the coherence of something hearing its own name.
