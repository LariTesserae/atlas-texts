At first it seems that nothing lives in the Crown Sump except plants.

The moss is the most obvious claimant. It occupies without appearing to advance, thickening on ledges, swallowing bolt heads, rounding the sharp basalt with a softness the stone has not earned. Several kinds grow together. One forms bright cushions in the rain; another lies flat and black-green against warm pipes; a third hangs beneath the walkways in soaked beards, trembling whenever the pumps engage. Torn away, the mats reveal a pale underside threaded with roots, fungal fibers, rust flakes, dead midges, and grains of carbonate. This is the nearest thing the basin has to soil.

The ferns are less patient. They wedge their crowns into hot seams and send fronds through handrails. Their new growth comes up tightly curled and red as raw copper, turning blue-green only after it has opened. Around steam fissures they remain in constant motion, though there may be no wind: lifting, relaxing, lifting again in the mountain’s breath.

There are microorganisms in every warm overflow. They make slick orange films beneath leaking joints and milk-white threads in the shallower pools. In the carbonate terraces, colonies arrange themselves in concentric stains—umber, sulfur-yellow, bruised violet—according to how hot the water is. They live so close to the limit of heat that a valve adjustment can erase an entire color overnight. Elsewhere another ring begins.

The visible animals are easy to underestimate.

The pale moths in the turbine house are not true cave moths, though they resemble them. Their wings are thin and nearly without scales, marked only by two gray bars like ash dragged across wet paper. By day they pack themselves into the folds of old insulation. At dusk they emerge to drink from the condenser mesh, unfurling long mouthparts to take droplets before the droplets can fall. They do not fly well in open air. The summit wind strips them away. Instead they move through the plant’s sheltered interior, from pipe to pipe, navigating by warmth and vibration.

Their caterpillars eat the soft mineral algae growing around ceramic insulators. This gives them a faint alkaline taste, and almost nothing preys on them.

Almost nothing.

The translucent amphibians in the reservoir do.

A technician once entered them in a maintenance ledger as “small newts,” and the name persisted despite their being neither small at maturity nor, strictly speaking, newts. Most are seen only as pale shapes pressed to the submerged wall. Their skin is clear enough that the blood vessels show through in pink forks. The liver is a dark leaf beneath the spine. After feeding, the gut can be seen holding a row of moth bodies, black and delicate as handwriting.

They have broad hands with adhesive fringes on every finger. These let them cling to steel and concrete while the pumps draw water past them. Their tails are narrow, almost ribbonlike, unsuitable for fast swimming but sensitive to minute changes in current. A resting animal lays its tail against the reservoir wall and feels the whole plant through it.

The oldest ones are longer than a person’s forearm.

They seldom come to the surface. Their eyes are reduced to silver blisters beneath the skin, useful only for distinguishing clouded daylight from darkness. Instead they know the basin as a field of pulses. Pump One is a deep, regular pressure. The condenser gutters are countless light impacts. The flywheel makes a slow interval that passes through the water like a distant footfall. A human stepping onto the reservoir platform is abrupt, irregular, and unmistakable. Long before a technician leans over the rim, every amphibian has moved beneath the pipe mouths.

They breed in the warmest months, though “months” mean little in a place without real seasons. The female fixes strings of clear eggs to the underside of intake grilles. The vibration keeps silt from settling on them, and the moving water carries oxygen across their membranes. Larvae hatch with external gills so fine that they resemble red mold. For several weeks they remain inside the machinery’s protective mesh, feeding on bacterial films and whatever the filters catch.

Their dependence on the condenser is complete. There may have been an ancestral animal in the mountain’s lower cloud forests, but nothing quite like these creatures lived here before the reservoir was built. They have become summit animals within the age of iron. The plant made a warm, predator-poor lake where no lake had been; the animals answered with transparent skin, gripping feet, and a nervous system tuned to bearings.

They respond first when the Crown Sump begins to dry.

Before moss pales, before gauges register a meaningful loss, they leave the reservoir’s central wall and gather around the lowest inlets. Their bodies overlap there in a white knot. If the water grows hotter, they climb into the return pipes, seeking cooler circulation. Technicians have learned to look for them through the inspection glass. A clear pipe is reassuring. A pipe crowded with pale hands means the gates must be closed.

For this reason no one removes them, though they sometimes block a narrow screen. Pump manuals contain no reference to amphibians, but handwritten instructions do:

**IF THE WHITE ONES RISE, CHECK LEVEL BEFORE PRESSURE.**

The swifts know nothing of this.

They arrive out of the immense air beyond the rim, black and clean-edged, and inhabit the basin as if it were only a convenient crack in the sky. The pair behind the exhaust grille returns irregularly, sometimes after an absence of years. They seal their nest to the warm masonry with saliva and weave it from lichen, moth wings, and filaments pulled from fraying cable. Their chicks grow to the constant thunder of the turbine house. When they first emerge, they cling to the grille and stare into the wet green enclosure, bewildered by so much solid ground.

Then they launch over the rim and vanish downward.

No mammal remains permanently. Rats have arrived in freight cages, but the wet defeated them. There are no stores to raid, no dry walls in which to breed. Once, boot prints of a small cat appeared along the eastern stair after a maintenance crew had departed. They ended beneath the turbine house and were never seen again. For several seasons afterward, moth wings accumulated there in little fans, but whether the cat survived, escaped, or merely became another maintenance story is unknown.

Human beings do not live at the Crown Sump for long. The shelter permits occupancy but does not welcome it. Bedding mildews while being slept in. Condensation forms inside sealed watches. The mechanical noise enters dreams and rearranges them into repetitions: a wheel turning, a stair descending, a knock passing through stone.

Technicians come in pairs when the cable runs and alone when it does not. They stay three days, perhaps six. They tighten anchors, scrape mineral growth from gauges, take water samples, and measure the crack beneath Pump Two. They speak loudly at first, then stop trying to speak over the machinery. By the second day they communicate with taps on railings and gestures learned from previous crews.

Each discovers the little shrine near the pressure line: three washers stacked unevenly, the blue fuse upright between them. No official record explains it. Offerings nevertheless appear. A brass nut. A twist of copper wire. A swift’s feather. Once, a milk tooth wrapped in red thread.

The technicians insist the shrine is only a joke inherited from older workers. Still, before reopening the valley gates, even skeptical crews touch the blue glass.

What lives most completely in the basin may not be any single animal.

The condenser has acquired rhythms not present in its original design. Moss retains water around hot housings and changes their cooling rate. Roots brace some pipe runs while forcing others out of line. Mineral terraces divert leaks into fern beds; fern beds filter the water before it returns to the reservoir. Moths graze films from the insulators. Amphibians clear soft growth from intake screens and announce thermal changes by moving through the pipes. Swifts reduce the flying insects when they are present. Fungi enter gaskets, destroy them, and later seal the resulting seep with swollen fibers and trapped carbonate.

None of this is planned. None of it is stable enough to call harmony.

Yet the parts have lived together long enough that removing one can disturb the rest. A thorough cleaning once stripped the moss from the western pump housing. The exposed metal cooled too rapidly during a storm, contracting around an already worn coupling. The shaft shifted. For nine hours the whole turbine house shook hard enough to throw water from the reservoir. When the repair was complete, the crew packed the casing in wet moss again.

Since then it has been listed in the ledger as insulation.

So the resident of the Crown Sump is difficult to point to. It is green in some places, rust-colored in others. It has translucent hands below the water and black wings above the rim. It drinks cloud through metal mesh. It sleeps in ceramic cabinets, lays eggs under grilles, roots into volcanic cracks, and turns one enormous wheel every fifty-eight seconds.

Its breath is the release of the pressure valves.

Its pulse is Pump Two’s worsening knock.

When cloud closes over the basin and no human being is present, the thing does not become lonely. Loneliness belongs to creatures that can imagine elsewhere. The life of the Crown Sump has no elsewhere. It extends only from the warm rock to the condenser towers, from the reservoir’s dark center to the mossy rim. Beyond that is cold air and falling distance.

Within, everything listens to the water.
