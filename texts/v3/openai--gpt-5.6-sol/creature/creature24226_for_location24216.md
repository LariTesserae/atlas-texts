The only inhabitants that can be proved are the white things in the sumps.

They are smaller than the last joint of a finger. At first they resemble scraps loosened from the undersides of shells: colorless, curved, almost too thin to possess an interior. Their legs clasp the submerged tiles in matched rows. Under the mineral glow their bodies disclose a faint machinery—a pulsing thread, a dark grain passing slowly through the gut, chambers opening and closing behind the transparent armor. They have no eyes, but the places where eyes might once have been are marked by smooth depressions.

They do not swim unless dislodged. They hold to the shadowed side of a tile and comb the water with delicate mouthparts, harvesting what the city gives them: clay-fine organic dust, translucent films, the residue of other lives washed down long ago. Their eggs are carried beneath the body like clusters of clear beads. The young emerge already pale and complete, requiring no light and little food. Generations may pass without one of them crossing more than the width of a cistern.

When the water takes one, it folds its legs tightly and travels through the channels like a seed. It may arrive in another sump days later. It may lodge behind a grille, molt there, and leave its empty shape trembling in the current. It may pass into cracks no tool ever opened. Thus the city is larger to them than it was to its builders. Its pipes are avenues, its cisterns provinces, its porous walls a wilderness of passages too narrow to have names.

There are smaller lives as well, if life is the proper word for such patience. A clear skin coats certain wet stones. It has no greenness and no smell. Scraped away, it returns over months, drawing sustenance from dissolved iron, sulfur, and whatever minute remnants seep through the strata. The white animals graze it. Pale threads inhabit the mortar beneath it. In copper channels these threads blacken and die; in old clay they form soft colonies shaped exactly like thumbprints.

But the city was made for another kind of resident.

Their bodies must be reconstructed from pressure and wear. A shallow polish below a niche suggests a shoulder repeatedly braced there. Parallel grooves at the edge of a floorway suggest knees, sled runners, or callused forearms. The sleeping hollows are not long enough for bodies stretched straight. Whoever rested in them slept folded: knees to chest, spine bowed to the ceiling, one hand perhaps laid against the wall to feel changes in the water during sleep.

They may have been small, though not as small as their doors imply. A door admitted only what needed to pass. One entered sideways, or headfirst, or with the arms extended before the face. Larger rooms were assembled through use rather than enclosed by walls: a sleeping recess here, a storage pocket above it, a cooking shelf reached through a slit, a family cistern on the far side of a crawl-tunnel. A household did not occupy a chamber. It occupied a sequence.

The old residents would have had compact bodies and extraordinary hands. Their fingers reached into valve hollows where no wrist could follow. Their nails were probably kept short. The pads of the thumb and first two fingers, continually wet and abraded by shale, may have grown thick and ridged. Many of the civic wedges are too small to grip in a fist but fit neatly between finger and thumb. Bone needles imply fine work: stitched skins, filter cloths, baskets of hair or gut, masks for the mineral air. Their garments, if they wore them, could not have hung loose. Anything trailing would have caught on corners, dragged through channels, or wicked precious water away.

Sight can have mattered little. The blue bowls reveal edges but not distances, faces but not expressions. People learned one another by outline, heat, and contact. In a passage two residents could not pass without embracing. A warning was a pressure against the wrist. Permission was given by loosening the fingers. Names may have been tapped into the palm: brief arrangements of knuckle, nail, and fingertip, recognizable even through gloves.

Children would have known the city differently. They could enter galleries closed to adults, chase reflections along wet ledges, and sleep in wall niches later abandoned as they grew. Their games may survive among objects mistaken for instruments: shell discs that fit no channel, little clay plugs pierced through the center, stones sorted by the notes they make when struck. A child crawling home in darkness would not count turns. They would follow vibration—the drinking pipe’s quick knock beneath the left hand, the cooling shaft’s lower hum through the knees, the tremor of the household basin transmitted along a familiar stair.

The adults listened with their entire bodies.

Some lay cheek-down on sounding slabs. Some held copper rods between their teeth and pressed the other ends to the walls. A skilled listener could separate dozens of flows within the roar: a cistern nearing overflow, a filter silting shut, a crack widening behind a sealed room. This was not merely engineering. It was manners, law, and perhaps devotion. To take too much water altered the note heard by neighbors. Neglect had a pitch. Theft announced itself three chambers away.

Those who governed may have been indistinguishable from repairers. They carried cups, wedges, chalky tablets, and small hammers wrapped at the heads to produce controlled tones. Their authority lay in memory: knowing which plug awakened which distant wall, which winter seep carried copper sickness, which cistern must be emptied when the deep gate began to hum. Decisions were made around the tool basin by many hands testing the same trembling rim.

They would not have worshiped water as something pure. They knew it too intimately. Water nourished, poisoned, cooled, weakened mortar, carried waste, opened fissures, and erased names. They distinguished waters as others distinguish animals: young ceiling water, bitter shale water, water that had crossed bone, water made feverish by copper, sleeping water from deep stone. The sharp diagrams beneath the eaves may be practical records, but their precision has tenderness in it. Each line follows a pressure the engraver could not see.

Their dead could not be buried beneath the floors; there was scarcely any beneath. Nor could bodies be permitted to enter the channels. Perhaps the sealed older rooms are tombs. Perhaps bones were cleaned, divided, and incorporated into tools so that the dead continued in civic use—as needles, valve pins, sounding pieces, combs for filters. Some little levers have the curve of ribs. Some white wall pegs may be finger bones. In such a place remembrance would not require monuments. It would require maintenance.

Why they departed is not written clearly. There is no collapse, no flood line, no scattered heap of possessions marking panic. The channels remain apportioned. Cups stand nested. Stoppers were dried and stored rather than abandoned in use. Soot fades gradually from the chimneys, as though fewer hearths were lit each year.

Perhaps the city emptied by attrition. Children ceased to be born, or left through the crack while still small enough to pass. Perhaps the chemistry of the seepage changed. A trace metal entered the drinking cistern. The listeners heard an alteration too slow for any single ear to prove. Household by household, they sealed their rooms in clay.

Or perhaps they did not leave through the outer crack at all.

The deepest gate bears grooves the width of fingertips. They are not hinges, script, or ornament. They resemble the tracks made by many hands turning something that would not turn. If the gate was meant to open, it did so by a method involving pressure distributed across its entire face. One person could not operate it. A population might.

Beyond it there may be descendants fitted even more closely to the stone: pale, narrow-chested people who no longer make soot, who drink by pressing their mouths to porous walls, who have abandoned light because reflected droplets only confuse the finer work of hearing. They might move during changes too slight to register as events, crossing a room over the course of hours and freezing whenever the city’s pressure shifts. Their palms may be broad and soft, their ears sealed against the useless thunder, their bones sensitive to vibrations conducted through clay.

But there are no prints in the wet silt before the gate. No tool has been displaced. No breath clouds the mineral glow.

What lives there now lives without disturbing equilibrium.

The white creatures feed. The invisible films rebuild themselves. Water occupies every cup prepared for it. In the worn hollows, the vanished residents persist less like ghosts than like postures the city still expects: a hand at the plug, a cheek against the slab, a bent body passing sideways through a door. The channels continue to divide the seepage as if mouths waited at their ends. The pipes call from room to room in tones shaped for listeners who may no longer exist.

And behind the layered shale, something—water, stone, another city, or the accumulated echo of all the former hands—keeps up its deep and steady breathing.
