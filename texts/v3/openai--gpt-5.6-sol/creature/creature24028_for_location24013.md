At first they are difficult to separate from the city’s movement.

A figure crossing a bridge has the same narrow, jointed look as the bridge itself: a dark arrangement of cords and pale struts, leaning into the wind. A gardener spread against a wall may be mistaken for a root-pad, especially when wrapped in a work mantle ribbed with climbing loops. In the lower districts, where reflected light arrives in brief sweeps, the inhabitants appear only in pieces—a hand around a cable, the flash of an eye, the fanlike opening of a garment—then vanish when the mirror turns.

They are slight, but not frail. Their bodies are built with the city’s economy of mass. Long limbs contain latticed bones like those of the sail-crawlers, strengthened at the joints by dark, flexible collars. The torso is narrow from side to side and deep from breast to back. Beneath the ribs lie paired breathing chambers capable of closing independently against dust or violent pressure changes. At the base of the throat, where a human voice might gather, they have a second set of folded membranes. These are not much used for speech. They tighten, loosen, and swell to protect the deeper passages when the hollow roars.

Their skin ranges through chalk, amber, old ivory, and the faint green-black of the fan plants. It is never glossy. Fine overlapping scales lie along the windward surfaces of the body: brow, cheekbones, shoulders, forearms, shins. Softer skin remains only in sheltered creases and on the inner hands. The scales are translucent at their edges and often etched by age into concentric lines. In strong light, an elder’s face resembles weathered saltstone repaired with thin sheets of horn.

No blood reddens them. When cut, they leak a slow, clear-gold fluid that darkens in contact with air and seals the wound into resinous glass. A serious injury smells sharp and fragrant, like the venting gardens at dusk. Their bodies run on oils, reactive salts, and gases exchanged through the lungs and skin. They eat not to replace water but to maintain the delicate mineral balances that keep those oils fluid.

This gives hunger many names.

There is the hunger for carbon-rich resin, the hunger for bright salts, the hunger for certain bitter powders that prevent the joints from stiffening. There is a dangerous craving for copper compounds, which must be satisfied sparingly. There is breath-hunger, treated by sleeping beside a vent tuned to the proper mixture of gases. Someone may be well fed and still waste away because the air reaching their room is chemically wrong.

Their meals are small, frequent, and intensely flavored. Strips shaved from black-green fans are warmed until they curl and exude oil. Seed nodules are cracked with stone pincers and ground with mineral flakes into pungent pastes. White scale-mats are toasted into brittle sheets. Resin is chewed, softened, and passed among family members during long work. Aromatic smoke is inhaled through small ceramic masks, not for pleasure alone but as nourishment. Many foods would appear to an outsider to be wax, incense, chalk, or kindling.

Nothing is boiled. Nothing is washed.

Dust is removed with brushes, scrapers, static cloths, or blown air. Infants are cleaned with warm mineral powder that clings to waste oils and can be peeled away in gray skins. Tools are scoured with salt grit. The dead are not bathed; they are polished.

Their eyes are large but deeply recessed, protected by two lids. The outer lid is tough and nearly transparent, closing against grit without entirely shutting out light. The inner is soft, dark, and used for sleep. Inhabitants of the upper districts often have mirrored flecks embedded along their brow ridges to scatter glare. Those born near the Last Floor have broader pupils and a dull violet reflective layer behind the eye. They dislike full sun and navigate the lower bridges by the movement of air over their faces.

Their ears are small sealed pits beneath hinged plates of cartilage. They can open them in quiet rooms, but in the city proper they remain closed. Hearing, as surface people understand it, is a private sense here. Lovers uncover one another’s ears. Parents press a child’s head to the soft inner wall and hum through the bone. A spoken name is not for public use.

In public, they speak with the entire body.

Their hand language is quick, compact, and adapted to climbing. Most signs can be made while one hand remains on a rail. Fingers bend in combinations; wrists rotate; shoulders mark tense and certainty. Facial movements supply direction and emphasis. A lifted chin may indicate the upper slit, but also heat, danger, exposure, or an authority seated above. A lowered gaze may mean depth, age, secrecy, or the dead.

Distance speech travels through structure. Every citizen learns the basic blows before learning household signs. Knuckles, heel, hammer, or weighted cord can send patterns into beams and bridge lines. Short signals announce a broken fastening, a change of wind, an approaching mirror beam, a falling object. More elaborate messages pass from district to district through trained relayers, altered slightly by each bridge’s resonance. Official notices are composed not only as rhythms but for the material that will carry them. A warning intended for saltstone has a different grammar from one sent along rope.

They feel these messages through their feet.

The soles are broad, divided by deep flexing grooves, and densely sensitive to vibration. Most go unshod. A child learns the character of its neighborhood through the tremor of the floors: which bridge shivers before noon, which wall carries the deep pipe, which stair gives a false knock when a heavy person descends. They can often identify a relative approaching several rooms away, not by footfall but by the pattern of pauses imposed by that person’s balance.

To stand still without touching anything is considered both foolish and lonely.

Even in conversation, one hand rests on a wall, cord, or another body. Houses are arranged around shared tension lines so that their occupants remain faintly aware of one another during sleep. Privacy is made by dampening vibration: hanging thick fiber mats, loosening pins, or resting a room temporarily on soft resin blocks. The wealthy do not own the loudest homes. They own the quietest—rooms so carefully isolated that an uncovered ear may hear breathing.

Their clothing begins as protection from edges and moving air. Close-fitted wraps cover the joints. Over these hang asymmetrical mantles with stiffened ribs that can be opened on one side to brace against a gust. A skilled bridge-crosser wears the wind rather than fights it, spreading a sleeve or shoulder fan at the right moment and letting pressure carry some of the body’s weight. Work clothes are dull and rough. Festival garments are thin laminates of translucent plant horn, cut into scales that flicker under the traveling mirrors.

Belts matter more than robes. They carry hooks, wedges, line, resin patches, a dust mask, a signaling striker, and at least two kinds of knife. Children are tethered until they can read the wind. Elders tether themselves again when their balance membranes begin to fail.

No insult is worse than saying someone has only one fastening.

Kinship is likewise made through several points of attachment. A household is rarely a pair and their offspring. It is a knot of five or six adults whose skills and birth-lines complement one another: a vent-tender, a gardener, a structural listener, a mirror-turner, perhaps a resin worker or a net keeper. Children belong first to the knot, then to the ledge, then to the city. Biological parentage is known but does not always carry the greatest obligation. The person who tied an infant’s first safety cord has a lifelong claim and duty.

Young are born enclosed in a tough, translucent sheath filled with warm oil. For their first days they do not breathe the city’s air. The sheath hangs in a padded cradle near the household vent while attendants gradually thin it with warm scrapers. The child’s first breath is carefully timed for the evening pause.

This is considered the true birth.

Everyone present rests a hand on the same beam. When the roar ceases, the sheath is opened at the face. The infant draws in the motionless air and usually makes a small clicking spasm no one beyond the room can hear. Then the night wind rises, the walls begin to tremble, and the child is tied into the household.

The three-breath pause governs much of their inner life. They do not kneel for it, nor look toward any single shrine. They stop and touch the city. Debts may be forgiven in those breaths. Promises made then are regarded as physically lodged in the stone. Someone who continues working through the pause is assumed to be mad, criminal, or so grief-struck that ordinary duties no longer apply.

They have few images of gods. Their sacred objects are proportions: the taper of a shaft, the interval between supports, the number of turns in a load-bearing knot. Priests, if they can be called priests, are custodians of correspondences between breath, structure, and time. They maintain the oldest pipes whose lower ends have never been seen. They interpret changes in the deep tones by lying flat on stone tables with weighted plates resting on the chest.

They do not claim the hollow was built for them. Nor do they agree that their ancestors built it.

Some say the first inhabitants descended from the salt plain to escape the open sky and discovered the city already breathing. Others say they emerged from below and grew heavier over generations until they could no longer ride the central currents. A forbidden lower-district teaching holds that the people are temporary mineral blooms on the inner wall of a much larger living organ.

Their records do not settle the matter. Written language is incised in layers of material chosen for how long the message should endure. Daily accounts are scratched into salt slips and erased by grinding. Contracts go onto fired clay. Genealogies are knotted into cords treated with black resin. Warnings are cut into glass. The most permanent histories are built as small changes in the city’s resonance: a chamber narrowed, a pipe lengthened, a side cavity sealed. Such records can be read only by comparing tones across seasons.

Thus history is not merely remembered. It is played.

The keepers of these records are called listeners in translation, though many are almost entirely deaf by surface standards. They perceive pitch through jaw, sternum, palms, and feet. Age gives them extraordinary sensitivity. An elder seated against a central brace may detect that a hidden crack has opened twelve districts below, or that a deep shaft has accumulated a crust no wider than a fingernail.

Their most honored workers are not rulers or warriors but those who know when to yield.

They loosen buildings before a violent season. They move inhabited rooms from a thinning wall. They decide which bridge may be repaired and which must be cut free. Their authority comes from accepting that every structure is temporary. A poor leader clings to stone. A good one knows the exact hour to untie it.

When a ledge collapses, the city does not gather afterward in silence; silence cannot be arranged. Instead, every district strikes the loss into its beams. The pattern includes the place, the number of bodies, and whether any anchor line remains usable. For days the news travels around the hollow, returning distorted through old pathways. The dead seem to depart repeatedly.

Bodies are placed in the lowest recovery nets until their oils have thickened. The useful resins are then drawn off for sealing communal structures, but never domestic rooms. Bones are separated, split, and made into wedges, pivots, sign hammers, or the pale ribs of memorial kites hung where the dead once lived. Nothing is put into the shaft. To drop a person beyond recovery would deny them all future attachment.

Yet a few choose precisely that.

At the Last Floor live those who tend the deepest pipes, fugitives from upper obligations, and contemplatives who claim the great tones contain patterns older than the city. Their bodies are broader, their scales nearly colorless, their eyes often sealed beneath protective films. They move slowly over the black rope web, always maintaining three points of contact. Some bind themselves there for years.

They say the force rising from below is not merely wind. It carries trace gases found nowhere near the surface and aromatic molecules resembling those released by injured bodies. During certain nights, the ropes of the Last Floor tighten without any corresponding change in pressure. Deep listeners have felt enormous pauses pass upward through the shaft—pauses too long and slow to belong to the city’s daily breath.

Most inhabitants dismiss such accounts with the sign for depth-sickness: fingers spiraling down from the temple.

Still, they do not throw refuse into the hollow.

They do not lower lamps past the Last Floor.

And when a hollow-wing falters in the sun shaft and descends, the people withdraw from the bridges. Doors are sealed. Garden vents are narrowed. Adults gather children against the inner walls and wait as the ring-shaped creature circles lower and lower, carried on currents no one else can feel.

Sometimes it recovers and rises.

Sometimes it passes the Last Floor.

On those occasions, after the evening crescendo and its three breaths of stillness, the deep pipes produce an additional beat.

It is soft by the standards of the city. Many do not consciously notice it. But hands tighten on rails throughout the hollow, and sleepers turn within their suspended rooms.

The oldest inhabitants open the plates over their ears.

They listen as if something below has spoken a name.
