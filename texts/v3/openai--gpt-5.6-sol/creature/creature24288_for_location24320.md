Those who live in the basin are not the ones who built its doors.

The doors remember a broader creature: upright, tool-bearing, numerous enough to require signs, rails, factories, warnings. Whatever people once crossed the plazas left behind proportions but almost no likenesses. Their handles remain polished beneath mineral skins. Their notices assume hands, schedules, civic obedience. But the present inhabitants fit none of these arrangements. They inhabit the systems obliquely, as weather inhabits a ruin.

The most visible are the pale listeners.

Seen nearby, they are taller than they seem across the plazas, their narrow bodies carried high on four jointed limbs. They have the tentative geometry of survey instruments. Their feet divide into soft, heat-resistant pads that spread on wet stone and contract before lifting, leaving prints like four-petaled seals. The prints fill at once with red slurry and disappear under the next wash of runoff.

Their porcelain sheen is not a hide but a continual deposit. Layer upon layer of silica forms over them as they move through rain and vapor. Old individuals are milk-white, crazed with hairline fractures; younger ones are translucent gray, and something dark can be seen pulsing slowly beneath their surface. Copper stains gather at the limb joints. Sulfur dust yellows the folds of the throat. When a mineral shell grows too thick, the creature stands over a hot vent until pressure splits it. It steps out soft and dim, leaving behind a hollow cast of itself that may remain upright for years.

They have no eyes because little in the basin can be trusted to remain visible. Instead, the front of the head opens into a shallow concavity lined with taut membranes. Lowered over a drain, this structure receives the city through vibration: pumps laboring below, sluice gates changing angle, distant water striking the inner walls of cisterns. A listener can remain poised this way for hours, so still that rain builds rims around its feet.

They do not drink from the channels. At certain drains, just before a pressure release, they place the tips of their forelimbs against the grating. Warm vapor passes through narrow pores in the pads and condenses inside the body. Dissolved metals are separated and laid down in the outer shell; the remaining water circulates through a network of clear vessels. Their slow internal pulse often synchronizes with the buried pumps.

Whether they feed on heat, minerals, vibration, or some combination of the three is unclear. They are never seen eating anything solid. Yet occasionally one bends over a newly cracked paving slab and extends a fine black filament deep into the fissure. Afterward the stone around the opening cools, and the listener’s body acquires a faint amber glow that lasts until the next dark hour.

They are solitary only in space. The city itself may be the medium of their gathering.

One listener taps a pipe with the hard edge of its face. Minutes later, far away, another answers against the wall of a reservoir. The exchange can continue across districts, transmitted through conduits and foundations: single impacts, then pairs, then long pauses timed to the turning of pumps. No two need come within sight. During dense fog, an entire population may be conversing beneath a traveler’s feet while the plazas appear empty.

Their young, if the smaller forms are young, are not carried. After the immense subterranean impact at the darkest hour, a listener sometimes approaches a place where fresh silica has welled up around a drain. It presses its body against the warm deposit and leaves a thin inner layer behind. For weeks the layer remains inert. Then it begins to tremble whenever the turbines engage. Limbs articulate gradually within the mineral blister. One dim cycle, the casing opens and a soft gray animal draws itself free.

Nothing attends it. The first thing it does is lower its head and listen.

The black fliers of the spillway are less easily mistaken for animals. Folded against warm concrete, they resemble scraps of carbonized cloth or the charred leaves of plants that never grew here. Each consists of a central knot surrounded by several membranes, not always the same number. They have neither feet nor wings in the usual sense. The membranes adhere to surfaces when electrical fields are weak and stiffen when charge accumulates.

During electrical weather they peel away by the thousands.

They do not beat the air. They tilt through gradients of charge, snapping from one invisible path to another while violet discharges expose the plateaus. Their bodies produce dry ticks that merge into a sound like sand crossing glass. Around substations they form revolving columns, every creature maintaining an exact distance from the cables. A discharge passes through the swarm; for an instant each flier becomes transparent, revealing a branching metallic skeleton. Then they scatter into the spillway tunnels.

They feed on interruption. Corroded contacts, leaking insulators, and frayed transmission lines attract them. They gather around a fault until the current stabilizes, after which they drop away as though stunned. This makes them seem like custodians, but their repairs are incidental. They consume electrical imbalance and leave behind tiny beads of conductive black glass. Over decades those beads bridge gaps, close circuits, and alter the machinery. Some of the city’s continuing operation may be their waste.

The translucent films in the condensers are more widespread than either creature. They coat sheltered basins, the interiors of gauges, and the warm faces of cooling fins. To touch one is to find no membrane at all—only water slightly more resistant than water should be. Under rising heat, the film divides into hexagons. Patterns travel across it without any visible material moving. If a basin is drained, the pattern reappears in the next connected reservoir, sometimes before the water arrives.

The films may be one organism distributed through the city’s hydraulic cycle. Or they may be the city’s means of calculation.

Operators once noticed that valves near a flourishing film opened earlier and with greater precision. The old diagrams classify the substance alternately as contamination, instrumentation, and “resident phase.” Attempts to sterilize it caused pumps in unrelated districts to stop. Attempts to cultivate it outside the basin produced only pale mineral scum. It seems unable to live apart from the full sequence of ascent, cooling, contamination, descent, darkness, and impact.

A listener placing its face over a drain is therefore listening not only to machinery. It may be listening to the film passing through the pipes. The black fliers may carry fragments of its pattern in their conductive skeletons. Mineral terraces may preserve earlier states of it, layer by layer, as a library no former citizen knew how to read.

There are other residents, if residence includes what never walks.

Something moves the pressure doors when no reservoir is full. Something rearranges the empty molds beneath the pistons. In sealed galleries, gauges sometimes turn together toward a person passing outside, though none measure the same quantity. These reactions are too coordinated to be simple malfunction and too dispersed to reveal a body.

Perhaps no body exists beyond the basin.

The city may have become inhabited by its own circulation. Its thought, if it thinks, rises as steam, condenses into local rain, passes through films and animals, enters buried heat, and returns changed. The towers serve as lungs only from one perspective. From another they are sensory organs. The long bridges are delays built into a nervous system. The numbered cisterns are not being filled in numerical order; their filling is what number means here.

At the darkest hour, every resident becomes still because stillness is also part of the cycle. The listeners brace their long limbs on the paving. The fliers flatten against the spillway walls. Hexagons vanish from the condenser pools, leaving their surfaces blank.

Far below, the immense weight is set into place.

No one knows whether it is a machine component, a geological plate, a valve larger than the central terraces, or the slow heart of the city completing a single stroke. The pale animals feel it first through their feet. Fine cracks race across their mineral shells. In the spillway, black membranes lift. Across a thousand basins, translucent patterns bloom simultaneously.

Then the inhabitants resume what may be maintenance, feeding, communication, worship, or simply life.

They do not occupy the empty city.

They are the forms its emptiness has learned to take.
