They are, at first, indistinguishable from the place.

The moths wear the color of mineral milk. The gliders close themselves into black splinters. Birds become pendant chips of basalt; mice become pale roots thrust into seams. Predators have gone further, giving up the appearance of animals for the appearance of opportunities: a hospitable crack, a dry shelf, a vein of crystal, a patch of harmless lichen. Even movement belongs partly to the stone. A wall shivers, a ledge exhales, soot rearranges itself—and only afterward does the eye understand that thousands of lives have turned in their sleep.

No inhabitant possesses much room. Their bodies are made for folding, flattening, hooking, and being pressed against other bodies. Wings wrap twice around narrow abdomens. Ribs bend. Shells remain soft long after maturity so their owners can squeeze into fractures that may close another fraction during their lifetime. The cliff mice are scarcely mouse-shaped when awake and not mouse-shaped at all when sleeping: they lengthen their spines, point their muzzles upward, and brace themselves inside the rock with alternating pads along the flanks. Several generations may occupy one vertical seam, the youngest tucked into the warmth between the elders’ feet.

The hook-footed birds hatch hanging.

Their eggs are gummy on one side and adhere beneath shelves in close, uneven rows. The chicks emerge blind into darkness and do not learn an upright world. Before their eyes open, they know the tremors of a parent’s return through the claws of their neighbors. Food passes beak to beak along the row, sometimes across dozens of unrelated birds, so that no chick can be said to have been fed only by its parents. A hatchling belongs first to the ledge.

They are not a peaceful multitude. They steal eggs, sever vines, pry larvae from one another’s mouths, and push weak sleepers into the traffic below. Yet crowding has made strict violence dangerous. A struggle can dislodge a hundred animals; panic can empty a warm fissure into a cold one. Aggression therefore tends to be minute and ritualized. A beetle taps another’s shell in a particular sequence. A moth lays one antenna across a contested surface. Birds scrape chosen flakes along the stone, producing thin, local notes. Most disputes end with one body folding smaller than the other.

Their world is not measured by distance as creatures of open country would measure it. It is measured by contacts.

A place five paces away may be unreachable because no familiar surface leads there. A chamber far down the basin may be considered near if a continuous line of bodies, vines, wet seams, and resonant stone connects it to the home crack. Routes have texture and temperature rather than names. The inhabitants remember them through claw, whisker, belly, and breastbone. Young animals learn by joining the traffic and allowing themselves to be carried until the basin’s narrow grammar enters their muscles.

Light matters less than vibration. Many eyes are reduced, clouded, or useful only for recognizing the brief gray of noon. Instead, the inhabitants attend to the mountain through their bones.

The long humming exchanges are not quite songs and not quite messages. They carry condition more readily than information: crowded here, wet below, eggs opening, stone warm, passage narrowed, something large is moving. No individual hears an entire exchange. A note begun by a colony high on one wall passes through layers of basalt, is felt by beetles in a buried crack, altered by the pressure of their bodies, and sent onward through the feet of hanging birds. Hours later, what returns may bear little resemblance to the first vibration. The basin speaks by accumulation and error.

This makes knowledge communal without making it deliberate. No creature understands the whole mountain, but the mountain’s changes are distributed among them. One species tastes new minerals in dripping water. Another detects shifts in air pressure. Another abandons eggs when the low pulse changes tempo. Together they respond with an accuracy none possesses alone. A migration may begin with three moths leaving a seam and end days later with an entire wall quietly transferring itself through a corridor no wider than a thumb.

They have no leaders. At times, however, certain old birds or scarred gliders become living junctions. Others follow the routes they repeatedly choose. A bird that has survived many widenings may carry a volcanic flake for years, opening beetle cases with it until the edge is polished. When it dies, the flake is taken, dropped, rediscovered, and used for another purpose. Nothing becomes an heirloom because nothing remains attached to one meaning. Wisdom persists as traffic: where bodies turn, where they pause, what they touch before entering darkness.

The gray vine is part road, part instrument, part prey. Older animals test it before trusting their weight to it. Mice bite shallow marks into the cords, leaving patterns that may indicate firmness or merely the flavor of the biter’s teeth. Birds carry severed pieces like extensions of the beak and insert them into blind cracks. Beetles follow the scent of damaged vine toward the moisture beading along its wound. When fog enters, the cords swell and tighten, and whole chains of roosting creatures are lifted by a finger’s breadth. This movement is enough to wake entire ledges.

Near the pools live the basin’s smallest and perhaps oldest inhabitants. The colorless shrimp rarely encounter open water larger than their own bodies. To them, each hand-wide pool is an inland world whose shores move whenever a thirsty mouse lowers its tongue. Eggs survive in the wet film inside cracks; adults may be carried downward in falling drops. Thus their migrations are vertical catastrophes. A shrimp can spend its life grazing one mineral hollow, vanish in an instant over the lip, and fall through darkness into a pool whose chemical taste differs so greatly that its descendants become another kind of creature.

The flat worms inhabit water’s surfaces rather than its bodies. They coil around droplets and travel with them. Some can delay a drop’s fall by spreading themselves across the stone, holding it beyond the point at which gravity should have taken it. When they release, they descend together. For a few seconds they are aquatic animals flying.

Everything feeds upon everything else’s mistakes.

Predators wait where traffic must constrict, but hunger here rewards patience more than speed. One may remain open-mouthed until dust coats its tongue. Another imitates a wet mineral seam and closes only when an animal comes to drink. Some are so perfectly integrated into routes that their prey continue using the passage after repeated disappearances. The loss of a traveler becomes simply one more property of the path.

The adhesive fronds are slower hunters. They open under fog like translucent hands laid flat upon the guano. Their captured moths may struggle for hours without drawing attention; wingbeats are too common to signal distress. As the moth dissolves, the frond darkens along its veins. Birds sometimes cut trapped insects free with stone flakes, though whether this is rescue or theft is difficult to tell. Often they eat the moth themselves and leave the frond damaged but fed by the remains.

Then there is the one below.

The smaller inhabitants do not flee its rising limbs. That may mean familiarity, indifference, or an inability to comprehend the limb as belonging to an animal. It appears first as pallor between rocks: a smooth segment where all other surfaces are fractured. Joints follow, too numerous and too widely spaced for any familiar anatomy. The limb settles across a cleft and becomes terrain. Moths sleep against it. Beetles test it with their mouths. Birds scratch it with tools and find neither stone nor edible shell. In time it gathers dust, droppings, condensation, moss.

Its stillness is longer than many lives.

Young creatures are born upon it and learn it as part of the basin. Colonies establish routes along its folds. Eggs hatch in the shelter of its joints. Only the oldest animals may remember that the pale road was not always there.

When it bends, it does so slowly enough that sleepers remain asleep. The angle of a whole neighborhood changes. Pools spill. Traffic reverses itself. Creatures climb to preserve what they imagine is their position, not realizing they are moving farther downward. At last the limb withdraws through a gap no body of corresponding size could pass, carrying its temporary ecology into the fissure.

Some passengers later return, altered by the depths. Moths come back without scales, their naked wings shining like mica. Beetles emerge bearing pale fungal threads. A hook-footed bird may reappear years after its cohort has died, fat and blind and humming a vibration no colony taught it. Such returnees are accepted cautiously. They smell of cold minerals and of the slow animal beneath.

Whether the buried creature eats is uncertain. The basin provides ample prey, yet no one has seen it seize anything. Perhaps it gathers warmth. Perhaps the multitudes clean its skin or seed the passages through which it grows. Perhaps what look like limbs are sensory structures extended upward to feel the compressed weather of living bodies. Its shape may have no center. Each pale segment could belong to a separate animal joined by touch, or to one organism braided through the mountain’s fractures.

It responds to the deepest pulse before anything else does.

A subtle tightening passes through the visible joint. Then the birds close their wings, moths cease grooming, mice brace their spines, and beetle jaws stop mid-click. Silence pours down the basin faster than sound could. For those few seconds, the inhabitants do not seem like a crowd of species. They seem like organs participating in a single reflex.

The droplets strike the pools.

The pulse passes.

Life resumes with such force that the air appears to thicken. No one announces safety. No one commands the return. Millions of bodies simply reopen, each following its own sensation, and together restore the immense sound.

They live brief lives in a place too young to be gentle and too old in its motions to notice them. Yet they are not merely trapped there. They have made compression into abundance. They inhabit each other’s heat, errors, cast-off skins, tools, vibrations, deaths, and remembered routes. They extend the usable surface of the basin by covering it. They turn fog into flesh and fracture into shelter. Their waste feeds the fronds; the fronds consume the moths; the moths line the cracks; the cracks carry the humming; the humming prepares them for the movement below.

From outside, they amount to almost nothing: warmth in an exhalation, musk on the cold air, a faint tremor behind stone.

Inside, they are the mountain’s hidden weather.
