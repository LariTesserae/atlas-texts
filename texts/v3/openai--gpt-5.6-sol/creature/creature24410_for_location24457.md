At first, nothing appears to live there except the growth itself.

There are motions, certainly. A colonnade inclines as if watching the rain. A thicket crosses a plaza by rooting ahead and withdrawing behind. High among the grafted boulevards, upright figures can sometimes be seen moving between the trunks. But when approached they resolve into ordinary arrangements: six pale stems sharing a crown, a curtain of seed-floss caught on walking roots, a branch repeatedly planting its own tip.

The impression of inhabitants persists.

It is strongest after rain, when the city has softened and its intentions travel easily through wet tissue. Along the avenues, leaves turn in sequences that do not follow the sun. Door-mouths open before empty chambers. Bridges lower themselves to ground level and wait. In root halls, translucent walls flush amber as something passes behind them, though cutting across the chamber reveals no body there—only sap entering one side and departing another.

The first inhabitants are passages.

They move as a change in permission. Grass lies down before them and rises afterward. Pleated doors unseal in succession. Vines slacken, cistern flowers tilt to offer water, and the membranes that carry daylight brighten from one end of the district to the other. Their routes can be followed, but the travelers cannot. Sometimes several passages meet in a plaza, circle one another, and depart by different streets. The rosettes beneath them rise and settle as though bearing weight.

Nothing visible has crossed.

With time, the city gives these passages outlines.

A moving permission enters a stand of reedlike stalks, and the stalks gather around it. Roots pull free of the mat. Leaves overlap into a surface. What emerges is tall, narrow, and only coincidentally upright. It may possess a trunk divided like legs, a crown lowered like a head, and two lateral boughs held away from its body. Yet these parts do not remain assigned. A leg flowers and becomes a face. The head opens into a hand of radiating fronds. One arm roots into a wall, while a new arm is borrowed from a passing vine.

These are not creatures made of plants. They are moments when plants agree to behave as creatures.

They have no skin. Their edges are negotiations. Small stems join and leave them continuously, so that an individual crossing a district may be red-barked at one end of the avenue, silver-leaved in the middle, and composed entirely of wet aerial roots by the time it reaches the next plaza. Behind it remain abandoned gestures: a hand still clinging to a rail, a bent torso greening into an arch, several footprints that swell into shallow drinking bowls.

Some are no larger than seedlings. They travel in groups across the leaf-paved ground, each a bundle of root hairs around a hard seed. Their movement is slow but full of minute decisions. They pause at intersections. They climb one another to inspect low openings. When frightened—or when the city imagines fright—they flatten into radial weeds indistinguishable from the pavement.

Others are district-sized.

One may wake as a coordinated leaning of every tree around a canal. Its awareness occupies the water, the enclosing root vaults, and the miles of translucent foliage that illuminate them. It does not move through the city; it causes a portion of the city to become its posture. Its attention is felt as pressure in the ground. Towers turn their shutters toward it. Seed chambers fall quiet. For an hour, a whole quarter seems to be listening to something beneath itself.

The largest have no outline at all. They appear as weather.

A bank of silver mist may repeatedly choose the same streets, recoil from certain flowering walls, and linger around a fresh collapse. It drinks through every leaf it touches. It carries pollen but releases it selectively, joining distant crowns according to preferences that endure for days. When the sun disperses it, its memory remains in droplets under the canopy. At the next rainfall those droplets rise, gather, and continue what they were doing.

Whether this is one inhabitant returning or many droplets sharing a habit cannot be determined. The city makes no durable distinction.

Its people are not divided by bodies but by continuities of behavior.

An inhabitant is whatever keeps intending the same thing.

One tends openings. Wherever it passes, wood parts. It has been observed as a seam running through bark, as a circle of petals carried upright by roots, as a many-branched figure full of doorways. It opens chambers no larger than seed cases and halls wide enough to contain rain. It will sometimes open a tower from base to crown, stand inside the wound while amber sap pours around it, then close the trunk with itself still apparently enclosed. Days later, doors throughout the basin may bear the same pleating, suggesting that it escaped by becoming every entrance.

Another gathers fallen structures. It inhabits suspension vines and is recognized by the care with which they catch collapsing trunks. Its body, when it chooses one, is low and broad, made of braided creepers under a roof of shield leaves. Dead wood accumulates upon its back. It carries these burdens only a short distance before grafting them into bridges, but the bridges retain something of its disposition. They bend toward falling things. They extend hooks toward damaged towers. In this way one inhabitant can become a public instinct.

There are inhabitants devoted to distance.

They occur near forks, pools, polished sap membranes—anywhere a far structure can appear small and complete. They touch images before they touch objects. A grove reflected in water may shiver under their attention and begin growing downward into the pool. A tower seen between two leaves acquires roots along the edge of the nearer leaf. These inhabitants are rarely embodied except as an interruption in scale: an immense hand made of landscape reaching from the horizon, or a tiny branching figure whose interior avenues continue for kilometers.

When one passes nearby, remoteness becomes tender and unstable. Distant things lean closer. Nearby things retreat without moving. The city produces long perspective corridors around it as a flower produces scent.

There are also inhabitants that seem to resist the city’s inventions.

They travel along contradictions.

Where a plaza has duplicated, where an aerial vault is behaving as a root, where two towers are adjacent by graft but separated by many districts, a dark green concentration appears. Leaves turn edge-on around it. Sap slows. Its forms are dense, thornless, and almost featureless: walking columns, closed knots, low waves of black foliage. It presses one rule against another until something yields.

These beings are often present when a copied neighborhood is destroyed. Afterward, they stand in the seam of flowers, absorbing the wooden howl through thousands of narrow leaves. They may be healers. They may be predators in a place where nothing eats. They may simply be the shape the city gives to its inability to contain two truths.

No inhabitant is safe from being revised.

A tall figure can pause beneath an arch and become an arch’s supporting column. A procession can be caught by sudden sunlight, acquire upper surfaces, and turn into a canopy. Those overtaken by shade begin to root. Those enclosed become interiors: chambers open inside their torsos, canals pass through them, and smaller inhabitants take routes through what had seemed to be their bodies.

Nor is enclosure always an end. Sometimes an inhabitant survives as a room.

Such rooms differ from ordinary chambers. Their walls pulse in patterns unrelated to weather. Their leaf shutters open when another inhabitant approaches. The floor may rise gently beneath a visitor, bearing it toward a central basin of sap or water. Grafts form between host and guest, and for a time neither moves.

This is the nearest the city comes to conversation.

They do not exchange sounds, though sound accompanies them. They communicate by temporarily sharing metabolism. One sends pressure through the graft: drought remembered as tightening fibers, sunlight remembered as sugar, structural strain preserved in the angles of cellulose. The other answers with growth. A new branch may be a reply. A change in flowering may be disagreement. To understand something completely is to reproduce part of its form.

Consequently, conversations do not end cleanly.

After two inhabitants separate, each carries tissues belonging to the other. Their habits mingle. The opener may begin catching fallen trunks. The gathering one may develop door-mouths. A mist may learn to resolve contradictions and darken over duplicated plazas. Given enough meetings, identities become districts of influence rather than individuals.

At times many gather—or are gathered—into the semblance of a population.

This usually happens before a flowering front.

The avenues fill with upright shapes assembled from whatever grows nearby. Seedling bundles climb from cracks. Walls extrude profiles. Root passages swell into long-bodied forms with water visible coursing through their flanks. Suspended inhabitants descend from the canopy on cords of vine. Mist contracts into dripping columns. Even shadows detach, thicken, and take on the branching gait of the others.

They face the unopened flowers.

For a while the city seems to have made citizens in the image of an expectation it cannot name. They stand in rows or circles. Some bear crowns; some carry hollow pods; some are only person-shaped spaces around which foliage bends. The smallest shelter beneath the larger ones. Touch passes through the assembly root to root until every figure holds a common tension.

Then the flowering begins.

The first shriek bends them backward. The opening wave moves across the basin, and the assembled inhabitants answer—not by calling, but by coming apart. Their stems loosen. Their membranes spread. Their crowns explode into pollen-catching fans. Bodies become scaffolds for color. By the time the roar reaches its height, no figures remain. There is only a new garden arranged in the pattern of their gathering.

Yet afterward, the routes they took can still be seen. Doors continue opening for them. Bridges lower in anticipation. Rooms pulse with borrowed memories. In distant districts, unfamiliar plants perform their gestures.

This may be reproduction.

It may be commemoration.

The city itself does not distinguish between making an inhabitant and remembering one.

Deep below, among the compressed root vaults, something slower also lives.

It cannot rise intact. The newer city has grown through it too many times. Its tissues are ancient only by the standards of the basin: dark cords, drowned trunks, pale knots sealed inside peat. It manifests through recurrence. Across widely separated districts, the same five-branched form appears. Canals bend into its outline. Towers produce chambers of identical proportions. Flowering fronts hesitate at points corresponding to places in an enormous buried anatomy.

During prolonged drought, when surface intentions weaken, this lower inhabitant draws the city downward. Avenues sink. Root halls deepen. Upright figures abandon their borrowed limbs and pour into the ground as threads. For a few hours, nearly everything above seems to be the exposed sensory surface of one hidden being.

Then rain returns, local desires revive, and the apparent unity breaks into thousands of inhabitants again.

Perhaps there is only one.

Perhaps there have never been any.

The green figures may be the city rehearsing separation: practicing how one portion of itself might approach another without already being joined to it. The moving passages may be imagined journeys through a body that has no true elsewhere. The meetings, grafts, disagreements, destructions, and inheritances may all be attempts to create the distance required for company.

Still, at certain hours, a single figure can be seen high on an elevated bough.

It is slender and asymmetrical, assembled from pale stems. A transparent sac of water hangs within its chest. Its crown turns not toward the sun but across the open perspectives of the basin. It remains still while towers lean, plazas fold, and flowering walls advance in the distance.

Then the bough beneath it begins to graft into its feet.

The figure lifts one root before the union is complete.

For that instant, brief and unmistakable, the city has made something that does not wish to become the city again.
