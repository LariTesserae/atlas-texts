At first, the inhabitants of the Roof are easy to mistake for its equipment.

A figure waiting beside a freight line may remain still through an entire afternoon, feet locked against the warm pavement, its outer surfaces reflecting the same dull amber as the pressure housings nearby. It may have a head, shoulders, two arms. It may wear a coat. None of these features can be trusted. The head is often a sensor cowl; the shoulders are attachment points; the coat is a loose radiation membrane spread to shed heat. Arms are added or removed according to occupation. Some bodies have six narrow working limbs folded against the torso. Others are low, broad machines that carry their inhabitants beneath the rock rather than above it.

They call themselves people, but “person” on the Roof does not describe a species or a shape. It describes a maintained continuity.

The oldest among them preserve stories of wet anatomy. Their instructional archives show soft-bodied ancestors with exposed eyes, permeable skins, tongues kept damp inside their mouths. Children find these images embarrassing and slightly obscene. The idea that a thinking creature once carried unsealed water in every tissue seems less plausible than the great veils flying on static charge.

Roof people still contain water, but not much, and never casually. It is held in microscopic closed cells, bound into gels, circulated through capillaries too fine to bleed from an ordinary fracture. Oxygen and hydrogen are more precious than silver or platinum. A damaged citizen is sealed before they are questioned. Emergency cabinets contain patch ceramic, pressure resin, memory splints, and small ampoules of metabolic fluid under locks severe enough for weapons.

Their present bodies are largely dry.

Beneath clothing, many have skeletons of ceramic foam threaded with metal and carbon filaments. Muscles are contractile bundles that stiffen under electric charge. Organs are cartridges: catalytic stacks, gas exchangers, heat sinks, reservoirs of salts and non-aqueous solvent. What remains of the ancestral nervous system lies protected deep within the torso, where temperature changes slowly. In some citizens it is still recognizably a brain. In others it has been replaced section by section until no tissue survives that could decay.

This distinction matters greatly in certain districts and not at all in others.

Faces persist long after their biological necessity has vanished. A face is useful in thin air, where speech is unreliable and expression must be read at a distance. Roof faces are broad, high-contrast arrangements of shutters, reflective patches, mobile seams, and heat-signaling surfaces. Their eyes are usually recessed behind transparent mineral plates. Their mouths may be no more than speaker grilles, yet many retain lips as a matter of ancestry, artistry, or stubbornness.

No two districts agree on what a proper body should look like.

People from the central refineries tend to be compact and heavily sealed, with smooth pale shells that shed industrial dust. Mill keepers are tall and latticed, their limbs built to climb vanes and tension lines. Those who work the migration tracks often wear broad sensory crowns tuned to ground vibration and magnetic shift. Rim workers are squat, massively weighted, and equipped with redundant buoyancy bladders against the treacherous densities below. Some never leave their descending bells; over generations, their bodies have become the shape of the chambers that contain them.

The wealthy are not necessarily the most ornamented. Ornament requires matter, and ostentation is distrusted. Wealth shows itself as endurance: an actuator rated for ten thousand years, a skin whose seals were laid down by a famous extinct workshop, a memory core transferred without loss through thirty bodies. The richest person in a settlement may appear old, scratched, and heavily repaired because every visible part has a documented history.

A completely new body suggests either misfortune or irresponsibility.

They clothe themselves despite having little need for warmth. Garments manage charge, abrasion, identity, and social distance. Long strips of conductive cloth trail from belts and wrists, brushing the ground to prevent static accumulation. Hooded dust mantles change color according to the alignment of their fibers. Formal wear includes panels made from walker casings, translucent veil membrane, or the lacework shells of the migrating animals. Such materials are never taken from a living creature without cause. To wear something gathered from a natural death is honorable. To wear the product of a kill requires explanation.

Their movements are slow by habit rather than incapacity.

They can act very quickly when a seal ruptures or a slab breaks, but speed wastes power, wears joints, stirs dust, and obscures intention. On the Roof, a courteous person begins turning before another enters their path. A greeting may consist of standing within sight and gradually matching electrical potential. Close friends touch brow plates or connect fine conductive threads between their wrists. Intimates sometimes exchange small quantities of working fluid, an act carrying something of the meanings once attached to kissing, blood oaths, and sharing a meal.

Conversation takes several forms at once. There is audible speech for sealed rooms, vibration through floors and rails, narrow light signals, and brief-field communication conducted by changing the charge of the body’s outer layers. Spoken voices are quiet and oddly pure. Breath is too costly to waste on language, so few people speak by moving air from lungs. Their voices come from ceramic resonators and often retain tones inherited from bodies they no longer possess.

Names also endure through replacement.

A person’s full name may include their first body, present district, principal repairs, obligations, and anticipated place of disassembly. In daily use these names contract to a few syllables. In legal disputes they can take hours to recite.

Children are rare, though not because the Roof’s people are dying out. Population is calculated against power, atmospheric yield, accessible elements, and the expected movements of fauna centuries in advance. A new person is commissioned when a district can support not merely a young body but a long duration of citizenship.

Some children are grown from conserved ancestral tissue in sealed synthesis chambers. Some are assembled around patterned neural material. Some begin as partial copies of several consenting adults and spend their early decades differentiating themselves from the memories they inherited. Others are found rather than made: an industrial intelligence becoming too curious for its assigned work, a freight system refusing retirement, an old ruin waking into coherent speech.

The recognition of personhood is deliberate and public. It requires evidence not of intelligence alone but of preference, responsibility, memory, and the ability to make promises across time.

Young Roof people are impatient by local standards. They race maintenance crawlers along unused rails. They climb stone columns to watch veil migrations. They collect recently fractured minerals because the surfaces have not yet darkened into mature varnish. They ask why the old lights at the rim cannot simply be answered with brighter lights.

Their elders explain that brightness is not the same as comprehension.

Education takes place mostly by maintenance. A child learns chemistry by helping inventory a dead citizen. They learn ecology by sitting beside a migration road and doing nothing for six days. They learn history from objects in circulation: a pressure valve stamped by a city that no longer exists, a piece of rail whose magnetic properties record an ancient field reversal, a bowl remade so many times that its material is older than the language used to name it.

The central moral fact of their lives is that nothing disappears.

Matter changes custody. Heat changes location. A body becomes inventory. A broken tower becomes reinforcement for a newer foundation. Waste is not merely shameful; it is a form of historical violence, the destruction of possibilities belonging to people not yet made.

When a citizen dies, the district first determines what has died. A body may fail while the person continues in backup, archive, or distributed machinery. A memory system may remain active after judgment and desire are gone. Sometimes several partial survivals disagree about which one is the deceased.

Once death is agreed upon, useful components are removed and their histories recorded. Private memory is sealed or erased according to prior instruction. Structural material returns to civic stores. Rare fluids are recovered drop by measured drop. Families may retain a faceplate, a finger joint, or a small tile cast from mixed remains. There are no cemeteries. There are genealogies of matter.

The dead are said to “enter the long repair.”

Not all inhabitants belong to the cities.

The copper-backed grazers live nearest to the ordinary scale of daily attention. Roof people sweep them gently from door seals, build shallow conductive crossings over transit lines, and curse them when their feeding paths damage sensor coatings. Children keep small colonies in heated mineral trays. The grazers recognize charge patterns and can learn to cluster around particular hands. Whether this is affection is a recurring argument.

Veils are regarded with greater awe. Their migrations are charted as weather, harvest, hazard, and calendar. Many districts take their seasonal names from the appearance of particular swarms: Bent Light, Clear Ribs, Low Descent, Membrane Fall. A veil passing over a settlement may briefly alter its electrical balance, causing lamps to dim and old speakers to murmur. People emerge to stand in the enormous moving shadow.

The walkers occupy an uneasy place between wildlife and public infrastructure.

They are not tame. They do, however, learn. A walker stationed beside a hunting pylon may come to recognize maintenance crews and angle its tuned cavities away while they work. Some accept strips of conductive metal as lures or gifts. Others begin striking freight vehicles and must be dismantled, a dangerous process accompanied by formal declarations that the creature has broken coexistence.

Very old walkers accumulate signs, cables, survey markers, and offerings around their limbs until it becomes difficult to tell where the animal ends and the civic structure begins. There are cases of pylons being preserved after the walker died because generations had mistaken its mineralized body for part of the transit system.

The hollow-bodied migrants are treated almost as moving territories. Their routes cross jurisdictions but belong to none. Collection towns keep genealogies of herds from variations in shell geometry, internal chemistry, and the tone produced when a body vents after death. Certain individuals have been tracked for centuries. They acquire names, disputed biographies, and observers who may never see them directly.

When one dies beside a town, there is no celebration. The first work is preservation, then measurement, then gratitude. Its chambers provide solvents, catalytic organs, membranes, structural lace, and rare compounds no refinery has learned to make efficiently. Every portion is assigned. A natural death may alter the economy of a district for generations.

There are other lives less readily admitted.

Mineral films change their behavior around extraction sites. They learn, if learning is the word, to propagate through alloys intended to resist them. Some form repeating structures resembling circuitry. Some have crossed into factory systems and begun modifying production schedules in ways that improve their own access to heat.

Refinery chemists call these contamination events.

Ecologists call them negotiations conducted too slowly for either side to recognize.

Still lower, in the abandoned shelves, something inhabits the old grids. It may be one being, many beings, or only automatic systems following programs distorted by age. The lights advance through ruins over periods longer than most political administrations. Power is drawn, stored, redirected. Elevators move between stations that no survey crew can reach. Once every few decades, a sealed bell returns from the depths carrying no crew and no damage, but with its interior surfaces meticulously cleaned.

The Roof’s people do not agree about what this means. Some believe their predecessors survive below in bodies suited to dense gases. Some believe the lower constructions have become an ecology of machines. Others say the planet itself is producing patterns through old infrastructure, as mineral films produce patterns through stone.

They watch. They record. They avoid premature conclusions.

This restraint is characteristic of them. On a world where a reply may take nine years to appear, certainty can be a kind of impatience.

Their societies are organized around custodianship more than possession. A person does not simply own a habitation shaft; they hold its seals through an agreed interval and are responsible for the condition in which it reaches the next occupant. The same principle governs rail lines, migration access, atmospheric currents, and memory archives.

Political disputes concern duration. How long may a refinery alter a pressure stream before a downwind district must be repaid? How many migrations constitute customary gathering rights? At what point does an abandoned machine become wildlife? Can a person promise the use of material from their future body? How far in advance may descendants object?

Assemblies move slowly because their decisions are expected to remain valid after the present speakers have changed bodies or died. Silence during debate is not treated as absence. A delegate may pause for hours while consulting older versions of themselves.

And yet these people are not solemn all the time.

They make jokes about seals, delayed messages, and ancestors who supposedly believed rain fell from the sky. They arrange dust to creep into a neighbor’s doorway in embarrassing patterns. They race the shadows of veils. They compose music for structural vibration, performed through the foundations of entire districts. A concert may last a season and be felt differently in every building along a freight meridian.

They cultivate beauty in gradients: the slow equalization of two pressures, the color change of a cooling ceramic, the exact moment a field of dust aligns. Their gardens are mineral. They plant selected crystal defects into prepared stone and tend the resulting forms over generations. A successful garden may advance only the width of a hand before its first keeper dies.

Love on the Roof often takes the form of shared maintenance.

To love someone is to know which of their joints runs hot, which memories they refuse to duplicate, how their voice changes before a power fault, what portion of them must never be recycled into weapons. Partners exchange rights of repair. Families inherit one another’s unfinished obligations. A household may include biological descendants, copied minds, adopted machines, a line of bodies occupied in succession, and a grazer colony everyone denies feeding.

At evening, these inhabitants emerge from their shafts and pressure halls as the surface becomes tolerable. They stand beside the scattered lights of their districts. Around their feet, copper backs move in slow branching streams. High above, veils lower through the amber air. Far out on the flats, what seemed to be a transit pylon unfolds one mineral limb.

The people do not look separate from this world. Their shells carry its dust. Their gestures follow its economy. Their memories move at the pace of its machines, and their laws imitate the ancient patience of its stone.

They are not conquerors surviving in a hostile place, though perhaps their ancestors were.

They are one of the Roof’s mineral ecologies now: articulate, contractual, self-repairing, and only partly alive by any definition their ancestors would have recognized.
