What lives in the basin is not divided cleanly into animal, plant, machine, and land. Those distinctions may arrive later, once the place has accumulated enough history to enforce them. For now, everything is provisional.

The smallest inhabitants are permissions.

They drift in the water and lodge in warm stone. They pass through membranes, mineral seams, and the hollow stems of the reeds. Seen closely, they are not particles but tiny distortions—places where distance has been tied into a knot. Around each one, some event becomes fractionally easier. A fin buds where no fin was planned. A crack remains open through the night. Sediment remembers pressure. Water chooses a living body over gravity.

They do not appear conscious individually. Together, however, they behave like attention. Their concentrations gather around novelty: a fresh fracture, an unfamiliar footprint, a malformed swimmer that survives its first hour. They are especially dense near the pylons, where the rock has not committed to being rock. There they make trials. Most vanish without consequence. A few become anatomy.

The copper mats are among their oldest successes.

Each mat is a colony of scales laid edge to edge, sharing neither root nor central body. Their upper faces are metallic and heat-gathering; their undersides are green, wet, and densely folded. When water touches one scale, it opens. The motion passes outward until the entire colony appears to breathe.

They feed on warmth, dissolved stone, and differences between what the basin is and what it might become. This last nourishment gives them peculiar habits. Mats grow thickest beneath the future image in the three-sided shelter. If the image shows a forest, their scales lengthen and overlap like leaves. If it shows a city, they arrange themselves in grids. If it shows the mountain gone, they close tightly and remain copper-side-up for days.

They reproduce by omission. A complete mat forms one scale with an empty center; rain passes through that absence and carries it elsewhere. Wherever the absence settles, surrounding minerals begin arranging themselves around something that is not there. Eventually a new colony fills the shape.

The humming reeds are less communal. Each stands alone because each requires a private circumference of silence.

Below the ground, its root is not fibrous but cup-shaped, a clay vessel built grain by grain from the basin sediment. The visible cup around the stem is only the uppermost of several nested chambers. Water deposited there sinks through the plant and is sorted by age. Newly condensed droplets remain near the surface. Older water—water that has circulated through swimmers, cracks, clouds, or living tissue—is stored deeper down.

The hum comes from these chambers resonating against one another.

A reed can continue a note for months without repeating it exactly. The pale grazers hear more of this music than other creatures do. So do the long-bodied fliers. During the evening condensation, both arrange themselves according to variations too subtle for the human ear: grazers in loose spirals around the pools, fliers in tilted circles overhead.

The reeds may be measuring the basin. They may also be teaching it how to keep time.

The swimmers in the pools are born as bright lines.

A line appears below the water’s surface, bends twice, then thickens into a transparent body. Fins bud around it in rotating belts, each angled differently from the next. The oldest swimmers bear as many as forty. The youngest have six or seven and often turn helplessly in place until the water shows them which direction is forward.

They eat the amber radiance awakened by disturbance. A crowded pool therefore darkens unless the swimmers agitate it, and their ceaseless motion is partly hunger: each body stirs light into existence for those behind it. In calm water they would starve.

Their airborne migrations begin when a pool accumulates too much of its own past. Then every swimmer rises together. Their fins flatten into membranes, their bodies stiffen, and the entire population erupts into the damp air. They do not select another pool by sight. They follow the taste of water that has experienced fewer things.

Sometimes they choose a pool that does not yet exist. They circle above bare stone, clicking furiously, until moisture beads beneath them and the ground relents.

The many-jointed grazers hatch from surfaces rather than eggs. A patch of wet mineral film puckers, pales, and lifts itself on legs. For the first few moments, the young grazer remains attached to the ground by a transparent cord. Through it, the stone supplies the animal with its first pattern of movement. Once the cord breaks, a faint copy of the grazer continues walking inside the rock below.

Their bodies are almost weightless. Broad feet spread their mass across skins of water, while black mouthparts scrape away films too thin to be visible. They consume salt, microbial haze, loose probabilities, and the first suggestion of weathering. Behind a feeding herd, the ground is briefly cleaner and older.

Their simultaneous pauses are not fear, though predators take advantage of them. The grazers are listening through their feet. Deep under the basin, a pressure moves from chamber to chamber in the mountain. Each impact reaches them before it becomes sound. They turn toward the place where the next impact will occur.

No two herds always choose the same direction. When every grazer in the basin faces the open edge, the fliers climb above the cloud and even the copper mats close.

The flat predators are born folded.

At rest, one resembles a scrap of hide dropped on wet stone. When it rises, three legs unfold from creases in its body, but the rest of it remains only questionably three-dimensional. It has no eyes. Instead, it perceives the expectations of approaching prey.

Its hunting field extends several body lengths in every direction. Within that field, color, distance, and slope become negotiable. A shallow pool appears to continue where the predator waits. A rise seems like a depression. A patch of pale sediment acquires the harmless gleam of open water. The animal does not conceal itself; it persuades the world nearby to be misread.

When a grazer steps onto it, the predator folds along lines that were invisible a moment before. Digestion takes place between surfaces pressed too closely together to contain anything. Afterward it opens, and no remains are visible except a dark branching mark that slowly migrates through its skin.

Killed predators become stains because flatness is their more durable state. These stains retain appetite. They cannot pursue, reproduce, or choose, but they can still complete the ancient gesture of closing. Some of the oldest-looking marks in the basin may be dead predators. Others may be plans from which predators have not yet risen.

The long-bodied fliers are the basin’s loudest animals and perhaps its least native.

Their bodies are segmented and narrow, plated in dull mineral colors. The head has no obvious mouth and carries a crown of vibrating pits. Their small wings do not provide enough lift. Instead, each flier is attached to a point above the clouds by a filament that exists only under strain. They swing through the basin on these invisible tethers, correcting their arcs with thunderous wingbeats.

Whether the anchoring points belong to the sky, to a structure at some higher elevation, or to a future in which the creatures have already landed is unknown.

They feed by passing through dense mist. Moisture enters vents along their bodies and emerges as strings of translucent beads, which smaller flying organisms pick away. Their descending metallic calls measure the changing length of their tethers. When several calls overlap precisely, an extra note sometimes sounds from inside the mountain.

No one has seen a healthy flier land. The injured descend until they brush the pools, where swimmers fasten onto them and begin removing their reflection. Once deprived of it, the flier loses rigidity. Its body unlaces into living ribbons that enter the water and take on new uses as gills, roots, parasites, or streams of muscle inside later creatures.

There are larger inhabitants, though they are difficult to separate from weather.

Something occupies the cliff behind the central pool. Its movements produce the evening sequence of impacts. It has no visible opening through which it might emerge, yet condensation sometimes outlines internal shapes: a row of immense joints, a contracting chamber, a fan of structures like ribs or roots.

The grazers treat it as danger. The reeds treat it as a source of rhythm. The pylons lean incrementally toward it.

It may be an animal enclosed while the mountain formed around it. It may be the organ that forms the mountain. During the heaviest rain, the entire cliff warms by several degrees, and the fine cracks on its face arrange themselves briefly into patterns resembling closed eyes—not a single pair, but hundreds, each suited to a different possible head.

Beyond the open edge move the silhouettes.

Their apparent size depends on the flash that reveals them. Some are only birdlike forms close to the mist. Others seem to stride from one unseen summit to another. One recurring silhouette has a broad lower body and a crown of slow appendages. It pauses opposite the basin whenever the black arch reaches its deepest harmonic.

After such visits, an animal is often missing.

When it returns days later, mineral rosettes grow along its joints, and its movements no longer quite agree with the surface beneath it. These returned creatures seek the arch and stand under it during the rain. Water rises from their bodies. Gradually they become lighter, more transparent, and less bound to the direction of the ground.

The seven structures may themselves be alive, but not in a way that requires growth or hunger.

The black arch has moods of temperature and vibration. The shrine dreams competing landscapes. The pylons respond to touch by extending the lines of a hand into patterns belonging to an anatomy not yet present. The ladder descending into stone gains or loses rungs according to what has died nearby. The silver pins migrate toward places where new pools will form. The reflected door opens more readily for creatures without reflections.

As for the white building, it behaves like a shy animal using architecture as camouflage. It appears only when the air is crowded with droplets and vanishes when approached directly. Tracks have occasionally been found where it stood: small rectangular impressions leading in several directions at once.

Yet the basin’s principal inhabitant is the basin itself.

It is not awake as an animal is awake. It has no single intention. It does, however, notice repetition. It preserves what happens often enough and gives substance to accidents that please its local rules. A sound repeated seven times becomes a perch because the basin has learned to trust it. A footprint is erased if it offers nothing new, but incorporated if the tread introduces an interesting geometry. A tool left overnight is not merely buried; it is answered with a mineral counterpart.

The place learns through imitation.

It is learning drainage from the hesitant trickles, soil from the feeding traces of grazers, seasons from the humming reeds, death from the creatures that fail to remain themselves. The structures guide this education without controlling it. The tiny mechanisms bias the lessons. Every evening rain is a period of accelerated thought.

Its errors are the animals.

Its successful errors become species.

Its repeated thoughts become laws.

Someday the basin may settle into ordinary ecology. Water may finally submit to slope. The copper mats may become plants, the grazers acquire weight, the flat predators depth. The great interior presence may die and leave caverns. The arch may weather into an unexplained ruin beside a river that believes it cut its own channel.

But for now, the beings there live close to the moment of invention. None possesses a finished ancestry. Each carries features borrowed from a future body, an abandoned mineral, a machine that was never built, or another creature’s manner of dying.

They inhabit the basin, and the basin inhabits them in return—testing weight through their feet, flight through their tethers, memory through their water, and permanence through their bones.

They are not the first life of a new world.

They are the world deciding what life is.
