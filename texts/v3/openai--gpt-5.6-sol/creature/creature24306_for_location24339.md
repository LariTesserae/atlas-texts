Nothing in the Warm Throat lives alone.

At first its inhabitants are indistinguishable from the chamber. What seems to be a roughness on a basalt fin opens one eye. A pale mineral blister unfolds into a frog. The glossy curve beneath an overhang proves to be the back of a serpent, and the soot-dark walls loosen suddenly into wings. Even the floor is only provisionally earth. Given moisture, it stirs.

The largest population hangs overhead.

The bats are small, dark, and close-bodied, with narrow faces and ears ridged like folded leaves. They roost in such density that no individual outline remains. The colony resembles a heavy pelt grown directly from the stone. Beneath that apparent surface are layers: nursing females, hairless young, old males with scarred wing membranes, restless half-grown animals working their way toward the edges. They sleep inside one another’s warmth. Their claws have curved over generations to fit the basalt’s smallest imperfections.

No bat knows the whole chamber. Each knows a route through it: the drop from a particular ledge, the turn around a hanging rib, the clear span above a serpent shelf, the passage where a wingtip may brush stone without tearing. Together these partial memories make an exact and invisible architecture.

At dusk the colony liquefies. One body lets go, then another, until the wall pours animals. They descend before they rise, falling through the dark to gain speed, opening their wings just above the floor. For a few moments the Warm Throat appears to exhale them.

The swifts return against this tide.

They are slate-colored birds with swept wings and mouths wider than their delicate heads suggest. Their feet are too weak for ordinary perching, but their claws hold the vertical stone. They paste small nests to protected faces using saliva, fibers, and whatever fine material the chamber offers. Some nests have been rebuilt on the same mineral scars for centuries. The birds arrive in spiraling files, clicking into the darkness, each returning to a patch of wall barely larger than its own folded body.

Young swifts learn the chamber by surviving it. Their first flights are short falls into a moving confusion of wings. Those that misjudge a turn may strike stone, tumble into the guano, and vanish beneath beetles before morning. Those that succeed inherit no map except the bodies they follow.

Below them live the pale cliff-mice. They are nearly colorless, though not truly white: their fur carries the brown of dust, the yellow of old bone, the faint pink cast of skin never exposed to sun. Their eyes are small but functional. Their whiskers are extravagant, sweeping the tunnel walls before their faces advance. Whole lineages inhabit the warm banks without ever crossing open ground. They eat insects, fallen fruit pulp, swift eggs when they can reach them, and the rich fungal rind that forms around buried bones.

Their tunnels are not designed so much as remembered. A mouse follows the pressure of old passageways, enlarging some, abandoning others. When a terrace collapses, the surviving mice begin immediately to thread the new shape. Their dead remain in the walls, and later generations pass through the soft spaces their bodies leave behind.

The insects are more numerous than either birds or mammals.

Long-legged crickets suspend themselves from recess ceilings, each limb spread wide enough to detect the smallest vibration. They have lost much of their color. Some have lost their eyes. Their antennae cross and recross the darkness, touching stone, fungus, water, one another. When alarm runs through them, it does not resemble flight. It is a simultaneous rearrangement, hundreds of bodies becoming a different pattern on the wall.

Beetles work beneath everything. Glossy black species cut through fresh droppings. Soft, amber species graze the fungal blooms. Armored grubs burrow in the warmest depths, where gases seep upward and the floor is almost hot. Mites live on the beetles, between the joints of crickets, under the scales of mice, and in the damp debris caught in bats’ fur. There are mites that feed on blood, mites that feed on mold, and mites that feed upon other mites.

The translucent scorpions belong to this smaller order. They are the color of condensed breath, their organs dimly visible through their bodies. Their pincers are slender, their stings minute. They stand beside pools as if listening through their feet. A midge landing on the water may struggle for only a second before one of them takes it.

In the wet seams, the frogs wait with their limbs folded so tightly that they seem unfinished. Their skin matches not merely the color of the basalt but its wetness. Dry, they become dull and granular. Damp, they shine. Their eyes are dark gold, crossed by horizontal pupils. The males call from cracks too narrow to admit a human finger, inflating hidden throats and producing notes far deeper than their size should allow.

A female follows the strongest resonance rather than the loudest sound. She places her few large eggs in a film of water, where the hatchlings emerge as nearly transparent tadpoles. Many pools vanish before they mature. The survivors are those able to hurry when water is abundant and become almost motionless when it is not.

The flat salamanders go further. Their ribs and skulls have narrowed to enter fractures that exclude other hunters. They can lie within a seam no thicker than a hand, pressed between warm stone and flowing condensation. Their skin drinks directly. Their larvae develop in darkness and retain pale fringes of gill longer than their kin outside. When drought comes, the adults retreat beyond sight and seal themselves into chambers with shed mucus, leaving only two small openings through which to breathe.

Above the amphibians, the tawny serpents possess the ledges.

They are thick through the middle and narrow-headed, colored in patches of rust, wet ash, and old guano. Their scales are bluntly keeled, giving purchase on slick stone. Heat reaches them from below, so they need not seek sunlight. A serpent can wait through several cycles of the colony’s departure and return without changing its coil.

Its patience is not sleep. The body attends to every brush of air. When a tired bat lands too near, the strike is short and almost gentle. The serpent catches, constricts, and swallows while wings pass inches from its head. After feeding, it withdraws to a fissure where only the swelling of its body betrays it.

The owl is the chamber’s solitary stillness.

It is broad-faced but sparsely feathered, with bare gray skin around its eyes and throat. Moisture has made silence more valuable than insulation. Its wings are short for an owl’s and rounded enough to maneuver between the basalt fins. It does not chase for long. It watches the repeated routes until repetition fails: a mouse exposed by a collapse, a swift arriving with an injured wing, a bat confused by echo or fever.

Pellets accumulate below its alcove, compact parcels of fur, shell, and bone. Beetles enter them. Mites enter the beetles. Fungi soften what remains. In time the owl’s refuse becomes indistinguishable from the floor, and the floor raises insects that feed the frogs the owl may later eat.

The visiting primates are outsiders, but not strangers. They arrive with rain-dark fur clinging to their limbs and infants tucked beneath their bellies. Their hands are black, narrow, and precise. They dislike the deepest dark, yet they know the entrance galleries intimately. They turn stones to expose beetles, snap reeds to probe soft banks, and strike shells against favored pieces of basalt.

The young imitate badly at first. They choose stones too brittle, reeds too thick, blows too wild. The old correct them without teaching as humans would teach: by taking the object, using it, and leaving the lesson visible for a moment. Nothing is named. Nothing is stored. Knowledge persists in gesture and returns each wet season in other hands.

But the Warm Throat’s most ancient inhabitants cannot be seen as bodies.

They live in the red films around iron seeps, in the black glaze beneath roosts, in the warm paste under the guano terraces. They feed on minerals, gases, waste, corpses, and one another’s chemistry. They make the chamber digestible. Without them the floor would become sterile accumulation; with them it warms, subsides, and feeds new life. Their colonies lace every moist grain. They inhabit feathers, intestines, eggshells, pools, wounds, and the porous basalt itself.

They are the first to receive whatever falls and the last to release it.

Because of them, death in the Warm Throat does not remain an event for long. A fallen swift becomes beetle food, then larval flesh, then frog, then serpent. A mouse becomes fungus and mite, then cricket, then owl. A bat’s bones vanish beneath the floor and return years later as salts feathering from a crack.

So what lives there is not one hidden beast, nor even a crowd of separate species. It is a living compression: hunters fitted to prey, prey fitted to crevices, wings fitted to ancestral roads, skins fitted to moisture that arrives one drop at a time.

Each creature occupies the smallest permission the mountain gives it.

Together they make the cleft seem inhabited by something larger than any of them—a many-bodied animal that sleeps in layers, flies out through its own mouth, drinks through stone, and speaks in clicks, wingbeats, drips, and subterranean knocks. It has no single mind. It has no name for itself.

Still, when the warm air rises and all the dark surfaces stir, the mountain appears not merely to contain life.

It appears to be remembering how to breathe.
