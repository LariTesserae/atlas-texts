At first, it seems that the basin is inhabited by trees.

There are so few of them, and each stands with such composure, that they appear less like a forest than an assembly whose members have chosen their places carefully. Their high crowns do not mingle. Their roots do not compete in the ordinary blind fashion. Each possesses an allotted darkness of mud, an island of moss, a portion of seep and stone. Around each lies a margin kept clear.

Yet the trees are not the only inhabitants, and perhaps not even inhabitants in their own right.

The ferns are stone-raisers. Their fronds are only the visible, light-taking portion of bodies largely occupied with wedges, braces, and damp chambers. A fern may spend years pressing beneath a basalt flake. When at last the flake tilts, the fern does not grow into the newly opened space. It holds the stone upright. Condensation gathers in the slit, mineral dust softens, and a protected pocket of wet soil comes slowly into being. Only then does the fern thicken.

The cup-leaved plants are keepers of silt. They work in groups without touching, each curved leaf catching one or two pebbles and passing them downslope when water loosens its grip. The crescents they make are imperfect, but imperfection seems part of their method: a complete wall would stop the seep or force it elsewhere, while the loose arrangements merely delay it. Behind them gathers a skin of black sediment, no deeper than a fingernail, sufficient to feed a root for years.

The three-leafed plants are distributors. Each of their leaves faces a different quarter, and the holes in them are neither wounds nor decay. They are apertures maintained by growth. When a hole begins to close, its rim dies back in a neat ring. Through these perforations, condensation is divided into threads and delivered to warm points beneath the soil. Something lies at those points—root knots, perhaps, or buried buds not yet permitted to rise.

The vines are wound-makers. Unable to cast fertile seed, they reproduce by injuring themselves with stone. They draw sharp chips against their own nodes until the tissue bruises and opens. Then the opened place descends, becoming root, while the length behind it pales and is absorbed. To such a vine, a cutting edge is not a tool held separately from the body. It is a circumstance placed where the body can use it.

And the trees are surveyors, or founders. Their exploratory roots travel aboveground with their tips hardened into dark, mineral-polished points. They test cracks. They reject some after years of pressure. They return to others, carrying grit beneath themselves, sharpening against the rock, deepening an entrance a fraction at a time. When a root finally establishes a new trunk, the event resembles neither birth nor branching. For a while there are two trees and one body. Later there are two bodies, though the place between them remembers the connection in a faint subsidence of mud.

This makes it difficult to say how many beings live in the basin.

There may be twenty trees, several dozen lesser plants, and broad colonies of moss. There may instead be only a handful of ancient lineages, each repeatedly dividing and assigning its descendants a separate station. The clear intervals suggest rivalry, but not ordinary rivalry. New shoots are removed without struggle. Fine roots find them while they are still soft, unmake them, and carry their substance back into the established growth. No individual trespasses. No border is marked aboveground, yet every border is exact.

It may be that all the vegetation belongs to one organism whose forms have become specialized: trunks for light and distance, ferns for leverage, cups for sediment, pierced leaves for measuring water, vines for renewal through injury. The open mud would then be not emptiness between plants but exposed tissue between organs, continually cleaned. The widely spaced trees would be less a grove than the upright senses of a buried body.

But there are signs against this. A root sometimes encounters another root and pauses. The two lie almost touching beneath the mud for a season or longer. Neither attacks, joins, nor crosses. Eventually one retreats by dissolving its own tip. This has the character of recognition between separate presences—courteous, perhaps, though courtesy may be too swift and animal a word for a decision that requires three years.

Whatever they are, they do not live at the speed of hunger.

They have no need to outgrow mouths, recover from grazing, lure carriers with sweetness, or release multitudes of offspring against chance. Their fruit is an obsolete gesture: flesh formed around an absence, seed chambers clean and empty. Their flowers may be older habits still, opened to visitors that have never existed here, or to visitors so long gone that even decay has forgotten them. The blossoms are not abandoned, however. Water pools in them. Their petals direct droplets toward the stem. A useless invitation has been turned inward and given another purpose.

The dead remain among the living only briefly. Since no swarm of small mouths takes them apart, they dismantle themselves. A fallen leaf releases the bonds between its layers. It pales, clears, and settles into fibers that roots can draw below. A broken stem softens along predetermined seams. Nothing rots because nothing must be conquered by outsiders. Death here is less an invasion than a withdrawal of structure.

The pool may also be alive.

It has no membrane, no visible growth, and no movement independent of the seep beneath it. Yet the plants orient themselves around it more carefully than around any source of water. Trees turn leaves toward it over decades. Roots approaching its green-scaled floor bend away before touching the stones. The perforated plants direct no threads into it. Fallen matter seldom reaches its surface, and when a leaf does fall there, slow currents carry it to the edge without wetting its upper face.

Its changing reflection may be what the basin possesses instead of eyes.

What it shows is never wholly alien. The blue belongs to this mountain, but not to the hour above. The gray cloud resembles those passing below the open rim, though its folds may not appear until the following day. The amber light might be evening held in advance, or an evening remembered. Nothing gathers around the pool to observe these changes. Still, the inhabitants respond. Before a reflected cloud arrives in the basin, leaf tips swell with water. Before reflected brightness becomes real, certain crowns turn by degrees that no single day reveals.

Perhaps the pool does not predict weather. Perhaps it shows the weather of some adjacent fold of the mountain, and the rooted beings have learned to read it. Or perhaps there is no distinction for them between forecast and memory. A life measured in centuries may experience noon, storm, and dusk less as a sequence than as pressures distributed through one long present.

The clearest sign of a common understanding is at the open rim.

No root crosses it. This is not because the stone is poor or the slope too steep. Roots work harder stone elsewhere. They bridge channels, climb shelves, and hang briefly in open air while seeking purchase. But at the lip they stop before contact with the drop. Their tips harden, flatten, and turn aside. Moss ends there too, forming a soft border that never spills downward.

Each plant obeys this limit without testing it anew.

That obedience may be inherited in the flesh. It may be communicated through the water under the soil. It may arise from something in the rim itself: a mineral warning, an absence of reflected warmth, a change in the deep pressure of the mountain. Or the basin may be the true inhabitant, and everything green merely the means by which it keeps its shape.

If so, it is not a creature sleeping beneath the ground. There is no hidden heart, no buried mouth, no central brain awaiting discovery. Its awareness—if awareness is the word—is spread among acts too small to command notice: a root choosing one crack over another, a leaf preserving a hole, a fern maintaining a stone at an angle, a shoot removed from forbidden ground. Its memory is embodied in grooves, clearings, wedges, and arrangements of pebbles. Its thoughts do not pass as signals and vanish. They remain where they were made.

The inhabitants are therefore neither a people nor a wilderness. They are a slow craft that has become alive, or life that has never distinguished itself from craft.

They quarry without mines, irrigate without channels, prune without blades, reproduce without mating, and dispose of their dead without scavengers. They do not speak, but alter pressure. They do not travel, but extend, anchor, divide, and withdraw. Their generations do not replace one another cleanly. A parent becomes a road to its offspring, then dissolves the road.

Only water moves freely among them. It enters as cloud, gathers on leaf and cliff, passes through pierced green surfaces, follows old grooves, slips beneath roots, rises through the pool, and leaves over the rim. It touches every separate body and preserves their separateness. It may be their messenger, their weather, their blood, or simply the one thing none of them attempts to keep.

Thus what lives in the basin is not quite a grove.

It is a dispersed, patient society of rooted forms—or a single being that has chosen to appear as many, leaving generous spaces between its parts so that water, stone, and time may perform the motions it cannot.
