What lives there does not stand apart from the city.

Beneath the streets, the oldest inhabitant spreads as a branching confusion of membranes, pressure fronts, and mineral appetites. It has no central body. Sever a district from the rest and neither portion dies; each continues responding to the gases in its own soil, laying down walls where gradients meet, hollowing chambers where reactions exhaust themselves. Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, are courtyards. Its hesitations become doorways. A pressure it avoids may persist aboveground as an avenue for centuries.

The colony does not know the forms it makes. It cannot enter its own rooms or climb its stairs. Yet it is sensitive to vacancy with an acuity other creatures reserve for warmth or prey. Emptiness draws it upward. Around an undisturbed volume, membranes gather below the surface and begin to sheath the absence in stone. The larger the unoccupied space, the more elaborate the response: ribs, fins, galleries, rows of narrow openings. Debris confuses or repels it. Sustained presence causes it to retreat. Thus its body continually reaches toward what is not there.

Individual organisms within the colony are little more than sealed boundaries around chemical inequalities. They touch, separate, and exchange no messages except alterations in the ground between them. Nevertheless, their accumulated reactions resemble intention. One district may narrow its entrances while another opens a succession of courts. A buried branch may remain still for generations, then wake when a neighboring chamber empties. The city’s migration is the slow migration of these hungers.

Above them grow the grey blades.

They are less like plants than exposed organs of the mineral seams. Each begins as a thread descending from a flake so thin it can travel on the violet tide. For decades the thread appears inert. Then, if it finds the exact boundary between two stones, it inserts itself there and feeds on their difference. A blade rises by imperceptible increments: hollow, rigid, pale as cooled ash. It has no sap. Reactive gases pass through its pores and alter films along its inner wall. When the vapor withdraws, the stored reactions release warmth so faint that only another blade might detect it.

Whether the blades detect one another is uncertain. Their arrangements suggest both fellowship and indifference. Seven may stand at identical intervals, all twisting toward the same buried warmth. Elsewhere a solitary stalk fills a room, branching until its tips nearly touch every wall. The branches do not bend around obstacles. They stop before them, leaving exact slivers of air.

Their lanterns are the closest thing the city has to fruit. At the nightly low, a lantern fractures along predetermined lines and becomes a handful of gliding plates. There is no burst, no scent, no moist interior. The offspring depart as pieces of geometry. Most land, rise on edge, and remain that way until buried by a growing floor. A few find a compatible crack. For perhaps a century they are indistinguishable from debris.

The plated grazers are more readily called animals, though they too seem assembled from the city’s substances. They are low-bodied, six-legged, and usually no longer than a forearm. Overlapping chalk plates enclose a system of mobile powders. As the creature moves, grains pour through narrow internal channels, carrying charge rather than blood. Its limbs do not flex with muscle; differences in charge cause fibrous mineral bundles to contract, loosen, and lock.

A grazer spends long intervals motionless at the edge of an open square. Then all six legs begin at once, and it crosses with abrupt, exact changes of direction. It seldom follows the middle of a street. It favors newborn walls, testing their translucent edges with paired mouth-scrapers. When it finds suitable growth, it shaves off curls so fine they collapse into dust before reaching the ground.

Feeding alters the colony below. Damage summons thickening. Behind the grazer, bitten surfaces swell into rims and niches; repeated bites produce arches, rosettes, clustered spires, and small blind enclosures. An old grazer can be followed by its ornament. Entire façades record a single animal’s route, though the animal itself may already have crossed into another district or settled into stillness beneath a stair.

They appear solitary, but this may be an effect of scale. Two rarely meet. If they do, they stop several body lengths apart. Their shell plates lift slightly, exposing dark seams. Powder shifts within them with a faint internal hiss too soft to carry. After an hour, one turns away. No mating has been observed. Small grazers occasionally emerge from sealed alcoves after the surrounding wall splits, suggesting that they may leave part of themselves inside the living architecture and allow the city to close around it. What develops there—egg, cast-off powder, dormant pattern—remains hidden.

The vault-dwellers are less certain creatures.

Folded beneath ledges, they resemble burnt scraps caught on stone. They possess no visible limbs or openings. Close examination shows layers: a dark membrane pleated around a rigid central grain. During the ground’s exhalation, some loosen their edges as though listening through their entire surfaces. When the vapor becomes deep enough, one lets go.

It does not fly. It rides the heavy gas across a court, unfolding just enough to alter its drift. It may pass three suitable ledges before fastening to a fourth. Once attached, it folds again and remains unchanged for days. No one has seen it consume wall, blade, vapor, or light. Perhaps feeding occurs only at the instant of landing. Perhaps the central grain absorbs tiny stresses from the building. Perhaps the apparent individual is merely a valve or spore produced by the colony below.

Taken across the city, the vault-dwellers gather above active breathing channels. Their distribution shifts before the vapor does. In this sense they form a visible diagram of the buried body—a dark constellation under lintels and cornices. Whether they map the colony or belong to it may be a distinction the city does not make.

There may be other inhabitants that never reveal themselves as bodies. The ticking that passes between façades sometimes follows no pattern of settling crystal. A click sounds in one wall; minutes later, another answers several streets away, then a third at the basin’s edge. The sequence may be mechanical stress traveling through connected foundations. It may also be the tempo of something whose conversations take a night and whose simplest response requires a building.

Even vacancy behaves like a life-form there. It gathers architecture around itself, maintains proportions, and moves as walls rise on one side and withdraw on another. A plaza is not merely an unoccupied place but an active lure. A cleared room acquires a column as a wound acquires a scar. An empty avenue lengthens, makes turns, opens new mouths into untouched ground. The city seems to cultivate absence, protecting it with ever more precise enclosures.

This is what all its inhabitants share: none is entirely separate, and none fully occupies the space made for it. The subterranean colony builds chambers it cannot enter. The blades stand in gardens they do not need. The grazers create ornament by eating and are driven onward by the growth they provoke. The vault-dwellers use the architecture only long enough to leave it again. Every form of life touches the city obliquely.

A multitude would undo the arrangement. Crowded rooms would thin. Busy streets would sink. Walls would withdraw from sustained bodies until no city remained around them. So the life of the basin persists in sparse signs: one grazer under a gateway, a row of seven blades, three dark membranes hanging from a distant arcade. Between them lies the true abundance—vacant courts, unfilled rooms, and streets extending themselves toward no destination.

The city’s principal inhabitant may therefore be neither colony nor creature, but the absence they continually produce around one another. It lives in the distance between the grey blades. It passes ahead of the grazer. It waits inside each new room before the ceiling has finished opening to the sky.

And wherever it settles, the ground begins to build.
