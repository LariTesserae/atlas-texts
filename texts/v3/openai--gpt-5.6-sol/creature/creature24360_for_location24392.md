At first, what lives there seems to be the white multitude in the channels.

They gather wherever the runoff narrows, small bodies wedged against the current in overlapping ranks. Each is scarcely larger than a nail clipping: milk-pale, many-jointed, with a shell thin enough for the greenish workings beneath to show through. Their forward limbs end not in claws but in combs. They hold these open against the water and strain from it exhausted repair dust, fragments of film, unfurled proteins, and the microscopic wreckage of older machines.

A single one is nearly helpless. In crowds they behave like a porous organ. The front ranks feed until their shells darken, then release their grip and tumble backward over the others; fresher animals crawl into their place. During the sudden rain they lock together. Thousands become a living spillway, redirecting floods from delicate wall pores and egg chambers. Sometimes the formation breaks loose as one white sheet and is swept around the ring channel. By the next cycle, its survivors have sorted themselves into ranks again.

They have no eyes. Along each side is a line of pressure pits, little cups sealed by membranes finer than skin. Through these they know the machinery’s rhythms, the approach of feeding water, and the wrongness made by a hunter moving out of time. When the forty-three-second howl rises, all their combs close at once.

Above them, the coin-grazers write across the walls.

They are flat, dark on top and pearl-gray beneath, with no obvious head. A fringe of hundreds of glassy feet carries them over the wet ceramic. At the center of the underside, rotating mouthparts shave away microbial films and softened scale. They advance slowly, but their processions can run from floor to ceiling: narrow roads of bodies following chemical marks too faint for anything else to read. Behind them the wall is left clean and nearly black, inscribed with branching trails that last until the next rain.

Their shells preserve a map of the chamber’s heat. Fine metallic veins grow in response to temperature gradients, so animals from the upper plates carry sparse, angular patterns, while those around the hot drains are covered in dense spirals. When two meet, they pause shell to shell, exchanging heat, microbes, and a minute electrical charge. Whether this is courtship, recognition, or merely calibration varies from one meeting to the next.

The walls tolerate them because their feeding keeps the ceramic’s pores open. The walls also cultivate them. Where a surface has begun to foul, it warms by less than a degree and exudes a trace of nutrient. Processions turn toward it within minutes. No command passes between machine and animal. The ceramic produces desire, and desire performs maintenance.

The transparent larvae in the pools belong to several creatures and perhaps, in part, to the pools themselves. They are almost featureless when first released—clear crescents with a black grain suspended inside. That grain is both gut and inheritance: a packet of symbionts, archived instincts, and obsolete maintenance instructions. Temperature decides what each larva becomes.

In cooler bowls they thicken into white channel-strainers. On warm terraces they flatten and grow the glassy feet of grazers. Those carried into steam pockets split open and emerge as stiff-winged mist fliers. A few washed through amber scale develop into forms not seen for generations: archaic, overlimbed things that crawl briefly into cracks and die there, depositing some compound the structure still requires but no longer knows how to manufacture.

Thus species are less fixed here than occupations. The chamber assigns bodies according to need.

The fliers are the shortest-lived and the most numerous. They rise after rain from seams, pools, nests, and the damp furrows between plates. Their wings do not beat. Four rigid vanes cant against the pressure waves, and the whole animal spins, slips, or braces itself in the moving air. Seen in the dimness, a swarm resembles bright filings arranged by an invisible magnet.

They hatch already gravid or bearing packets exchanged before birth. Their adult interval lasts only through the chamber’s breathless heat. In that time they feed once, usually from the copper frond’s resin; mate if mating is required; and press their eggs into the sweating ceiling. When cooling begins, their steering muscles stiffen. They fall into the channels in a soft, continuous patter inaudible beneath the roar. The crustaceans consume most before the next rain.

Under every suitable overhang waits one of the gray hunters.

They are smooth, palm-sized, and compact, with broad adhesive feet and a blunt seam where a face might be. No eyes interrupt the skin. No nostrils open it. Their mouths are transverse folds concealed beneath the body until they strike. They cling upside down with their legs spread, feeling the plate through pads threaded with fluid-filled hairs.

The machinery is their landscape of light and shadow. Each impact throws a pressure image across the chamber. Every ledge, cable, falling drop, and moving body appears to them as a distortion in that image. They do not leap at motion; the chamber is never still enough for motion to mean anything. They leap at subtraction—at the tiny absence where a grazer’s body interrupts a familiar vibration, or where a swarm changes the density of the mist.

Their eggs are laid in shallow cups waterproofed with resin gathered from the copper fronds. The hunter cannot digest the resin itself. It scrapes the plant with its hind feet, then lets specialized mites living between its toes process the substance into a black paste. A nest is therefore the work of animal, plant, and parasite together. Newly hatched young carry the mites away on their backs.

The copper frond is not quite a plant.

Its fan rises from a knot embedded deep in the wall, each blade thin as foil and veined with channels of orange liquid. During condensation it remains folded, armored against the ceaseless dripping. At the release of rain it opens directly into the downpour. Water flattens it against the ceramic, exposing its underside to a narrow pulse of waste radiation passing through the chamber.

It feeds on that pulse rather than on light. Its copper color is a by-product of layered collectors older than any surviving patent record. Tiny bites appear along the fan where grazers test it and withdraw. The frond dislikes this. It can tighten individual blades hard enough to crush a grazer’s rim, though it seldom does. Dead grazers attract channel-strainers, and their crowded feeding abrades the frond more severely than the original bite.

Its resin smells—if one comes close enough to separate any scent from hot water and metal—faintly of bruised fruit.

The hair-fine reeds around the drain are more ambiguous still. Each translucent stalk is hollow and open at both ends. They do not root so much as insert themselves into leaking capillaries. Warm exhaust rises through them, carrying microscopic particles that adhere to internal cilia. Once a cycle, all six or seven reeds bend together and empty what they have collected into the crack from which they grow.

Something below accepts the offering.

The long inhabitants of the ring channel are seldom visible enough to name by shape. A back surfaces, articulated in dark plates. A vertical breathing slit opens and seals. Once in a while a pale fin touches the wall like a hand. Judged from the intervals between appearances, the largest should be longer than the courtyard, yet they pass through conduits no wider than a person’s wrist.

Their length is not stored all at once in ordinary space. The channel’s old transport geometry folds portions of them elsewhere as they swim. Each animal may be only a sequence of adjacent segments agreeing to behave as one creature. Severing one does not necessarily kill it. A lost section circles through hidden pipes until it encounters the animal again, then reseats itself with a soft mechanical click.

They feed chiefly on grazer eggs. The grazers lay dense, rubbery plaques over warm exchanger seams, and without predation those plaques would seal the terraces within days. The long animals rasp them away with inward-facing teeth, leaving a precise residue: enough eggs to maintain the population, never enough to block the flow. Whether instinct or ancient programming governs this restraint is unknowable. Young channel animals eat indiscriminately. Only after passing several times through the central aperture do they acquire the measured appetite of adults.

Inside that aperture lives the largest presence the chamber permits.

It rises during heavy runoff, upright in the falling water. The first thing seen is usually the open mouth: a narrow oval blacker than the drain around it. Within the throat, rows of green lights kindle one after another. They are not lures. They are colonies of luminous organisms growing on filtration vanes, brightening as water oxygenates them.

The animal anchors itself somewhere beneath the floor and extends upward like a flexible chimney. Rain pours into its mouth, carrying fliers, larvae, loose scale, grazer eggs, and repair debris. Most food is filtered out. The cleansed water passes through its body into reservoirs below. Between floods it retracts, leaving only a slow pulse in the dark.

Its outer skin bears scars shaped like old access codes.

Perhaps its ancestors were hoses: mobile intake assemblies released when fixed drains clogged. Perhaps they were animals modified to serve as hoses. The distinction has been erased by reproduction. Adults bud small, blind lengths into side channels. These juveniles spend years learning the pressure grammar of the installation before they join end to end and become long enough to enter the central shaft.

They are feared only briefly. When one rises, the crustaceans flatten, the grazers halt, and the gray hunters draw their feet close. As soon as the green throat lights reach full brightness, ordinary feeding resumes around it. Terror here is calibrated. Nothing can afford to waste an entire cycle on alarm.

There are mites in the adhesive feet, worms in the luminous throats, films inside the worms, and slow colonies beneath the wall plates whose only visible sign is a blister of warmth. There are predators no larger than repair particles. There are infectious patterns that pass from one grazer’s shell to another and alter the routes of whole processions. There are dormant things sealed in creamy technological stalactites, waiting for a chemical condition that has not occurred in centuries.

And the chamber itself lives among them.

Its plates tighten around touch. Its membranes drink. It keeps certain wounds open because the water has learned to use them and heals others before an egg can be laid. It remembers former hands only as pressure preferences, former instructions only as appetites. No mind appears to govern it, yet local decisions accumulate: a ledge deepens, a warm patch migrates, a rain is delayed until larvae mature, an obstruction is bypassed by making water run uphill.

The animals sense these decisions as weather.

The machine senses the animals as maintenance.

Neither possesses the whole arrangement. Life there is distributed across shells, resin, ceramic memory, throat-lights, pressure fields, obsolete code, and falling water. An individual creature can be held in the hand, but the true inhabitant cannot. It is the cycle that passes through them all: condensing, feeding, hunting, filtering, repairing, wearing.

It has no single body except the hidden mountain that is not a mountain.

It has no voice except the thunder.
