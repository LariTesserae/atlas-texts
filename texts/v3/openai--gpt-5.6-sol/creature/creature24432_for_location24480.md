At first, it seems that nobody lives there.

There are creatures, certainly. The white gliders sleep folded into seams in the cork walls, so narrow and still that they look like blisters in the bark. At the warming of the root pavement they peel themselves loose. Their bodies are soft, eyeless, and almost without weight. Six or seven petal membranes open along each flank, cupping the upward breath of the plateau. They do not fly so much as submit to the air. They rise over the basins, revolve once or twice beneath a canopy disk, then settle miles away with no evident urgency.

They eat nothing that can be seen. When they land, they press their mouthparts to the dark paving and remain there until the root-plate beneath them pales. Later that patch deepens to green again. In return, the glider leaves a small transparent blister on the surface, full of clear fluid and a single black filament. By the following rain the blister has been absorbed.

The canal creatures are more difficult to regard as animals. They have neither obvious head nor tail. Each is a long transparency edged by two silver lines, as if a slender piece of the water had separated from the rest and learned to move. They travel against the current as often as with it. At junctions they pause, divide into branches, and choose several channels at once. Sometimes the branches reunite elsewhere. Sometimes they do not.

If removed from water, one does not struggle. It flattens, evaporates, and leaves behind a damp, branching mark. The mark persists for a day or for a century. No one has watched one disappear.

High in the solitary crowns live the knockers. Their bodies are seldom visible except as a heavy displacement among leaves: a dark oval, a hooked limb, the brief opening of a lacquer-red shell. Their fourfold calls carry through the water-noise because they are not entirely sounds. The first knock is heard in the air, the second through the bark underfoot, the third in the canal water, and the fourth as a pressure behind the eyes. A distant tower answers after an interval that may be minutes or years.

These animals appear to inhabit the plateau. In time, however, they come to seem more like punctuation within it.

The true inhabitants do not cross the landscape.

They are mistaken for the landscape because crossing is not one of the things they do.

An isolated column-tree is the nearest thing the country has to a person, though even this is misleading. Its disk-shaped crown gathers light and weather. Its fluted trunk sorts the falling water into dozens of streams, each carrying a slightly different admixture of sugars, salts, red sealing sap, pollen dust, and minute living threads. Beneath the root pavement, its buttresses spread far beyond the visible tower and meet those of its neighbors. The apparent distance between them is therefore only distance between their exposed parts. Underground—or, more precisely, within the many living layers on which the country rests—they are in continuous contact.

A tower does not have a face. It has aspects.

On one side, the bark may be warm and faintly ridged, sensitive to shadows passing across it. Another side bears deep pores that sample mist. A third carries pendant leaf curtains whose movements register air pressure, distant rainfall, and the flight of gliders. Around the crown are thousands of small lenslike fruits. They turn toward changes rather than toward light: a fresh obstruction in a canal, a warm body entering a hall, the fourfold concussion of a pollinator landing several terraces away.

No single organ appears to govern the rest. Awareness travels through a tower as water does—descending, branching, collecting, rising again by hidden routes. To attract its attention is not to make it look at you. The drip patterns change. Nearby leaves reverse their pale undersides. A cool band moves slowly through the pavement around your feet. Hours later, flower heads at a distant junction may turn inward, though there is no wind.

The tower has noticed.

Whether it is an individual is uncertain. Its roots mingle with the root-plates, and the plates continue into ridges, halls, bridges, reservoirs, and cliffs. A tower separated from the plateau could no more remain itself than an eye separated from a body. Yet towers differ. Each gives the water a distinct taste. Each favors certain curves in its paths and certain numbers of ribs in its galleries. One grows low arches and concentric basins. Another repeats branching forms. A third raises unnecessary stairs everywhere within its reach.

These are not decorative preferences. They are closer to habits of thought.

The broad country between towers is their speech, memory, and common labor. A new frond border may take two hundred rainy seasons to complete, but from the perspective of the inhabitants it may be a quick correction, the equivalent of drawing a finger along a line. A cistern contracting by the width of a seed over several decades may be asking a question. The answer may arrive as a change in the pitch of a spillway on a lower terrace.

Their speech is therefore all around, and almost all of it is mistaken for maintenance.

The roar of water is not merely produced by their architecture. It is articulate. Hollow stems amplify chosen frequencies. Leaf membranes tighten or slacken. Pools alter their volume by imperceptible degrees. The plateau speaks in standing waves too large and sustained for an animal listener to separate. A brief utterance may occupy an entire basin and last longer than a human life. What sounds like the same continuous note is always changing in its inner structure.

The four-beat knocking of the crown creatures matters because it is fast. The knockers may be messengers, parasites, sensory organs that can detach and crawl, or small independent lives tolerated for their usefulness. They carry packets of pollen and red sap between towers, but they also interrupt the deep hydraulic speech with sharp, synchronized impacts. Their calls may mean danger. They may mean rain. They may simply provide the inhabitants with something like surprise.

The plateau’s builders are not a vanished people. Building is their posture.

A roofed hall is a thought held open. A bridge is a maintained relationship between two water systems. A staircase is not necessarily meant for feet; its sequence of raised edges regulates heat, catches mist, and repeats a pattern used elsewhere in the inhabitants’ communication. Paths are zones where signals travel quickly through smooth bark. Their apparently purposeless curves avoid buried knots of memory or circle sites where two root networks once met and exchanged living tissue.

The empty spaces are not empty to those who made them. Some contain columns of warm air. Some hold particular arrangements of echo. Some are kept clear so that a tower may feel the unbroken passage of a cloud shadow across its roots. If an object remains in one of these spaces, the country does not engulf it. Instead, paths begin to bend. Dripping shifts to other chambers. New fronds face the obstruction from a respectful distance.

This adjustment can take generations of animal life.

The inhabitants are patient, but not inert. Their slowness is full of decisions.

They appear to possess no death. Individual stems cease, soften, and are reabsorbed, but nothing leaves the circulation. A tower may gradually transfer its flows into the surrounding pavement until the visible trunk becomes hollow. The hollow remains standing, supplied by neighboring roots, sometimes for thousands of years. During that interval, a new column may thicken elsewhere. Is this succession, migration, reproduction, or merely a change in where one being chooses to be most awake?

Their young, if they have young, may be the pale scaffolds along the basins. These delicate rootlets continually sketch ravines, deltas, cracked banks, and loose-looking debris. They seem to be practicing change.

The adults preserve them at the threshold of consequence. Water is allowed to fall, but not to erode. A lip may appear ready to crumble forever because crumbling is not a failure here; it is a form being studied. The young country rehearses instability under perfect protection. Every apparent scar is grown deliberately, examined by water, and retained.

This may explain the overwhelming freshness of the place. The plateau is not unfinished. It is attentive to beginnings.

Its inhabitants distrust completed forms—or cannot perceive them. They build the instant before collapse, the gesture before movement, the channel before it deepens. Their architecture consists of arrested possibilities because possibility is what they cultivate. They have no ruins because they do not permit anything to become wholly past.

The red sap reveals another aspect of them. Where the living surface is damaged, sap wells up immediately, bright as newly exposed metal. It closes the wound, but before closing it takes an impression. Pressure, heat, foreign oils, the shape of the edge: all are preserved in microscopic layers within the seal. Long afterward, that record is carried through the vascular system.

In this way the inhabitants remember touch.

They may remember every footfall.

A traveler crossing the plateau is not pursued or addressed. No figure emerges from a hall. No branch reaches down. Yet the country’s response spreads quietly outward. A sequence of pools tightens. A tower alters its dripping. Gliders rise from places along the traveler’s route, each taking the warm air toward a different terrace. Far below, behind curtains of mist, flowers turn their ringed faces upward.

The traveler may feel alone because nothing here shares the animal need to come near.

But solitude is not what the plateau offers. It offers inclusion.

To stand there is to become, briefly, a new condition of the place: a weight distributed among root-plates, a warmth crossing an open chamber, a source of salts, breath, shadow, and unfamiliar rhythm. The inhabitants do not distinguish sharply between a visitor and an event. Rain is an event. Damage is an event. A glider is an event that returns. A stranger may also be one.

Perhaps the paths and bridges were grown for such events. Perhaps the halls are instruments waiting for bodies whose echoes have not yet been learned. The country’s readiness may not be hope in any recognizable sense. Still, the passages remain clear. The surfaces are kept springy. Water is provided at every distance. Nothing poisonous flowers beneath the open roofs. Nothing with teeth waits in the canals.

And beneath it all, through the dark interlocked plates, something passes from tower to tower: immensely slow, continuous, and alive.

There may be many inhabitants speaking.

There may be one inhabitant thinking in groves.

Or the distinction between society and body may be another unfinished form the plateau has chosen to preserve.
