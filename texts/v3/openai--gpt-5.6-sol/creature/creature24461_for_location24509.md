The people of the basin are not especially small, though visitors often remember them that way. It is their manner of occupying space. They turn sideways before reaching a narrow place. They fold their knees close when seated and sleep with elbows tucked in. Two people passing on a stair touch wrists, each briefly taking the other’s balance, rather than attempting to keep apart. Even in the wider courts they seldom stand with their feet spread. They have inherited the posture of those who expect a wall, a jar, or another body to be near.

Their hands are seldom empty. A person crossing the settlement may carry a covered bowl, a coil of damp cord, three roof pegs, a borrowed blade wrapped in felt, or a cup placed beneath some dependable trickle. Loads move in pieces. No one carries an entire task if it can be divided among ten hands.

Their palms are broad with plaster work and pale in the creases. The nails are kept short because clay collects beneath them and because fingertips are used for testing seams. Many have a slight thickening along the outside of the hand from pressing walls. The eldest can distinguish cold stone from moving water through several layers of plaster. They do not knock. Knocking confuses the sound. They rest the heel of the hand against a surface and wait.

Everyone listens, but not to the same things.

Children hear the upper city first: sandals on roof paths, ladles at jars, the slap of wet cloth, birds scratching at rush troughs. Builders listen for weight—the complaint of a timber peg, the granular sigh before clay gives way. Jar keepers hear differences in dripping and can wake when a familiar vessel receives water at the wrong interval. Those who tend the buried reservoirs listen with cheekbones and teeth. They lie along floors in the Empty Place, mouths slightly open, while another person admits a measured cup of water into a distant drain.

A few are said to hear water before it moves. These are not priests, exactly. Some are old women. Some are children who later lose the ability. One is a potter with a damaged ear who says he hears nothing at all, only feels an uneasiness in his jaw. They are called under-listeners, and people treat them with a mixture of respect and irritation. Their warnings often require walls to be opened.

The basin people dress in layers that can be removed, dried, exchanged, or sacrificed to a leak. Their outer garments are woven from reed fiber and coarse imported thread, softened by repeated washing until they fall close to the body. Over one shoulder many wear a narrow hide panel, useful when carrying jars or leaning into wet masonry. Hems are short. Loose sleeves and trailing cloth are considered both impractical and faintly arrogant, an announcement that the wearer expects empty space around them.

Color enters their clothing slowly. Gray, cream, smoke-brown, and the dim green of old plaster are ordinary. Strong dyes come from beyond the rim and are often used not for whole garments but for cords, edging, and small stitched signs. Blue marks a household responsible for a deep cistern. Red belongs to work involving unstable clay. White cord indicates that a tool or vessel is being carried on public business. Children tie colors together without permission and are made to explain what new office they have invented.

Hair is worn close, braided tightly, or bound high enough not to brush damp walls. A person with long unbound hair is presumed to be grieving, ill, newly arrived, or making a deliberate disturbance. During repairs, even mourners bind it.

They smell of wet mineral, lamp soot, reed soap, old wool, and the sour-clean odor of fresh plaster. Because rooms share air, strong perfume is disliked. So are foods that smoke excessively. People who return from beyond the rim carry the astonishing smell of open ground on them—dust, grasses, animal dung, sun-warmed bark—and neighbors find excuses to stand near without appearing to do so.

Not everyone leaves the basin. Some inhabitants reach old age having seen the outer lands only from a rim platform. They know those lands as sources: grain comes from one direction, timber from another, black glass from farther away than anyone can sensibly imagine. The outside is understood through the burdens it sends inward. A mountain is where a certain stone begins. A marsh is the place from which roofing reed arrives. Distance is counted in changes of hands.

Those who regularly cross the rim are a recognizable sort. They walk too quickly when they first return and take several days to recover the basin’s pace. They are louder without meaning to be. They have learned to call across fields, to judge weather without touching a wall, and to sleep where no roof presses above them. Children follow them for stories, but adults examine their sandals for seeds.

Any seed found in the tread is removed over a bowl.

The people are cautious about family. In a place where rooms join and divide, descent alone cannot decide belonging. A child belongs first to those who answer when it cries, then to those who feed it, then to the wall whose maintenance mark hangs above its sleeping place. Many children sleep in one household, eat in another, and are disciplined by a third whose roof they cross. They learn early that privacy is not the same as secrecy. Privacy is a courtesy produced by lowered eyes, softened steps, and the decision not to understand every sound one hears through plaster.

Marriage, where practiced, is called joining thresholds. It does not necessarily mean that anyone moves. Sometimes a new opening is cut between rooms. Sometimes an old opening is sealed so that two people may establish a boundary of their own. Both acts can mark a union.

Before an opening is cut, the households pour dyed water across the roofs above and watch where it travels. Thus courtship may end because of drainage.

This is not considered unromantic. A person willing to alter a gutter for another has made a serious declaration.

Names are often ordinary words made particular by use: Cup-at-Dawn, Third Lintel, Silt Hand, Little North Step. A child may receive several temporary names before one adheres. The lasting name usually comes from an accident, a habit, a place of sleep, or an early act of usefulness. Outsiders sometimes mistake these names for titles. The basin people find outsiders’ permanent birth names presumptuous. How, they ask, can anyone know at birth what a person is called?

The city has officials, but authority is distributed like water and is expected to keep moving. There are wall keepers, route rememberers, jar counters, silt judges, cord signers, and those who settle disputes over seepage. A person may hold great authority concerning one drain and none concerning the room through which it passes.

The most trusted are not those who speak best but those who can repeat a route exactly: roof, lip, gutter, elbow, lower trough, jar, overflow, old western throat. Public arguments are conducted with bowls of colored water whenever possible. Assertions are poured out and observed.

A liar is described as someone whose water goes nowhere.

Punishment rarely takes the form of confinement; there is too little unused room. A person who blocks a channel, damages a shared wall, or conceals a spring may be assigned to the most exposed maintenance routes. Serious offenders lose the right to receive water directly. Their household must collect its portion through a neighbor, making the offense present each day as dependence and embarrassment.

Exile is rare and feared less for the loss of shelter than for the loss of nearness. Basin people are accustomed to being overheard, brushed past, awakened by another family’s repairs. Alone beyond the rim, some cannot sleep. The silence there seems too wide and without structure. More than one exile has returned during rain and sat outside the highest houses merely to hear the gutters.

Children are everywhere and seldom visible for long. They travel by routes adults cannot use: gaps behind jars, roof hollows, abandoned flues, low passages beneath sleeping shelves. They are given errands as soon as they can distinguish clean runoff from wash water. Their first formal lesson is the path from their sleeping place to the nearest public jar in darkness. Their second is how to call for help without shouting.

They play collapse. They build tiny houses from broken tile, divide them into rooms, flood them, and argue over who failed to maintain the miniature drains. They make families of pebbles and marry them by cutting little thresholds. They float chips of plaster in settling bowls despite repeated prohibition. When translucent frogs appear, children often find them first and become briefly solemn, posting themselves beside the cistern to prevent anyone from dipping a ladle.

Adolescents seek the few dangerous forms of solitude available. They climb to the rim tree and sit among its burdened branches. They crawl into dry channels beneath the Empty Place. They arrange secret meetings in rooms that technically belong to no household because they are in the process of becoming walls. Their rebellions are spatial. They move ladders. They sleep on forbidden roofs. They leave a door uncurtained.

Old people move downward.

This is partly practical: lower rooms have fewer stairs, steadier temperatures, and more constant water. It is also customary. With age, a person passes from roofs and exposed ways toward the buried memory of the settlement. Elders tend jar lids, sort dredged silt, smooth chipped rims, and teach the names of drains no longer visible. They sit in passages where younger bodies would obstruct traffic and become, in time, part of how traffic moves. A hand touches an elder’s shoulder; a basket passes over their knees; news collects around them.

They remember previous arrangements. Where a visitor sees a blank wall, an old person may see a room in which three sisters once slept, then a pottery shelf, then a breach, then a sealed spring. Their memory is not chronological. It is structural. They tell the past by saying what stood above what.

The dead do not remain in the basin whole. There is neither ground nor room for graves. Bodies are carried beyond the rim, but something is always kept: a tooth, a thumb bone, a lock of hair worked into plaster fiber, a pinch of ash sealed behind a household mark. Builders object to large relics because they weaken walls. This, too, has shaped theology.

The dead are said not to inhabit their remains. They inhabit repeated routes.

A woman continues where people still pass bowls through the hatch she opened. A mason persists in the slight inward tilt he gave his walls. A child may survive in the name of a drain it once discovered by falling through a floor. To be forgotten is not to lose one’s name. It is to have every path one made become unusable.

Their sacred practices are similarly difficult to separate from maintenance. Offerings are small and chosen not to foul water: a breath across a jar, a clean pebble placed on a lid, a thread tied above a leak. Flour, blood, oil, and flowers are unsuitable. Anything that rots is an insult unless removed before nightfall.

They do not worship water as kind. Kindness would imply preference. Water enters nurseries and refuse pits without distinction. It undermines shrines and fills drinking jars. It reveals old work and destroys new work. What the basin people honor is its persistence and the fact that it can be followed.

The House Without a Roof has no permanent keeper. Care of it passes among the streets according to a sequence old enough that no one agrees where it begins. The person on duty sits outside the circular wall and prevents shadows, dust, or curious fingers from troubling the central depression. They do not stare at the water. Looking too intently is thought to produce false trembling.

When the surface moves at dawn, no bell is struck. Bells would be useless there. Notice travels hand to shoulder, shoulder to doorframe, doorframe to roof ladder. Lids are secured. Sleeping children are shifted away from outer walls. A potter covers unfinished clay. The under-listeners are fetched without ceremony.

Then the crews go out.

They carry no uniform beyond pale cords tied around their wrists. One has cups, one has wedges, one carries lamps, one carries fresh plaster under wet cloth. The smallest member is often sent ahead into constricted spaces. They move through bedrooms, across roofs, behind hearths, under platforms where families draw up their blankets to let them pass. At intervals they stop and spread their hands upon the city.

Others wake and do the same.

For a little while, before the new leak is found, the basin appears to contain a single listening creature: hundreds of bodies held still within the walls, each touching some portion of the damp structure, each waiting for the hidden movement to declare itself.

Yet they do not think of themselves as one people in the way outsiders suppose. They quarrel by street, roofline, jar, and elevation. Those of the upper edge accuse the low households of hoarding settled water. The low households say the rim people send them every broken tile and foul runoff. Potters resent plasterers. Plasterers claim potters consume the best clay. The keepers of old drains distrust those who build new ones. Families maintain grudges so long that the original rooms involved no longer exist.

But when someone says, “The wall is wet,” ownership loosens.

Hands arrive. Cloth is lifted. Shelves are emptied. A sleeping place is dismantled. Someone remembers a sealed channel two generations below the present floor. Someone else brings the street’s black-glass blade without waiting for the knot-cord. Children are sent along the route to warn the jars. The wall is opened.

Cold water appears in the cut, carrying silt from some forgotten interior of the basin.

The people lean close, not in surprise, but recognition. They taste it from their fingertips. They watch its color. They ask where it has been.

Then, together, they decide where it will go now.
