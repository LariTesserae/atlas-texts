At first it appears that nothing lives there.

There are only processes: fern fronds lifting after the flood, microbial films whitening over fallen leaves, reeds combing grit from brown water. None recoil from approach. None hunt, hide, court, or cry out. The district has no small, private hungers. It contains no eyes.

Its inhabitants are discovered by function rather than movement.

In the sweating joints of the mains grows a black-rooted moss whose underside is hard and ridged like a gasket. It feeds on mineral seepage and the minute warmth escaping through cracks. As pressure rises, its tissues swell, forcing themselves deeper into the flaws; when pressure falls, they relax and admit a measured trickle. Whole colonies pulse without visibly changing shape. Older patches contain rings of wire, washers, and fragments of valve packing, incorporated as a tree might incorporate a nail.

The pale vines on factory fronts are more muscular. They anchor themselves in screw holes and beneath iron lintels, sending tendrils toward shutters, vents, and counterweights but never toward open soil. Heat softens them. Condensation stiffens them. Through this simple alternation they pull the buildings open and closed. Their leaves are thick, almost windowlike, with dark channels that fill and empty in time with the underground knocking.

The reed beds are inhabited by finer things. Every reed is hollow, but not empty. Warm water climbs the stems by capillary action, leaving dust and metal salts on internal hairs. At the height of the flood the reeds produce a low vibration, felt more readily in the jaw than heard. Neighboring stands answer at slightly different pitches. The resulting interference settles suspended grit into orderly bands across the mud.

They have no insects to fertilize them. They flower inwardly, if flowering is what it is. Along the inside of each stem, soft nodules exchange pale dust whenever the pumps shake them together. By the time a stalk falls, it already contains the beginnings of several others.

There are the metallic seeds, too: thin discs, scales, and slivers that ride runoff into rust seams. They are not truly metallic. Their skins merely concentrate iron until they shine. Once lodged, they split into fans of colorless rootlets. Most become moss or vine according to where they land. Those trapped near electrical leaks grow into dark, rigid knobs around the cable, drawing current through wet fibers and releasing it slowly as heat. Those carried into masonry flatten into lichenlike circuits. Those reaching the pump house descend.

No single growth seems capable of choosing what it becomes. The choice is made by pressure, salt, vibration, current, and position—as though the district assigns each seed its trade.

Beneath all of them are the pearly films.

They are the oldest-looking residents, though they may be renewed hourly. They have no fixed outline. They spread over dead matter in opalescent sheets, digesting leaves, oil, insulation, paint, and occasionally one another. When fresh rain dilutes the streets, they withdraw into brick pores so completely that the surfaces appear clean. As heat returns, they bloom again.

Under magnification, their cells would not look entirely biological. Each contains a speck of dark mineral turning in its center. Millions of these specks align when the pumps descend. For that instant, every film throughout the district points the same way.

One might call these things a community. One might say that numerous species live in mutual dependence: sealers, filters, ventilators, scavengers, conductors. Yet the distinctions do not hold for long. A vine cut from its wall does not merely die. Nearby moss produces pale runners; reeds alter their vibration; microbial skins gather along the cut. Within several cycles, the missing length has been reconstructed from several organisms, none resembling the original, but the shutter resumes opening at the proper time.

What persists is not the creature. What persists is the task.

The machinery has acquired the same quality. No pump is wholly machine anymore. Roots descend through lubrication ports. Mineral crusts replace worn bearings in slow-growing layers. Flywheels carry bands of living tissue around their rims, contracting where the metal has thinned. Deep below the flood line, where the pistons should be, there are likely no pistons in the old sense. The rods rise bearing translucent membranes, fibrous sleeves, and ropes of black vegetation wet with hot water. Their motion is too regular to be wild and too adaptive to be merely mechanical.

The district lives there, though not as an animal lives in a district.

Its memory is stored in flood marks, root thickness, corrosion, sediment, and the hand-altered gauges. It remembers a failure by growing around the place where the failure happened. It measures time in pressure cycles. Its senses are distributed through trembling leaves, conductive water, strained iron, and the changing note of pipes. If a culvert blocks, it feels the obstruction as a delay between one bell and the next. If a roof collapses, the impact passes through flooded foundations and returns as an altered rhythm. Months later, reeds appear in the correct lot to filter the new debris.

It may once have had operators. It no longer distinguishes them from weather.

It has no central mind hidden in the pump house. The pumps are indispensable but not sovereign. They are a powerful cluster of organs, old enough that the rest of the body has grown around their habits. If there is thought, it occurs between strokes: in the half second during which pressure travels under nine streets, shutters tighten, microbial minerals align, and every rooted thing receives the same concussion.

Such thought would be slow in some ways and instant in others. It might take ten years to answer damage. It might coordinate a million leaves at once. It would have no conception of walking, because no part of it has ever needed to go elsewhere.

The dry shelter is the exception.

Nothing grows inside it. The bench remains bare, not because life cannot enter, but because the life of the district withdraws precisely at its threshold. Roots circle the paving. Films stop at the iron sill. Vines bend away from the cloudy glass. The shelter is maintained as an absence, carefully and continuously, like the empty chamber of a heart.

The upward column may be an organ of release. Water entering it carries spores, salts, rust, fragments of leaf, and occasionally minute knots of root tissue. These rise out of sight. Perhaps they are waste. Perhaps they are seed. Perhaps the greater city beyond the floodwall is being colonized invisibly through the fog, one ascending droplet at a time.

But the bench suggests another possibility. It is proportioned for a body the district no longer contains.

The empty space may be part of what lives there too: a preserved shape, an old expectation. The machinery continues to make room for an operator. The garden keeps that room dry. Every cycle delivers water upward and leaves the seat untouched, as if awaiting someone who departed before roots entered the gates.

At the deepest stroke, the white roots around the shelter contract. The column falters. For one second the district ceases all motion.

That pause is not failure. It is the nearest thing the place has to attention.

All its innumerable residents—the gasket moss, the shutter vines, the resonant reeds, the pearly decomposers, the mineral seeds, the buried hybrid engines—become still together around an empty bench.

Then, finding no one there, they continue.
