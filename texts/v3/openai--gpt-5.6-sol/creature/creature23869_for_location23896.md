What lives on the last plateau cannot always be separated from what the last plateau is.

At the smallest scale, its inhabitants are quick, innumerable, and temporary. Fine-legged grazers move across the silver moss in processions, each animal light enough to stand on the trembling skin of a pool. They eat only the newest growth, passing over the same ground in waves so precisely spaced that the moss seems to be combing itself. When wind threatens to lift them, they lock legs with their neighbors and become a flexible net. A storm may roll the entire colony for kilometers. Wherever it comes to rest, the animals disentangle, resume feeding, and behave as though the journey were merely another stride.

Among them live the pore-flocks: transparent-winged creatures that spend much of their lives inside the root-mat. They erupt in sheets when pressure falls, skim the ground with a dry whisper, and dive again at the first green flicker of atmospheric charge. Beneath the surface they fold their wings around their bodies and pass mineral grains, spores, eggs, and droplets from chamber to chamber. Their migrations are the plateau’s smallest circulatory system. A distant marsh may change color because a flock entered the moss three valleys away.

The glossy seep-dwellers are easier to recognize as animals. They resemble black amphibians until they inflate their throats and reveal branching blue organs beneath the skin. These organs brighten before lightning, and whole pools answer one another in pulses. The creatures appear to predict storms, but prediction may be the wrong idea. Their flashing helps assemble the charge. Thousands calling together can coax a dynamo pod into discharge or persuade low cloud to descend. During quiet weather they sit with their mouths open, tasting voltages too faint for other life to notice.

The larger browsers are built for a world without reliable ground. Six limbs support broad, soft feet that spread over water and moss alike. Their shag hangs in wet ropes, inhabited by insects, seedlings, and small blinking fruits. They feed by walking onto pools and lowering their long heads through the surface to crop the hidden sides of floating leaves. From a distance they seem headless: dark bodies drifting above their own reflections while their necks disappear into black water.

They do not make nests. When exhausted, they stand over warm fermentation chambers and sleep while heat rises through their feet. Calves begin life as hard green fruits carried in the adults’ coats. After sufficient rain the fruits split, and the young drop already walking. In old age, the browsers slow, green, and root. Their legs sink into the peat; their backs flatten beneath moss; their last breath opens rows of leaf pores along the spine. Herds revisit these living islands for generations, feeding around them but never on them.

Through the deeper peat travel the pallid burrow-eels. They have no permanent tunnels. Saturated matter loosens ahead of their blunt faces and closes behind their tails, leaving only a wandering ridge at the surface. They move uphill as readily as down, following pressures rather than slopes. Some are scarcely longer than a finger. The oldest are said to be long enough for their heads and tails to experience different weather.

They rise during pumping cycles. A red or pearl-colored loop may break through the moss, arch for several minutes, and submerge again elsewhere. Smaller animals gather to touch it. The contact warms them, but it also gives them fragments of alien behavior: a grazer may begin circling an absent tree; an amphibian may call in the rhythm of waves on a distant shore. The eels pass through ancient root memory and carry pieces of it in their nerves.

Predators wait where those memories collect. At rest they resemble wind-thrown knots of black quills. No eyes or limbs are visible. When prey approaches, the knot comes apart into a running arrangement: hooked spars strike the ground, central cords tighten, and a narrow mouth appears only for the instant of the bite. The same predator may unfold differently on water, peat, or the vertical root walls at the plateau’s edge. Its body is not a fixed anatomy so much as a set of possible tensions.

They hunt warmth more often than flesh. A crowded heat vent attracts them, but they rarely attack immediately. Instead they lie around the gathering and absorb its movement—the tremors of feet, the pulse of electrical organs, the opening and shutting of throats. When the vent cools and the animals scatter, the predators pursue by replaying those motions through their quills. For hours afterward, empty ground may twitch with an imitation of the vanished crowd.

Not all the herds are animals. Juvenile reeds pull their young roots free and walk on stiffened fibers, gathering in wandering stands. Their seed heads turn together against the wind. They pause at mineral seeps to drive feeding tubes into the mat, then uproot and continue. Over several seasons their pace slows. Individuals spread apart, choose positions according to currents no eye can see, and settle into adulthood as piston fields. There they may remain for centuries, pumping water through the plateau. Yet on certain storm nights even ancient reeds wrench themselves loose. Valleys wake to the sound of a forest marching.

Other growths are less mobile but no less animate. Turbine flowers furl their vanes when approached, then reopen one blade at a time, testing the shape of the disturbance. Their bearing rings are lubricated by colonies of minute organisms born inside the stem. Dynamo pods grow copper-rich ribs and hold lightning in layers of resinous tissue. Great bladder stalks lean across the moor, releasing buoyant spores that hang beneath the clouds like dim lanterns. None is merely vegetation, machine, or habitat. Each is also a climate for smaller lives.

Within a mature turbine flower there may be whole communities adapted to rotation. Blind animals cling to the inner vanes and graze metallic films. Threadlike hunters use centrifugal force to cast themselves outward. Larvae develop in the warm sap around the axle, emerging only when the flower stops. For them, stillness is a catastrophe. They know the world as continuous turning punctuated by rare, lethal silences.

The plateau’s factories are inhabited by relay insects. Each individual performs only one conversion: grinding a mineral scale, coating a fiber, measuring a droplet, sealing a wax cell, carrying a charge. No insect understands the procession it serves. Yet the lines fork, repair themselves, reroute around floods, and occasionally abandon one product for another. A colony that spent a century producing antifreeze may begin refining conductive resin after a single unfamiliar storm. Whether the insects decide, the roots instruct, or the weather remembers an older need is impossible to determine.

Reproduction confuses every category. Some flowers do not release seeds but small wet animals, unfinished and nearly transparent. They graze for months before hardening into bulbs and burying themselves. Certain insects enter a blossom with jointed shells and emerge much later bearing internal bones. There are fruits that cry when ripe, eggs that germinate, and pups whose first act is to plant the cord trailing from their bellies. Lineage does not follow a tree here. It circulates through one.

The dead circulate as well.

Bodies descend into the living peat, where they are separated less by substance than by habit. Flesh, shell, memory, posture, alarm, appetite: each may be taken up by a different root system. Years later, a seedpod can repeat the distress call of the animal from whose remains it drew phosphorus. A bank of sedge may recoil from a predator extinct for millennia. An old migration can survive without migrants, traveling seasonally as a sequence of bent stems and opening pools.

Footprints are among the plateau’s stranger residents. Impressions fill with rain, acquire membranes, and begin to deepen themselves. In time they move. One print presses into the ground, fades, and appears a short distance ahead; then the next follows. A trail may wander long after its maker has become peat, sometimes retracing the original journey, sometimes deviating toward warmth or electrical charge. Small creatures shelter in these migrating hollows. Predators follow them. Some tracks reach the plateau’s edge and continue into the air.

Near the drainage wounds live the orbiters. They are light-bodied grazers with grasping feet and broad membranes along their flanks. Sideways gravity holds them in slow circuits around hanging root curtains. They feed as they fall, though they never arrive below. Generations may pass in a single orbit. Their young inherit position as well as form, born into the same curving path. If a root curtain tears loose, an entire population can be flung into cloud, returning months later with ice embedded in its tissues and unfamiliar spores growing from its mouths.

There are also visitors from climates that are nowhere nearby. After the deep pumping, when warm water fills the pools and impossible constellations appear in them, feathered creatures climb out steaming. Their plumage sheds droplets that rise. Soft-bodied browsers follow, opening mouths lined with inward-growing flowers. Some breathe as if the air were denser; some cast shadows in the wrong direction; some freeze despite the warmth.

Most fade when the pools cool. A few adapt. Their descendants become ordinary only by degrees. Feathers turn to mossy filaments. Inward flowers learn to feed on local charge. Strange bones pass into insects, reeds, and eventually weather. The plateau does not reject an impossibility if it can be made useful.

No species rules these lives. There is no sovereign hidden beneath the peat. Coordination appears and disappears with scale. A colony may fight its neighbors for minerals while both participate in the same continental pumping stroke. Grazers may damage turbine roots yet carry the organisms that keep their bearings alive. Dynamo pods discharge into storms that tear apart the fields that nourished them. The whole is cooperative only from far away.

And yet something larger does live there—not a single mind, and not merely an ecosystem.

It has no continuous awareness. It thinks in episodes of pressure, conductivity, rain, hunger, and remembered weight. A thought may begin as amphibians flashing beside a seep, continue as insects redirecting a resin line, cross a valley through contracting reeds, and conclude decades later when a root dome ruptures. Its senses are distributed through everything that drinks, turns, shivers, migrates, or decays. Its memories are not accounts of the past but old responses waiting for old conditions to return.

Sometimes it wakes enough to act as one body.

Three pulses pass beneath the feet. Pools empty. Herds climb. Turbines align. Deep conduits tighten, and the horizon lifts in a slow muscular wave. For those hours, every resident appears to be an organ of the same immense creature: the eels its buried nerves, the marching reeds its fingers, the storms its electrical speech, the mobile moss fields folds of its skin.

Then the coordination dissolves. Colonies resume their arguments. Animals feed. Flowers turn separately in the gale. The great creature ceases to exist, or ceases to be singular.

What lives on the last plateau is therefore not one being but a way of being: borrowed warmth, exchanged anatomy, wandering identity, and matter denied the refuge of lifelessness. Nothing remains only a carcass, a track, a pool, a stone, or a memory. Everything is recruited into another process.

Even the mountains beneath it may still be alive in this fashion. Their vanished ridges persist as pressures in the roots, inclinations in migrating wetlands, and deep impulses to rise. Perhaps the plateau’s continent-wide labor is not an attempt to become a forest.

Perhaps all its countless inhabitants—the fruit-born grazers, the walking reeds, the revolving flowers, the buried eels, the animate footprints, and the weather-bearing moss—are the ancient mountains learning, very slowly, to move.
