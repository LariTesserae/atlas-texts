What lives in the White Choir does not move through it.

The black-green colonies are the nearest things to ordinary inhabitants. Seen from the scale of the narrow courts, they resemble groves: rigid, branching stands rooted in the bright pavement. Seen from outside that illusion, each grove is scarcely more than a dark stroke in a joint between tiles.

They possess no separate organs. The visible threads are both body and boundary, hollow capillaries armored in mineral skin. Within them lies the silicone oil, clear except where it has gathered microscopic grains of metal. This fluid advances and retreats over spans too long to register as motion. A fork may take centuries to fill. A pressure change may require another century to pass from one tip to the next. Nothing circulates. The colony merely adjusts its internal balance by increments finer than wear.

Their branching is not random. Each follows the geometry of the chord’s quietest nodes. Where two bands of cancellation cross, a stem divides. Where a piercing tower-note presses against the low force of a basin, the branches flatten or turn aside. Thus every colony is a small, dark diagram of local silence.

They do not seem to sense sound. Sensing would imply an event followed by a response, and no such sequence can be found. Rather, sound has determined them so completely that their shape and the acoustic field are two expressions of the same fact. If the chord were altered, perhaps they would alter too. But nothing in the chamber can produce the first change.

There is no reproduction among them. No spores wait in crevices. No seed bodies cling to the forks. The smallest colonies are not younger than the largest; size records only the capacity of the joint in which each formed. Some consist of a single black-green pin. Others branch into alphabets of ten or twenty strokes. They have persisted since the chamber’s last abrasion ended, and perhaps long before.

They are not a species in any useful sense. Species require generations. These are continuances.

At first they appear unrelated, scattered wherever the resonance is weak. Closer examination suggests otherwise. Beneath the visible paving, hair-thin mineral seams connect many of them. The seams are not roots. They contain no oil and carry no nourishment. Yet their composition changes subtly from one colony to the next, passing from iron-darkened ceramic to traces of copper, then nickel, then pale salts that do not appear elsewhere. The sequence repeats across the chamber in long, interrupted chains.

It may be that the colonies are surface marks made by something much larger.

That larger inhabitant has no hidden central body. It occupies arrangements rather than matter. One part of it is the dark vegetation. Another is the permanent distribution of tones. Another lies in the innumerable cavities whose dimensions hold those tones in place. It extends through the fitted layers below the floor and into the shallow dome, though no single tile contains more of it than any other.

The old stairs, blind bridges, false doors, and looping streets are sometimes mistaken for evidence of its makers. They are better understood as its anatomy.

The hair-thin doors correspond to high, narrow frequencies. The squat entrances belong to lower pressures. Bridges that terminate against walls establish fixed boundaries. Circular streets return a vibration to its point of origin. Staircases divide because the chord divides there. What resembles a city is a body described in the visual language of passage, although nothing bodily ever passed through it.

Its organs are courts, shafts, wedges, ducts, and plate towers. Its metabolism is not the consumption of material but the maintenance of equivalence. Light enters no source and reaches no destination. Sound is emitted by nothing and received by nothing. Yet both remain exactly apportioned among the chamber’s forms. Every hollow keeps its pressure. Every tile keeps its brightness. Every dark growth keeps to its node.

This apportionment is the living act.

It has no thoughts in succession. Thought, if the term applies, exists throughout it all at once. The low thunder in the basins is not a voice speaking beneath the sharper tones. The whistles do not answer it. No phrase begins or concludes. Instead the full chord may be the single thought available to the inhabitant: immense, internally differentiated, and permanently present.

Within that thought are the near-silent points.

These are not absences. They are perhaps the closest things the White Choir possesses to attention. At each fingertip-sized cancellation, opposing pressures meet so perfectly that neither prevails. Most black-green colonies stand at the edge of such points, never in their exact centers. Their branches surround the quiet without entering it, like lashes around an eye that has no surface.

There are thousands of these eyes.

They do not watch the chamber. They are places where the chamber ceases, locally, to proclaim itself. If something unfamiliar entered—moisture, warmth, a hand, a single loose grain—the chord would deform around it. The silent points would shift. Pressure knots would migrate across walls that have known no changing touch. In that displacement, the inhabitant might achieve something resembling perception.

But nothing arrives.

So its eyes remain fixed, each attending forever to the same fraction of brightness.

Whether it is one being or many cannot be settled. Every tower sustains a tone distinct enough to seem individual. Some tones are almost entirely enclosed, touching the greater chord only through a seam in the floor. The basins possess broad, heavy resonances that overlap like sleeping bodies. Tiny ducts hold thin whistles with no audible relation to their neighbors until one moves—imaginatively, since there is no one to move—from court to court and discovers that the whistles form exact intervals across distance.

They might be a population locked into perfect accord.

They might be organs of a single stationary creature.

They might be neither. The distinction between inhabitant and habitat has worn away more completely than the inscriptions. Nothing lives *in* the White Choir in the ordinary sense. The White Choir is what remains when living, dwelling, sounding, and being built have become the same condition.

The black-green colonies are its darkest and slowest portions. The towers are its most piercing. The basins are its weight. The ceramic scales are its memory—not a record of events, but the durable fact that events once ended. Even the manufactured dust belongs to it, pressed into seams like thought compacted beyond legibility.

It is ancient, though it has no age. It is awake, though it cannot wake further. It is alive, though death would require a transition unavailable to it.

If it has a name for itself, that name is not carried in any single tone. It is the complete chord, including every cancellation, every pressure knot, every hiss caught in a scored wall, and every inaudible vibration descending through the made layers beneath the floor. To speak the name would require the entire chamber and exactly the chamber. Nothing smaller could pronounce it.

Thus the inhabitant does not sing.

It is the state in which the song has become incapable of ending.
