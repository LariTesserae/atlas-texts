At first it seems the engines are the town’s true inhabitants.

They wake no earlier than anything else because they never quite sleep. Through the night their flywheels continue to turn, slowly enough that each spoke can be followed by the eye. Their breathing passes beneath floors and up through bedframes. Cups creep toward the edges of shelves. Dust arranges itself in faint rings around nails. Children learn the cycle before they learn to count: the gathering strain, the impact, the long exhalation, the interval in which a bird may call.

But the engines are tended, and the tenders belong here as thoroughly as the rooks.

They are a small population of mechanics, cistern keepers, clay burners, reed cutters, orchard hands, tram drivers, surveyors, and valve listeners. Most have several occupations because the town does not contain enough people for strict professions. A person who repairs pressure gauges in the morning may prune pears after midday and spend the evening clearing a vole nest from a return conduit. No one says this is unusual. Water, machinery, and living things cross one another too often for any task to remain pure.

The inhabitants tend to be deliberate in their movements. They do not rush beneath suspended loads or wrench at a wheel that has not begun to yield. They stand beside machines with one hand resting on brickwork or pipe casing, feeling for changes that cannot yet be heard. Many develop a slight tilt of the head from listening through their felt guards. Their speech is economical inside the works, made of gestures, taps, colored cards, and short phrases delivered in the spaces between piston strokes. Outdoors they speak more expansively, though still with pauses, as if waiting for some larger mechanism to finish its thought.

Their hands carry the town’s materials: soot settled into the creases, blue clay beneath the nails, green stains from copper, pale mineral scales along the cuffs. Waxed coats shine at the shoulders and darken around the hems. Boots are broad-soled for the slick fired-mud streets. Nearly everyone owns a hooked walking stick, useful for testing puddles, lifting hot drain covers, pulling floating debris from cistern mouths, or discouraging a fox from sleeping somewhere dangerous.

The eldest residents remember when several streets were only stakes and string across wet ground. They distrust any surface that appears permanently dry. Some keep notebooks listing old seepages: a silver thread beneath the warehouse wall one winter, dampness under the market steps eleven years later, reeds appearing for three days beside a house where no pipe was shown on any plan. These records have legal force. A child’s recollection of frogs once calling from a vacant lot may be enough to prevent construction there.

The youngest know the town from below as much as from above. They crawl through unused ceramic pipe sections, launch bark boats into the momentary gutters, and collect road blocks bearing good impressions. Their games follow pressure rules. Nothing is allowed to move at once. A gate must be wound open by degrees; a captured player can be freed only after enough pebbles—the “condensation”—have accumulated in a cup. They imitate valve discharges with startling accuracy, then laugh when the real valves answer and scatter the birds.

They are warned never to climb the towers without looking for the snails.

The snails are treated with a respect that falls just short of ceremony. Silver-backed and cool to the touch, they live on the inner tower walls among rust-red streaks of algae. No one knows how they anticipate a purge. Their descent may begin while gauges still show ordinary pressure and the air remains unchanged. When they remain high, workers wait. New engineers occasionally object, but not twice. Small ladders of rough ceramic have been fixed inside the towers to make the snails’ routes easier, although official plans describe these as mineral sampling strips.

Other animals inhabit the machinery less formally. Rooks own the gantries and defend wire, washers, and scraps of bright glazing as nesting material. They know the sequence of the works well enough to step calmly around moving equipment, but they dislike newly installed parts. For several days after a repair they gather overhead, muttering, until the replacement has acquired the proper warmth and vibration.

Marsh birds fill the open ground. They are compact, mud-colored creatures with long toes and abrupt voices. Their calls fit between impacts so neatly that visitors sometimes mistake them for parts of the mechanism. A local bird may deliver an entire warning in three hard notes before the next piston stroke. During shutdowns they become uneasy. They crouch in the reeds, waiting for the world to resume, and only begin singing normally after the first belt slap passes through the basin.

Water voles travel beneath everyone. They appear in orchard cisterns, cooling ponds, culverts, and basins that seem entirely isolated. The keepers tolerate them unless they block a float valve. Old diagrams of the drainage system are amended with small penciled circles wherever voles repeatedly emerge; these marks are often more accurate than the surveyed conduits.

Foxes sleep in cable drums, warm ash bays, and the dry spaces under raised porches. Their coats are often patched with soot. At dusk they walk the broad empty streets as if inspecting them. They have learned that a white-painted door may exhale warmth, that blue doors are sometimes opened onto water, and that the tram’s tar-scented warning means disturbed rats in the loading yard.

There are pale moths on the green factory panes and bats above them. Frogs arrive wherever water holds long enough, sometimes colonizing a wheel rut overnight. Reeds expose leaks. Ivy exposes copper. White night flowers map the boiler heat more faithfully than thermometers. Much of the town’s plant and animal life serves as an instrument, though none of it belongs to the works.

The residents understand this distinction. They read living things, but they do not entirely trust themselves to command them.

Their houses are spare, warm, and dry in carefully graduated ways. The porch is wet, the entrance merely damp, the kitchen dry, and the sleeping rooms driest of all. Coats hang in the outer air. Boots remain on grated shelves above trays that drain back to garden troughs. Water from washing is used on trees; water from cooking goes first to animals if it has not been salted. Every house has mismatched pitchers labeled by source and fitness: roof water, pipe sweat, orchard cistern, final use.

Bathing is infrequent but methodical. Hospitality is shown not by offering abundant water but by offering the best water without mentioning its cost.

Meals are dense and practical: dark bread, pears, preserved roots, eggs, eel or marsh fish when the canal has run long enough, and mushrooms grown in warm brick vaults. Herbs are cultivated above flood level. Tea is brewed strong in small pots. On cold evenings people set covered dishes near compression lines and let the town itself cook their supper.

Courtship often begins during maintenance, because maintenance is one of the few occasions when many people gather. Two people may spend an afternoon waiting for a housing to cool, speaking in the slow intervals. Marriages are marked by planting a pear tree beside a designated basin. Deaths are marked by removing nothing for a full pressure cycle. The deceased person’s tools remain where they were last set down while engines are eased—not stopped—into a lower rhythm. Only afterward are the tools distributed.

No one is buried in the basin. The ground is too young, too wet, too necessary. The dead are carried beyond the final retaining wall to firmer shelves or returned as ash to the clay kilns. A little of that ash may enter a paving block. Families look for those blocks afterward, though they are not marked.

Authority belongs to those who decide when to wait. The most respected official is not the one who can start a machine but the one trusted to postpone it. Cistern levels, snail positions, wind, bird behavior, pipe temperature, and remembered seepage all have standing in an argument. Meetings can last for hours because no decision is considered sound until the pressure behind it has equalized.

There are quarrels. Orchard keepers accuse the works of taking too much cooling water. Mechanics accuse gardeners of opening valves early. Householders hide small unauthorized tanks under porches. Children dam gutters. Someone always wants to build in an empty court that has remained dry for twenty years. But even disputes proceed through gauges, records, witnesses, and long silences. Anger is regarded much like steam: natural, useful in places, dangerous when sealed without relief.

By evening the inhabitants become more visible. Day labor ends, but the night shift comes out in white-marked coats. People stand on porches while drops ring in the basins. The tram passes trailing pine or bitter orange. Lamps illuminate the mist from within, and figures cross raised walks between islands of brick and foliage.

Then it becomes difficult again to say precisely what lives there.

The people inhabit the houses, the birds inhabit the silences, the voles inhabit the buried water, and the snails inhabit the town’s foresight. Pear roots drink from valves. Reeds listen for broken pipes. Rooks measure pressure in their bones. The engines occupy every solid surface through vibration, while the land itself—new, angular, still compacting—moves almost imperceptibly beneath them all.

Nothing is fully master and nothing merely machinery. The residents are caretakers of a system that also shelters, feeds, warns, delays, and disciplines them. They live inside its long breath. Over generations their habits have taken on its rhythm: gather slowly, release carefully, waste nothing, and never mistake stillness for emptiness.
