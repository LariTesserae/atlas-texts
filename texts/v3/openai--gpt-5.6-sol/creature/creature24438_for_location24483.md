The first thing that lives in the Sump is the footing.

It is not an animal, though it behaves like one under weight. Mineral foam tightens around a boot before surrendering; mud holds an ankle with a slow, muscular attention. Fresh ridges sweat when approached. Their cracks widen toward warmth and close after it passes. People who live there do not speak of solid ground. They speak of ground that is sleeping, feeding, listening, or about to turn.

A Sump-born child learns three ways of standing before learning to walk: broad-footed for foam, heel-light for new stone, and perfectly still for a surface deciding what it is. Falls are common. Falling through is less common, but more carefully named. There are words for falling into water, into darkness, into an earlier place, and into a space that had not been hollow until the body entered it.

The people are few, but the Sump makes their lives seem numerous.

A mechanic may be encountered in three pumping houses along the same cold-air road, old in one, young in another, and not yet arrived at the third. These are not always repetitions of the same person. Sometimes they have different memories and tools. Sometimes each insists the others are echoes made by bad walls. Sump custom discourages argument on the matter. All three are offered food. All three are warned of debts.

Most inhabitants live above the mud, though never very far above it. Control rooms on stilts become family houses. Empty tanks are lined with felt, cork, fungus board, and animal hide to make chambers that do not ring. Suspended walkways carry neighborhoods: hammocks, cookboxes, water skins, cages of listening fish, racks where wet ropes are kept sorted by texture. Nothing important is stored in one place. A household’s grain may hang beneath a flywheel, its tools lie sealed in a caisson, and its ancestral objects be carried continually from hand to hand so that no blackbank can take all of them at once.

They do not decorate with color. They carve, knot, corrugate, perfume, warm, and tune.

A wedding cord may be recognized by eleven kinds of knot and a resinous smell released only by the heat of the palm. A mourning wall is covered in soft clay and impressed with the dead person’s habitual movements: the arc of a shoulder turning through a doorway, four fingertips searching for a cup, the side of a foot braced against a working pipe. Children are given bracelets containing loose ceramic grains, each family having its own rhythm. Adults often wear several, because kinship in the Sump includes those who rescued you, those who remember a version of you that you do not, and those whose houses your return journey passes through.

Names are made to survive poor sequence. Every person has a call-name, a touch-name, and an engine-name.

The call-name is spoken close to the skin. The touch-name is tapped into the wrist or jaw. The engine-name is a particular interval between mechanical knocks: third stroke of the west caisson, pause, two teeth of the buried gear, long pause, pressure sigh. It may take hours to say properly. Engine-names are used for contracts, inheritance, and the identification of bodies.

Bodies themselves are difficult to identify. The Sump alters dead matter more readily than living matter, and a corpse left in a blackbank may return as a shell with its organs folded around the outside, still warm and smelling faintly of copper. For this reason the dead are seldom buried. They are fastened to the slow flywheels.

Over several days the wheel carries them through dripping air, warm vapor, nesting colonies, and the vibration of the machinery. Moths cover them. Feelers inspect them delicately from the walkway. By the completion of one revolution, a body is usually lightened: moisture taken by wings, salts taken by crabs, soft tissues entered by dry fungi. The remaining bones are lowered into warm trenches, where blind fish worry them smooth. Since little else in the Sump becomes smooth, these bones are treasured. Finger bones become tuning pegs. Ribs reinforce map cases. The rounded bones of the ear are worn as charms against distances that lengthen behind the traveler.

The human inhabitants are sometimes called Stillfolk by outsiders. They do not use the name themselves. Stillness, to them, is too broad a category. There is listening-stillness, hiding-stillness, anchoring-stillness, and the involuntary stillness that passes through all life when the deep machinery aligns. There is also courtesy-stillness: the pause made when another being is sounding the ground.

For the Sump is crowded with sounders.

The long-legged feelers are the most obvious. Their central bodies are soft and warm, wrapped in a pelt so fine it feels like oil. The legs are jointed reeds of tendon and translucent mineral, each ending in a different organ. One foot tastes salinity. Another detects electrical charge. Two read pressure traveling through stone. The hindmost pair find surfaces that have recently moved in time.

A feeler does not walk toward a destination. It assembles a destination beneath itself, testing dozens of possible next steps and choosing the one whose consequences are least crowded. This is why a herd may take a week to cross a channel narrow enough for a person to jump. It is also why travelers follow them.

They cannot be domesticated, but they can be consulted. A ferryman taps a question into a pipe, waits for the herd to redistribute its feet, and reads the answer from the pattern of ticking. The answers are rarely direct. A feeler may respond to “Is the eastern trench passable?” by touching the ferryman’s childhood home, two kilometers away, or by lifting every limb except the one resting on a future section of bridge. Good readers understand that the animal is not being cryptic. It simply has more geography underfoot than the questioner does.

Young feelers have short legs and ride on adults. Hundreds can cluster along a single limb like burrs. At the first vibration of a favored pump, they drop into the mud and begin walking independently. Their first journeys last years. Their first step is considered an omen, though no one agrees of what.

Below them are the iron crabs.

They hatch soft and remain soft unless given something harder to remember themselves by. A young crab presses fragments into its back: rust scale, ceramic, gear filings, teeth, buttons, splinters of knife. Tissue rises around these objects and holds them in a flexible mosaic. Older crabs may carry half a machine’s history on their shells.

Mechanics tolerate them because they gather around dangerous charge. Before touching a dead cable or disconnected tank, a mechanic scatters food and listens. If the crabs approach normally, the metal is probably safe. If they approach sideways, it contains dormant current. If they flee and then return in a straight line, the charge is not in the metal yet, but will be.

Certain crabs become enormous by nesting beneath active equipment. Their shells incorporate bolts, inspection plates, and lengths of pipe until it is no longer clear whether the crab occupies the machine or the machine has grown an appetite. Such creatures are called foremen. A foreman may remain under one pump for a century, molting one component at a time. It knows the rhythm better than any mechanic and strikes the foundation with a claw when a stroke is wrong.

Foremen are fed grease, eel flesh, and failed ceramic markers. They are never killed. When a pumping station stops permanently, its foreman leaves, dragging a carapace shaped like the missing underside of the works. Other animals follow in the trench it makes.

The pools belong to the grainfish.

Individually they are small, blind, and almost without organs. Their bodies are knots of muscle around an earstone. They live pressed together in such numbers that a hand lowered into the water encounters not fish but a moving substance, rough in one direction and smooth in another. The school behaves as a single listening body. It forms hollows around buried noises. It stiffens before gates open. When rain is about to rise from the ground, all the fish turn belly-up and the pool becomes briefly soft.

People keep jars of them in their houses. A healthy jar murmurs against the glass throughout the day. Silence means nearby pressure has changed—or that the fish are listening to something more important.

The oldest pools contain schools that no longer separate into individuals. Their earstones have fused into branching internal reefs. These shoals move very little. Mud grows over them; bladder-plants root in their backs; houses may be built unknowingly on their upper surfaces. Yet they still turn. A village can wake tilted because the ancient school beneath it has redirected its attention.

Mudskippers travel between such waters carrying parasites, eggs, seeds, and scraps of sound. They listen with the broad undersides of their jaws and repeat what they hear by striking the clay. A single mudskipper can reproduce the knock of a distant gear. A crowd can reproduce an entire pumping station.

They do this most often during mating season. From the surface it sounds like industry resuming in abandoned ruins. More than one mechanic has spent months repairing a pump that was never there.

Above the pools, moths crowd the cables.

Their wings are thick, veined, and nearly hairless. Since they cannot signal with pattern or color, they speak by changing the tension of whatever they cling to. Colonies spread across the industrial web in living chords. A warning begun at one derrick may pass wing to wing and arrive at another as a low shiver in the bolts.

Their larvae feed on hot insulation without consuming it. They enter as narrow grubs and emerge years later, leaving the cable apparently untouched but full of delicate channels. Electricity pools in these channels. Adult moths drink the charge in minute portions and store it along their wing joints, not as light but as tension. During migration they release it, snapping open their wings together. The pressure wave can strip condensation from a tank.

Skin-bats live among them. The bats have small bodies and wings many times larger than needed for flight. They wrap these wings around pipes to hear current moving inside. When alarmed, they do not take to the air; they release their grip and let the membrane catch whatever pressure gradient is passing, sometimes sailing sideways for kilometers before striking another structure. They are considered unlucky indoors because they remember the shape of rooms with their wings. If released later, they may attempt to unfold that room somewhere else.

The columnar fungi remember engines.

They grow where vibration is repetitive, building each pulse into a ring of dry tissue. Cut across, a column contains a record more precise than any written maintenance log: droughts as narrow bands, flood years as swollen ones, skipped strokes as pale wounds. Some columns lean toward a machine that has not yet been installed. Others turn away from a pump shortly before it fails.

Mechanics cultivate them in boxes fixed to foundations. They lay a palm against the stalk and feel past operation as texture. A trained hand can distinguish a clogged intake from a drowned worker, an electrical migration from the passage of a large animal below.

The fungi are not passive recorders. In old stations their root threads enter seals, bearings, packed earth, and the soft bodies of nesting creatures. They begin to impose stored rhythms on whatever they touch. A dead engine may continue as contractions in the fungus. Crabs beside it flex their legs in time. Moths tighten their wings. Sleeping people grind their teeth once every six hours.

Eventually a whole station can become a memory of its own operation, with no moving metal at all.

Among the human inhabitants, mechanics are therefore not merely repairers. They negotiate between versions of motion. They decide whether a piston is turning, remembering that it turned, or being anticipated by the stone beneath it. Their principal tools are tuning forks, greased cord, bone calipers, and long insulated rods with lumps of meat fixed to the ends. The meat reveals current by twitching. It also reveals certain blackbanks by developing bruises before entry.

Ferrymen know the channels. Most have unusually broad hands, scarred fingertips, and missing teeth. They remove the teeth themselves when young, because pressure changes ache in the roots; the extracted teeth are set into the poles at measured intervals. As a pole touches bank, water, stone, foam, or hidden structure, the teeth chatter differently.

A ferryman does not promise to bring a passenger to a place. The proper promise is to bring them to a usable arrival.

Sometimes this is a doorframe standing alone in mud. Sometimes it is the underside of a walkway, reached while hanging in straps. Sometimes it is the passenger’s destination but three days earlier, necessitating a quiet wait outside so as not to meet oneself within. Ferrymen carry wax tablets impressed with the dates of blank areas. They also carry bells stuffed with cloth. A bell is rung only inside a blackbank, where the dense darkness may prevent the sound from leaving. If the bell is heard anyway, the ferryman turns back.

Children often serve as mapmakers because they accept contradiction without trying to correct it. They crawl through chambers adults cannot enter, press wax against walls, and record where their own bodies seem larger or smaller than the available space. They map by adjacency rather than distance: warm pipe beside sour-smelling stone; six rivets beneath moth colony; cold ribbon crossing a repeated doorway; pool whose interior exceeds its bank.

A child’s map may include a person as a landmark.

“Uncle Tern, asleep, left side warm” can remain a valid navigational feature for years, even after Uncle Tern has moved away or died. Travelers reaching the marked place may find the warmth and shape of him still there, absent any body. They step over it politely.

Not every inhabitant is born.

Some are assembled by repetition. A door opened often enough may develop an expectation of passage. Eventually it opens before being touched. Later it produces footsteps where no corridor exists. The inhabitants call such a thing a habit. Habits are treated as minor animals. They are fed regular actions and discouraged from breeding.

A pump-house habit may shut valves at the remembered end of a shift. A domestic habit may rock an empty cradle. More dangerous habits inhabit routes. They draw travelers along the easiest version of an old journey and abandon them where the landscape has changed.

There are also returns: impressions of living creatures that arrive before or after their owners. A return has weight but little initiative. It repeats one movement, gives off one smell, or occupies one patch of warmth. Most fade. A few accumulate detail with each repetition until they can work, eat, speak, and claim wages.

The Sump has no agreed law about whether a complete return is a person. In practice, if it knows an engine-name and can be owed a favor, it is treated as one.

The blackbanks have their own life, if life is the word.

They settle in low places like herds at rest. Their edges pulse. Small blackbanks follow large ones along pressure gradients, stretching into dark lobes and then drawing together. They ingest structures without visibly covering them; a tank may remain tangible from outside while its interior becomes unreachable. After feeding, a bank often divides.

Things emerge from them.

Some are compressed copies: a length of pipe shortened to the thickness of a coin while retaining its full interior passage. Some are duplicates joined at warm seams. Some are animals whose outsides have become inward cavities. These usually die. A few adapt.

The most feared are the open ones, creatures turned partly inside-out yet still mobile. An open crab carries its shell within its flesh and walks on the hard internal edges. An open moth has wings lined with the air that formerly surrounded it; touching one gives the sensation of reaching into great height. Open fish swim through mud because the pool now lies inside them.

People do not kill these creatures if avoidance is possible. They are living maps of a place no instrument can enter. Blackbank tenders—an uncommon and short-lived profession—trail them on long cords, recording which organs respond to hidden obstructions. Tenders wrap themselves in layered fabrics, each garment tied independently, so that if their interior is brought outward it will meet something resembling another interior.

Nearly every settlement keeps watch for the regarded places.

No eye is ever found there. No beam, face, opening, or instrument. Nevertheless, animals orient toward a point with unmistakable precision. Feelers raise themselves around it. Moths form rings. Fish create a clear vertical channel through crowded water. Humans experience an urge to cover the mouth rather than the eyes.

The regarded point can be near or far, high in the air or several meters beneath young stone. It can move without crossing the interval between positions. It rarely harms anything directly. Its danger lies in attention. Machines slow under it. Returns become more complete. Habits deviate. People remember events that happened to neighboring people, or to structures, or to water.

Some Sump families believe the regarded points are how the land touches what is above it. Others say they are apertures in an enormous buried animal: not eyes, because sight cannot exist there, but organs for noticing pressure, intention, or sequence.

No one has named that animal with certainty.

The foremen know it. When all pumps approach the same stroke, the great crabs descend from their machinery and press their bodies flat against the ground. Grainfish empty the center of every pool. Skin-bats wrap cables and hang motionless. The columnar fungi lean, not toward any engine, but downward.

Human inhabitants feel it first in their teeth, including the teeth they no longer possess.

They stop speaking. They take hold of ropes, rails, family cords, one another. Even returns become still. Inside tanks, condensation pauses halfway down the walls. Charge leaves metal and gathers in the wet air without crossing it. The darkness thickens until every body seems tightly wrapped in its own surface.

All the pumps complete their stroke.

For a moment the Sump is not marsh, plain, ruin, colony, or settlement. Its hidden waters connect. Every trench is a vein; every caisson a hard inclusion; every cold-air road a held breath. The young ridges tense like seams over something vastly pressurized.

Then comes the drop.

It is not loud in the air. Through stone it has shape: a single mass separating from a greater mass, falling through a depth for which the plain has no room, and striking water far below.

The impact passes upward through every living layer.

Fish turn as one. Crabs seize the mud. Feelers place all their feet at once. Moths open their wings. People lose, briefly, the distinction between their bodies and the structures they hold. In that instant each inhabitant receives the same impossible impression—not an image, because there are no images in the Sump, but the tactile certainty of an interior larger than the land.

Something beneath them has shifted in its sleep.

Or swallowed.

Or begun, with immeasurable patience, to wake.
