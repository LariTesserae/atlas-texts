Nothing lives in the Drum Hollow in the ordinary manner. Nothing settles fully, breeds abundantly, fills the chamber with its own business. Life occupies it by edges and intervals. It clings, waits, stores, listens, and leaves.

Nearest the entrance are the plants that still remember daylight. The ferns are thin and pallid, with long crooked stems and fronds turned almost sideways toward the slit. They do not rise from soil so much as suspend themselves from cracks, rooted in a paste of stone dust, old soot, and the remains of earlier ferns. Creeping sorrel spreads low around them. Its leaves are small, sharp-tasting, and dark except where the entrance light touches them, when their veins show briefly red.

Deeper in, the mosses possess what little country there is. Each steady drip has founded a separate green holding. One is thick and springy, built up over generations into a pad that conceals the shape of the rock beneath it. Another is no more than a stain around a cup of water. Liverworts overlap in damp scales. Algae hangs in slick threads from a ceiling seam, lengthening until the pressure-thunder shakes it loose. These gardens rarely meet. Between them lie broad tracts of bare basalt, so that a moth crossing from one patch to another travels through a wilderness.

The plants live by a rhythm too small to notice against the sea. They swell after wet weather. They tighten and darken during a dry wind above. A fern may take a season to uncurl one frond and lose it in a single night to frost. Growth is measured in the narrowing of a crack, the softening of a stone’s edge, the slow capture of soot.

Among the moss live creatures almost fitted to its scale: pale mites, black springtails, translucent larvae no longer than a grass seed. They feed on rot, fungus, and one another. The chamber’s violence reaches them only as a constant trembling of their world.

The beetles are larger and more deliberate. Broad-backed and lightless, they shelter beneath old bundles of rush and in the seams between storage stones. They eat spilled meal, fish scales, mold-softened cordage, and the leathery remnants of mushrooms. When the hollow stands unused, they venture across the open floor. The thunder hides them completely, but lamplight catches the oil-dark shine of their shells.

Higher up, tiny moths sleep with their wings folded close. Their color is not pure white but the gray of bone kept out of the sun. They remain on the roof for days, enduring each subterranean concussion through six minute points of contact. When disturbed, they flutter badly and briefly, appearing in lamplight like flakes of ash returning to the smoke-blackened ceiling.

The spiders occupy the cracks least troubled by moving air. Their webs are dense because elegance is useless here. Every strand trembles whether prey has touched it or not; a spider cannot simply wait for a vibration and strike. It must distinguish the light, irregular struggle of an insect from the great pulse passing through stone and silk together. The oldest spiders are exceptionally still. They rest with their feet distributed across several anchor lines, reading differences too fine for a human hand. During storms they withdraw deep into the rock, as if the sea had entered their bodies before it entered the passages below.

Larger animals are visitors.

A fox sometimes comes in during autumn, narrow with hunger and rain-darkened to the skin. It enters readily enough, drawn by dried fish or the odor of buried fat, but becomes uncertain once inside. Its ears, so useful on the moor, cannot separate one sound from another. It advances with its whiskers spread and its nose near the floor. Often it startles at a movement it should have heard long before: a person turning, a hide sack shifting, water rising through a cup in the stone. The fox seldom penetrates to the rear clefts. It steals what it can carry and returns to the open country, where a footfall has direction again.

The seals come by routes no person knows. They do not enter the chamber itself. Once or twice in the cold part of the year, one passes through a submerged throat beneath it and finds an air pocket in the buried rock. Perhaps it is young; perhaps storm water has driven it from the outer shelves. Its call rises through water, fissure, and resonant stone until all the ordinary qualities of an animal voice have been pulled apart. What reaches the chamber may resemble grief, warning, or an immense being speaking from underground. The seal itself is only breathing, calling, waiting for the tide to turn. By morning it is usually gone.

The human inhabitants are visitors too, though their visits extend across thousands of years.

They belong chiefly to several moorland families who know the entrance and maintain some claim upon the storage bays. Their rights are not marked at the slit. They are remembered through use: who rebuilt which wall, whose grandmother replaced the antler rack, who may open a covered pit in a famine, who owns the green copper hook beneath the slab. An outsider might see abandoned objects scattered through darkness. Those who use the hollow see boundaries.

They come bent under loads—driftwood, fish, butchered meat, baskets of roots, coils of new cord. Even the tallest appear reduced by the entrance, made to bow before they arrive. Inside they straighten slowly and stand with one hand against the wall until they know the present temper of the sea.

Their bodies have learned habits specific to the chamber. They walk with short sliding steps so a foot does not enter an unexpected pool. Their tools are tethered. Their hair and loose clothing are tied close. They do not shout across the room; they touch an arm, shoulder, or wrist, then speak directly into an ear. Much of their communication is by lamplight and gesture. A lifted palm means wait. Two fingers drawn downward mean rising water. A closed fist against the chest means the thunder has changed—stop work and feel it.

Those most familiar with the hollow are sometimes called listeners, though hearing is only part of what they do. A listener may kneel and set the heel of a hand to the basalt. They notice whether the vibration is smooth or broken, whether it arrives as a long pressure or in separated blows. They watch the water cups for rings. They study the sideways lean of a flame and the shivering tips of the ferns. From these they judge the tide hidden beneath them.

The best listeners are often older people. Quickness matters less than memory. They have felt the chamber in winter storms, during calm summer lows, under ice, after days of western rain. They remember the dangerous silences: not true silence, but a sudden thinning, a place where the expected beat fails to arrive. When that happens they do not debate. They hood the lamps, abandon any untied load, and drive everyone toward the entrance.

Children are brought only after they are old enough to obey a touch in darkness. Their first fear is usually not the roar but the disappearance of their own voices. They call out and feel the sound in their jaw, yet cannot hear themselves. Some cry. Some laugh and cry harder when the laughter vanishes too. An elder takes the child’s hand and presses it to the floor or wall. The lesson is simple: what cannot be heard may still be felt.

No one likes to sleep there. People doze when work or weather forces them to remain, wrapped in hides in the driest bay, but they wake often and without knowing why. The body continues listening beneath sleep. Dreams acquire the chamber’s pulse. A sleeper may dream of running while the ground remains fixed, or of something enormous breathing under a door. On waking, there is always a moment when the darkness seems occupied by another person.

Sometimes it is.

Because approach is inaudible, encounters in the hollow are abrupt. A lamp opens and there is a face where there had been only blackness. A hand reaches toward a peg and meets another hand. Those who use the chamber announce themselves not by calling but by placing a small light near the entrance, where its reflection can be seen before the bearer emerges. To enter without doing so is considered more than discourteous. In lean years it is treated as a threat.

The hollow’s people are not priests, nor do they believe the thunder requires worship. Their rules are practical and their explanations exact: wind, tide, trapped air, frost-loosened rock. They know the distorted winter cries are seals. They know water wells upward because lower passages fill.

Yet knowledge does not make them casual. They handle the place with the seriousness given to anything powerful, useful, and indifferent. They do not strike the walls for amusement. They do not waste food there. They do not boast that the sea cannot reach them. Before leaving, the last person checks the lamps, closes the pits, lifts every cord clear of damp stone, and rests a hand briefly against the wall.

It may look like reverence.

Perhaps it is only farewell.

When the people are gone, the chamber returns almost at once to its older inhabitants. Beetles emerge beneath the rack. Spiders spread their feet over trembling lines. Condensation thickens on the roof. The isolated gardens drink. Far below, a seal may pass through black water, or nothing larger than a blind crustacean may move along the flooded stone.

And beneath all of them is the closest thing the Drum Hollow has to a permanent creature: the sea, though no part of its visible body appears there. It inhabits the chamber by pressure and breath. It contracts the air, shakes water in the cups, tests every loose stone, presses its pulse into moss, web, tool, tooth, and bone. All the smaller lives have adapted to dwelling inside that pulse.

They do not conquer the hollow. They endure its breathing for a while.
