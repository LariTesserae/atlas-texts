At first, the inhabitants seem to be insects.

They issue from seams at every hour: lacquered beetles shouldering through valves in the bark, moths folded into the pale undersides of leaves, ants moving under translucent floors in lines as exact as writing. Cicadas occupy the upper city in such numbers that their empty skins form a second, brittle architecture around the first. Flies with mirrored abdomens gather wherever a sunbeam has become trapped. Tiny wasps sleep inside the city’s fruits and wake knowing routes through districts that no longer exist.

But insects are only the most legible citizens. They are numerous enough to resemble intention.

The ants maintain warm chambers beneath the avenues. They move eggs when the streets move and wall up passages several hours before floods arrive. Their colonies are not divided by ancestry so much as by task: resin-carriers, scale-herders, corpse-folders, sound-measurers. The last have long, jointed antennae that they press against root walls. When a dangerous resonance begins, they race ahead of it and alter the density of the traffic, massing in doorways or withdrawing from bridges. By these small adjustments they sometimes change which streets will exist by evening.

The beetles are more private. Some spend their entire lives inside the walls, carving galleries through dead wood while avoiding the living vessels. Their hammering is part excavation, part courtship, part negotiation with the plant. A beetle that cuts too greedily may find the passage closing behind it in wet green increments. Others are permitted to hollow chambers and are fed beads of sap for doing so. These beetles never see daylight. Their eyes resemble knots in timber, and when exposed they continue walking as if enclosed, turning at invisible corners and climbing absent stairs.

The frogs live nearer the water, though “near” changes with the weather. They are glassy, soft-bodied things, their organs lit by whatever sky the cistern predicts. Before dry weather their bellies are colorless. Before rain, clouds pass visibly through them. Before the rare storms that come from beneath, tiny forks of lightning can be seen flickering along their spines.

They crouch in drain mouths and drink condensation with their whole skin. Their calls are low enough to trouble the surface of standing water. A chorus may bring up images from the bottom of a well: tomorrow’s clouds, yesterday’s predators, a moon that belongs to another season. Tadpoles feed on these reflections. Most mature normally. A few emerge with memories instead of limbs and must wait for the city to grow them bodies.

The lizards are everywhere and almost nowhere. They flatten themselves against sun-warmed walls, changing color not to match bark or leaf but to match the hour. Morning lizards are gold around the eyes. Noon lizards bleach nearly white. At dusk they become red-backed and long-shadowed, even in alleys where the sun never enters. Those caught by the moving silence may be separated from their proper color for days. They search for it afterward with grave concentration, tasting walls, peering into cisterns, pursuing blue or green flashes through the canopy. Sometimes they recover the wrong one and keep it.

Geckos serve the city accidentally. Their fan-shaped toes lift spores, eggs, pollen, mites, and minute droplets from one living surface and press them into another. Because certain doors open only under a particular pattern of touch, a running gecko may unseal a chamber closed for decades. It rarely notices. Larger creatures do.

The birds notice everything.

Tall, spare hunters pace the plazas with the ceremonious patience of officials. They listen through the pavement and strike at mice, crabs, frogs, buried grubs, or pieces of weather passing through the roots. Each bird develops a route and defends it, but the route rather than the ground is the territory. If the streets rearrange, the bird continues its old circuit through halls, up walls, over cistern mouths, sometimes stepping across empty air where an avenue used to be. The city briefly hardens beneath its feet.

Their voices do not return unchanged. A simple croak may come back from an arcade as a whistle, from a tower as wingbeats, from a buried courtyard as the voice of a different bird. Older hunters learn to use these altered echoes. They call into one district to flush prey in another. Very old ones stop making sounds of their own and speak only with voices that the city has returned.

Small birds nest in roof seams, weaving together shed root fibers and the transparent skins of frogs. They steal loose shadows to darken their nests against predators. At fledging, each chick must be coaxed out beneath its own shadow, or it will spend its life following the wrong body.

After rain, crabs emerge from the wells.

Their shells are stained black, umber, and green with lichens cultivated in deliberate patches. They climb columns in slow files and scrape mineral crusts from the walls. When threatened, they retreat into recesses that close around them like fists. Some grow too large to return to the cisterns. These old crabs take up residence beneath bridges, where falling droplets polish their shells. Moss colonizes their joints; seedlings root in the grooves along their backs. Eventually it becomes difficult to tell whether the crab carries a garden or whether the garden carries the crab.

The mice are seed thieves, fungus eaters, and cartographers. They leave scent marks at corners, but the walls often move the marks elsewhere. Consequently, their maps are not representations of streets. They are instructions: turn when the air tastes bitter; descend after the third pulse; wait where the floor is warm; ignore the doorway that breathes in time with you. A mouse can cross the city faster than any larger animal because it does not expect one place to remain beside another.

They nest in abandoned fruits shaped like faces. The young gnaw eyeholes through the rind and watch the streets from inside. During the noon resonance, whole families may be lifted gently from their nests and deposited in a different season. They generally return with new seeds caught in their fur.

Bats inhabit seedpods, hollow lintels, and the narrow darkness between overlapping leaves. By day they hang in packed colonies, each wrapped in the smell of fermenting fruit. By night they sound the city. Their cries reveal columns, prey, passageways, and chambers—but also impossible depths, absent towers, and enormous bodies curled beneath the streets. Young bats chase every echo. Adults learn that some objects exist only when called to.

Among the larger animals is the spotted cat, or perhaps several cats passing through one appearance. It is lean, long-footed, and scarred along the muzzle by defensive roots. It sleeps beneath the same bridge even when the bridge shifts districts. No other predator challenges it there. When awake, it hunts by listening to the architecture rather than to prey: a trunk’s amplification, the warning-clap of leaves, the abrupt hush of insects along a likely route.

Its spots continue moving after the cat lies still. In deep shade they sometimes detach and climb the walls like black beetles. Before the cat wakes, they return and arrange themselves upon its coat, not always in the same pattern.

The cat obeys the neutral intersections. It may crouch an arm’s length from a drinking mouse while the truce leaf remains shaded. It watches the edge of the light instead. The instant sun touches the leaf, every creature vanishes in a different direction, and the cat chooses among them without anger. Hunger here is not hatred. Predation is one of the city’s traffic laws.

The split-jawed grazers live in the roofs. They have narrow bodies, gripping feet, and lower jaws divided into paired pruning blades. Moving upside down along the inner canopy, they clip leaves, roots, and fruit stems with quiet precision. They never take more than a surface can replace. Whether instinct or instruction guides them is unclear; the plants open pathways before them and close those paths afterward.

Their young are born with ordinary mouths. The jaw divides only after they have eaten their first architectural leaf. From then on, each animal acquires the shape of the district it grazes. Those from the arcades have long, curved jaws suited to vaults. Those from the tower have short, heavy shears that can withstand resonance. An old grazer’s skull is, in miniature, a map of its home.

The constrictors along the cornices are pale and patient. Their resemblance to vines is incomplete in one peculiar respect: they cast the shadow of a serpent even when their bodies seem vegetal. The city’s true vines sometimes cast the shadows of snakes. Birds inspect the shadow before landing.

These serpents hunt warmth. A mouse, frog, fledgling, or warm fruit will all attract them. After feeding, they coil around vascular trunks and lend their digestion to the building. Heat passes from snake to plant; sugars move from plant to snake. In the coldest chambers, several may entwine into a living radiator, indistinguishable from a braided root until hunger separates them.

Not every animal is an individual.

A dark, dog-sized shape sometimes crosses the Orchard Courts at twilight. It has a steady gait and a head that turns toward noise. Seen from a distance, it appears furred. Seen closely, it is composed of beetles walking over and under one another, mice enclosed within the swarm, moths fluttering at the surface, and fungal threads holding the whole assembly in temporary tension. No single part directs it. The shape forms because the creatures all wish to cross the plaza and because crossing as one large animal is safer than crossing as many small ones.

At the far arcade it comes apart. The mice enter cracks. The beetles climb. The moths lift away. A faint outline of spores continues for several steps after the rest have dispersed.

Other coalitions are less easy to recognize. A “bird” calling from a roof may be three frogs, a vibrating seedpod, and the returning echo of a real bird. A serpent may be a line of caterpillars beneath a single shed skin. Once, after the silence passed through the Reservoir Quarter, every animal there lost its weight. They clung to one another until gravity returned, forming a trembling sphere of wings, claws, shells, and tails beneath the ceiling. For years afterward that sphere occasionally reassembled during storms, though many of its original creatures were dead.

There are also inhabitants with no bodies in the usual sense.

Shadows accumulate. Most belong faithfully to animals, leaves, towers, and passersby, but some become detached during the city’s irregular afternoons. They gather in cool rooms and lie across one another like sleeping reptiles. A loose shadow can move only when something nearby moves, borrowing the gesture: the opening of a flower, the step of a bird, the bend of a branch. In this way it travels slowly through the streets.

Some seek their owners. Others avoid them.

A creature without a shadow is treated with great caution. Predators make way. Ant columns divide around it. Even the walls relax their thorns. Such a creature carries its missing darkness internally, and for a time it is deeper than its body. When its shadow finally emerges, it may bear outlines of places through which the animal never passed.

Echoes live here as well. Repeated enough, a sound ceases to need its source. Footsteps continue along empty arcades. Wingbeats circle sealed rooms. The cry of an extinct bird migrates seasonally between the tower and the orchard. Some echoes prey upon fresh sounds, swallowing their last syllables and growing more distinct. Others are harmless and attach themselves to animals like invisible young.

The oldest are the low roars inside the roots. They may once have been rain, rivers, or the movement of a vanished herd. Now they travel beneath the city and alter whatever grows above them. Where one pauses, a plaza forms. Where two cross, a tower begins.

The moving silence may be an inhabitant too, though none can say whether it lives in the city or comes there to feed.

It has no visible outline. It is known by the reactions preceding it: cicadas breaking rhythm, frogs flattening against the mud, leaves turning pale-side outward. The silence advances neither quickly nor slowly, because time is among the things it sometimes removes. Within it there is no muffling, no sense of ears being blocked. Rather, sound becomes a property the world has not yet acquired.

It takes small things because it cannot carry large ones. The red from a beetle’s shell. The downwardness of ripe fruit. A corridor’s destination. A bird’s memory of flight. An observer’s sight.

What it takes is rarely destroyed. It is misplaced. Color may later appear as a taste in a cistern. Weight may attach itself to a moth and pin it harmlessly to the sky. A stolen direction may settle in the split jaw of a grazer, which then walks east no matter how it turns. Sight sometimes enters the roots. On those nights, thousands of new buds open like eyes along the walls.

Beneath all these residents lives the city itself.

Calling it one plant is convenient but inaccurate. Its trunks do not share one ancestry. Its leaves employ incompatible chemistries. Some roots flower; some branches produce spores; some walls are formed by organisms that elsewhere would parasitize one another. Fungi run through every foundation, translating alarm into sugar, pressure into growth, injury into route. The city is less a single body than a permanent act of cooperation—though “permanent” and “cooperation” both conceal how much is consumed to sustain it.

It eats its oldest quarters. It traps parasites inside rooms and thickens wood around them. It allows beetles to tunnel, grazers to prune, birds to hunt, fungi to pass messages, and roots to strangle roots. It has no central organ and makes no decision all at once. A doorway begins as irritation. An avenue is a prolonged compromise between sunlight, migration, and thirst. A tower is what happens when many unrelated stems agree about thunder.

Its apparent emptiness is part of its life. The bare halls are breathing spaces. The plazas are open mouths for heat and rain. The streets keep roots apart as much as they draw animals together. What looks like planning may be the remembered pressure of countless bodies passing in the same direction until growth learned to leave room for them.

This may be why a new alley appears after someone watches the city vanish.

The city has felt the dimensions of a possible inhabitant.

It remembers width as distance between walls, height as the lift of a roof, stride as a rhythm of root joints. It remembers hesitation in the shape of a turning. By morning it has made a route not merely for the observer’s body, but for the habits carried inside it: where they would pause, which opening they would choose, how quickly they would flee.

Such alleys remain empty for a time.

Then the small residents investigate. Ants establish lanes beneath the threshold. Geckos press spores into the new walls. A bird walks its length and calls, listening to the altered voice that comes back. The spotted cat sleeps across the entrance. Roots taste the air.

Eventually the alley may close again.

Or it may continue inward, preserving the shape of someone who never entered, until that shape becomes another inhabitant: a hollow citizen made of expectation, walking forever toward the city’s center along the loudest street.
