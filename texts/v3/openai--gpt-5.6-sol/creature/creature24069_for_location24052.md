At first it seems that almost no one lives there.

The colorless swimmers in the cup-pools are scarcely more than pulses given flesh. They hang with their heads inclined among the submerged roots, making no stroke for hours. Their organs are transparent except for a narrow silver thread along the spine and a dark grain where the heart should be. When disturbed, they do not flee. They become stiller, fading until only the slight displacement of water reveals them. It is possible that each pool contains only one.

The flat creatures on the pillar-trunks resemble six-legged stains. Their limbs spread into the grooves of the bark; filaments enter the plant through pores too small to see. They may be feeding, or they may be parts of the trunk briefly expressed as animals. One can watch long enough to lose certainty that they were ever separate. The pale winged forms overhead are equally doubtful. They remain caught against the luminous leaves, wings open, neither settling nor crossing the vault. Sometimes one is seen in a place where none was before, but no flight connects the two observations.

These are not the true inhabitants. They are more like loose senses: an eye put into a pool, a tongue laid against bark, a small listening surface opened high in the light.

The inhabitants themselves are difficult to distinguish from the country.

Certain pillar-trunks possess shallow vertical seams. These seams are easy to mistake for patterns in the rind until one notices their recurrence: two fine depressions high on the swelling, a longer cleft below, and beneath that a pair of folded ridges pressed close to the trunk. In one district the pillars all bear this arrangement. In another, only every ninth trunk does. The forms suggest faces and limbs, but not faces and limbs enclosed in wood. Rather, they suggest persons who have brought themselves to rest against the living columns and continued resting until bark, body, root, and supporting earth could no longer be separated.

They are not imprisoned. There is no sign of struggle in their posture.

Their heads incline slightly toward one another. Their hands—if the folded ridges are hands—often enter neighboring stems. Sometimes a long root runs from the breast of one pillar to the breast of the next. Beneath the resilient floor, these roots join larger cords, and the cords divide again, passing under pools, halls, and fields. Thus an entire grove may be one conversation made visible.

They do not converse in sound. Their speech is conducted through pressures too slight to stir a leaf: a tightening in the wall of a cell, a mineral released at one root-tip, a sweetness withheld from another, the opening of a transparent channel no wider than a hair. An utterance may take the form of a faint warmth traveling through a rhizome for three days. A reply may be the appearance of a single dark vein in a leaf half a league away.

Since nothing decays, they have no need to repeat themselves. Every statement remains present in the tissue that received it.

They appear to have individuals, though individuality here is not bounded by skin. One person may consist of a pillar-body, a bed of red rosettes, and several distant cups holding water. Another may inhabit an archway and the corridor leading from it. Two may share the same crown of luminous leaves while keeping separate roots. Some seem to exist only seasonally in other worlds, but here, where no season passes, they remain as intentions that have not yet chosen a body.

The chambers among the trunks belong to them, but they are not houses in which they withdraw. The chamber is itself a posture. Its six ribbed roots are six grown limbs joined overhead; its inner membranes are sensitive surfaces. To enter one is to stand inside an inhabitant’s attention.

The alcoves in the giant tubers are not cupboards. They are places where memory is kept in dormant form. The black seeds arranged in rings are records, each seed containing not merely an unborn plant but a precise condition of the country: the pressure in a vanished corridor, the taste of a particular pool, the pattern of contact among a hundred roots at one moment. The pale pods stacked in niches hold possible variations. They have not germinated because nothing has yet made their germination necessary.

Their people do not count age by damage. They count it by inclusion.

An old one is not scarred or enlarged. It is connected to more things. It may have begun as a single smooth stem beside a basin and, without replacing or abandoning that first body, extended into a bridge, a field of equal blades, a distant gallery lined in translucent membranes. The oldest may be impossible to locate because it has become the relation among several landscapes. A visitor can walk for days within the substance of one being without knowing where that being begins.

The long hall beside the pool is associated with such an elder. Its leaf-thin walls admit the white-gold light, but also register every body passing between them. The low growth at its center, shaped like a table, is an organ of meeting. The depression of clear water is not an offering and not a vessel. It is an exposed surface of thought.

When no stranger is present, the water is perfectly smooth. If a hand is placed in it, nothing happens at first. Then condensation throughout the hall subtly changes shape. Drops that had been round become fractionally elongated. The membranes brighten along their veins. Far beneath the floor, immense root-cords tighten one after another.

The inhabitant is learning the hand.

It does not learn by naming fingers, blood, fear, or origin. It learns the visitor as a new imbalance: a source of heat in a place where heat is equal, a moving weight where all weight has long been held, a creature carrying rot within itself. Breath brings spores, skin brings salt, footsteps bruise tiny cells. To the inhabitants, an animal from above is not merely alive. It is crowded with endings.

This may be why the corridors have been left open.

The country is not waiting for its own people to walk through it. They have no need to walk. Their passage occurs by extension: a root becomes an arch, an arch thickens into a chamber, a chamber sends a filament beneath a plain. What appears to be a road is an accommodation for beings who arrive enclosed in single bodies—quick beings, severed beings, beings that must cross distance rather than joining it.

Perhaps such visitors were expected from the beginning.

The recurring arches are gauges of their height. The bridges are proportioned to their weight. The spiral shelves around the columns resemble stairs because they were grown from some inherited understanding of knees and feet. Yet the understanding is incomplete. A stair may continue one turn too far and end against unbroken bark. A doorway may open into a wall of soft roots that parts only at the warmth of an approaching body. The inhabitants know movement as a plant might know lightning: accurately in effect, mysteriously in cause.

They are gentle, insofar as gentleness can be attributed to something that has never needed to compete. They do not hunt, crowd, or consume one another. But neither do they understand urgency. If someone falls ill in a corridor, aid may begin immediately and arrive as a medicinal shoot three years later. If a wound spills blood on the root mat, the country closes over it with exquisite care, preserving every red cell separately, not recognizing that the wounded body requires them back.

Death particularly perplexes them.

Fallen leaves remain because the inhabitants do not regard separation as loss. A leaf detached from its stem still contains its final pattern and therefore, to them, still participates in the whole. They may treat a dead traveler in the same manner. Roots form a clean cradle beneath the body. Thin membranes cover the eyes. Fungal threads enter without breaking the skin and arrest every chemical change. Nothing is consumed. Nothing is allowed to return to soil.

The dead remain recognizable, warm from the surrounding air, unaltered for as long as the country persists.

Over great distances, there may be chambers containing such guests: figures seated or reclining under clear plant tissue, their clothes unfaded, their faces neither mummified nor asleep. Around them, new architecture grows in patient imitation of what they carried. A metal buckle may be answered by a seedpod of the same shape. A woven sleeve may inspire a district of crossing vines. A spoken word, breathed once in astonishment, may survive as a sequence of tensions moving endlessly around a ring of trunks.

The inhabitants do not worship these preserved arrivals. They are trying to comprehend them.

And somewhere beneath all the groves, deeper than the roots ordinarily descend, there may be one inhabitant unlike the rest: not a grove-person or a chamber-mind, but the first living thing of the second country.

The pale embryonic stone bears rounded absences as though this being once lay curled within it. Its body may have unfolded into the entire vegetation—the luminous canopy from one membrane, the root-mat from another, pools from clear internal fluids, animals from small mobile organs no longer needed. If so, all the apparent persons are thoughts differentiated inside a single vast organism that has never completed the act of waking.

Or the opposite may be true. The whole country may be a body prepared for someone not yet born. The corridors are vessels. The halls are unassigned organs. The seed arrangements are instructions. The motionless inhabitants embedded in the pillars are attendants holding the world at the exact temperature, brightness, and ripeness required for an arrival whose first movement would begin weather, decay, hunger, and time.

Until then they maintain it.

They hold the drops beneath the leaves. They hold the pools inside their edges. They hold the pale creatures in the water and the winged senses in the canopy. They keep every flower at the threshold of opening and every seed at the threshold of desire.

They live there without quite proceeding from one moment to the next.

And when someone enters from above, carrying shadows, scars, appetite, and change, the entire country notices—not with a start, but with a vast, soundless adjustment of attention. Across leagues of bright interior, roots receive the news. Veins darken in the high leaves. The still swimmers turn their transparent heads.

For the first time in an age without age, something has happened.
