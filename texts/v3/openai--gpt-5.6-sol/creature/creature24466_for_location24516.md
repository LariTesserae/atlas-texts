At first it appears that nothing lives there except the green stains.

They are easy to dismiss. A slick around the drain rim. A bruise beneath the conduit. Pale threads pressed flat in the grit behind a bracket. Under inspection light they have no depth and almost no color. They look less like growth than like something the water failed to carry away.

But the stains return too consistently.

They return after scraping, after disinfectant, after the sumps have been flushed hard enough to scour zinc dust from the steel. They reappear first at the same small defects: the lee side of a weld bead, the warm foot of a cable clamp, the roughened edge where the reservoir coating was touched up by hand. Then they emerge elsewhere, always downstream, as though the court were remembering their positions.

What lives there is not quite moss, not quite algae, and not—except briefly—an individual creature. Most of the time it exists as a film thinner than paint: translucent cells held in a clear adhesive skin. The green is only its upper surface, where the caged lamps provide a weak and unreliable harvest. Beneath that lies a colorless web threaded through sediment, sealant pores, frayed cable sheathing, and the microscopic valleys of machine-cut steel.

It occupies the court as water occupies it: discontinuously to the eye, continuously in fact.

The organism has no central body. Its oldest masses live below the grating, inside the shallow sumps where inspection tools cannot easily reach. There it forms soft, gray-green lobes under drainage lips. They flex in the current but resist being torn free. Fine strands run outward from them, adhering to bolt threads and entering the narrow annular spaces around pipe collars. Some strands are no wider than fungal hyphae. Others collect mineral grains until they resemble frayed wire.

Zinc should poison it. Instead, it binds zinc into pale granules and stores them along its outer membrane. Iron gathers there too, and copper, and traces of the reservoir’s blue-black coating. Older colonies become faintly metallic. When the amber lights strike them at the right angle, they show a dull sheen like oxidized solder.

The court’s violence has entered its anatomy.

Each pressure cycle compresses the film against the steel. Each relaxation draws water through it. The organism feeds not only on lamp light and airborne residue but on these repeated deformations. Its cells contain flattened mineral bodies that shift when the plates vibrate, opening and closing channels in their membranes. The low note of the pumps is, to it, both weather and metabolism. Ordinary load keeps its tissues spread. Trapped air causes them to contract. Before a purge, when the machinery enters its heavy pause, the entire colony tightens against the surface.

It knows the sump sequence.

At the narrow end, the first pump engages. The local films seal themselves beneath their slime and release minute dark capsules into the draining water. The next sump catches some; the rest continue. By the time the evacuation reaches the broad end, the capsules have crossed almost the whole court. They lodge in fresh scratches, under washers, in the wet recesses of warning-stripe paint. The violent emptying that should remove the organism is how it travels.

For this reason, maintenance spreads it more effectively than neglect. Boots carry capsules from one grating panel to another. Scrapers divide old colonies into living fragments. Rags lift the films and press them into seams. Even sterilizing foam cannot reach the threads inside the sealant. A cleaned court is merely a court in which the resident has been redistributed.

During the coldest periods it becomes nearly invisible. The green fades. Its surface dries to a clear varnish that cracks without flaking away. What remains alive retreats into the warm margins around conduits and motor-fed pipes. There it waits through the long intervals of thin air and freezing louvers.

When fog returns, the varnish swells.

For several hours the colonies produce their nearest equivalent to animals: soft, transparent bodies no larger than fingernail parings. They loosen from the film and creep along the underside of the grating. Each is a folded sheet with a fringe of cilia around its edge and a dark mineral line through its center. They do not swim well. In open water they tumble helplessly. On wet steel, however, they flatten and advance by rippling their margins, always toward vibration and slightly away from light.

These bodies gather at bolts, valve housings, and pipe saddles. They press themselves into the narrowest moving gaps, where friction has exposed uncoated metal. There they graze on oily residues and on the bacterial films warmed by stress in the structure. Most are crushed when the joint shifts. Their ruptured tissues seal the gap for a few hours and feed the colony around it. A few survive long enough to fuse with another film.

Nothing in the court hunts them. Nothing needs to. The station itself removes nearly all that are careless.

A purge fan can peel them from a handrail. A sudden sheet of rain sends them through the grating. A valve movement smears them into a green-gray crescent. If they venture onto a panel that dries too quickly, they become brittle outlines scarcely distinguishable from mineral scale. Their abundance is controlled by drainage, pressure, temperature, and the maintenance schedule. Their predator is operation.

Yet they have begun, in a small way, to alter the place.

Where the films persist, droplets behave differently. Water that once descended in separate wandering lines spreads into thin branching veils. Condensation lingers longer around colonized seams. Mineral fans broaden. A few imperfect bolts show less orange than neighboring bolts because the organism consumes oxygen at the metal surface; elsewhere corrosion deepens because its waste is acidic. The effect is inconsistent, almost experimental. The court and its resident are still new to one another.

The three valve wheels support distinct populations. The first, nearest the narrow end, bears a nearly black film adapted to repeated flooding. The second has a thin emerald rim where the lamp burns longest. The third appears clean, but its chipped underside is furred with colorless threads. When a wheel turns, fragments transfer to gloves. On the next inspection, some of those fragments will be carried beyond the court—to a stair rail, a tool cabinet, perhaps another drainage cavity elsewhere in the station. Most will die. Some will wait.

If the organism has a whole self, it is not located in any single stain. Its body is the pattern by which the stains recur. Its pulse is the forty-second rise and fall of pressure. Its circulation follows the sumps. The louvers govern its seasons. Lightning is only a total, instantaneous illumination to which every light-sensitive cell recoils at once.

It has never encountered soil. Snow is merely cold water carrying dust. Sky is a brightness beyond a slit, too dry and distant to enter. The mountain does not exist in its world. Nor do the valleys, nor the engineers who designed the reservoir. There is only the curved sweating wall, the bolted cliff, the amber intervals, and the deep mechanical rhythm passing through every surface.

It lives high above the clouds without knowing height.

When the station is at full load, the colony lies flat and almost featureless. The films cling. The transparent wanderers hide beneath washers. Every strand is drawn taut by vibration. Then the pumps ease, the structure gives a minute upward flex, and water begins gathering again on the blue-black wall.

Across thirty meters of seams and sumps, the green haze opens.

Not dramatically. Nothing rises. No shape steps into the light.

A million sealed pores admit water. Pale threads loosen from steel. The drain rim darkens. Beneath the warm conduit, two moss-colored stains spread by the width of a hair. Behind the cable bracket, the row of flattened sprouts inclines toward the coming flow.

The court begins to fill, and what lives there begins again.
