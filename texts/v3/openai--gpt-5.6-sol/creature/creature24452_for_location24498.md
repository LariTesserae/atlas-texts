The people of the city are not pale in any uniform way. Some are chalk-dusted until they seem carved from the walls; others are darkened by fat smoke, fungal powder, and the fine gray soil that enters every fold of cloth. Cleanliness has nothing to do with water. They groom with combs of bone, blunt scrapers, handfuls of absorbent dust, and pads of dry lichen. Hair is kept close or braided tightly beneath caps, because loose hair collects moth scales and bat lice. Skin is rubbed with animal fat where it cracks. The smell of a person—smoke, herd, fungus, old wool, family rooms—is considered part of the person. To remove it entirely would be both impossible and disorienting.

Their clothing hangs close to the body. It is made from hide shaved thin, felted hair, woven fungal fiber, and cloth repaired so many times that no original piece remains. Nothing flaps unnecessarily in the bat ways. Tassels are worn only where their movement has meaning: at a wrist to show the direction of a draft, at a shoulder to make children visible in dim chambers, along the hem of a vent keeper’s coat to announce changes in the air. Those who work beneath the great colonies wear hooded mantles with smooth, steep shoulders so dung and clinging animals slide away.

Most go barefoot within their own district. Soles learn what eyes cannot: the soft sink of a buried room, the ripple of an old stair beneath dust, the chill line that marks moving air. Travelers wear sandals with thin stone or horn pieces sewn into the soles, but even these are made flexible enough to feel a hollow before it gives way. A skilled pathfinder can cross several quarters without light, one hand on a polished wall and one foot testing the ground ahead.

They do not think of themselves as living underground. The cavern is not below anything in their ordinary speech. It is the breadth of the world. The plateau above appears in stories as a hostile roofless place where distances have no walls, light comes from everywhere, and water may fall out of the air in quantities large enough to kill. Those who climb toward the upper fissures—spore gatherers, vent clearers, hunters following bat passages—are regarded with the mingled respect and concern given to people who willingly approach an abyss.

Their eyes are well adapted to dimness but not transformed by it. In darkness they are as blind as anyone. Their true adaptation is attentiveness. They notice when an echo returns too quickly, when a familiar draft has taken on the mineral smell of a newly opened crack, when the bats above a roofless court have shifted three body-lengths along a seam. They recognize districts by the taste of their air. A child learns the city first as a sequence of surfaces and currents: rough wall, smooth wall, three thresholds, cold cheek, fungal odor, descending floor, open space.

Names often come from routes rather than ancestry. A person may be called Sela of the Bent Air, Orun Along the Red Wall, or Tavi Three-Courts. When a family moves, its designation may change within a generation. To insist on an old district-name after abandoning it is considered a form of boasting, as if one claimed to own the stones left behind.

Speech is quiet, close, and rich in contact. Conversation begins with a touch at the forearm so the listener knows who is addressing them. In the loudest hours, words disappear almost entirely. Hands flatten, hook, cross, and turn; fingers indicate numbers against another person’s palm. The signs for danger are large enough to be seen in fungal light, but intimate matters are spoken directly into the hand, throat, or shoulder, where vibration carries better than sound.

Children sign before they speak fluently. Their first warnings are not “no” and “hot,” but “hollow,” “flight,” and “bad air.”

They grow up among animals without imagining a clear separation between domestic and wild. The hoofed grazers are not owned in the manner of livestock. Herds have habitual routes, recognizable leaders, and long associations with particular neighborhoods. Families mark favored animals with collars of braided fiber, treat their cracked hooves, guard their young from predators, and take milk or blood sparingly when the animals are strong enough. An individual may be familiar for twenty years and still refuse to be touched.

The long-eared burrowers live closer to households. They nest in wall cavities and under warm sleeping platforms, tolerated because they eat certain fungal pests and give warning before a floor settles. Their sudden silence can empty a building faster than any shouted alarm. Children catch them, but killing one inside a house is unlucky—not as superstition, exactly, but because a house without burrowers is often a house already becoming unfit.

Beetles are everywhere and receive almost ceremonial patience. Dung workers are carried in pierced pots when new fungal beds are established. Carrion beetles are placed with the dead. Powder beetles that attack fiber stores are destroyed without mercy. The city’s people distinguish hundreds of kinds by touch, shell sound, feeding trace, and odor. What an outsider might call vermin is, to them, a population of laborers, thieves, cleaners, warnings, and food.

The bats are something larger than animals but not gods. No one expects mercy or intention from them. They are weather with fur, a moving ceiling, the city’s loudest law.

Families know the timing of local flights as precisely as other peoples might know sunrise. Meals pause before the first descent. Infants are covered. Fat lamps are pinched low. Then the air fills, and everyone waits while bodies stream along the avenues. A person caught in a major flight crouches with face hidden and elbows tight, not because the bats will attack but because panic spreads through millions faster than judgment through one.

Bat keepers climb the towers and upper ruins to watch the colonies. They count by occupied seams rather than individuals. They know which ledges hold nursing mothers, which dark clusters are sick, and which species feed high beyond the cavern while others comb the fungal courts. They collect fallen pups when they can, isolate diseased bodies, and close certain rooms during breeding periods. Their work is dangerous, filthy, and politically important. A mistake can redirect a flight through inhabited chambers or deprive an entire district of guano.

No ruler governs the whole city. Distance, noise, and the slow reversal of air make that impractical. House clusters send hands to district councils, and districts send remembered decisions along the main routes. The most trusted people are not warriors but counters, vent keepers, route readers, and those with long memories for structural change.

A vent keeper may spend a lifetime attending to a single fissure. They clear only loose dust, never widening the stone. Around each vent stands a common garden of clay vessels, ribbon plants, hair nets, condensation surfaces, and markers showing whose turn it is to drink. The keeper records yields with scratches on bone: not drops, for drops are rare, but darkening, weight, and taste. A vessel that gains the weight of a mouthful over a season can sustain argument for weeks.

Water is given first to the fevered, the very young, and those who must speak for long periods. Most moisture comes less directly—from plant flesh, fungal tissue, blood, milk, rendered organs, and foods sealed during the city’s colder breaths. People do not casually say they are thirsty. The word implies a claim upon others. They say instead that the tongue is rough, that the lips are splitting, or that a child should be watched.

Meals are small and frequent. Lichen is scraped, powdered, soaked in fatty broth when possible, and pressed into bitter cakes. Ribbon leaves are chewed for their inner tissue and then twisted into cord. Cork fungi are shaved thin, toasted over nearly spent coals, or ground with insect meal. Moths are trapped along cultivated luminous walls. Beetle grubs are prized. Meat is eaten after accidents, culls, and hunts at the margins, but no large animal is killed thoughtlessly; its body contains too much irreplaceable moisture.

Cooking fires belong to groups rather than households. A single sheltered hearth may serve several courts. People bring sealed pots to its keeper and sit well back from the smoke. The coals are buried after use and coaxed alive later. New flame, made by friction, is possible but costly. Someone who carries a live coal safely across districts is welcomed before being questioned.

Their crafts are works of abrasion, pressure, patience, and reuse. Stone polishers keep trays of harder grit inherited from predecessors. Bone workers remember which parts of which animals flex without splitting. Fiber braiders can make a rope from plant ribbons, hair, tendon, and old cloth, with each material placed according to the strain it will bear. Clay vessels are rarely fired hot; many are sunless, smoke-hardened forms repaired with resin, fat, dust, and wrapping. A vessel may have a name and a lineage longer than its keeper’s.

They make few objects solely for beauty, but beauty enters everything that must endure handling. A cup has a thumb hollow polished black. A door cord is knotted in the pattern of a household’s route. Bone pegs are incised with bat wings, beetle backs, folded leaves, and the paired lines that mean moving air. Their oldest art is not pictorial. It consists of touched surfaces: grooves followed by fingers, alternating textures, walls embedded with smooth stones at meaningful intervals. Some stories can be read only by walking them.

They have songs, though singing beneath the colonies is an intimate activity. Most are hummed into shared floors, resonant pots, or another person’s back. Percussion is soft: palms on hide, nails on hollow bone, beetle-shell rattles wrapped in fiber. Their grandest music is performed during the quietest point of an air reversal, when flight paths falter and the great chamber briefly seems to draw breath. Hundreds may gather across adjoining courts, unable to see one another, and sustain a low tone that passes from district to district. No individual hears the whole piece.

Courtship often begins with route sharing. To guide someone safely through an unfamiliar quarter is an act of trust. Gifts are useful and minute: a pouch of good abrasive, a strip of uncracked hide, a spoonful of fat, a map scratched into a shoulder blade. Vent access cannot be given as a bridal possession, but turns at a vessel may be exchanged publicly. Marriages join sleeping rooms more often than bloodlines. Separation is accomplished by unbraiding the common door cord and dividing its usable strands.

The dead are not burned. Fire is too dear, and smoke lingers. Nor are they buried deeply. A body is dried in a screened room where selected beetles and the moving air do their work. Bones may then be placed in sealed wall niches, made into tools with the person’s prior consent, or carried to one of the old subsided houses that serves as a communal ossuary. Names are spoken for as long as someone remembers the route to the place where the bones rest. When that route is lost, mourning is considered complete.

Some dead remain active in the city. A grandmother’s finger bone may become the toggle of a family water cover. A pathfinder’s polished kneecap may be set into a dangerous corner as a hand marker. This is not regarded as desecration. To be useful without thirst, hunger, or exhaustion is a gentle final state.

Fear in the city is rarely sudden. People fear slow things: a vent yielding less each cycle, a fungal bed becoming powdery, grazers abandoning a route, a ceiling seam widening by the thickness of a moth’s wing. Each household keeps memory cords knotted to record such changes. One knot means a new crack. Two mean movement. A wrapped knot means the matter was inspected. A cut cord means the rooms were surrendered.

Abandonment is performed without drama. Useful stone is removed. Beetles are gathered. Wall fungus is scraped into carrying sheets. The threshold is marked with an open hand crossed at the wrist: no sleeping beyond. Then the family leaves, often moving only a few streets away. For years afterward, someone may still turn instinctively toward the old doorway.

There are also people who prefer the emptier districts. Salt walkers cross the basin to gather crusts, set traps, and inspect the worn pillars. Guano miners work beneath remote colonies. Dust readers search buried rooms without opening them, tracing subtle depressions and testing echoes with padded taps. A few solitary cultivators live among the antler fungi and speak of them as companions, though they are careful not to claim the colonies as property. These people may tend one branching growth from youth to old age, harvesting no more than it can replace.

Predators live at the city’s edges and sometimes within it. Lean, low-bodied hunters wait where lichen avenues meet rubble. Pale snakes take burrowers in warm districts. Large insects haunt refuse hollows. The people kill what enters sleeping rooms, but beyond those rooms predation is accepted as part of the circulation that feeds the fungi and keeps the herds moving. An avenue too safe for too long becomes overgrazed.

Nothing living there is entirely independent. The bats bring fertility. Beetles distribute it. Fungi turn it into flesh. Grazers open the lichen mats and carry spores in their coats. Burrowers aerate old dust. People redirect all of these processes slightly, never enough to command them. Even the city’s laws are less a system imposed upon nature than a record of past mistakes.

The deepest distinction among the inhabitants is not between human and animal, or tame and wild. It is between those who move and those who hold.

The bats, herds, moths, hunters, and families move. The lichen, vent plants, fungal antlers, and beetle nurseries hold. The walls hold until they do not. The vessels hold almost nothing, and therefore are watched most carefully of all.

From a distance, a lived-in district may appear deserted. Its lights are too faint to see. Its people remain under roofs during flights. Its animals blend with stone. Only after waiting does the life separate itself from the ruin: a hand resting in a wall groove, a grazer stepping down from what seemed a terrace, a child’s face behind a fiber screen, moths spaced through the dark, a vent tassel lifting one thread at a time.

Then the ceiling loosens into wings, the avenues fill with moving air, and the whole city becomes inhabited at once.
