The people who live in the valley are called keepers, though they keep almost nothing fixed.

There are seldom more than six of them below the rim at once. Often there are only two: one moving the eastern walks, one at the upper weirs, each intermittently visible as a fleck of mineral orange or electric blue against the green. Their clothing is deliberately brighter than the warning signs. In rain it darkens almost to red; in sunlight it shines with embedded glass beads so that a lamp can find a fallen person beneath spray, fog, or the shadow of a root shelf.

They wear broad-soled boots that spread their weight, but no boot makes the marsh trustworthy. Each keeper carries a sounding staff with a blunt forked end. They do not thrust it into the ground so much as lay it against the surface and feel. A firm-looking mat may have a meter of water beneath it. A black pool may be only a skin over clay. Safe ground is known by a combination of resistance, color, tremor, and the way nearby sedges answer pressure. New workers look down as they walk. Experienced ones watch several paces ahead, reading faint tilts in the vegetation and ripples appearing where no wind touches.

Their most important instrument is not a gauge but an educated body.

They feel a turbine losing alignment through the handrail. They recognize a clogged underflow from a vibration in the soles. They can distinguish the deep, even note of well-burdened water from the irregular knocking of a pipe beginning to lift in its bed. After years in the valley, many develop an unconscious habit of touching walls, pylons, valve housings, even the grated floors beneath them. During conversation indoors, a keeper may stop with two fingers against the table, listening through the bones of the hand.

Outside, speech is nearly useless. Their working language is made of large, economical gestures. A flat palm lowered slowly means the marsh is rising. Two fingers divided around a fist mean water has found both sides of a footing. A hand held at the throat means not danger to the body, but danger to a channel: constriction, pressure, something forced where it should not be forced. At night they use lamps with hooded lenses, sending short pulses through rain. Radios are reserved for cabins and emergencies; in the open, the valley fills every frequency with water and metal.

Keepers do not think of themselves as operators of three powerhouses. Operators command flow. The keepers negotiate with it.

Their work consists of small permissions. They lift a grate to let willow stems pass without catching. They cut a notch in a peat lip so that a swelling pool will empty toward an old drainage run rather than invent a new one beneath concrete. They raise a pipe by the width of a hand. They clear moss from a sensor but leave it around the mounting bolts, because the moss steadies the surrounding silt. Where a bank begins to slump, they do not armor it immediately. First they weave living willow through it and wait to see whether roots can do the work more gently.

Every intervention is marked on maps, though the maps are strange documents. Solid lines are rare. Most channels are drawn as pale fans or fields of arrows, with dates layered over one another. Pools are not given outlines but centers of probability. Walkways are certain; buildings are nearly certain; everything else carries qualifications.

A keeper’s notebook may contain entries such as:

*Western seep audible under high water.*

*Mat breathing near pile 18.*

*Cold stream returned beneath the old road.*

*Willow island attached itself overnight.*

None of these is considered figurative language.

They sleep in the maintenance shelter on the eastern rise, where the continuous roar becomes a pressure in the walls. The shelter has six narrow bunks, but no one chooses the upper two because their frames resonate with the transmission lines in wet weather. Gloves hang above the drying grilles. Mugs migrate along the window ledge. Cabinets contain coils of cable, sterile dressings, climbing equipment, antifungal powder, emergency oxygen, and sealed packets of seeds belonging to plants already abundant outside.

The keepers bring little decoration. Anything light enough to hang will tremble, and anything absorbent will mildew. Instead, they collect stones found where stone should not be: a rounded cobble lifted onto a sedge raft by frost, a slab exposed when a pool shifted, a polished blue fragment coughed from beneath a root tunnel. These sit in shallow trays with their dates and locations written below them. Over years, the collection becomes another map of the valley’s movement.

Most keepers stay for rotations of ten or twelve days. A few remain through an entire thaw season and come away altered in small ways. In lowland towns they pause beside storm drains. They dislike rooms in which no pipe can be heard. They wake when refrigerators stop humming. Silence, at first, feels less like peace than mechanical failure.

No children are raised permanently in the basin. There is nowhere dry enough for ordinary childhood, and too many surfaces are only pretending to be land. Yet keepers sometimes bring older sons, daughters, nieces, apprentices, or students during the bright middle weeks. The first lesson is never machinery. It is how to fall.

They are taught not to struggle upright if a root mat breaks. They spread their arms, turn slowly, and distribute their weight. They do not seize willow branches without testing whether those branches are attached to shore or merely woven into another floating mass. They learn that the black water is cold enough to disable a hand before fear has finished arriving. Only after this are they shown gates, bypasses, transformer rooms, and turbines.

The keepers share the valley with a second, far more numerous population that they rarely see.

Under the root shelves, where the roar is reduced to a dense underwater murmur, live colorless crustaceans and translucent larvae brought in as eggs on birds’ feet or in mud clinging to equipment. They graze on films of bacteria coating drowned stems. Tiny worms inhabit the boundary where mineral flour becomes peat. Fungal threads pass between willow roots and heath roots, crossing channels too narrow for either plant to bridge alone. Gas gathers in silver blisters beneath the mats, made by organisms working without light.

This hidden community is the valley’s real digestive system. It turns the glacier’s ground stone into something roots can occupy. It consumes the dead foundations of each season and builds the next surface from them. The keepers monitor pH, oxygen, methane, temperature, but the measurements never quite capture the scale of the work. Beneath every bootstep, millions of bodies are converting exposed geology into memory.

The frogs near the transformer outfalls are the most conspicuous permanent animals. Their skin is dark olive, almost black, and the warmed drainage lets them remain active longer than frogs elsewhere at that elevation. They shelter in cavities under cable troughs and lay eggs in shallow basins where the water trembles continuously. Their tadpoles grow crooked tails from swimming against vibration. Keepers find them stranded in filter boxes and carry them out in cupped gloves.

There are moths as well, powder-pale and nearly without markings. During storms they enter through ventilation louvers and settle on warm switch cabinets. In the morning they must be moved before the machinery starts. Some keepers use a folded card; others let the moth climb onto a finger. In the tremendous scale of the power system, this has become a point of professional pride: nothing soft-winged is to be killed by a fan if someone can prevent it.

Ravens are visitors rather than residents, but the keepers know individuals. One bird favors the western pylon and drops objects onto the grating—bones, cable ties, once a stainless-steel nut stolen from an open tray. It calls into the valley’s roar and appears untroubled when no answer returns.

The oldest keeper says the raven is not calling to them. It is testing whether the valley has learned to answer yet.

No one is certain how much of this is superstition. The work encourages a careful kind of animism. The valley gives constant evidence of intention without possessing any: pools migrate toward warmth, roots close wounds, pipes climb from their trenches, channels abandon gates left open for them. To remain safe, the keepers speak as if every part has preference. They say a culvert is refusing water, a bank wants to open, a willow is holding the road together. The language is technically incorrect and operationally useful.

Over time, the distinction between the inhabitants and the inhabited place becomes difficult to maintain. The roots hold the sediment. The microbes feed the roots. The weirs hold the marsh. The keepers tend the weirs. The turbines pull water through the basin, and their waste heat creates winter refuges for frogs, ferns, insects, and films of algae. None of these lives independently enough to claim the valley.

Even the keepers are temporary structures within it.

Their walkways subside. Their records yellow. Their bright jackets move for a few seasons between the turbine houses and then are replaced by other bright jackets. Moss covers the impressions left by their knees. Willow enters the holes made by their sounding staffs. The water accepts their labor without preserving their names.

What lives there, most completely, is the green surface itself: not one creature, but a thin, interlocked common body stretched over unfinished stone. It breathes through sedge stems, feeds in darkness, shifts its organs sideways, and has acquired the machinery as another set of vessels. The keepers move across it like cautious impulses, locating injuries, opening constrictions, making sure no single current becomes fatal.

They do not keep the valley as it is.

They keep it able to become what it is becoming.
