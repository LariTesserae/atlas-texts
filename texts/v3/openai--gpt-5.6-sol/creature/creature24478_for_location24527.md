The inhabitants of the Low Ward are, at first glance, people.

They are not pale in any uniform way, nor thin, nor marked by some common deformity that would permit a visitor to recognize them beyond the basin. They have ordinary faces, ordinary illnesses, ordinary disagreements about money and noise. They wear layered coats with high, softened collars. Their gloves are patched at the fingertips because much of the city must be tested by touch. Their shoes have narrow soles, suited to stairs, grilles, wet tile, and passages where two people cannot comfortably pass. Many carry a piece of chalk, a small mirror, a length of string, and something personally irreplaceable to offer a machine in an emergency.

They walk without appearing hurried. Haste is considered a failure of preparation, and in the Ward it can also be spatially dangerous. A person who runs may reach a corner before it has become the correct corner. Children are taught to pause at every threshold until they can identify three confirming details: temperature, sound, and the behavior of their shadow. Adults do this almost invisibly. A hand brushes the jamb. The head tilts toward a pipe. One foot tests whether the floor accepts weight. Only then do they enter.

They do not give directions. They recite them.

A good set of directions has cadence and redundancy, and may include instructions that seem devotional: touch the rusted panel with the back of the hand; ignore the first smell of bread; if the stair has twelve steps, apologize and return. Route-keepers preserve these sequences in long, narrow books. The books resemble hymnals, and in some households are inherited like them. Marginal notes record seasonal changes:

*The fifth lamp has begun dimming second.*

*The green door is now blue when approached alone.*

*Do not take this route while grieving.*

Small children learn the neighborhood as a repertoire of verses. They may not know that the exchange lies twenty meters from their home. They know how to summon the intervening city.

Ward speech is quiet and precise. People avoid naming uncertain things too quickly, since names can encourage temporary phenomena to become permanent. They say *the sound under the cupboard*, not *animal*; *the room available on Tuesdays*, not *bedroom*. Questions of location are answered with verbs. A person is not *in* the western stacks but *having crossed* the warm bridge, *waiting beyond* the porcelain posts, *not yet returned by* the cistern route.

This makes outsiders think the residents are evasive. They are not. They simply regard nouns as promises.

Names, including personal names, are used carefully. Most inhabitants have a public name for municipal records, a household name used by relatives, and a private identifying sequence known only to themselves and one or two trusted people. The sequence might be a pattern tapped on a radiator, the order in which five objects are arranged on a sill, or a short account of a memory no one else possesses. It is used when a person’s continuity is in doubt.

Continuity is often in doubt.

Apartments overlap. Reflections lag. Cisterns show rooms from incorrect hours. A resident may return home to find a nearly identical individual already removing wet shoes by the stove. Such meetings are handled without spectacle. Both parties sit apart, drink something hot, and answer questions posed by a household witness. Neither is asked which one is “real.” The more useful questions are: Who remembers the cracked cup before it broke? Who is owed three measures of salt by the woman downstairs? Who promised to inspect the northern grille? Which one does the cat-shaped absence under the boiler avoid?

Sometimes both remain.

The Ward has terms for these people that do not translate cleanly. They are not twins, copies, or alternatives. They are said to have become *mutually additional*. Municipal law treats them as one person for debts incurred before the division and separate people for injuries sustained afterward. Families are less tidy. A spouse may live with both, one, or neither. Children often adapt fastest, assigning each version a descriptive suffix: the father who whistles, the mother with the warmer hands.

In other cases, the two slowly exchange memories until distinction becomes inconvenient. Then they resume a single name, even if two bodies continue to attend work.

Birth is common enough, though pregnancy is watched closely near probability regulators. Midwives carry mechanical clocks, iron keys, glass marbles, and strips of cloth from rooms that have remained stable for at least a generation. A newborn is not formally named until its shadow has attached, usually within three days. Before that, it is called only *the guest*.

Some infants cast shadows that anticipate their movements. Some shadows trail by several breaths. These variations are recorded but not treated as illnesses unless the shadow attempts to leave the dwelling. Nursery walls are painted in matte colors and kept free of mirrors. Cradles are anchored to at least two versions of the floor.

Ward children are serious in corridors and reckless in designated safe rooms. They play games involving impossible architecture because to them it is domestic material. They race messages through shared windows. They fold paper buildings and test which ones acquire interiors overnight. They dare one another to drink from shallow cisterns and then spend hours arranging their retained shadows into rude postures.

They also feed measurements to the black maintenance shapes, despite every notice.

They leave lengths of string marked in centimeters under malfunctioning exchangers. By morning the strings are usually blank, tangled into dense knots, or stretched across distances longer than themselves. Children collect these results and trade them. The rare string that comes back with an additional unit is highly prized.

Education is divided between ordinary subjects and civic survival. Students learn arithmetic, history, material science, sequence literacy, threshold etiquette, and the ethical treatment of alternate outcomes. They are taught never to wedge open a door that wants to close, never to follow their own voice through a drain, and never to make a promise in a room that appears larger on the inside unless they intend the room to enforce it.

Their history lessons are difficult. Records contradict one another not through error but because the past changes its load-bearing arrangements. Teachers present several incompatible foundations of the city and ask pupils to identify which version currently supports the school. Examinations may be postponed if the correct answer becomes dangerous.

The people who maintain the Ward are its most respected inhabitants, though they seldom agree on what maintenance means.

They wear dark waterproof aprons over insulated clothing. Their kits contain calipers, tuning forks, blank keys, wax, copper staples, scented oils, old photographs, sealed vials of room air, and written apologies addressed to devices. Each tool has a technical purpose, even when no one can articulate it in conventional terms.

A regulator may fail because its ceramic housing has cracked. It may also fail because nearby residents have ceased believing the intersection is unlucky, depriving the device of the hesitation it uses to prevent collisions. A technician must distinguish these conditions. One requires resin. The other requires rumor.

Senior maintainers are often recognizable by their mismatched pockets. Years of working around exchanged properties leave small inconsistencies. A coat pocket may be deeper than the coat. A woman may carry a wrench whose weight belongs to her left hand even when held in the right. Some have voices that emerge from slightly above or behind the mouth. These are occupational effects, discussed in the same tone as stiff knees.

They keep the city working by cultivating precedent. If a machine cannot be repaired, they arrange for it to have never quite failed. They reproduce the objects around it, repeat the last successful service conversation, restore the smell of the corridor, and persuade the local sequence to continue. The best technicians are part engineer, part actor, part historian, and part liar. Their lies must be structurally sound.

There are specialists who tend weight. They cross the cable-webbed alleys before snow and listen for tension through their teeth. There are clock negotiators who work in shifts beneath the municipal dial, returning with days of stubble after what colleagues experienced as an hour. There are hallway archivists who compare nearly identical corridors, storing public records in scuff marks, stains, and the fractional differences between lamps. There are condensation readers, frost interpreters, cistern witnesses, door shepherds, foundation listeners, and surveyors whose professional oath forbids them from completing a map.

Surveyors are considered brave and slightly indecent. They move through the Ward imposing temporary distances on things. Their measurements often remain true long enough to distort nearby homes. Residents complain of kitchens becoming exactly four meters wide, or of a bed suddenly acquiring a fixed relationship to the street. Surveyors therefore work behind cloth screens and erase their figures at the end of each shift.

The inhabitants eat mostly foods that tolerate indirect storage: roots, hard cheeses, smoked grains, dense breads, fungi cultivated in heat exchangers. Fresh produce is expensive, not because the basin cannot receive it, but because biological freshness is difficult to distinguish from temporal displacement. A green apple may be newly picked or may simply not yet have experienced the months since harvest.

Cooking is social and practical. Ovens stabilize rooms. Boiling pots give local humidity a harmless place to gather. Shared meals keep several people synchronized. In older households, everyone touches the table before eating so that the room registers the full number present.

Restaurants advertise not by cuisine but by reliability: *the same soup since the third deepening*; *seven tables, always seven*; *windows remain exterior through supper*. The most popular tea stall in the central square is operated by a man who is never seen arriving or leaving. He claims that his kiosk predates all accessible routes to it. His customers believe this only on cold days.

Ward religion, where it exists, is modest and plural. There are shrines to continuity: a nail hammered through several layers of wall, a lamp that has been kept burning despite drawing no fuel, a shelf holding cups from demolished homes. There are congregations that worship the unfinished ground as a first substance, and others that consider it an intrusion from a world where matter still behaves naïvely.

Some people pray to the deep cisterns, though no one agrees whether the pools listen. Offerings are never dropped into the water. They are placed beside it, because anything given to a cistern directly may appear in an adjacent life rather than being received.

Funerals are quiet and procedural. The assembling ground makes burial unreliable, and cremation risks exporting a person’s heat into some unused afternoon. The dead are therefore usually brought to civic rooms built to hold a single interpretation. There, relatives speak the identifying sequence, close the person’s accounts, detach lingering shadows, and list all doors through which the deceased is no longer expected to return.

For several weeks afterward, the family leaves one minor habit unfinished: a cup unwashed, a sleeve unbuttoned, a daily greeting unspoken. This gives stray versions of the dead somewhere small to arrive without taking over the household.

Occasionally one does return. The Ward distinguishes return from survival. A returned person may have the correct memories but no body heat, or may be visible only through shared windows. They are not driven out. Nor are they simply welcomed home. A committee of relatives and civic witnesses determines what kind of continued existence is possible. Some become archive attendants, cistern observers, or night custodians in rooms inaccessible to fully present people.

Thus the population includes those who are no longer alive in the ordinary sense but remain locally useful.

There are also residents who have become architectural.

An elderly woman may gradually transfer into her apartment: first her habitual warmth remains in the chair, then her breathing moves into the ventilation, then doors begin refusing visitors she disliked. Eventually her body dies or disappears, but the dwelling continues her preferences. Her descendants maintain it, speak to it, and argue with it. Such a home is listed as an inhabited person. It can own property, though it cannot move except by exchanging rooms with neighboring structures.

Conversely, rooms sometimes become human enough to leave.

They emerge slowly. For months a chamber may show signs of interior restlessness: furniture turning toward the door, dimensions contracting during sleep, pipes developing pulse-like knocks. Then a person unknown to the household appears in the corridor, dressed in colors matching the wallpaper, carrying no history beyond the sensations of enclosure. These people are called vacancies, though the word is not insulting. Many become excellent builders. They understand shelter as an anatomy.

Vacancies dislike wide plains. They sleep curled tightly and become anxious when no wall is within reach. Some retain architectural traits: a voice that absorbs echoes, skin cold on one side and warm on the other, dreams in which strangers pass through them. They tend to marry either surveyors or route-keepers, professions capable of giving their lives boundaries without making them feel trapped.

The black shapes beneath failing machinery may also be inhabitants, though municipal doctrine classifies them as by-products. They vary from fist-sized softnesses to low clusters large enough to obstruct a passage. They possess no stable limbs. When undisturbed, they huddle around uncertainty, feeding—if feeding is the word—on quantities that equipment can no longer reconcile.

They consume discrepancies: missing pressure, excess duration, unassigned weight. A machine surrounded by them may resume operation while becoming impossible to calibrate. This is why notices forbid giving them measurements. A named measure gives them form.

Children who disobey have reported black things becoming briefly specific: a little animal with too many elbows; a hand walking on its fingertips; a hooded person no taller than a kettle. Specificity frightens them. They scatter and resume softness.

They may be the city’s immune system. They may be pests. They may be the discarded remainders of failed calculations. Maintainers speak to them politely and carry no opinion in writing.

The one tree is treated less as vegetation than as a distributed neighbor. Each courtyard assigns someone to watch it. These six keepers meet monthly to compare scars, frost, buds, and the objects caught in its branches. They are careful never to prune only one instance. When a limb must be cut, six saws begin together.

During budding, the keepers sleep poorly. Each leaf opens onto a small landscape, and occasionally something looks back through it. Most are uninhabited expanses: yellow grass bending under wind, red scree, shorelines under unfamiliar moons. But one spring, several leaves showed clustered lights moving across a dark valley. Another year they displayed a room containing six people gathered around a leafless tree.

The keepers closed the courtyards and recorded nothing publicly.

As for the small quadrupeds whose tracks begin at the cisterns, residents have developed many practices and no knowledge. Bowls of food are left out and found untouched but older. Fine ash spread around the pools records prints leading away, sometimes toward walls, sometimes into apartments whose doors remained locked. On rare mornings, a resident wakes with damp grit at the foot of the bed and the conviction that something stood there all night trying to remember domestication.

No one has seen the animals. Several people remember having seen them.

These witnesses disagree on every detail except tenderness.

The long-winged bird is perhaps singular, perhaps a category of event. It crosses above the basin without effort, rigid against the sky while buildings adjust minutely beneath it. Residents look up when its shadow passes, but they do not point. Pointing has been known to transfer its fixed orientation to the hand. Older people bare their heads. Children count windows until it is gone.

The bird never lands. Feathers are sometimes found caught in the silver cable webs, though examination shows them to be made of layered floor plans.

Below all these lives is the ground itself.

Maintainers who work in trenches become reluctant to call it inert. The soft stone closes around bolts with something like healing. Strata select what they preserve. Mineral films gather beneath frequently spoken names. The new geology appears to notice repetition and to mistake habit for pressure.

The oldest workers tell of tools recovered from layers that had not yet formed. One foundation listener found the impression of her own hand in stone below a building erected before her birth. The palm print showed a scar she acquired years later. She no longer enters excavations, but each winter fine gray grit appears inside her gloves.

Around the unfinished patch in the central square, people behave as they would around a sleeping infant or a dangerous machine. They lower their voices. They keep the rail clean. They do not throw coins. Municipal observers record the tiny ridges, the locking and loosening grains, the first attempts at slope.

Yet residents also bring things there.

Not offerings, officially. Accidents of proximity. A button placed on the rail. A house key forgotten in the powder. A child’s drawing blown under the barrier. A screw, a tooth, a piece of blue tile. By the following week these objects may be gone, incorporated into some microscopic seam, while elsewhere in the Ward a new threshold develops the same curve as the lost key, or a wall acquires the blue of the tile.

Some believe the powder is learning construction from the city.

Others believe it is learning the inhabitants.

They point to ridges that resemble sleeping bodies, to damp hollows the size of fingerprints, to paired grains that separate and reunite when someone crosses the square. They say the geology is not merely assembling stone but practicing attachment, memory, weight, and loss—the pressures from which a person might eventually be made.

This belief has produced a small vigil. At every hour someone stands by the rail and describes ordinary life to the unfinished ground. They tell it how to boil grain, how to recognize one’s own door, how long to wait when the third lamp dims. They explain jokes. They recount debts. They describe the difference between a room and a family, then admit that in the Ward the distinction is uncertain.

The listeners consider this foolish.

The speakers consider it maintenance.

And deep beneath them, where dark laminae form around the future impressions of discarded things, something registers the cadence of their voices. It does not yet know whether it is bedrock, city, machine, animal, or citizen.

In the Low Ward, these may only be stages of residence.
