What lives in the Blackwater Knot is difficult to separate from what the Knot has preserved.

The plants are alive, certainly, though their life is almost indistinguishable from patience. They do not seek light. They feed on pressure, on mineral cold, on the minute differences between one darkness and another. Their roots are sensitive as tongues. A root encountering open water hesitates; one touching an old leaf follows the leaf’s veins exactly, laying a pale replica over them. Moss closes around bubbles without breaking their reflected skies. Liverworts grow thicker on the sides facing distant rain.

They are less a garden than a slow nervous system. A touch in one quarter may be answered months later by the curling of a fern elsewhere. When the black fish passes, plants fold ahead of it, not behind, as though its route has already traveled through their roots.

The colorless amphipods live closest to the familiar world. There may be many, or there may be only one appearing at different ages. Each is clear except for a thin brown line running through its body: swallowed clay, spores, fragments of ancient leaves. They walk on the undersides of things because “under” is the orientation least likely to change. Their feet test every surface before trusting it. If a leaf has become a wall since their last crossing, they stop and wait until it becomes a leaf again.

They graze on the pallor inside old foliage. Where they have fed, the memory of light goes out.

The black fish is not truly blind. It has no eyes because sight in the Knot does not arrive from a direction. Instead, its whole skin perceives enclosure. It feels the size of the chamber the way a surface fish might feel warmth. Every root is a line against its body; every niche is a cool mark along its flank. In open water it becomes restless, but in dense growth it moves with assurance, passing through spaces narrower than itself without touching them.

It is seen only singly. Whether this is because there is only one is uncertain. Sometimes, far beyond it, a second sequence of plants closes in answer to its passage. The two sequences never meet.

It eats the sideways-drifting bubbles.

It does so rarely and only when a bubble’s captured sky has become overcast. The bubble disappears into its mouth without distortion. For a while afterward a faint weather moves beneath the fish’s skin: clouds crossing the belly, a brief blue along the spine, once the white flicker of distant lightning. Such brightness does not illuminate the water. It makes the surrounding darkness seem to turn its attention toward the fish.

The little constructions belong to smaller inhabitants, if they belong to inhabitants at all.

These are never seen bodily. They are known by intervals: a niche emptied of silt, three stones newly touching, a twig ring tightened around nothing. Their measurements remain consistent even when the chamber changes. Every arch is high enough for something no larger than an alder seed. Every threshold bears a shallow central groove. No tracks approach or depart.

Perhaps the builders live inside the clay and extend only their work into the water. Perhaps each is soft, transparent, and spread across a wide area, with a stone arch no more than the hard tip of some buried organ. Perhaps there are no builders, and the structures are what stones become when left long enough in water that remembers doors.

Yet the arches are maintained. Moss is allowed to cover their outer faces but not their openings. Fungal threads stop at their thresholds and divide neatly around them. Sometimes one contains a bubble too small to have drifted there, and within that bubble is not sky but a room: dry walls, a low fire, figures sitting with their hands folded. The view is too minute to determine whether those figures are people or arrangements of twigs.

The seven-step stair may be connected to them, though nothing fingernail-small could comfortably use it. The proportions are wrong. Each tread is shallow, but the descent implied by the stair feels immense. Near it, the unseen builders become active. Pebbles turn in the mud. Clay gathers in ridges. New niches appear facing the steps, like seats provided for witnesses.

Nothing is ever seen ascending or descending.

There are also the mouths.

They appear where silt has lain undisturbed long enough to acquire depth. At first one resembles a small circular fault edged with crystals. Later the crystals are plainly teeth—not the mineral imitations of teeth, but wet, colorless points rooted in a rim of denser mud. They incline inward. Between them is a shaft containing water darker than that of the chamber.

The mouths do not bite. They filter stillness.

When something struggles nearby, the opening grows faint and disappears beneath stirred sediment. When all movement ceases, it becomes distinct. Minute particles begin drifting toward it, though loose stones fall elsewhere. It draws in hesitation, the held breath of animals that do not breathe, the long unspent instant before a root snaps. This may be why nothing has witnessed one close: the act of waiting for closure feeds it and holds it open.

Deep behind certain mouths there is a pulse, but not a heartbeat. It is too slow and irregular. Centuries may pass between one inward pressure and the next. Each pulse slightly rearranges the chamber. Walls exchange their burdens. The stair grows old or young. An amphipod finds itself on another leaf without having crossed the interval.

Whether all the mouths belong to one buried animal cannot be known. If they do, the animal is larger than the place that contains it. The chamber may lie within its porous flesh, with roots and moss growing through it as weeds grow through the ribs of a drowned carcass. But it may be the opposite: the mouths could be tiny independent things, and their apparent unity only another effect of distance becoming strange.

Some of the inhabitants come from elsewhere by accident.

Larvae enter through flooded hoofprints and remain after their pools above have dried. They continue developing according to the remembered shape of those pools. One carries a skin patterned with the reflection of a fence that no longer stands. Another grows legs suited for climbing a blade of grass that exists only in a bubble. Few complete their transformations. Those that do emerge into pockets of greater darkness, unfold wet wings, and fly without moving away.

Seeds fall into the Knot and root before landing. Each retains an allegiance to its lost surface. Duckweed makes a pond within the wall. A sedge seed may produce a long green strand at whose far end hangs a summer ditch, complete with gnats and the tremor of cattle passing nearby. These borrowed places are small and inaccessible, but their inhabitants sometimes look inward. Tiny pond skaters can be seen standing on the vertical boundary of the clay. Frogs call from distances no wider than a leaf.

Their calls make no sound in the chamber. They appear instead as rings in the silt.

The oldest resident is likely not an animal.

It inhabits the compressed leaf beds and mineral crusts, occupying copies rather than bodies. Whenever clay dissolves and reforms in the shape of what it touched, something passes with the shape. It moves from leaf vein to clay vein, from root scar to shale edge, from the impression of a pebble to the pebble itself. It cannot reproduce an entire object at once. It accumulates one contour at a time.

Over ages it has assembled approximations: a frond made of clay, a root made of mineral stain, a fish-shaped cavity lined with old leaf skin. None of these move while observed. Yet after the chamber rearranges, they are often nearer the entrance.

The little arches may be its attempts to copy the split between the alder roots.

The niches may be copies of the hollow.

The stair may be something remembered from a water that once gathered on a human-made step, seven shallow puddles joining through cracks, their undersides descending here.

If so, the oldest inhabitant is trying to construct an exit without understanding what an exit is. It repeats openings at smaller and smaller scales, each leading farther inward. Behind every door lies water. Beyond every copied threshold is another crowded chamber, another pale root ending in a fresh break, perhaps another black fish turning through the dark.

And there may be no single oldest resident. The copying may simply be how life behaves here. Things endure by becoming the surfaces around them. Animals become traces, traces become structures, structures become organs, and organs become rules of the water.

Thus the Knot itself is not alive in the ordinary sense. It has no will, no center, and no boundary it reliably keeps. But it notices pressure. It retains injuries. It grows more intricate where it is entered. A creature moving through it teaches the walls the creature’s shape, and long after the creature is gone, passages continue narrowing around that absence.

Those who live there survive by offering the place as little shape as possible.

The amphipod makes itself a line.

The fish makes itself a passing closure of leaves.

The builders, if they exist, remain behind their doors.

The mouths become circles in mud.

Only the Knot grows more definite: quietly filling with roots, reflections, ruins, and patient imitations, making inhabitants of everything it cannot let decay.
