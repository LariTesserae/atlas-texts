The people of the Unfinished Escarpment are difficult to distinguish, at first, from its machinery.

They wear the same colors as the city: soot black, oxidized blue, wet-concrete gray. Their coats are long and many-layered, stitched with pockets for chalk, tuning forks, folded permits, emergency cord, and small objects whose continued existence must occasionally be checked. Their boots are broad, iron-weighted, and resoled repeatedly. Nearly everyone wears an ankle bell. In crowded districts the bells produce a soft, irregular ringing beneath the factory noise, each note confirming that a foot has reached the ground and that the ground, for the moment, consents.

They move without hurry. This is not lethargy. Haste belongs to things insufficiently established.

A local child learns early never to leap across a crack merely because it is narrow, never to stand beneath a sound whose source has not yet occurred, and never to answer someone glimpsed at the far end of a bright corridor until they have turned their face. They learn to count stairs by touch rather than sight. They learn that a doorway may be honest in the morning and approximate by evening. If lost, they are taught to remain near something slow—a rusting pipe, a growing tree, a resting animal—because slow things have the strongest allegiance to place.

The oldest residents are called anchors, though never to their faces. They remember streets before the streets acquired alternative destinations. Their recollections are consulted alongside cadastral records and bore samples. An anchor may be invited to sit all afternoon beside a disputed building and say what used to stand there, while engineers measure whether memory exerts enough pressure to keep the foundations from shifting.

Some do. Some do not. Memory, like altitude, changes when entered into official documents.

Families are broad and somewhat negotiable. Kinship follows birth, adoption, shared residence, transferred names, and occasionally architectural accident. Two households that discover they have occupied opposite versions of the same kitchen for thirty years may declare themselves cousins. If a dead person’s name migrates to another marker, the receiving family does not inherit property immediately; first they inherit obligations. They attend anniversaries, repair the grave house, and learn the deceased’s preferred songs. Only after several years do they decide whether the new relation is genealogical, civic, or merely orthographic.

Newborns are taken to an exposed platform as soon as they can safely be carried. The person holding the child turns slowly through every available horizon. Smoke, cloud, mills, distant lowland lights, the black line of the escarpment—all are named aloud. This is less a blessing than an introduction.

“This is the city,” the adult says. “This is the edge. This is above, when above is here. This is where we will look for you.”

Only then is the child considered fully present.

Many inhabitants work at trades that would make little sense elsewhere. There are prospective masons, who build abutments for bridges not yet required. There are crack clerks, who visit painted seams in the rock and record their widening in fractions of a millimeter. There are rain conservators licensed to collect water that has fallen out of order. There are route singers who accompany freight through spatially unreliable districts, chanting landmarks in sequence so the road does not become distracted.

Surveyors form a large, melancholy profession. They are identifiable by their brass cases and the white dust on their cuffs. One staff measures elevations, distances, grades, and angles. Another follows behind to measure the effects of recording those figures. The two groups maintain a courteous rivalry. Marriages between them are considered either extremely stable or inadvisable.

Miners are more patient than either. They sit in low galleries beside the ascending veins, listening for the minute pressure of ore approaching through stone. Their tools resemble razors, spoons, and surgical planes. They do not speak of striking riches. They speak of receiving them. A good miner knows how much copper may be removed without startling the remainder back downward.

Factory workers cultivate a similar restraint. They spend years beside a single engine as it advances through the works. One crew may cast its housing, another wait eighteen months for the metal to forget the furnace, another fit bearings, another listen through winter for any uncertainty in the gears. Workers often grow proprietary toward machines they will never see completed. They leave notes inside access panels for crews six districts and four years ahead:

*The left shaft prefers cold mornings.*

*Do not tighten until it has heard thunder.*

*This assembly has mistaken brass for an ancestor; indulge it.*

The highest-status factory workers are the certifiers. They stand before a finished object and determine whether it has become itself. No instrument can fully establish this. The certifier touches the casing, listens to the idle rhythm, reviews the production history, and asks a few ordinary questions aloud. An engine that causes the room to feel suddenly hypothetical is sent back for another season.

Lift operators are a people apart. They spend their lives traveling between incompatible heights, seated beside duskglass brakes and banks of worn levers. Many keep blind fish in buckets beneath their consoles, taken from condensation ponds in the tower. The fish respond to changes in gravity before gauges do. When they all turn nose-down at once, the operator stops the cage, even if nose-down points toward a wall.

Operators develop powerful legs, pale faces, and an indifference to views. They say the worst passengers are visitors from the lowlands, who insist on asking whether the cage is going up or down. In the towers, this is considered a private matter between the lift and its destination.

The municipal administration is immense. It occupies several buildings that may once have been one building. Clerks issue permits for temporary riverside dwellings, suspended orchards, mobile foundations, and rooms that possess more entrances than walls. Gravity revisions are posted in tram stations on yellow paper. Citizens read them carefully, then add handwritten local corrections.

Inspectors carry red thread, wax seals, plumb bobs, tuning forks, and collapsible poles. They test staircases by ascending halfway, descending farther than they ascended, and checking whether they have aged. They verify that bridges over solid ground remain capable of opening when the gorge arrives. They fine landlords who illuminate hallways to the point of excessive clarity.

Yet bureaucracy is not mocked as much as outsiders expect. The people understand that forms, stamps, and registers are crude devices for persuading events to occur in an acceptable order. A permit cannot prevent a canyon, but it may convince the canyon to open after a warehouse has been moved.

Those who live in the Condenser Ward are greener than the rest. Moss stains their cuffs. Orchard workers wear hooked shoes and carry pruning knives made from ceramic, since steel can encourage certain branches to grow backward into seed. Their pale roof fruit is sold wrapped in black paper. Children there climb vines before they walk confidently on pavement. They know which boiler flowers close before a pressure surge and which mushrooms repeat overheard conversations after rain.

Other districts have their own human types: foundry families with metallic dust permanently caught in the lines of their hands; switchyard dwellers who can identify locomotives by the vibration of gravel; market people who dismantle a stall faster than the Hesitant can deepen around their ankles; outer-platform shepherds who guide chimney stags between exhaust plumes.

The stags are not domesticated, though they tolerate being counted. Their hollow antlers collect the city’s airborne residue and turn it into music. Foundry workers recognize individual animals by chord. A deep three-note stag called Old Furnace may be known across several districts and for several generations, though no one is certain whether this is one animal or a hereditary resonance passed through the herd.

Traffic yields to them because the stags remember older streets. When a herd stops before a wall, surveyors are summoned. Often, behind the wall, an intersection remains in some dormant spatial sense. Small shrines accumulate at these places: bent nails, salt, tram tokens, antler-shaped pieces of bread.

Cliff hares live wherever condensation and structural strain coincide. Their narrow paws flower into gripping fingers against vertical surfaces, allowing them to browse on walls and sleep beneath beams. Warehouse crews feed them bitter lichen and pretend not to. Their warning screams are unpleasant but trusted. When every hare on a column suddenly descends, workers clear the cable floor without waiting for an alarm.

Children imitate them, pressing spread fingers to masonry and claiming to hear the building think.

The gliders are less beloved. In flight they appear by subtraction: scraps of darkness crossing dim lamps, folding and unfolding an uncertain inventory of wings. No one agrees whether a flock contains hundreds of animals or one animal repeatedly occupying the air. They nest in cracks and emerge flat with dust, sometimes bearing small lost objects embedded between their translucent ribs.

There are licensed glider catchers who climb towers at twilight with soft nets. They retrieve keys, wedding rings, machine tags, teeth, and letters that entered gaps years before. Payment is due only if the returned object still belongs to the claimant.

Bellbacks are kept in courtyard basins and beneath leaking cisterns. Their wet skins ring when struck by drops. Households learn the particular music of their colony. A sudden note in dry weather may mean an out-of-order storm is approaching, or has already passed somewhere nearby. Some composers write works for bellbacks and roof runoff, though performances may take months to finish.

There are, inevitably, things no registry classifies.

Workers in the deepest galleries report warm impressions in the rock, as if enormous bodies have recently risen from stone benches. Night watchmen see figures waiting on hinged bridges over unbroken ground. These figures never approach. They lean over railings and stare downward into the future gorge.

In abandoned stations, vines sometimes arrange themselves into the posture of commuters. They stand beneath dead signs with flowers turned toward tunnels. When trams pass on nearby lines, all the blossoms incline together.

And inside the mountain, where unborn rivers can be heard, something seems to listen back.

The city’s inhabitants do not give this presence a single name. Engineers call it subsurface adjustment. Miners call it the listener. Children call it the one under tomorrow. A few of the oldest anchors insist it is not beneath the city at all, but that the city is being slowly remembered by it—and cliffs, rivers, animals, factories, and citizens appear as the memory gains detail.

This possibility does not alter the work schedule.

People marry. They complain about tram service. They grow black-needled trees in boxes outside windows. They drink hot mushroom broth after shifts and argue over lowland constellations. They attend river arrivals with baskets and river departures with ropes. At funerals they wait for the engraved name to settle. At festivals they hang thousands of hooded lanterns, taking care that no square becomes too bright.

Their songs are quiet and long. Melodies repeat with slight corrections, as if learning their own shape. Work songs leave spaces for distant hammer blows. Children’s counting rhymes include numbers that may be omitted if the room is overlit. Love songs rarely promise forever. They promise to remain recognizable through revisions.

This is the quality shared by nearly everything that lives there: not permanence, but persistence without certainty.

The moss advances into boiler rooms. Blind fish circle in suspended ponds. Hares test cables. Stags follow buried roads. Surveyors rewrite altitudes. Families accept wandering names. Workers give metal the years it needs to become trustworthy. All inhabit a place that is not collapsing and not complete, a place whose dangers come less from destruction than from premature definition.

Even the mountain appears to live according to this custom.

It does not erupt or stride or speak. It takes lifetimes to open a crack. It rehearses rivers beneath unbroken basalt. It raises ore toward waiting hands. It leans its cornices outward by the width of a nail and listens to each industrial concussion travel through its still-forming body.

Then, very slowly, it makes room.
