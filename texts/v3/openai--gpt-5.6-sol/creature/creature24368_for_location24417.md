Nothing there is born naked.

The smallest animals begin inside a grain of stone, a film of living matter occupying a fault too fine to admit dust. They inherit a shell before they inherit movement. Mineral accretes around them in plates, needles, cups and dark translucent skins, so that growth is partly the making of a body and partly the slow selection of a prison.

For many, this is enough. They never become mobile. They spread conductive threads across adjoining crystals, close themselves against contamination, and live on differences too slight for any hurried organism to detect. A vein of nickel beside sulfur-bearing basalt is pasture. A stressed quartz seam is stored food. The warmth of decay is not merely background but climate, blood and sun together.

The palms are the most visible expression of this life, though “visible” usually means only that a hand or instrument eventually touches one. Their overlapping scales resemble fragments laid down by an attentive mason. Underneath is a lace of black tissue finer than hair, dividing and rejoining around grains of metal. A palm has no head. Its whole rim tastes the rock. It knows the mineral beneath each edge, the direction of charge, the pressure of neighboring bodies and the faint shadow cast when another feeder interrupts a current.

A valley of palms is therefore not quite a herd and not quite a pavement. Each animal is separate, but the electrical fields passing through them entangle their responses. When one closes its scales, those nearby adjust. Over centuries, this produces broad, slow arrangements: spirals around metallic inclusions, dark lanes where charge is poor, crowded crescents along fresh fractures. Some patterns persist longer than the individuals composing them. The builders read these configurations as travelers elsewhere might read moss, smoke or the behavior of birds.

Wire beasts graze above them.

At rest, a wire beast seems less alive than the columns it spans. Its legs are hollow, each made from nested mineral tubes tightened by conductive fibers. The joints do not flex continuously. They hold one state, release, and snap into another. Thus the creature’s ordinary movement is a succession of long statues punctuated by violent rearrangements.

Its sensory organs lie in its feet. Through six or twelve points of contact it listens to stress traveling through the rock. It can distinguish the slow loading of a crystal from the impact of a falling scale, and the strain of a useful lattice from the approach of a predator. When it feeds, the entire animal collapses toward the chosen point. A horn enters the crystal. Blue charge runs through the body. For an instant every joint becomes visible.

Then it unfolds elsewhere and waits.

Old wire beasts carry miniature ecologies. Palms fasten to the outer legs. Threadlike scavengers inhabit the hollows of their joints. Some grow curtains of insulating shell on one side of the body, compensating over centuries by changing their stance. The largest eventually become unable to move at all. They remain bridged between pillars, alive but fixed, and may be used by the builders as ladders or foundations. Whether this is tolerated, unnoticed or desired is uncertain.

The choirs are more evidently communal, though no single sac appears to command the whole.

Each sac is a soft pressure-organ protected by a transparent mineral envelope. Silica rods connect it to several neighbors. A touch, a feeding event or a change in atmospheric density travels through the colony as compression. Along that passage, membranes briefly align and emit violet light.

The patterns are not speech in the builders’ sense. They are too slow, too repetitive and too dependent on local pressure. Yet they are not meaningless. A choir retains disturbances. Its framework changes minutely each time a wave crosses it, making some routes easier and others harder. After thousands of years, an old colony answers familiar events with patterns unlike those of a young one. It has habits. It may even have memory, distributed through rods that contain almost no living material.

Predators exploit such memory. Ribbon hunters lie beside established feeding paths and release false pressure pulses into nearby colonies. A wire beast, sensing the familiar sequence of a harmless fracture, may set down a leg. The ribbon closes around it with rings of hooks.

The meal can take decades.

Neither hunter nor prey has enough available energy for prolonged struggle. The wire beast locks its joints. The ribbon tightens. Living filaments advance across armor one microscopic gap at a time. Scavengers arrive to feed on flakes loosened by the contest. Sometimes the prey survives until mineral deposits seal the predator’s mouth. Sometimes both remain joined, consuming and resisting, long after their tissues have become impossible to distinguish.

Among these animals move the builders.

They are low, broad creatures, usually mistaken at first for a cluster of shields. Four major legs carry the body. Between them are smaller bracing limbs used on broken or vertical ground. Around the front rises a crown of hard vanes, rods and cupped plates. No two crowns are alike. They continue growing throughout life, and every scrape, fracture and repair alters how the bearer perceives vibration and charge.

They do not see images as creatures of light do. A fresh electrical discharge gives them a brief, painful sense of surfaces, but their dependable world is made of contact and return. A tap against stone expands outward through their sensory crown as distance, density, flaw and boundary. Nearby bodies are warm interruptions. Metal is a clean ringing. A hollow shell answers twice. Open space is known by the failure of an echo to return.

To them, the cavern is not primarily dark. It is delayed.

They move with care because every footstep enters the rock and announces them. On familiar routes they can identify old shelters and markers long before touching them. They recognize one another through patterned knocks, shell-scrapes and the characteristic resonance of individual armor. Names are not sounds but sequences of impact, each adapted to the local stone. A person’s name may be impossible to say properly in another province.

Their armor is both skeleton and archive. New plates grow beneath old ones, lifting them without shedding them completely. Individuals carve marks into these outer layers: routes crossed, tools mastered, debts incurred, dangerous seams encountered. Sulfur and metal are pressed into the cuts to alter their resonance. An elder may be read by touch or by a series of gentle taps, though the record is never complete. Plates crack. Repairs obscure earlier signs. Animals settle in the grooves.

They feed as the palms do, by bridging differences in the rock, but more quickly and at greater cost. At a resting place, a builder lowers conductive organs from its underside into a prepared bowl or mineral seam. For days or months it remains anchored while charge passes through it. Thought slows during feeding. Limbs stiffen. Companions may build a low wall around the resting body, not for privacy but to keep wire beasts from mistaking its crown for a stressed crystal.

Because energy is scarce, consciousness itself is intermittent. The builders do not remain equally awake throughout their lives. Long periods of low awareness lie between episodes of work, travel, negotiation and making. A journey of ten leagues may contain years of immobility. Two strangers meeting at a shelter may exchange greetings, fasten themselves to separate charge bowls, and resume the conversation a generation later.

They reproduce rarely. Several adults contribute filaments, conductive tissues and fragments of living shell to a ceramic brood vessel fitted into a stable mineral boundary. No embryo could grow from stored food alone, so the vessel becomes its first feeding place. It may remain there for centuries while a small body assembles itself around the inherited tissues.

The young emerge soft only in comparison with their elders. Their armor is thin, their sensory crowns short and painfully sensitive. They are carried between the legs of an adult or housed within a wall niche until they learn to distinguish useful resonance from meaningless settling. Many never leave the brood site. They become attached to its circuits and mature into stationary forms that tend bowls, regulate charge or preserve signals. The builders do not always distinguish these rooted kin from architecture.

Their tools occupy the same uncertain category.

A listening rod may contain the nerve filament of a choir, kept alive in a channel of damp sulfur compounds. A wedge may be grown around a wire-beast horn rather than shaped. Charge discs are layered from metal, ceramic and dormant animal membrane; when full, the membrane contracts, separating the plates before they discharge. Some tools respond only to the touch-pattern of a particular maker. Others outlive their makers by millennia and gradually acquire different uses.

There is little waste. A broken implement becomes a wall fitting. A dead builder’s crown becomes a rack of sounding rods. Armor plates are fitted into bridges with the carved histories facing inward, where later travelers can touch them. The deepest shelters contain so much reused body and tool that they are less buildings than accumulated lineages.

The builders do not rule the cavern. They clear little and domesticate almost nothing. Their greatest works are negotiations with creatures and processes that cannot be hurried. A choir may be encouraged to grow along a bridge, its light marking the edge once every few days. Palms may be rotated by minute changes in a charge field until they leave a path open. A stationary wire beast can be induced to shift one leg, though completing the act may occupy several builders for most of their active lives.

They fear rapid events: fracture storms, uncontrolled discharge, the collapse of a crystal forest. Speed belongs to catastrophe and predation. To act quickly is sometimes necessary, but never admirable.

What they honor is continuation.

A working bowl that has gathered charge for ten thousand years. A path still readable under generations of shells. A tool edge sharpened so many times that only a sliver of the original remains. A rooted relative maintaining the same electrical junction while builders are born, travel, harden and die around it.

Near the bottomless shafts, even the builders become cautious. They approach the luminous shell colonies only along ancient marked ways. The folded animals at the rims do not react to touch as ordinary life does. Their continuous glow passes from body to body, around the shaft and onward beneath the floor. Attempts to remove one have caused distant sections of the pattern to go dark. Once, according to marks preserved on inner armor plates, a light returned centuries later in a place where no shell animal had been before.

The builders neither worship the diagrams nor claim to understand them. They maintain openings in their walls so the shell colonies can extend. They leave tools beside certain rims. Sometimes, after many generations, a tool is found incorporated into the circuit, its metal carrying the same steady light.

Then it is no longer retrieved.

In such a world, this may be the nearest thing to an offering: not destruction, not surrender, but permission for a made thing to enter a pattern whose life is larger and slower than its maker can comprehend.
