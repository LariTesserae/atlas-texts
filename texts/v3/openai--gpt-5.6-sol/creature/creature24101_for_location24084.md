No creature here is entirely an individual.

The first inhabitants are films: colorless colonies spread one cell deep across wet leaves, root hairs, eggs, eyes, and the inner surfaces of lungs. They digest the bitter compounds of the plateau and make them usable to larger lives. Without them, almost everything would starve or poison itself. They pass between species in rainwater. A newborn receives them from the skin of its parent, from the wall of its nursery cup, from the first leaf it mouths. Each animal carries a small version of the plateau inside it.

Below the films live the thread-makers.

They lace the vegetal ground with pale fibers finer than hair. Some are fungi; some are colonies of minute rootlike animals; some alternate between the two forms so completely that no distinction remains useful. They soften dead wood, seal leaks, and draw minerals upward from the deeply buried forests. When the ground is injured, they gather at the tear. Over them come mites, molds, mosses, and roots, in that order. A narrow wound can close in a season. A broad one may take centuries.

The thread-makers also carry information. A feeding animal disturbs them; a nearby rosette closes. Salt enters a pool; plants far downstream begin thickening their membranes before the water reaches them. During great flowering intervals, signals move beneath the plain at the pace of root growth. No single strand knows the whole message. Yet the plateau prepares.

In the upper mat are the leaf-burrowers, broad, warm creatures no longer than a hand. Their bodies are soft wedges fringed with backward-pointing bristles. They do not dig permanent tunnels. Instead, they separate the layers of living leaves, graze the old surfaces beneath, and let the opening close behind them. From above, their passage is visible only as a low swelling that travels against the wind.

They are born with dark backs, but gardens soon grow there. Spores lodge between the bristles. Tiny cups form and fill with water. By maturity, each burrower bears a patch of vegetation almost indistinguishable from the ground. Predators searching by sight overlook them. Plants benefit from being carried to new feeding grounds. When a burrower dies, its back takes root before the rest of it has cooled.

The pools have their own populations.

Near the light drift thin swimmers with transparent ribs and leaf-shaped fins. They graze on submerged pollen and hang motionless among pale stems. Farther down, where the water enters the older root chambers, they give way to eyeless animals that feel pressure through branching whiskers. These deep swimmers are nearly white, except after feeding, when dark water can be seen passing through them.

Some never encounter open air. Their eggs adhere to drowned bark in strata buried long before the present surface formed. A population may inhabit one flooded forest for thousands of years, separated from another population by a wall of compressed peat only a few meters thick. When the wall finally softens, two lineages meet that have become strange to each other. Their courtship signals no longer match. They circle, touch whiskers, and often begin to feed upon one another.

Other pool-dwellers move between waters by less obvious routes. At night, ribbon-thin amphibians climb inside hollow stems, following condensation upward. They emerge into plant cups high in the canopy and breed there. Their young pass downward through the vascular channels of the host plant, small enough at first to swim in the water drawn up by its roots. A single animal may therefore spend its larval life beneath the ground, its youth inside a tree, and its adulthood in a pool suspended fifty meters above both.

The glass-winged insects are not silent, though their sounds are rarely carried through air. They vibrate the leaves on which they stand. A whole thicket may be full of communication inaudible to anything not touching it: warnings, mating patterns, claims of moisture, the measured pulse of approaching feet. Some plants have learned these signals. Others imitate them badly but persuasively enough to lure pollinators or frighten grazers away.

The warm, dark-furred branch travelers listen through their palms.

They are compact animals with six grasping limbs, though the front pair are fine and often held against the chest. Their coats drink light, leaving them as moving absences in the canopy. Families follow established branchways between the great columns. The oldest goes first, testing each surface for rot. The young ride underneath the adults, enclosed by limbs and fur when the rain becomes heavy.

They eat fruits, eggs, bark grubs, and the sweet skins that grow over certain fungal knots. Before feeding, they tap the branch in elaborate sequences. This warns hidden insects, wakes sensitive leaves, and sometimes causes fruiting bodies to open. It may once have begun as simple probing. Now each family has its own patterns. Neighboring families recognize one another by rhythm, and branchways used over generations become polished instruments on which routes are remembered.

They freeze when rain stops because cessation is dangerous. The soft ticking of water conceals movement; silence exposes it. More importantly, several canopy hunters use the end of rainfall as a cue. One resembles a fork in a branch until it unfolds. Another hangs beneath epiphyte mats and extends a warm, furred surface on which small animals are willing to rest. By the time the false refuge tightens, its prey is already asleep.

The larger hunters scarcely move at all.

A reed basin may contain one that appears to be a fallen trunk with a row of water-filled hollows along its back. Small animals breed in these hollows. Ferns root in the seams of its hide. It tolerates them for years. Their droppings nourish its camouflage, and their alarm vibrations inform it of approaching prey. When something heavy steps across its jaw, the basin floor closes.

The tightening pools are related to these animals, though “related” may mean only that they share the same thread-makers and have borrowed the same genes through parasites. Their rims are rings of muscular root, each disguised beneath living moss. They do not snap. They wait until a drinking animal has shifted its weight forward, then contract by increments too small to provoke panic. Escape remains possible until the final moment. Most victims do not attempt it soon enough.

The night blossoms are more mobile. In daylight they resemble folded lilies growing from rotten wood. After warmth and darkness coincide, they detach their rootlets and crawl a short distance on hundreds of moist cilia. They settle near nesting sites and open. Their fragrance imitates ripe fruit, wet fur, or the mineral smell that follows a lightning strike. By dawn they have rooted again. A traveler may use the same flower as a landmark on successive days and never notice that it has followed.

Above these smaller lives move the tall ones.

From a distance they seem narrow and delicate, but this is an effect of proportion. A mature tall one may stand three times the height of the dark-furred branch travelers’ largest columns of shelter. It walks on four long legs, placing each divided foot across several root bundles so that its weight is spread over the trembling ground. Its neck can lower between its forelegs to drink. Along its spine rises a trellis of horn, old scar tissue, and modified hair.

A garden grows there.

The garden begins accidentally in youth. Windborne seeds catch in the dorsal trellis; moss seals them in; droppings from visiting insects provide nourishment. Adults tend what takes hold. They comb out strangling vines with the hooked tips of their mouths. They angle themselves beneath condensation so that water runs along channels in the horn. They roll against particular trunks to inoculate their gardens with useful spores.

Each carries a recognizable assemblage. One may bear red cups favored by small amphibians. Another supports silver moss and a colony of stinging insects. An old individual may carry shrubs large enough to fruit. The plants cool the skin, conceal scent, repel parasites, and provide food during long crossings. In return, they travel farther than rooted plants ever could.

A tall one deprived of its garden becomes restless and physically ill. Orphaned young approach adults not only for protection but to receive cuttings. Garden inheritance marks kinship more reliably than appearance. Two animals meeting in mist smell one another’s vegetation before they touch noses.

They do not form herds in the ordinary sense. Their processions are temporary agreements among individuals moving toward the same bloom, mineral seep, or ancestral crossing. Because their legs can span channels that stop smaller creatures, many species follow them. Leaf-burrowers travel in the compressed wake of their feet. Insects hatch in the warm prints. Seeds drop from their backs. A tall one does not merely cross the plateau; for a while it draws a moving corridor of habitat behind it.

They are responsible for some of the straight causeways.

Not by planning them, perhaps. A tall one repeatedly chooses the firmest route. Its descendants follow the scent of its garden. Their feet press loose roots together. Thread-makers seal the bundles. Moss covers the seam. After a thousand generations, the result is a raised road aimed across a bog.

The rain columns beside these roads may also originate with them. Tall ones sometimes push hollow dead trunks upright to rub parasites from the inaccessible center of the back. Rain gathers inside. Animals drink from it. Seeds root around its base. Eventually the trunk is held upright by living growth long after the behavior that placed it there has been forgotten.

Yet the roofed chambers do not fit them.

Some are too small. Some are too enclosed. Their entrances respond to heat and breath, loosening when a body waits outside. The living branches from which they are woven belong to several species that do not ordinarily graft, but in the chamber walls their tissues meet without rejection. The interior is dry, faintly warm, and often lined with shed fibers from animals no longer found nearby.

The dark-furred branch travelers use these rooms, but they do not make them. They enter respectfully, tapping the walls before settling. Amphibians breed in the roof cups. Tall ones sometimes stand over the smallest chambers during heavy rain, as if guarding them. Certain predators avoid them altogether.

In a few chambers, the floor slowly rises beneath a sleeping occupant and holds it just above the damp. In others, fruit appears only after repeated visits. A chamber left unused closes over and becomes an ordinary knot of vegetation. This suggests an organism rather than a building—but no one has found where that organism begins or ends.

The rings around empty pools are older and more deliberate in appearance. Each trunk in a ring fruits at a different interval. Together, their flowering spans centuries. Animals carry pollen from one ring to the next, although the trees themselves are unrelated. Beneath the water, their roots exchange sugars, defensive compounds, and slow electrical pulses.

When one trunk dies, seedlings of the correct species often appear already growing in the gardens of distant tall ones. In time, one is carried to the ring. The animal pauses there, sometimes for days, until the seedling detaches or is brushed into the waterlogged margin. Whether the tall ones understand their role is uncertain. They may be gardeners, couriers, livestock, worshippers, or merely the long limbs of a reproductive system too large to walk by itself.

Then there are the buried rooms.

Nothing living is usually found within them, but the plateau treats them differently from other cavities. Roots trace their outer ribs and turn aside. Water beads on their walls without soaking in. The pale seeds inside remain soft because they are not entirely dormant: once every few years, each absorbs a minute quantity of moisture and produces a pulse of heat.

On rare occasions, after an erosion face opens one of these chambers, the seeds begin to beat together.

The rhythm travels into surrounding roots. Nearby leaves close. Insects leave the cliff. Pools cloud with ascending swimmers. For several nights, the chamber exhales warm air carrying a smell like bruised stems.

No seed has been observed to germinate. But afterward, structures sometimes begin growing elsewhere on the plateau: a new roofed chamber on a dry swell, a ring of incompatible saplings around a young pool, a line of roots gathering under a route no large animal yet uses.

It may be that the seeds are not seeds. They may be organs of memory—condensed remnants of an inhabitant that abandoned separate bodies long ago.

For the broadest resident of the plateau has no visible outline.

It exists wherever roots meet roots, wherever the thread-makers pass from living wood into peat and from peat into drowned forest. It is discontinuous. Fire, if fire could persist here, would not reveal its boundary. Nor would cutting, because severed portions continue their local lives and may join again centuries later. It has no single brain, no common pulse, no unified intention that a quicker animal could recognize.

Most of the time it appears to do nothing beyond remaining alive.

But it delays water. It closes paths and opens others. It preserves dead trunks in suspension until they become bridges. It lets certain hollows deepen into pools and fills others with buoyant groves. It guides roots around the buried chambers. It coordinates blooms so slow that generations of animals are born and die during the turning of a single botanical thought.

The structures may be its habits. The tall ones may carry its gardens. The branch travelers may sound its nerves by tapping. The buried seeds may hold instructions from earlier versions of the plain. Or all these interpretations may be the familiar error of mistaking cooperation for a mind.

The inhabitants do not need to decide.

To them, the plateau is shelter, predator, ancestor, road, weather, and flesh. They eat it and are eaten by it. They carry portions of it on their backs and in their blood. Their nests grow closed after them. Their tracks become streams. Their dead do not vanish downward into inert soil, because there is no inert soil to receive them.

They become another inhabited layer.

Long afterward, far beneath the feet of their descendants, their bones remain wrapped in roots, still warm, still wet, while pale swimmers move through the chambers around them and the immense dark plain continues, very slowly, to deepen.
