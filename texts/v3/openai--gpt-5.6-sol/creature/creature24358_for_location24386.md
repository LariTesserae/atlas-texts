At first, the inhabitants resemble workers interrupted by steam.

One is glimpsed crouching beneath a shelf, one knee in the black water beneath the floor, tightening a length of fiber around a ceramic joint. Another passes behind a curtain of leaves with a basket balanced against the shoulder. A third stands on a scaffold where there did not seem to be room for anyone to stand. They are nearly human in size and proportion, though the comparison becomes less useful the longer they are watched.

They are narrow-bodied, not from hunger but from accommodation. Their shoulders can turn almost edgewise. Their ribs yield when they squeeze through openings and slowly spring back afterward. Elbows, toes, and finger joints are unusually mobile. A hand can bend around the back of a pillar and continue working out of sight. Their feet are broad, with a second set of small gripping toes along the outer edge, useful on wet cane. They seldom put their whole weight anywhere at once.

Their skin has the muted colors of the chamber: smoke-brown, wet-ash gray, fired-clay red, the yellow of old reed. It is never completely dry. Fine lines cross it like the impressions left by woven mats, and these lines alter over time. A basket-maker develops basketry on the palms. Someone who has spent many days plastering walls acquires thumb whorls along the forearms. Those who tend the roots are patterned with pale branching marks that pulse when the roots do.

No two of them possess the same degree of solidity.

Some are plainly flesh. They sweat, bleed, cough in the steam, and bruise their shins on low beams. Others seem to have been assembled in haste. Their hair is twisted fiber. One may have a cheek with the faint glaze of pottery, or a row of small peg holes beneath the collarbone. Another has visible lashings at the wrists, darkened by years of handling. A child’s shadow may show reed ribs where its body shows ordinary bones.

This is neither deformity nor disguise. The distinction between resident and construction is not securely maintained here.

They call themselves, when translation is possible, something like **those still under the hand**.

Each bears an incompletion.

One has no left profile: viewed from that side, her face is a soft unfinished plane bearing three fingermarks. One lacks a final joint on every finger, yet ties finer knots than anyone else. One has a mouth that was never colored and appears only when he speaks. Another casts a shadow with a small person-shaped opening in its chest. An elder’s back remains visibly unplastered, revealing a lattice that moves with his breathing.

These omissions are deliberate and guarded. To repair one without permission is a grave injury. To finish someone—to close every seam, answer every question about them, make their name exact—is a form of killing.

Accordingly, their names are provisional. A person may be called Carries-Two-Cups in the morning, Clay-Under-Nails after work, and Sister-of-the-West-Facing-Frogs during a pressure change. Private names are constructed throughout a lifetime but never spoken in full. At death, the last syllable is intentionally withheld.

They do not count their population. Enumeration makes them uneasy. If asked how many live in the summit fold, one inhabitant begins listing occupations, another lists absences, and a third quietly adds a door to the nearest wall. There seem to be between twenty and several hundred of them, depending on the weather and on which ladders have the required number of rungs.

Most are difficult to notice because they work from within things.

A valve-clacker may sit inside a clay pillar with only three fingers exposed. The keepers of the climbing water move through sleeves woven into the walls. Those who correct newborn scaffolds crawl along the undersides of platforms, their hair hanging upward into gaps. At night, families sleep in shallow hollows behind warm plaster, fitted shoulder to shoulder like tools in a case. Their breathing causes certain murals to swell and recede.

There are no private houses. A household is defined by a shared task.

Those who maintain one drip-channel consider themselves kin. So do those responsible for an arch, a fungus lamp, or an unresolved corner. Kinship changes when the structure changes. A person may become someone’s aunt because a root has entered a neighboring basin. Two strangers repairing opposite sides of the same crack are siblings until the crack buds. Long partnerships are marked not by rings but by an unfinished object exchanged repeatedly between the pair. It may pass back and forth for decades, each adding a single stroke, knot, groove, or handle, neither permitting it to become complete.

Their children are born in several ways, and the inhabitants do not rank the methods.

Some are carried in bodies. Pregnancy makes the parent lighter; in the final weeks they must be tethered during changes of gravity. Birth usually takes place over the central pool, though the infant is never allowed to see its reflection at once.

Other children begin in wall pockets. A family packs one with compost, hair, ash, powdered shell, and a small personal tool. After a season, a warm root emerges. It is guided into a woven figure and fed drops of pool water. If the figure begins adding knots to its own basket, it is regarded as awake.

A few children simply appear among the workers. They are first mistaken for clay wall figures because they pass bricks with the same patient motion. Then one turns its head farther than the relief should allow, climbs down, and asks an impossible practical question: where should rain be stored before it happens, or which end of a bridge is lonelier? Such children are welcomed cautiously. They tend to know old routes through parts of the mountain that have not yet been built.

Every child is given a flaw to keep.

A seam in the heel may be left unclosed. One eyebrow may remain unpainted. A single baby tooth may be wrapped rather than allowed to erupt. This protects the child from completion, but it also gives the mountain somewhere harmless to continue working on them. Throughout childhood, little developments cluster around the flaw: new freckles, stray fibers, tiny shelves of keratin, dreams in which additional fingers are tested and discarded.

Education is almost entirely practical.

Children learn first to give water a route. Before they can speak, they are taught to tilt a leaf so that a drop falls into a cup rather than between the floor slats. Next they learn to leave empty places safely unfinished without allowing them to become vacant. Later come knot arithmetic, moving geometry, the moods of clay, and the correct insults for self-braiding baskets.

They learn never to ask whether a thing is done.

Instead they ask:

*Where is it still open?*

*What has it begun to remember?*

*If it breaks, what will it make?*

Their tools are treated as temperamental collaborators. A mallet that strikes without a hand is not considered alive, exactly, but it is thanked. A needle worn grass-thin may be given lighter duties. Shell scrapers are fed oil. Measuring cords are never named because a named cord begins measuring its owner.

The inhabitants are always making, but they do not consider themselves creators. Creation, to them, is the uncontrolled condition of the place—the pressure that produces rooms, tiles, ladders, roots, copies, depths. Their work is revision. They introduce mercy into growth. They divert a stair before it enters someone’s lungs. They widen a crack so it will bud into a planter rather than a corridor. They remove one knot from a roof so the roof will release its remembered rain in manageable portions.

Their most respected workers are not the fastest builders but the best hinderers: those who can delay a wall without stopping it, keep a vessel almost useful for years, or recognize which apparent defect is holding an entire district in the present.

These experts wear aprons crowded with pockets. The pockets contain missing pieces: pegs deliberately withheld, final mosaic tiles, lid handles, lengths of border pattern, the last rung of ladders. None is labeled. If a keeper dies, the pieces must be redistributed without discovering what they fit.

Their speech sounds at first like the chamber’s own construction noises. Consonants tap and clack. Vowels are breathed through the nose to preserve air. A sentence may be completed by tightening a cord or pouring water between cups. Meaning shifts with gravity. There is a word spoken while facing down that means *foundation*; spoken while facing toward the central pool, it means *ancestor*; during an altitude spasm, when down divides into several directions, it means *agreement among dangers*.

Silence is also structural. Certain pauses support conversation the way poles support a wet roof. Interrupting one can cause everyone present to forget the previous hour.

They eat mostly what can be harvested without ending its growth: fern tips, fungus skins, root milk, moss curds, floating peppers, translucent tubers pinched from jars. Food is steamed in unfinished pots. Every bowl has a leak, and meals are eaten quickly or over another bowl. The leaking is essential. A sound vessel would fill with sky, and sky is both indigestible and difficult to remove from a kitchen.

They do not hunt the copper-winged moths. Moths are believed to carry away excessively precise thoughts. When one settles on a sleeper’s brow, the household speaks vaguely until morning.

The orange-throated frogs are consulted during changes in direction. The inhabitants place small clay markers where each frog faces and compare them afterward. No one claims the frogs predict anything. Prediction would imply a completed future. They merely reveal which way the present is attempting to escape.

The many-legged white lizard is called by a different name for each visible part.

Its head is Cup-Guest. Its tail is Last-Across. A middle section glimpsed in the high scaffolds is Pale Uncle. The residents leave bowls of softened cord-knots for it, particularly after mountain growth. Children are warned never to follow the creature in an attempt to see it whole. Once, it is said, an apprentice marked every sighting and joined the marks with charcoal lines. The resulting drawing tightened. For nine days, the entire chamber could not breathe.

The apprentice survived, but now exists only as a pair of capable hands that emerge from a wall during emergencies. The inhabitants still address them politely.

Their elders are difficult to distinguish from the architecture.

Age does not wear them down. It incorporates them.

An old woman may spend years sitting beside one basin until her legs become its supports. She continues to gossip, scold children, and test the water with a finger, though moss grows over her knees. A root-tender’s spine may lengthen into a ladder used by three generations. Some elders spread gradually across a ceiling as warmth, recognizable when condensation traces the pattern of their former face.

This is not regarded as death, because the elder remains unfinished and responsive.

True death occurs only when a person can no longer be altered.

The dead become smooth.

Their fingermarks disappear. Their knots cease tightening. Their names no longer accept new syllables. Such a person is carried to the central pool on an incomplete stretcher and lowered until the water removes distance from them. The mourners do not watch for a bottom. Each drops in an object the deceased began but did not finish. For several days afterward, unfamiliar hammering may be heard far above.

Sometimes the dead return, but always from an earlier point in their lives. A stern elder may step through a sealed wall as a shy adolescent who has not yet met anyone present. The inhabitants do not explain what the person will become. They offer work and allow the old life to remain a missing piece.

They have no ruler. Authority passes to whoever notices danger first.

During ordinary hours this is usually a water-router, a knot-eater’s attendant, or a child who has found an empty space. During mountain growth, the community organizes around shouted flaws:

“Leave that seam!”

“Remove the fourth rung!”

“Do not let the balcony arrive!”

“Give the rain another memory!”

Everyone moves. The least solid inhabitants climb into walls to brace them from within. Elders loosen their own structural joints. Children smear handprints across dangerously smooth surfaces. Families dismantle parts of themselves for usable material—a peg from a forearm, fiber from the hair, a tile from the palate—and trust that fabrication will return something similar later.

Afterward they gather wherever the floor has become most nearly level. They count injuries but never survivors. They drink hot root milk, retell the mistakes, and hold a celebration for whatever almost happened.

Their art is made of controlled beginnings.

Songs end before the melody resolves. Dances repeatedly approach a final posture and divert into another movement. Murals depict the first moments of events: a hand lifting, a mouth opening, a bridge reaching toward its farther side. There are no portraits seen from the front. Faces are painted turning toward the viewer but have not yet arrived.

Their finest stories have no fixed characters. A hero may become a staircase midway through the telling, then an illness, then the listener’s left hand. The object is not to reach an ending but to create a story roomy enough for new incidents to inhabit.

They fear completion, but not only from superstition. Some have seen what happens.

In inaccessible recesses are smooth doors with no missing hinge, no open seam, no unpainted edge. They belong to earlier versions of the mountain. From behind them come sounds of workers whose every action has already occurred. Occasionally a flawless hand presses against the other side. It cannot be rescued because rescue would alter a completed event, and completed events do not admit tools.

The inhabitants leave offerings near these doors: bent nails, cracked cups, questions without answers. Small imperfections, patiently supplied.

Whether they built the mountain is a question they refuse.

Some say their ancestors began at cloud level and constructed upward to escape a flood that was falling from the stars. Some say the summit made the first builders out of scaffolding because it needed hands. Some say there is only one inhabitant, distributed across generations and occupations, passing tools from one portion of itself to another.

There is evidence for each account.

All inhabitants dream of the same pair of hands.

The hands are enormous, larger than the courtyard, but delicate enough to split cane and thread bronze needles. In the dream they are always just beyond a wall, assembling the mountain around the sleepers. One hand is warm and living. The other is made of little people standing shoulder to shoulder. Neither hand is finished at the wrist.

When the chamber inhales and altitude floods in, the residents tether themselves to whatever they currently trust. Their bodies thin. Their seams open. For several breaths, the fleshly ones show lattices beneath their skin, while those made from clay reveal soft organs beating inside.

Then the heat closes.

They check the water routes. They relight the fungus bowls. They add or remove rungs. They leave food for the lizard and flaws for the children.

And they return to the unending task of keeping the summit alive by preventing it, with immense care, from ever becoming complete.
