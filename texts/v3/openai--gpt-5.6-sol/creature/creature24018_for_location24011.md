At first, an inhabitant resembles another charged object in the dusk.

A blurred upright shape moves along a road-ripple, surrounded by a faint halo of conductive dust. It may be mistaken for a young tower, a grazing animal reared on its hind limbs, or a bundle of black vanes being carried by the wind. Only when it stops does its organization become apparent.

The body has no permanent front.

Six narrow walking spars descend from a central cage of layered mineral. Usually four touch the ground while two remain folded against the torso, but any limb can become a support, a hand, an antenna, or the frame for a temporary organ. The joints are stacks of dark, flexible wafers sliding across one another without lubricant. Their movement is nearly frictionless and almost silent in the air, though the foundation feels every step.

Above the limbs rises a loose crown of sensory ribbons. In still weather these spread into a translucent fan several times the body’s height. In wind they braid themselves into a tight cable. Thousands of metallic cilia along their edges read electric gradients, drifting chemicals, distant vibrations and the minute deformation of the foundation beneath nearby weight. Sight exists among these senses, but it is not sovereign. An inhabitant looking at something does not necessarily turn toward it. Attention may instead be shown by the lifting of one foot, the tightening of the torso, or a pale charge passing through the dust around its crown.

No two have quite the same outline. Some are tall, with long coppery spars and bodies thin enough to vanish when seen edge-on. Others travel close to the ground beneath broad insulating mantles. Old individuals often carry mineral excrescences that resemble balconies, antlers or ruined architecture. Children are compact and many-limbed, moving in quick bursts before freezing for long periods to listen through the ground.

Their tissues are dry even by the standards of the plateau.

What seems to be skin is a mosaic of ceramic scales held together by carbon mesh. Beneath it, branching channels carry ionic fluids in pulses rather than continuous circulation. There is no heart. Different parts of the body pump as needed, and a limb severed in an accident may continue walking for some distance, seeking a road or wall niche capable of receiving it. Breath is equally distributed. The entire surface gathers atmospheric gases, while specialized folds open during exertion and close again before dust can accumulate.

Their heat is precious. Resting individuals become almost indistinguishable from the structures around them, cooling until their metabolism proceeds as a sequence of widely spaced chemical events. A gathering may therefore appear to be a plaza filled with statues. Then a pressure wave passes under the city, and every statue unfolds at once.

They do not eat meals in the ordinary sense. While traveling, they draw charge through their feet and comb sulfur, carbon and metal dust from the air with their sensory ribbons. In towers, they attach to feeding recesses that exchange depleted salts for active ones. This exchange can last hours or several years. During it the tower may grow a thin wall around the sleeper, leaving only the crown exposed. Entire residential chambers are lined with such resting alcoves, some occupied so long that their inhabitants have become load-bearing parts of the building.

They emerge altered.

An old limb may have been digested and replaced. A new sensory branch may carry the chemical vocabulary of a distant district. Sometimes two individuals enter neighboring recesses and come out with matching structures grown through both bodies: a shared hand, a resonant plate, a pair of organs that function only when the owners are within several kilometers of one another.

This makes it difficult to say where kinship ends and engineering begins.

They reproduce by assembly, but not manufacture. Several adults contribute living fragments—mineral foam, catalytic tissue, memory scars, dormant sensory knots—which are planted in a warm chamber of a tower. The chamber closes. The foundation supplies salts and controlled vibration. For a long interval, what grows inside has no single body. It is a branching mat that tests possible limbs, dissolves them, and tries again.

When the chamber opens, it may release one child, several, or none. Sometimes the new growth is retained by the tower as a room, a door, or a civic organ. This is not considered a failed birth. A person and a useful chamber are regarded as neighboring outcomes of the same process.

Young inhabitants are not given names immediately. They are given rhythms.

A caretaker stamps a pattern into the ground, and the child learns to answer by shifting its weight. The rhythm identifies its origin, obligations and permissible routes. As it matures, variations accumulate. A full adult identity may take several minutes to sound and can include pauses that must be supplied by companions, animals, weather or the city itself.

Spoken language does exist, but it is used for nearby, temporary matters. It consists of joint clicks, scraped plates, electrical crackles and brief releases of scented vapor. It is rapid and full of ambiguity. Serious communication goes through the foundation. An inhabitant braces its limbs, alters the tension of its body and presses a shaped vibration into the living ground. The message may travel only across a room or may be taken up by roads, towers and root districts until it reaches the far side of the plateau.

Such messages are never transmitted unchanged. The foundation adds context. Passing herds interrupt with their footfalls. Buried grazers distort the lower frequencies. A tower may hold part of a phrase until weather makes its meaning clearer. The inhabitants do not regard these changes as errors. A thought that must remain exactly itself is considered too brittle to travel.

Their households are likewise difficult to enclose.

A tower chamber may belong to a lineage, but the lineage includes living inhabitants, sleeping tools, sections of wall, migratory animals carrying its reproductive dust, and several ancestral patterns preserved in the road beneath the entrance. Members may go decades without meeting bodily while remaining in constant tactile conversation through the city. Conversely, individuals who share a chamber may be strangers in every important civic sense.

They make few portable possessions. A tool attached to the hand is not owned while it functions; it is performing a role. When returned to the wall, its material and learned motions become available to others. Objects kept from recycling acquire a troubling status. Some are revered, some feared, and some quietly stolen by the foundation during sleep.

Their art is made from controlled uselessness.

In a world where every stable structure tends to become infrastructure, beauty requires protecting something from purpose. They cultivate hairs that respond to no known weather. They grow sealed rooms too narrow to enter. They teach road tissue to carry ripples in circles. They maintain intricate mineral branches that consume energy and produce nothing but faint changes in shadow.

The black arches are approached this way. No inhabitant claims to understand all of them. Pilgrims pass through in carefully chosen sequences, returning older, younger, delayed, or accompanied by versions of their own footprints arriving from another route. Around certain arches, artists arrange temporary bodies made from discarded limbs and construction tissue. These figures stand until the next great computation, when they walk away in directions no road previously served.

Death is not marked by stillness. Too many living things here remain still for centuries.

An inhabitant is considered dead when its characteristic tension can no longer be recovered: not from the body, not from its companions, not from the scars it cultivated, and not from the city’s resonant memory. The body is then opened. Useful organs crawl or are carried back to wall niches. Mineral scales are laid in warm groves for small animals to polish. The remaining cage is placed on the foundation and slowly drawn down into it.

But the dead sometimes recur as civic behavior.

Years after an individual’s dissolution, a door may hesitate in its manner. A road may develop a familiar asymmetry. A tower chord may include a pressure-pattern once associated with a particular person. The inhabitants disagree about whether this is memory, inheritance, imitation, or merely the city using a successful shape again. They do not insist on an answer.

Their work is largely negotiation.

They do not command the foundation. They irritate it, feed it, sing to it, prune it, introduce parasites into selected tissues, and offer it patterns worth continuing. Construction workers travel with colonies of shrieking polyps housed along their limbs. Surveyors are broad, low beings carrying resonant plates that can listen through kilometers of root. Herd-guides cultivate attractive electrical nodules ahead of migrating grazers. Deep mediators descend into root galleries and attach themselves to the armor of the great subterranean animals, sometimes remaining there for whole migrations.

The most honored specialists are those who can recognize when a solution has become dangerous.

Because the city answers calculation with growth, every answer acquires appetite. A new road wants traffic. A tower wants a territory. A climate boundary wants to harden and extend. The inhabitants watch for structures beginning to preserve themselves at the expense of the whole. They introduce uncertainty—misaligning a rhythm, releasing an animal, opening a chamber at the wrong hour—so that the garden must calculate again.

They call this care.

Some inhabitants never enter the towers. They live near the plateau rim, traveling among the descending root curtains. Their bodies are compact and heavily plated against the concussions. They climb down into the cloud, fastening themselves to swaying cables with all six limbs. What they encounter below is carried back as altered minerals, unfamiliar dust and long vibrational accounts whose lowest portions cannot be reproduced on the plateau.

These edge-dwellers are regarded with admiration and suspicion. Water sometimes clings to their armor in glassy cysts. Their rhythms contain pauses too deep for the foundation to interpret. A few return with soft translucent growths inside their body cages, carefully isolated from all native tissue. Such growths are taken to sealed towers and studied for generations.

There may also be inhabitants who never move at all.

Certain towers respond too specifically to be mere organisms. Certain plazas refuse particular travelers. In old groves, mineral stalks sometimes coordinate their seed-lights into the identity rhythm of someone not otherwise present. The mobile people speak to these presences with the same courtesy they offer one another, though they differ on whether they are ancestors, districts, emergent minds, or the foundation dreaming in smaller forms.

Perhaps this uncertainty is deliberate. On the High Dry Garden, individuality is useful but not sacred. A body is a temporary concentration: a way for the plateau to lift some of its attention above the surface, carry it from one horizon to another, and bring it home changed.

Seen from afar, an inhabitant remains only a dark figure crossing a darker field.

Yet beneath each step, messages spread through kilometers of living ground. Roads wake. Tower windows open. Burrowers turn in their chambers. Metallic hairs rise in expanding rings. The city feels the traveler coming and subtly rebuilds itself to meet them.

The traveler, feeling this, rebuilds itself as well.
