What lives there is difficult to count.

The basin contains many plants, certainly, and innumerable small lives in the soil, but these are joined beneath the visible surface so thoroughly that an inventory gives the wrong impression. Fern-crowns, pale trees, hooked vines, mosses, the fungi inhabiting drowned branches: they are distinct organisms, yet they behave like members of a slow household. Water passes from one to another. Sugars move through fungal threads. Minerals lifted by one set of roots appear seasons later in the leaves of another. Even the sideways-running threads near the ledge are drawn, in part, by this buried exchange. The roots do not merely drink from the basin. Together they pump it.

Among them lives a rarer creature, responsible for the tracks, the wingbeats, and the knocking.

At first sight it resembles the gray lizard glimpsed on warm stone. Its body is low and narrow, about the length of a forearm, with a blunt head and no visible neck. The skin is finely folded rather than scaled. When dry it is the color of ash; when wet it darkens into irregular patches of olive, violet, and brown, matching the mineral bands over which it moves. Along the spine are short pale marks like flecks of lichen. These are not fixed. They blanch or darken over several minutes, so that the creature’s outline seems to dissolve after it has stopped moving.

Its legs end in three long toes. Each toe has a soft, ribbed underside capable of gripping wet bark and smooth rock. The solitary tracks are misleading: it places each hind foot almost exactly into the print of the forefoot, leaving what appears to be the passage of a three-toed biped. It also pauses often, sometimes for most of an hour, with one foot raised and its weight distributed among the other three.

This posture is how it listens.

The toes are crowded with pressure-sensitive tissue. Through them it detects the low speech of the fissures, the tremor of water under stone, the minute crackle of a seed-pod preparing to split. A sudden change in subterranean pitch sends it motionless. A particular sequence of vibrations may draw it across the entire basin, though the journey can take half a day.

What look like loose folds along its flanks are wings.

They open from the forelimbs to the tail in a dark translucent membrane, strongly veined and patched with tiny leaf-shaped scales. The creature is not an agile flier. It climbs before launching and usually glides from a pale tree to a lower shelf. When forced upward, it beats its wings with slow, muscular strokes that seem too loud for so small a body. The enclosing rock magnifies each stroke. Thus the heavy wingbeat overhead may belong to something scarcely larger than a crow.

It does not fly beyond the brink in clear weather. It waits for rising mist, then enters the cloud and crosses short distances along the mountain face. Perhaps other basins exist nearby. Perhaps the isolated track is not evidence of a solitary animal but of several visitors arriving by routes that leave no tracks at all.

Its mouth is small and hard-edged, suited less for biting than for scraping films from bark and stone. It eats moss mites, fungal skins, softened wood, pollen gathered from leaf-cups, and the gelatinous coatings of certain seeds. Yet feeding occupies little of its visible activity. Much of its time is spent touching things.

It turns fallen pods so their hooked sides catch the wind. It pushes dead twigs into the forks of roots. It worries at the bases of blade-edged leaves until they lean against rotting wood. It carries seeds, but rarely eats them immediately. Instead it presses them into cracks with the flat of its snout, often in places where the mature plant will alter the movement of water or shade another species.

None of this resembles construction in the usual sense. It makes no shelter and retains no object. If it uses tools, its tools remain rooted, continue growing, and may not become useful within the lifetime of the individual that positioned them.

A hooked vine planted beside an exposed slab will require years before its pods begin striking stone. A pale tree may take decades to thicken around a fallen branch and hold it in the flow. Generations pass before a blade-leaf opens a clean groove through deadwood. The creature cannot be waiting for a simple result.

Its craft, if craft is the word, is inherited as placement.

Young animals learn no sequence of gestures from adults. Indeed, adults and young are almost never seen together. Instead they move through arrangements left by previous inhabitants. A certain hollow root invites a seed to be pressed beneath it. A mineral stripe leads the feet toward a vine. A branch held in running water releases a smell that causes the animal to begin scraping. The basin itself serves as memory: not a collection of artifacts, but a set of unfinished instructions embodied in living things.

For this reason the creatures may be called tenders, though it is equally possible that they are being tended.

Their breeding is bound to the dark pool beneath the tallest wall. At long intervals—perhaps only after a particular sequence of rains—one enters the water shortly before dawn. It does not swim. It lowers itself until only the nostrils and folded wings remain above the surface, while the pool’s vibration passes through its body. Fine silver threads emerge from pores along its flanks and break apart in the water. Whether these are eggs, reproductive cells, or some kind of shed sensory tissue is unclear.

For days afterward, the branching patterns on the pool become unusually elaborate. Pollen gathers into repeated three-forked forms. The sideways-running water strengthens, carrying spores and minute translucent grains uphill into holes in the rock.

The young do not hatch openly. Months later, tiny gray forms begin appearing where warm seepage issues from the upper shelves. At this stage they have no wings, only loose skin along the sides, and could easily be mistaken for lizards. They feed within moss cushions and vanish into fissures at the slightest shadow. As they grow, the flank folds widen. The pale markings appear last.

No one has seen an adult die.

Old individuals become slow and spend increasing amounts of time beside the pool. Their skin roughens, acquiring green films that are not merely camouflage but living colonies of algae and fungus. Some eventually enter a fissure beneath the rock wall and do not return. Years afterward, a new pulse of pale fungal growth may appear around that opening. The mineral bands nearby may darken. Water may begin taking a slightly different route.

It may be that the tender is only the mobile phase of something much larger.

Beneath the shelves lies a web of roots, fungal cords, porous stone, and warm microbial films extending through the wet fissures. This buried life has no single center, but the pool acts as one of its exposed surfaces. Its trembling patterns are not writing, though they may perform what writing performs: holding differences, repeating forms, preserving the effects of an event after the event has passed. The warm water rising against gravity is moved by pressure, capillarity, and living tissues acting together. No part alone produces the phenomenon.

The tender participates in that network whenever it stands listening. Its three toes receive the mountain’s vibrations; its weight, movements, scraping, and knocks return vibrations of their own. Most are incidental. Some are not.

The wooden knock heard in mist is made by the hard edge of its snout against a hollow trunk. Usually there is one blow, a long pause, then another. The pauses vary with the bass murmur beneath the ground. Sometimes, after the second knock, a nearby leaf-cup releases its water because a root has shifted imperceptibly beneath it. Sometimes a seed-pod begins to rattle. Sometimes nothing apparent happens.

This need not be language. It may be closer to testing a surface before stepping onto it, or tapping fruit to learn whether it is hollow. But the creature listens too long afterward for the gesture to seem casual.

So the basin is inhabited on several scales.

There are the visible plants, each standing in its generous interval. There are the gray winged tenders, perhaps one at a time, perhaps several whose paths seldom overlap. Beneath both is the diffuse, warm, water-moving consortium threaded through the mountain—a life too distributed to possess a body and too slow to display an intention.

None can be understood separately. The underground web guides water. Water shapes stone. Stone tunes the wind. The changing tones guide the tender. The tender relocates seeds. The plants split wood, trap branches, and redirect water back into the web.

At night, when mist closes the ledge and the basin becomes a room again, the tender folds itself beneath a horizontal fern crown. It hangs there with all three toes of each foot pressed to the trunk, wings wrapped close, feeling the low vibration pass through wood and bone.

Then it raises its head and knocks.

After a long interval, from somewhere under the dark pool—or perhaps from another hollow tree obscured by mist—something answers.
