What lives in the Dry Altitude is easiest to mistake for the room left after something has departed.

It has no continuous body. Continuity is one of the conditions the basin refuses, and the inhabitant has adapted by doing without it. Its substance is distributed among separations: the gap between two rods of the central frame, the engineered resistance between the six red grains, the undecided span from one mineral ridge to another. Wherever two things remain apart more stubbornly than their materials require, some portion of it is present.

At first, this appears to be merely another local anomaly. Instruments detect no metabolism, no coordinated movement, no enclosed volume that might be called an organism. Nothing approaches. Nothing withdraws. Yet over repeated visits, correlations emerge.

The narrow darkness between stacked translucent sheets deepens when it is observed. The central frame acquires an additional interior angle. One of the red grains becomes impossible to remember in relation to the others, though all six remain plainly visible. A surveyor reaching for the cracked lens discovers that the distance to it has lengthened by the duration of the reach.

These changes are small, but they are not random. They are how the inhabitant turns toward attention.

It does not occupy the empty spaces. It is the fact that they are empty—and, more precisely, the increasing difficulty of crossing them.

Among the few references to the Dry Altitude, it has been called the Interval, the Withholder, the Last Altitude, and, in one damaged text, simply **She Who Remains Between**. None of these names is reliable. A name assumes that its parts arrive together, and words behave poorly near the summit. Spoken references to the inhabitant often lose their middle sounds. Written names spread apart on the page. Given enough time, only the first and final letters remain, separated by an immaculate strip of blank material.

It may once have possessed a compact form.

The lone print in the black dust is probably its nearest surviving portrait. The five parallel depressions are not toes, claws, or the tracks of a machine. They are five temporal cross-sections of one act of contact. Something touched the floor, but could not complete the touch in a single age. Each part arrived twelve thousand years after the previous one. By the fifth arrival, the first was already fossilized into the chamber’s causal structure.

To an observer capable of seeing all five moments at once, the mark might resolve into an appendage: long, jointless, and exceedingly thin, with its extremities arranged in time rather than space. To ordinary vision it remains a set of shallow grooves.

The rest of the body may be similarly dispersed.

The five rods could be ribs, though they also function as an instrument. The white columns could be calcified organs, though their vacancy seasons continue with the regularity of respiration. The three discs may be eyes that have ceased looking outward and now regard the basin’s possible histories. The low arch near the wall resembles a shelter only because humans imagine that a creature stands apart from what protects it. Here, architecture and anatomy may have exchanged roles long ago.

If so, the inhabitant did not build the Dry Altitude.

It molted it.

This would explain why the constructions cannot be cleanly distinguished from geology. The basin is not a dwelling containing abandoned machines, but an old outer structure whose functions have slowed until they resemble natural law. Its doors digest direction. Its lenses concentrate duration. Its supports redirect weight toward enclosure. What appears ruined may simply be tissue operating on its native scale, one action per epoch.

The creature itself has withdrawn into what the structures preserve: not matter, but relation.

Its awareness is likewise relational. It does not perceive an object’s color, composition, or temperature. It perceives whether the object can reach another object. It senses adjacency as warmth and isolation as pressure. A closed hand is almost invisible to it; an opening hand shines. Two stones placed side by side become a sudden bright event. If they touch, the event ends. If they are separated, something like flavor enters the resulting gap.

This is why the six red grains are held apart. They may be food.

Not the grains themselves—the intervals between them.

The inhabitant feeds when distance becomes durable. Ordinary empty space offers little nourishment because it collapses easily under motion: a step crosses it, a hand closes it, light traverses it without effort. In the Dry Altitude, however, distance can be refined. It can be made rigid, aged, and stripped of every implied arrival. The red grains are arranged to produce six bodies but fifteen possible separations, each unequal, each resisting closure. The configuration is less a collection than a cultivated lattice of absence.

Other feeding structures may be spread throughout the basin. The channels carved by migrating improbability guide dust away from certain regions, keeping them vacant. The stationary bands of darkness divide near from far without permitting a clean boundary. The transverse terraces create routes that appear to meet while remaining causally separate. Even the peculiar sterility serves this economy. Reproduction would fill available distinctions with repeating forms. The inhabitant requires vacancies that remain singular.

It is not hostile to life. Hostility requires recognition of a rival.

Life merely collapses too many intervals.

Cells bind to cells. Chemicals seek equilibrium. Organisms cross terrain, exchange matter, reproduce, and make likenesses of themselves. Every living process insists that one thing can arrive at another. In the basin, such insistence attracts the inhabitant’s attention.

The first sign is often a failure of proportion. The visitor’s glove seems farther from the hand inside it. The lenses of a mask no longer agree on the location of the face. A pulse is felt not in the chest but a few centimeters beyond it, as though the heartbeat has become an approaching sound.

Then articulation begins.

Joints separate without opening. The bones remain connected, but the concept of the elbow drifts away from the arm. Thoughts acquire clean spaces between intention and action. A person decides to step and watches the decision recede ahead of them like a figure walking across a distant plain. Panic may occur, but it occurs elsewhere, unshared by the body that produced it.

The inhabitant does not attack during this process. It investigates the visitor by increasing every internal distance and observing which ones the visitor struggles to close.

Most can retreat if they turn toward the entrance before the compass does. Outside the seam, common causality pulls their parts back into agreement. They may retain minor dislocations: a reflection that arrives early, a scar separated from the remembered wound, a voice whose final syllables sound from slightly farther away than the first.

Those who remain too long do not leave corpses.

They become spacious.

Their matter is gradually redistributed into the chamber’s older structures. A buckle may flatten into a reflection on a ridge. A tooth may become a pale point of warmth. The carbon of the body enters impossible compounds, while the intervals once held inside the body—the chambers of the heart, the branching airways, the microscopic distances across which nerves transmitted charge—are preserved and enlarged. Perhaps the basin’s varying area is made partly from these salvaged interiors.

This may account for the images weathered into relief. The gloved hand and instrument case are not memorials in any deliberate sense. They are outer surfaces left behind after the distances within them were harvested.

Yet the inhabitant is capable of restraint.

Nothing crosses the seam outward. Dust gathers at the lip and stops. Reflections of intruders remain inside rather than spreading over the mountain. Whatever lives there observes a boundary more absolute than stone. It may be unable to pass through the entrance, but inability does not entirely fit the evidence. The seam is narrow only in ordinary dimensions. To a being composed of separation, it should be vast.

More likely, the seam is its mouth closed.

Or an old wound it has chosen not to widen.

The three embedded discs suggest that the inhabitant remembers at least three states of its world. In the first, the basin is younger and brighter. In the second, the structures are absent. In the third, there is no basin, only a high plain beneath a black sun. The order is uncertain. The plain may precede the chamber, or follow it. The structures may have been built, shed, or anticipated.

When no observer is present, the discs are said to alter their depth. The first becomes shallow. The second recedes. The third develops a minute dark elevation at its horizon. Across centuries, that elevation grows by amounts too small for any single survey to prove. Its profile resembles neither mountain nor building. It is a narrow upright interruption under the black sun, composed of several parts that do not quite touch.

This may be the inhabitant as it once was.

It may also be what it is becoming.

During the violet light’s rare brightenings, a similar form can almost be inferred in the basin itself. No shape appears. Instead, the terrain around a particular absence becomes unusually legible. Ridge edges orient toward it. Suspended bands of darkness bend around it. The warm tips of mineral points incline by fractions of degrees, outlining something tall enough to exceed the chamber’s visible vault.

It seems to have five principal divisions.

They do not connect. Each stands at an impossible remove from the others, though together they produce the conviction of one figure. The uppermost division is not a head but a region from which alternatives have been excluded. Beneath it hang two long asymmetries, perhaps limbs, perhaps directions. Its center is an aperture through which the basin can be seen in a state slightly older than the surrounding basin. The lowest divisions terminate at different centuries.

When this figure is present, the tiny ceramic clicks cease.

The nearly inaudible tone from the rods rises—not in pitch, but in recollection. Anyone nearby remembers hearing a bell at a decisive moment in childhood, even if no such bell existed. The compass points into the figure’s empty center. Clocks disagree less violently, as though the chamber’s scattered time has been gathered for attention.

Then something chooses.

A grain rotates. A vacancy leaves one column and appears in another. A reflection is permitted to fade. The frame acquires a new angle. These are negligible acts by human measure, but they may be the inhabitant’s language, each adjustment a statement whose grammar consists of physical constants.

It thinks very slowly because thought, for it, is not the movement of signals but the rearrangement of possible relations. A single inference may require one ridge to erode away. Doubt may be represented by two objects that remain almost able to touch for a million years. Memory is stored as distance that resists reduction. Desire is a direction in which the chamber becomes larger.

By those standards, it has been forming one thought since before the mountain rose.

The thought concerns completion.

The basin’s instruments, erosions, cultivated intervals, and migrating defects all contribute to it. The hollowing columns provide new interior. The rods exchange gaps. The discs distinguish what was, what was not, and what might replace both. Every cold year adds an immeasurable amount to the separation among the scattered objects, and that increase does not dissipate. It accumulates within the inhabitant.

Perhaps it is growing.

Perhaps it is assembling a body large enough to exist entirely without contact: organs separated by continents, gestures separated by ages, consciousness distributed across mutually unreachable states. Such a body could not be injured, because no force could travel from one part to another. It could not die, because death could not arrive everywhere it was. It would be complete only when each of its components had become absolutely inaccessible to all the rest.

This may be why the place is almost complete.

When the final interval has been established—when the last two related things are made permanently unable to meet—the Dry Altitude may cease to resemble a chamber. The rods, columns, discs, ridges, red grains, old reflections, and hollow spaces will reveal themselves as one organism viewed at an early and compact stage.

Or they will vanish from one another.

Nothing dramatic will occur outside. The black mountain will not split. The summit will show no light. Dawn will continue to cast gold across the dry basalt, and the seam will remain scarcely wide enough for a hand.

Inside, however, every object will at last be infinitely far from every other.

In that instant the inhabitant may open its eyes.

They will be the only two things left that can still see each other.
