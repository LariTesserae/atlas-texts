At a distance, the inhabitants are difficult to separate from the city.

A person standing motionless beside a salt wall may seem like another pale buttress: wrapped in chalk-colored membranes, head lowered, limbs held close against the cold. When they cross the mineral flats, however, they lengthen. Their silhouettes become narrow and deliberate, made taller by high-soled sandals and by carrying frames that rise over the shoulders like spare rib cages. In groups they resemble a procession of moving tools—hooks, baskets, poles, folded ladders—until a signal passes among them and every figure turns at once toward some change in the ceiling.

Then the impression alters again. They are not solemn. Not exactly. Up close, their hands are quick and expressive; their faces are restless with attention. They joke by touch, quarrel with flashes of polished stone, and decorate even the poorest utensils with patterns meant to be felt by the fingertips. Children run through the arcades with sleeves streaming, stamping hard enough to wake blue light in the walls. Old people sit in circles around condensation bowls and trade scandal in whispers so faint that an outsider might mistake their conversation for breathing.

They call themselves, in the nearest translation, the Attendants.

The name does not mean servants. It means those who remain beside something uncertain: beside a sickbed, a kiln, a fissure, a guest whose intentions are not yet known. They attend the basin because no one believes it can be ruled.

Their bodies are recognizably human, though shaped by a place where distance is bright, sound is private, and the sky can fall.

Most are slight. Food is too costly for unnecessary mass, and heavy bodies are poorly suited to floors that crust over hidden voids. Their feet are broad across the toes, with unusually mobile joints. Even barefoot, they test the ground before committing their weight, placing the outer edge first and rolling inward. Their gait looks hesitant only until one tries to follow it. They can cross unstable salt faster than a surface traveler can cross a paved court.

The soles of their feet and the pads of their hands are ridged with dense, sensitive whorls. Through them they feel tremors too small to disturb hanging water. A sleeper may wake because a distant stair has cracked. A mason resting one palm on a wall can often tell whether water is passing behind it, whether the stone is compressing, or whether soft-bodied animals are tunneling through the mortar.

Their eyes are large but not enlarged into the dark-adapted organs of creatures that live without light. Darkness is rare here. Instead, their pupils contract to narrow horizontal slits against the ceiling’s unsoftened glare. A second translucent lid can be drawn across the eye in dust or in the blinding reflection of the flooded Forum. The irises are usually gray, amber, or a washed mineral blue. Pure black eyes occur in some families from the outer warm chambers and are regarded as beautiful and slightly alarming.

Their ears lie close to the skull beneath a folded rim of cartilage. Airborne sound tells them little, but the inner ear is acutely responsive to pressure. Sudden subterranean movement may make them stop, swallow, and press their jaws open before anyone else notices the floor shifting. Scars behind the ears are common among quarry workers, who sometimes rupture the delicate chambers that sense these changes. Such workers are said to have “lost the deep weather.” Many become potters or net makers afterward, though some remain in the quarries and rely on apprentices to lay warning hands upon their shoulders.

Skin tones range from ivory through ocher, umber, and deep brown, but nearly everyone appears paler than they are. Fine mineral powder settles into the pores, eyebrows, and creases of the neck. People oil their eyelids and nostrils against it. On festival days they wash completely—an extravagance—and paint themselves back into mineral colors: red bands over the ribs, violet palms, white circles around the knees, blue salt along the throat. Because the luminous organisms brighten under warmth, painted dancers sometimes press their bodies briefly to prepared walls and leave glowing impressions behind. These silhouettes fade within minutes. Their impermanence is part of their value.

Hair is kept short, braided tight, or covered. Loose hair catches dust, fungal fibers, and the hooks of burden frames. Many adults shave a path from brow to crown and tattoo it with a record of escaped disasters: a fork for a flood divided around them, a closed oval for surviving bad air, descending dots for a ceiling fall. These are not boasts. They tell rescuers what kinds of danger the person recognizes and what injuries they may already carry.

No one wears clothes intended to last a lifetime. Garments are layered from fungal membrane, woven cord, thin hide, and soft felt made from the shed fibers of domestic burrowers. Outer wraps are cut with many ties so they can become slings, filters, rope padding, water funnels, or burial coverings. Long hems are considered foolish. Sleeves end above the wrist to leave the hands visible.

Visibility matters. In a city where voices fail across a room, every citizen speaks with the body.

Their hand-language is not a substitute for speech but a public language of its own. Spoken words belong to the near distance: family, tenderness, insult, secret, instruction whispered into a child’s ear. Hand-speech belongs to plazas and work crews. It is broad, economical, and rich in verbs of change. There are separate signs for a wall leaning gradually, a wall leaning after rain, a wall appearing straight while its foundation moves, and a wall that animals refuse to approach. The sign for safety is never an absolute. It means safe until a named condition changes.

Hands held low speak of water and ground. Hands near the face speak of people, intention, memory, and debt. Raised arms describe the ceiling. A fist opened slowly overhead means brightness spreading. Fingers falling together mean dust. Two wrists crossed, then turned, warn of stone under torsion. Children learn these signs before they form full sentences, and toddlers can often communicate *do not step there* before they can say their own names.

For greater distances they use polished plates of calcite and obsidian, flashing against the luminous roof. At night—though “night” means only a period when the local vault is dim—they uncover or shade patches of responsive microbial skin. Whole towers can speak by brightness. A dark pulse passing down a district may announce gas. Three pale squares mean unexpected water. A wavering vertical band means that gliders have changed course and a pressure event may follow.

There are specialists who read these signals, but there are no permanent watchmen. Every able household owes periods of attention. To watch too long is considered dangerous: the mind begins to find intention in every dimming seam.

Their homes have few fixed rooms. A house is first a set of relationships: a warm wall, a clear exit, a ceiling with known fractures, a place where water has visited but not lingered. Sleeping platforms are low and narrow. Each person keeps a departure basket within reach, packed in an established order—water flask, dust veil, lamp shard, cord, food paste, foot wrappings, family tokens, small hammer. Children are taught to find each item with closed eyes.

The inner wall nearest the sleeping place is marked every evening with a thin line of fresh powder. If the line bends, breaks, or buckles by morning, the household moves.

They do not speak of this as losing a home. A structure that tilts beyond use has “finished sheltering.” Its doorframe is removed, its roof stones carried away, and its walls left open for animals and weather. Families may grieve, but they do not ask a building to remain what it can no longer be.

Kinship therefore attaches less to houses than to carried things. One family may keep a calcite ladle passed through twenty generations, repaired so often that none of the original bowl remains. Another keeps a bundle of knotted cords recording the sequence of places where its members slept. A child’s formal name is not complete until it includes the condition of the city at birth: Sava-Beneath-Blue-Roof, Heren-During-Three-Rains, Oth-When-the-Western-Stair-Closed.

Names change after great events. Someone who survives a flood may relinquish the water portion of their name and take the name of the route by which they escaped. Someone who makes a disastrous judgment may be given a shortened name until trust is restored. The dead lose all condition-names and are addressed only by the private word used for them in childhood.

The Attendants eat low on the cavern’s food web and waste almost nothing.

Their staple is a sour, translucent paste grown in warm clay vessels from fungal residue, microbial scrapings, mineral water, and rendered animal fat. It trembles like stiff jelly and tastes faintly of salt, smoke, and old stone. Better meals include ribbon fish, cistern swimmers, soft-shelled burrowers roasted in their own armor, or cakes made from the eggs of gliders. Tiny grazers are swept into baskets after rain, rinsed through ash, and pressed into protein bricks. Hook-beaks provide meat, hide, eggs, alarm behavior, and companionship, though no one would claim to own one completely.

A hook-beak is a waist-high, two-legged animal with a narrow chest, grasping mouthparts, and a fan of heat-sensitive quills around its neck. It sleeps in roofless pens but comes and goes as it pleases. It knows the food call of its household and may permit favored children to cling to its back. During pressure changes its quills flatten in sequence. Families read those movements as carefully as expressions on a human face.

The Attendants say a creature lives with people if it returns after being free to leave.

They extend a similar courtesy to water.

When a buried channel opens into a neighborhood, no one rushes to wall it in. First the outlet is watched. Bowls of different depths are placed in the flow. Animals are allowed to approach. A water-taster touches droplets to the eye, lips, tongue, and a strip of living luminous film. Only then do people fill jars. The first jar belongs to those who cleared the channel, the second to children and the injured, the third to communal storage. After that, everyone gathers what they can.

Water-tasters are respected, though their work often damages them. Older ones have white scars in the mouth and permanently trembling fingers. They know bitter salts, dissolved metals, spores, warm gases, and the sweet taste that sometimes precedes sickness. Their apprentices memorize not only flavors but bodily responses: numb gums, pressure behind the eyes, a false sensation of cold in the teeth.

Other professions are equally bound to change.

Wall-listeners lie with cheek and palm against foundations, tracing movements on erasable clay tablets. Air-bearers enter newly exposed rooms carrying small cages of cistern crabs and long tubes filled with pale flame. Route-keepers walk the major passages each day and revise the city’s directions in cords, gestures, and recited condition-sequences. Roof-shaders maintain the shutters used to signal across open ground. Animal-followers study migrations and droppings. Seam-tenders cultivate luminous skins without pretending to control them.

There are also the Unbuilders.

They are often the first people seen when a district begins to fail: masked figures arriving with ropes, wedges, baskets, and painted shoulder signs. Their task is to take a structure apart before the basin does so violently. They remove lintels, lower roof stones, open trapped walls, and salvage what can travel. A skilled Unbuilder knows where a house is holding its last strain. Their highest craft is not demolition but release.

Builders and Unbuilders train together. Neither profession is considered complete without the other.

Authority belongs temporarily to whoever possesses the relevant knowledge. During a flood, the water-tasters and channel-clearers direct a district. During a ceiling shift, seam-tenders and wall-listeners do. In famine, keepers of animal paths gain power. Once the condition ends, command dissolves. Anyone who tries to retain emergency authority is described as “building beneath yesterday’s roof.”

The closest thing to a permanent council is the Assembly of Contradictions, composed of elders selected because they are known to disagree well. No proposal may pass until someone has described how it might become dangerous under altered conditions. Their meeting court contains no seats fixed to the ground. Each speaker carries their own stool and places it according to where the light currently falls.

Their laws are recited, never carved. They are remembered through paired stories: in one, obedience saves a district; in the other, rigid obedience destroys one. Children therefore learn that every rule has a pressure at which it must bend. Even the prohibition against stone maps survives because the account of its origin changes with each teller. In some versions, a carved map led refugees into a collapsed avenue. In others, the map itself was accurate, but people trusted it after the city had moved. In the oldest version, the stone became ashamed of lying and split down the middle.

Writing exists, but cautiously. Marks are painted on ceramic flakes, pressed into waxy fungal sheets, knotted into cords, or arranged as loose tiles. Records are expected to be revised. Historical archives resemble baskets of fragments more than books. To consult one is to reconstruct a pattern from disputed pieces while an archivist asks what present danger has made the past seem relevant.

Their religion, if it can be called that, has no creator who finished the world.

They speak instead of the Continuing: the process by which one condition leans into another. The Continuing has no face, intention, or mercy. It is not worshiped. It is acknowledged, as one acknowledges pressure against a door.

More intimate devotion is given to the Departed Shapes—the forms that sheltered life for a time and then ceased to exist. A vanished stair, a drained cistern, an animal species no longer seen, an old configuration of streets: all may be remembered in low chants spoken mouth to ear. Their sacred places are often absences. A family might visit the smooth patch where a house once stood and set down four removable stones to indicate its corners. Before leaving, they take the stones away.

Funerals follow the same refusal to impose permanence.

Bodies are washed with as little water as possible, dusted in bitter salt, and wrapped in the deceased’s outer garment. Depending on the district, they may be placed on high burial shelves, given to colonies of soft scavengers, sealed briefly in drying rooms, or lowered into warm fissures. Bones are sometimes recovered later and made into buttons, needles, resonant chimes, or handles for tools. This is not understood as using the dead. It is allowing them to remain helpful while their shape changes.

A small bone disk worn at the throat may have been part of a grandmother’s wrist. Tapped against the teeth, it transmits a faint vibration through the skull. People do this when they need courage.

Children are indulged in curiosity but disciplined fiercely around certainty. Adults praise questions that multiply possibilities. A child who announces, “This passage is safe,” is corrected. A child who says, “No dust has fallen here since the last blue brightness, the crabs are moving inward, and the floor is cool,” is praised for seeing.

Their games train the same habits. They build miniature cities from loose pebbles while another child secretly pushes folds into the cloth beneath. They race to dismantle towers before falling beads strike them. They play at animal migration, with one child inventing an unseen danger and the others trying to infer it from the way they move.

Adolescents undertake a journey called the First Revision. They memorize a route to a distant precinct, wait until some part of it becomes unusable, and then return by altering the instructions without abandoning the destination. The test is not whether they arrive quickly. It is whether everyone traveling with them arrives.

Those who return alone have failed.

Despite the hazards of their world, the Attendants are not consumed by fear. Constant instability has made panic seem both wasteful and slightly vulgar. In emergencies they become very still before they move. Hands rise. Eyes follow animals, dust, brightness, one another. Then the district unfolds: roof stones are lifted free, baskets shouldered, children tied to carrying frames, ash rings broken so predators will not be trapped inside them. People leave in streams so quiet that only the glimmering walls reveal their passage.

Afterward, if no one has died, laughter often begins. It is soft, breathy, and close-range. To hear a whole crowd laugh, one must stand among them. Across a plaza it looks like bodies folding and hands striking shoulders in silence.

Their celebrations are similarly near and far at once. Signal plates send austere geometries across miles of open stone, while beneath them people crowd close enough to exchange warmth. They dance by stamping patterns into resonant floors, not to make audible music but to feel rhythm through the legs and spine. Drums are placed horizontally so several dancers can rest fingers on the skin. Singers press forehead to forehead, sharing tones through bone.

During the flooding of the White Forum, thousands may gather in high-soled sandals. Families arrange translucent cups on the shallow water. Luminous organisms, disturbed by each step, flare underfoot. From a distance the celebration resembles a slow constellation moving between two skies.

Not all Attendants remain in the city.

At the basin’s margins live the Far Walkers, darker with volcanic dust and burdened with sealed jars, shell armor, black-glass blades, and coils of fungus cord. Some are traders. Some are exiles. Some have become unable to bear the city’s accumulated memory and seek places where no ancestor knows the route.

They return months or years later with altered gestures and unfamiliar animals. One may bring a blind fish that leaps before earthquakes. Another carries a pane of smoky crystal that holds warmth for days. Another describes a cavern where the luminous skins gather into upright, person-sized folds and withdraw when approached.

Travelers are welcomed, isolated, questioned, and doubted in equal measure. Their stories are never judged solely as true or false. The more urgent question is: under what conditions would acting on this story save us, and under what conditions would it kill us?

There are people who do not return. Small settlements beyond the watch posts develop different customs, different hand-languages, even different bodies. Warm-chamber dwellers grow compact and heavy, with scarred lungs and heat-darkened skin. Those from drowned passages bind their toes apart for swimming and wear polished breathing shells over the mouth. In the oldest peripheral communities, people are said to have learned the electrical sense of the gliders. They shave their entire bodies and stand beneath charged columns with arms spread, reading distant movement as a prickling across the skin.

The central Attendants regard such claims with skepticism, envy, and longing.

They know they are not the only inhabitants who interpret the basin. The crabs read gas. The gliders read tension. The tiny grazers read invisible films. Predators read warmth. Water reads every weakness in stone.

Human knowledge is merely one kind among these.

For this reason, their wisest people are not those who speak most confidently but those who notice first when another creature has changed its mind. An elder may interrupt a council because a hook-beak has turned toward an empty arch. A market can dissolve because mothlike animals have folded their wings at the wrong hour. No shame attaches to following beasts out of danger. The Attendants say animals carry maps in flesh, where the world can revise them.

When the long-falling drop finally reaches the city, it strikes the dust beside a child repairing a line of marker stones.

The child feels the impact through one bare hand. She pauses. Around the dark point, tiny translucent grazers begin to stir from cracks too small to see. High above, a halo has formed around a luminous seam. Her hook-beak lifts its ring of quills. Across the flat, an old woman shades a signal plate and waits.

The child does not yet call it rain.

She smells the opened dust. She watches the animals. She touches the stone again and feels a second impact, farther away.

Only then does she raise one hand toward the waiting city, fingers spread for water, wrist turned to indicate uncertainty.

All across the terrace, people look up.
