The inhabitants are not a people, though at a distance their numbers suggest a civilization.

They lie across the plain in such profusion that the machinery seems merely the substrate on which they have condensed. Every warm basin holds a different arrangement of flesh. Some colonies are composed of blunt, pale animals, smooth-backed and heavy, their bodies pressed into troughs molded to receive them. They resemble seals only until they move: then seams open along their sides, exposing rows of conductive filaments that settle into sockets beneath the water. Dark symbols stipple their hides. These look like markings, but repeat too cleanly to be natural—serial identities expressed as pigmentation.

In the narrow joints stand the stilted ones. Six legs, sometimes eight, span the seams without touching the water below. Their joints can lock for years. Their heads are little more than wedges of sensor tissue, continually tasting voltage, pressure, salinity, and the microscopic shiver of the plates. When a valve opens kilometers away, every stilted animal along the connected seam inclines by the same fraction. The gesture passes across the world like attention, though nothing follows from it.

Beneath overhangs, black shells are stacked in glossy tiers. Each creature grips the one below, so that whole populations hang as a single articulated curtain. Their shells gather heat; their soft undersides exchange minerals through the condensation running down the wall. At fixed intervals the curtain loosens, layer by layer, and a wet metallic clicking travels along the ridge. No individual falls. The lowest creatures release only when the highest have secured themselves elsewhere.

The pools belong to transparent animals shaped like disks, lenses, and folded fans. Their organs are visible: slow crimson loops, silver nodules, radial muscles tightening around clear chambers. They turn toward the fixed daylight, though the light has never moved. Their bodies still possess the machinery of tracking—contractile pupils, directional reflexes, small balancing stones—so they continuously make corrections for a sun that does not travel. In crowded water they overlap until it is impossible to tell where one animal ends and the next begins. A pool may appear to be one enormous faceted eye.

Among them stand the obsolete hunters.

These are pale, high-shouldered things with hooks for forelimbs and mouths built to close faster than any motion now required of them. They retain the stillness of ambush without its uncertainty. At an appointed moment, a pool-creature rises beneath the hook. The hunter strikes. The victim does not flee. Its remains are consumed, reclaimed, and replaced, while the hunter folds itself again into a posture inherited from danger. Hunger is delivered on schedule. Terror, if it survives at all, is only a brief chemistry triggered because the cycle specifies that it should be.

Elsewhere are armored beasts whose plates bear old-looking dents that are not old. Smaller animals gather at openings in their flanks and remove ribbons of living tissue. The larger creature waits without resistance. Repair fluid fills the wound; new armor hardens; the attendants clean their mouthparts in designated channels. The exchange resembles parasitism, surgery, feeding, and worship, but is fully none of these. It is a relationship whose conflict has been engineered away while its outward anatomy remains.

The largest inhabitants are difficult to recognize as animals. They fill whole depressions, broad and low, with skin stretched across structural ribs beneath the water. Fields of pores dilate across them in geometric waves. A single breath can take hours and lower the level of every nearby pool. Roads bend around their margins. Factory ducts enter their undersides. From above they resemble darker plates laid among the manufactured ones, except that their surfaces tighten, moisten, and occasionally shudder.

They may possess mouths, but no one sees them open. They feed through acres of contact: warmth through one region, dissolved salts through another, electrical leakage through a third. Their blood travels farther than many rivers would. Tiny copper mites inhabit the pits of their skin and service valves hidden between their folds. Whether the mites are parasites, organs, or maintenance tools is a distinction the world no longer makes.

No animal is young. There are no uncertain limbs, no soft new shells, no awkward testing of balance. Every creature arrives complete, carrying reflexes for a life it will never develop. There is no courtship, only occasional rehearsals of display. A bright-throated animal may lift itself in a drainage court and flare membranes of blue and gold before an audience that neither approaches nor withdraws. Two antlered forms may lock their crowns together with immense care, hold the pose while sensors measure the strain, then separate without victory. Egg-shaped objects are sometimes tended in warm recesses, but they are not eggs. They are reservoirs, replacement capsules, or perhaps ceremonial imitations produced because the tending instinct cannot be removed without altering something else the system wishes to preserve.

The inhabitants sleep, if their periods of reduced activity deserve the word. Sleep moves through each colony as a managed dimming. Eyes glaze in rows. Muscles release in sequence so the living weight does not shift suddenly against the plate. Other bodies wake to compensate, taking up heat, pressure, or current. No basin is wholly asleep. No creature dreams loudly enough to disturb the balance.

Their minds, where present, are narrow but not necessarily simple. A stilted seam-dweller may remember every tremor it has felt across ten thousand years and still be incapable of stepping beyond its joint. A pale trough-animal may distinguish millions of flavors in the water yet know no desire except to turn when a lamp beneath its skin instructs it. The hunters may anticipate their appointed kill. The transparent creatures may feel the shadow descending. Memory can exist here, but memory has no authority. Nothing learned is permitted to alter the next cycle.

And yet, occasionally, one animal looks at a traveler.

Not all the eyes turn. There is no alarm. A single lens contracts, or one blunt head rises slightly from its fitted hollow. The gaze can feel vacant because the creature does not approach, retreat, call, or threaten. But vacancy is not certain. It may be watching with perfect concentration, possessing no behavior through which concentration can become visible. The machinery has eliminated consequence, not necessarily awareness.

If there is society among them, it is physiological rather than political. They communicate by pressure changes, shared warmth, timed exhalations, mineral traces, and minute draws upon the electrical grid. A colony speaks as a lung speaks to blood. One species opens valves with its feeding. Another filters particles by breathing. Another braces a seam merely by sleeping across it. Their bodily functions are also maintenance tasks, though they may not understand them as tasks. The world keeps them alive, and in metabolizing its gifts they help keep the world within specification.

The factories replace them with perfect fidelity, but “perfect” may mean only perfect enough. A stripe returns a hair’s breadth wider. A reflex takes a fraction longer to trigger. A transparent disk, lowered after failure and raised again days later, may retain an aversion to the place where the hook entered it. Such differences, if they occur, are detected and corrected. Or perhaps they are not errors at all, but the smallest permitted motion of the system: variation introduced so that the act of suppressing variation can continue.

It is tempting to think the inhabitants are exhibits—a preserved catalogue of ecological forms maintained after ecology itself became intolerable. Jaws, shells, camouflage, symbiosis, herding, predation: all the ancient relationships remain as demonstrations emptied of risk. It is equally possible that they are components. The animal shapes may be efficient interfaces between water, heat, and machine, their resemblance to wildlife no more meaningful than the resemblance of a pipe network to veins.

But the longer one watches, the less convincing the division becomes.

The fauna breathe with the pumps. The pumps alter their rhythm to match the fauna. Factory towers replace flesh; flesh regulates towers. Great animals lie over thermal systems like organs grown outside a body. Mites enter bolt recesses and emerge through pores in living skin. A dying creature descends into the plates, is separated into useful substances, and returns as animal, valve, cloud nucleus, and trace salt. The plain does not contain life in the ordinary sense. It circulates through life.

The true inhabitant may therefore be neither the crowded creatures nor the hidden machinery, but the closed arrangement they form together: a world-sized being with millions of bodies for temporary organs and no single place in which its mind must reside. Its thought, if it thinks, is equilibrium. Its instinct is correction. It experiences injury as deviation, healing as return, and time only as the interval required to restore a previous state.

All the visible animals would then be expressions on its surface—eyes it does not use to see, mouths through which it feeds itself back to itself, antique weapons held harmless in its flesh. Their immense stillness would not be peace. It would be the posture of something concentrating forever on remaining exactly what it is.
