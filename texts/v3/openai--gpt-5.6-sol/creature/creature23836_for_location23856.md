At first they are difficult to distinguish from the wet materials they handle.

One stands beside a gutter and appears to be a twist of brown root wrapped in moss. Then the root bends, raises a narrow face, and puts two fingers into the current. Another clings beneath a bridge while a rain surge passes overhead, all four limbs spread against the reedwork. A third remains motionless in a doorway, eyes open and black, holding a shell of greenish light against its breast.

They are small enough to shelter beneath the cap of a marsh mushroom, though size varies strangely among them. Some are scarcely longer than a finger; some reach nearly twice that height and must stoop in older passages. Their bodies are spare, with little soft flesh exposed to the cold. The chest is deep, the belly narrow, the shoulders rounded forward from a lifetime beneath low roofs. Their arms are long enough to brace against opposite walls. Their hands have four slender fingers and a short opposing thumb, all roughened at the tips by minute hooks of skin. The youngest can hang from root hairs before they can walk.

Their feet are broad and soft. A thin fringe grows along each toe and spreads in water, allowing them to cross mud without sinking deeply. On dry reedwork they curl this fringe inward. Their tracks, when they leave any, resemble tiny open leaves.

The skin of the marsh people is not green, though lamp mold often makes it seem so. It ranges from the gray of submerged bark to peat-brown, silt-blue, and the translucent pallor of a root newly cut. Beneath it run dark, visible vessels. Cold mottles them quickly. Adults rub themselves with oils expressed from beetle larvae and marsh seeds, both to retain warmth and to prevent fungal threads from taking hold in the folds of the body. Neglect announces itself first as a pale bloom behind the ears.

They have little hair. Instead, fine sensory filaments grow along the jaw, forearms, and ribs. These tremble at the slightest movement of water or air. In a silent passage, an inhabitant can feel someone approaching around a bend by the pressure their steps send through the root floor. Before heavy rain, every filament lifts. Whole workshops pause at once, without warning or command, because every worker has felt the roof begin to burden itself.

Their faces are flat and intent. The nose closes with two muscular slits. The ears are small openings protected by overlapping folds, but their hearing is acute enough to make ordinary speech painful at close range. Their eyes are large, glossy, and almost entirely dark. In bright surface light the pupil tightens to a point and they become nearly blind; those forced above at midday wear veils made from the translucent lining of seedpods. Underground, they see motion better than shape. Color matters less to them than sheen, dampness, and the minute differences between blacks.

Their mouths are wide, with flexible lips suited to whispering directly against another’s ear. Behind the lips are small, close-set teeth. The incisors continue growing throughout life and must be worn down on reed pith, fungal cork, and the hard rims of shell tools. A nervous person can often be identified by the little bundle of fibers they chew.

No two inhabitants are dressed alike for long. Clothing here is temporary and partly alive. They wind themselves in strips of inner bark, moth down, scraped reed membrane, and felted plant fiber, fastening everything with thorn pins. Outer layers are smeared with wax and mud. A coat may sprout mold, stiffen, and be cut into wall insulation after a season. Sleeves are usually tight so they cannot trail in water. Hoods fit close around the sensory filaments but leave the jaw free.

Their most characteristic garment is a back mantle woven with loops, pockets, and short lengths of cord. It carries almost nothing heavy. A worker may possess a shell knife, a bone awl, a twist of dry tinder sealed in wax, three pruning hooks, and a lump of salt no larger than a mite. Tools are passed between generations. Copper belongs more often to a neighborhood than to an individual, and the disappearance of a copper hook can halt work in several streets.

Ornaments are quiet. No metal hangs loose. Beads are threaded tightly into collars. Rank is shown by knots, scars, and the direction in which strips of bark are wrapped around the forearm. Children wear snail-shell fragments over the heart until they have survived their first flood. Those who work near the surface stain their eyelids with black fungus. Those who tend the dead have white palms from the alkaline compost clay.

They do speak, but speech is only one layer of their language.

At conversational distance, their voices are dry murmurs shaped in the front of the mouth, with few open vowels. Personal names are often a single breath and cannot be heard more than a body-length away. Across a room they use their hands. Across crowded markets they use cords: knots for quantity, damp knots for urgency, split fibers for danger, loops for promises. Messages travel by being retied rather than repeated, and old agreements are kept as bundles in cool archives where insects are picked from them each morning.

Through walls they tap. One stroke means water. Two mean clear the passage. A rapid brush of fingernails means something alive is moving inside the rootwork. Hard knocks are reserved for collapse, fire, and the heron’s beak. To strike a wall in anger is treated much as drawing a weapon would be elsewhere.

They are not one people in the simple sense. The new city has gathered survivors from drowned pockets, frost-sealed burrows, uprooted reed villages, and floating islands that broke apart during the thaw. Their bodies retain those origins. The long-limbed northern families have bluish skin and sleep curled tightly around heat stones. Those from the open pools have wider feet and a clear inner eyelid. Root-dwellers are smaller, pale, and nearly sightless, communicating through finger pressure more readily than signs. A few old households possess faint dark stripes down the back, said to come from ancestors who lived among mud minnows and could remain underwater for half an hour.

They do not agree on what they are called. Surface scouts call them the Under-Sedge People. Compost keepers call everyone, including animals and fungi, the Warmth. Surveyors use a term that means approximately *those who keep an opening from closing*. In ordinary speech, most simply say *we who are here*.

Their children hatch neither from eggs nor spores; they are born live, usually one at a time, slick and silent. A newborn’s first cry is dangerous, so birthing chambers are thickly padded and situated near the noisy churn of compost. Mothers, fathers, siblings, and unrelated heat-companions sleep together around the infant through its first cold cycle. Kinship follows warmth more often than blood. Whoever carried a child through winter is counted as a parent. Whoever surrendered a sleeping place beside a heat knot may claim an aunt’s or uncle’s obligations.

The very young are carried in reed cradles on the chest. As soon as they can grip, they are given cords to knot and unknot. Their games teach restraint: crossing a floor without stirring droplets, passing beneath sleeping moths, identifying a neighbor through a wall by the rhythm of their breathing. They race seed husks through drainage gutters after light rain. They keep mites on threads and trade beetle wing cases. Like children anywhere, they discover forbidden loudness early. They shriek into sealed fungus cups, laughing at the trapped vibration, and sometimes dare one another to touch the unused doors.

Adults discourage this not with stories of monsters, but by becoming very still.

The people are divided less by wealth than by access to dryness, warmth, and knowledge of the living structure. The most influential are the root persuaders. They know where to bind, bruise, shade, feed, or irritate a growing plant so that months later it will become a beam, ladder, cistern lip, or doorway. Their fingers are stained by sap. Many are missing tips where a root closed faster than expected. They carry little maps made from braided fibers, but trust them less than touch. Before entering a district, a persuader lays a palm against the nearest wall and waits to feel what has changed.

Water readers govern during rain. They sleep lightly near the upper gutters and can tell from the taste of the first drops whether the storm is clean, bitter with bird filth, or carrying surface rot. They decide which cisterns open and which streets are sacrificed to flooding. Their authority lasts only as long as the water rises. When it falls, they return the sluice cords to their pegs.

Mold tenders cultivate the city’s light. Their chambers are among the warmest and most dangerous, dense with shallow trays whose growths glow blue, green, or the weak gold of old bone. The molds must be fed but not allowed to fruit. Some cause fever. Some consume bark lashings. One brilliant strain draws moths into a frenzy and is kept sealed except during rescues. Tenders trim the cultures with eyelash brushes and speak to them through humming, a risky privilege granted because certain molds brighten in response to low vibration.

There are also listeners who patrol the outer passages. They carry no lamps. They kneel with cheeks against walls and distinguish shrew, eel, settling root, burrowing swarm, surface footfall, and the slow exploratory pressure of a heron. The oldest listeners have enlarged jaw filaments and cloudy eyes. They are often the first to know a district is going to be lost. They mark condemned doors with a knot of black grass and remain nearby until everyone has gone.

No one rules the entire city. Decisions are made at separate platforms, with distances left between speakers so that no gathering becomes a single vulnerable mass. Proposals are laid out as cords on damp boards. Agreement is shown by joining loops. Objection is a hooked thorn. Silence does not mean consent; it means the matter has not yet become safe enough to answer.

Their disputes concern water rights, pruning boundaries, stolen heat, diseased walls, and whether a newly opened chamber should be inhabited at all. Violence is rare but intimate. There is nowhere to flee noisily, nowhere to slam a door, nowhere to burn an enemy’s house without burning the roof above everyone. Anger is enacted through withheld labor, severed bridge lashings, misleading knots, or the cruel placement of sound. The worst criminals are those who drum to attract predators toward another household.

Yet they are not solemn all the time.

Their humor is largely tactile. A joke may be a false warning knot tied into someone’s sleeve, a cold snail slipped into a hood, or a hand sign deliberately altered so that “three baskets of fungus” becomes “three baskets of grandmothers.” They make tiny puppets from shed insect skins and perform slow dramas behind luminous membranes. Their music is felt rather than heard: strings stretched between roots, stroked so softly that the audience receives the tune through palms and feet. At festivals, entire plazas lie prone against the floor while musicians play the city itself.

They eat often and in small quantities. Meals consist of seed meal, softened reed pith, mushrooms, algae skimmed from cisterns, beetle grubs, fish eggs, and pale strips of root grown specifically for harvest. Food is fermented whenever possible, both for preservation and warmth. Families keep sour pastes in stoppered shells. Salt is treasured. A grain placed on the tongue can serve as oath, medicine, payment, and feast.

They do not regard the other animals as a separate lower order. Hares are partners with limited patience. Lizards are workers who must be bargained with through heat. Frogs are tolerated squatters and excellent flood alarms. Snails are slow citizens of disputed usefulness. Moths belong partly to the roof and partly to the dead. Even mites have recognized seasons in which they may be killed freely and seasons in which they are merely brushed aside.

Some creatures are trained, some domesticated, and some simply incorporated into custom because resisting them proved impossible. A pannier hare knows six hand signals and will refuse a seventh. Mill lizards recognize their keepers by body heat. Small fish are released into fresh cisterns to test for poison. Beetles clean wounds. Threadworms are used to locate hidden leaks: the inhabitants place them on a damp wall and watch where they burrow.

The animals, in turn, change the people. Hare keepers adopt the animals’ foot-stamping warnings, softened to a vibration. Water readers paint false frog eyes on cistern plugs. Children raised near moth chambers dust themselves compulsively and tend to sleep hanging in fabric slings. Old lizard keepers sit for hours on warm tiles, blinking slowly, reluctant to return to colder rooms.

Death is familiar, but not treated as disappearance.

The dead are washed only if water can be spared. Their useful gut cultures are preserved. Tools are removed, cords untied, names whispered once into the ear so the body does not attempt to carry them into the compost. Then the dead are placed in a court layered with fungus, beetles, and chopped sedge. Families visit not a grave but a sequence of transformations. On the first day, there is still a body. Later there is heat. Later still, a flush of pale mushrooms or a thickening in the nearby wall.

Children are shown which roots contain their ancestors.

This does not make grief smaller. It makes it difficult to locate. A mourner may refuse food grown from a certain chamber. Someone may press their forehead to a warm pipe every morning because the warmth passing through it came, in part, from a lost companion. Root persuaders sometimes shape memorial alcoves around the last hard fragments: a tooth, a copper stain, the polished eye of a needle.

The unused door in every home belongs to this uncertainty. It is made carefully, often more carefully than the entrance used by the household. Its threshold is kept clear. No bridge leads to it. Beyond lies compressed darkness, wet fibers, and the soft internal movements of the marsh.

Offerings vanish from these doors.

Usually mites take them. Sometimes roots absorb them. Sometimes a white filament grows through the meal and withdraws. But there are nights when salt is gone without a trail, when shed skins have been folded into shapes no inhabitant recognizes, or when damp prints appear on the inner threshold—broad, soft prints like theirs, except that the toes point toward the house.

The marsh people do not speak loudly of this. They say only that they were not the first to discover the cavity.

Perhaps nothing singular lives beyond those doors. Perhaps the whole marsh lives there: root tips, fungal nerves, buried larvae, drowned memories, cold water feeling its way through the dark. The inhabitants resist giving it one face. One face could be bargained with or feared. The thing around them is less merciful and more intimate than that.

Over years, the oldest inhabitants begin to resemble their city. Their joints stiffen in the shape of ladders. Moss stains remain permanently in the creases of their skin. Fungal threads colonize scar tissue and sometimes glow faintly during fever. Sensory filaments lengthen. Their breath takes on the smell of wet bark. An elder sitting motionless in an alcove may be overlooked for hours, mistaken for part of the wall.

Some do not object to this resemblance.

Their deepest belief is not that the city shelters them, nor that they have tamed it. It is that they and the chamber are engaged in a slow mutual test. They prune it to preserve themselves. It closes passages, floods streets, grows through floors, and alters their bodies. They feed it their dead. It gives them walls, warmth, food, and darkness. Neither side has decided whether this is cooperation, digestion, or the early stage of some more complete joining.

So they leave one doorway open.

They leave gaps between homes.

They speak softly enough that the roots need not flinch.

And in the coldest hours, gathered around the buried compost heat, the people hold their broad feet against the living floor and feel the immense marsh shifting above and around them. No one mistakes the movement for safety. Yet no one pulls away.
