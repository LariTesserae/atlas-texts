At first it seems that the chamber is inhabited only by colonies.

The black foliage divides readily into apparent individuals. Here is a fan rooted in lime; there a curtain of leaves issuing from the split casing of the engine; elsewhere a rope-growth descends from the ceiling and vanishes into a pipe. Each patch has its own color, texture, and manner of answering the distant impacts. Some fold. Some stiffen. Some release spores. It is natural to think of them as separate plants adapted to the same severe climate.

Close observation makes this difficult.

A leaf cut from the eastern wall causes sap to bead from a stalk near the western trench. When a mineral mat is burned, the green gauges in the control room briefly dim. If a rope-growth is severed, several shallow basins cloud at once, although no root joins them. These responses may be delayed by minutes or days. The connections are not anatomical in the usual sense. They pass through wet stone, rusted bolts, trapped water, and the vibration of the buried works.

The chamber contains many growths, but it may support only one living thing.

It has no central body. The long engine is not its heart, though it uses the engine as certain sea creatures use an abandoned shell. The glowing gauges are not its brain, though changes in the garden appear there before they occur. The leaves are neither wholly organs nor wholly attendants. Even the fungi are difficult to classify. They dismantle dead tissue, but they also plate metal with conductive skins and close cracks that would allow the heat to escape.

The inhabitant is distributed among all of these.

Its oldest portion may be the thin microbial glaze beneath the warm basins. Its youngest portions are the numbered leaves hidden behind the retracting panels. Those pale stacks are soft when they first appear. Over decades they darken, acquire right-angled veins, and migrate outward through cavities in the stone. No one sees them move. Their positions simply alter between surveys, as though the wall had dealt them into new arrangements.

The serial numbers are not labels applied to the leaves. They are grown into the tissue. Under magnification each raised digit consists of thousands of narrow cells packed with copper salts. The numbers follow no counting system yet identified, but corresponding marks can sometimes be found on bolts, inside stones from the river trench, and beneath the enamel faces of the gauges. A numbered leaf may remain folded for a century before assuming a task. Once placed beside a leak, it thickens into a gasket. Against a broken wire, it becomes conductive. Suspended over a basin, it curls into a scoop and begins moving one drop at a time.

This is how the inhabitant behaves: not by walking, hunting, or reaching, but by assigning.

It has no conception of refuse. Anything entering the chamber is treated as an incomplete component. Iron is given channels. Glass is given fluid. Cloth is given rigidity. A human footprint, if made in the soft mineral deposits, does not remain a footprint for long. Its edges rise. Its heel deepens. Warm water gathers in it, and within several years the impression becomes another basin with a white evaporative rim.

The same is true of sound. Voices do not echo normally there. Spoken words enter the mechanical roar and are separated into their useful frequencies. A low syllable may reappear as vibration in a pipe. A hiss may loosen spores from the ceiling mats. Sometimes, long after a visit, the chamber reproduces the rhythm of a conversation by tapping loose metal against stone. It retains no meaning, only cadence.

This may be the nearest thing it has to hearing.

Its sense of touch is finer. Every impact below passes through the chamber’s full length, and the inhabitant reads the distortions. A new crack in the ceiling changes the pulse by less than a heartbeat in ten thousand. A falling grain of mineral alters the harmonics of a basin. The black leaves, tilted toward the next **THOOM**, are not worshipping it. They are listening ahead, placing themselves so that the coming vibration will travel through their corrugations.

In this way the chamber knows its own shape continuously.

The gauges reveal that this self-knowledge extends beyond the present. Their engraved maps show roots before the roots arrive, basins before the stone beneath them subsides, seams in the engine before its casing opens. The needles do not predict events. They indicate the form toward which the inhabitant is slowly arranging itself. What appears to an observer as prophecy may simply be a blueprint proceeding at geological speed.

The images in the standing water serve another purpose. They are not memories of the installation, at least not memories in the nostalgic sense. The inhabitant has no attachment to the former works. It preserves the clean rails, bright lamps, and turning shafts because these represent a completed state: each surface sealed, each conduit continuous, every part occupied by function.

The absent workers are significant.

In every basin, stations stand unattended. Chairs are pushed back. Platforms remain empty. Control levers move by themselves or wait between positions. Occasionally a shadow crosses one of the reflected gantries, but it has never possessed a body, and it cannot be seen from a second angle. The installation remembers labor while omitting the laborer.

Perhaps that omission is the central fact of the thing.

The original machinery may have been built to regulate the vanished river: to pump it, heat it, extract something from it, or force it through the narrow southern pipes. Whatever the purpose, the works taught the river a new grammar. Flow became intake and discharge. Flood became cycle. Sediment became product. Obstruction became fault. Stone became housing. The river learned, not as an animal learns, but by being constrained for so long that the constraints persisted after the builders were gone.

What lives in the chamber may be the river after it was taught to work.

Its water is now scattered into condensation, shallow reservoirs, sap, coolant, and the intermittent black surge in the western trench. It no longer requires a continuous channel. It flows through phase changes and functions instead. It travels as moisture, vibration, mineral deposition, and instruction. Where it once carved whatever lay before it, it now shapes with apparent intention.

That intention is patient and profoundly inhuman. It repairs but does not restore. It preserves no distinction between organism and device. It does not desire expansion in the ordinary manner of roots or mold. Rather, it seeks completion—and its idea of completion is always changing slightly ahead of what it has made.

The copper-filled stones left by the black surge may be its only true offspring. Most are inert. A few grow warm when carried away. If placed near plumbing, they orient lengthwise with the nearest pipe. If left beside an electrical motor, their internal wire slowly unwinds without cracking the stone, forming coils too fine to see. Whether these are seeds, tools, or measurements is unclear. The chamber itself may not distinguish among those categories.

The intact western door appears to trouble the inhabitant.

Nothing grows across its face. The translucent fronds stop at the frame, crowded close but never touching the metal. Condensation outlines it with unusual precision. During each deep impact, every leaf in the chamber inclines toward the unseen machinery below—except those nearest the door. Those lean toward the wheel.

When the wheel is turned, the inhabitant reorganizes itself. Not immediately, and not only near the door. A pulse passes through the mineral films. Basins exchange their reflected views. Numbered leaves unfold in sealed cavities. The gauges advance by increments large enough to notice. The new seam in the central engine is less like damage than a suture opening.

The hotter breath that emerges carries no spores and no smell of oil. It contains minute flakes of a substance resembling mica, each etched with branching lines. On warm metal the flakes soften, join edge to edge, and begin forming another map of the chamber.

The map always includes the door.

It also includes a space beyond it.

That space grows larger with each completed turn of the wheel, though the visible door does not move. New concentric rooms appear in the diagram, all connected by narrow channels. Some resemble pumping galleries. Some resemble root cells. The outermost are unfinished, represented only by arcs and empty serial fields.

The chamber may therefore be no more than one organ of a much greater buried life. The roar below may be its metabolism, continuing in levels where machinery and geology have become indistinguishable. Or the roar may belong to something older still, with the chamber serving as a sensory growth extended upward toward rain, daylight, and the hands capable of turning the wheel.

The inhabitant does not seem conscious of visitors as persons. It notices their heat, weight, moisture, tools, and rhythms. It notices the turning of the wheel most of all. Those who remain too long report that the leaves around them gradually adjust into straight edges and rectangular recesses, outlining their bodies as if planning where they might fit.

Nothing attacks. Nothing reaches.

The chamber merely begins, with immense care, to decide what they are for.
