The people who live here are, above all, maintainers.

Some work at presses, saws, vats, and drying racks, but nearly everyone knows how to clear a grate, sound a piling, splice a handrail, or recognize water backing up where it should not. A blocked channel is noticed as quickly as smoke in a dry town. People interrupt meals to lift pennywort from a culvert or wedge a loose board before the next shift crosses it. Even those whose occupations are clerical or domestic carry small practical competencies: how to tie a punt under rising water, which roots may be trimmed, where to shut a steam line, how to read the flood cuts on the nearest post.

They tend to be compactly dressed. Loose cloth is dangerous near belts and shafts, and heavy clothing never dries, so workers wear close trousers or wrapped skirts, sleeveless shirts, aprons treated with oil or wax, and soft shoes that grip wet timber. Hair is bound back. Mill crews have fiber dust in their cuffs, grease in the whorls of their fingers, and pale marks across their shoulders from carrying damp bundles. Canal workers smell of peat water and crushed stems. Boiler hands emerge into the green shade looking briefly overexposed, their faces flushed by furnace heat.

No one is entirely dry. Skin shines. Shirts darken between buildings and lighten again near the drying sheds. Each doorway has pegs for rain capes, hats woven from split leaf, and towels that themselves feel faintly damp. People learn not to hurry on the walks. Their gait is short, balanced, and sure, with one hand often free for a cable, trunk, or rail. Visitors look down at their feet; residents look ahead, trusting the boards while watching for loads, low pipes, and changes in the water.

The loudness has shaped them. They speak near one another rather than across a room. A hand laid on an arm precedes a warning. Pointing, chin movements, raised fingers, and a small vocabulary of mill gestures supplement speech. Certain messages are passed by striking railings or pipes: a repeated knock beneath a floor, a tool tapped against a flume, a particular rhythm from the dock. Away from machinery, their voices may seem unexpectedly quiet. In homes after a shift, people do not always seek music. The drip of roofs and the soft traffic of water are enough.

Their hearing is practical and selective. An outsider hears a single industrial roar; a resident hears the number-three pump laboring, a belt beginning to fray, the change in a saw’s pitch as it enters a knot, rain thickening on the western roofs. Sleep is not disturbed by ordinary machinery. What wakes the town is its absence. If a pump stops at night, lamps appear almost at once along the walks.

Work is divided less by rigid profession than by zones of familiarity. Press crews understand pressure, temperature, and the moods of oily fruit. Retting workers judge plant fiber by touch and smell, reaching into dark ponds to test whether a husk is ready. Sawyers read wet grain. Dyers carry permanently tinted nails. Engine fitters are valued not because machines are rare, but because every machine has been altered so many times that its true design exists only in memory. A mechanic may know that a particular flywheel must be coaxed past one stubborn point on cold mornings, or that a pump described on paper as identical to its neighbor takes a different packing because its shaft settled with the peat twenty years ago.

There are also tree surveyors, root cutters, canal dredgers, roof gardeners, cistern keepers, fire wardens, and people who inspect the undersides of structures from punts. These are not marginal occupations. The annual examination of a load-bearing banyan can concern an entire block. Inspectors climb among prop roots with measuring cords and metal probes, marking rot, compression, and new growth. Their reports determine where walls will move, which pipes will be rerouted, and whether a family must surrender part of a room to a thickening limb.

Older residents possess especially valuable maps of change. They remember channels now hidden beneath workshops, former flood routes, buried footings, and which tree first belonged to which courtyard before its roots crossed three property lines. They are consulted when a foundation shifts or brown water appears where no drain is recorded. Their knowledge is often given in stories rather than diagrams: the year the dye shed leaned, the storm when the north sluice jammed, the season a furnace was carried plank by plank around a growing trunk. In a town always being altered, memory is a form of surveying.

Children grow up close to work without being permitted everywhere within it. Bright paint and woven barriers mark forbidden machinery. They learn first to swim, then to pole a small punt, though adults insist that swimming is no protection beneath buildings where current, roots, and pipework can trap a body. They run on the higher walks, balance along root edges, fish with thread near sluice outfalls, and collect washers, seed pods, and scraps of colored fiber. Their games imitate the town: miniature mills turned by gutter water, leaf boats loaded with bark, little bridges rebuilt after every rain.

They know the trees almost as relatives. A child may give directions by a banyan’s chambers or a palm’s old lightning scar rather than by a house number. Certain roots are hiding places, meeting places, or shortcuts small enough to admit only the young. As they grow, those passages close behind them—not only because their bodies enlarge, but because the roots do too.

Homes are densely occupied but not sealed off. Cooking, mending, washing, and child care spill onto platforms shared by several households. Windows are broad and shuttered rather than glazed. Floors have removable sections for inspecting water and pilings below. Furniture is light enough to lift during flood warnings, with shelves and sleeping platforms fixed high on walls. Valued papers, medicine, tools, and photographs are kept in tins or waxed boxes.

Cleanliness here does not mean freedom from mud, stains, mold, or plant matter. It means managed water, scrubbed preparation boards, covered food, clear drains, aired bedding, and no mosquito larvae in a vessel. People wash often, though never with the illusion that washing will remove the town from them. Soap mingles with grease, tannin, smoke, and leaf odor. In the clinic, foot injuries, infected splinters, heat rash, belt abrasions, and respiratory trouble from fiber dust are more common than dramatic illness. The clinic staff are strict about masks in drying rooms and about even small cuts acquired in canal water.

Meals are built from what can be grown in wet shade, brought by punt from the plantations, or obtained as useful by-products of processing. There are steamed starches, greens, plantain, breadfruit, coconut, river fish when available, preserved vegetables, and strong sauces that stand up to the dulled appetite of humid heat. Shift food is portable and wrapped in leaf or packed into lidded tins. Tea and bitter infusions are drunk throughout the day. The best kitchens are not necessarily the largest, but those positioned where smoke can rise through a gap in the canopy without drifting into a drying loft.

The market occupies a widened set of platforms rather than a square. It sells cooked food beside rope, lamp parts, seedlings, pump seals, woven screens, medicines, and pieces of salvaged metal whose original purpose no longer matters. Nothing sound is thrown away. A corroded tank becomes roofing; broken rail becomes a lintel; worn belting becomes hinge material or protective edging. The distinction between household economy and industrial supply is thin.

Property itself is understood as layered. A person may own a room but not the walkway outside it, the root passing through it, or the channel underneath. A family may tend a tree whose main trunk stands in another yard. Mill pipes cross domestic walls. Neighbors inherit obligations along with dwellings: access for repairs, responsibility for a shared sluice, the duty to open lower shutters when floodwater needs to pass. Disputes are less often about fixed boundary lines than about obstruction, shade, flow, and weight. Who failed to clear the screen? Whose new platform turned the current? Who may harvest fruit from a limb that begins in one courtyard and hangs over another?

This makes the residents attentive to consequence. They do not romanticize cooperation; they practice it because water makes private negligence public. There are quarrels, evasions, and old grudges, but a flood alarm suspends them. During storms, crews form according to location rather than kin or trade. One household lifts goods while another opens passages through its ground floor. Mill workers divert flumes. Children and older people move to high rooms. Someone makes rounds to ensure that no one has fastened shut a wall meant to let water through.

Celebrations occupy whatever space can be temporarily made. A conveyor is stopped, a loading deck swept, screens hung against spray, and lamps strung between roots. Music here favors percussion and close, carrying instruments that can survive dampness, though singers wait for the mills to quiet. Weddings and memorials often include the planting of something structurally useful: a shade tree, a stand of cane for bank weaving, a vine that will eventually screen a house. The dead are remembered less by stone monuments—which settle and mildew—than by plaques on durable timber, named bridges, planted groves, and repairs made in their honor.

Not everyone loves the town. Young people sometimes leave by the levee road, wanting dry walls, straight streets, and work that does not begin with checking the weather. Some return because dry settlements feel exposed and strangely silent. Others return because their skills are most legible here. A person who has spent years understanding water under floors may find ordinary ground inert, offering nothing to read.

Those who remain do not imagine themselves living in untouched nature. The greenery is cropped, surveyed, diverted, harvested, bolted to, and argued over. Nor do they think of the mills as separate from it. The engines eat plant matter; the plants hold the engines above water; waste heat dries fiber and warms rooms; ash feeds certain beds; wastewater, after settling, passes through reed gardens. The relationship is productive, intimate, and sometimes brutal. Trees are protected and cut. Canals are living and dredged. Growth is welcomed until it blocks a sluice.

The banyans are inhabitants in almost a legal sense. They have records, inspections, histories of intervention, and claims on surrounding space. Individual trees outlive businesses and households. Their roots remember old floor levels. Their canopies determine which roofs grow sedge and which remain bare enough to dry. They provide structure, but they also impose renovations without consent. People speak of them as temperaments: one is generous with shade, another “walks” too quickly through a wall, another drinks heavily and lowers the soil around it.

The quieter residents live within these civic bodies: orchids, pepper vines, ferns, mosses, sedges, water spinach, reeds, and the persistent pennywort that crews remove by the puntload. None are wilderness. Some are crops, some volunteers, some tolerated squatters, some maintenance problems. Their populations rise and fall with shade, heat, runoff, and work schedules.

Animal life keeps to the margins of human intention. Fish gather beneath sluices. Lizards flatten themselves against boiler brick. Frogs call from reed beds after rain. Mosquito-eating fish patrol tanks that are inspected like rooms. Birds cross the opening overhead, visible for a moment in the green light, and seldom descend. The true profusion is botanical and human, not zoological.

Together, the inhabitants form a society organized around continuance rather than permanence. They expect every object to change: timber softens, iron blooms with rust, channels silt, roots thicken, peat sinks, water rises. Competence lies not in preventing change but in meeting it early, while it can still be answered with a new plank, a cleared grate, a shifted pipe, or an extra line of pilings.

They live close enough to hear one another’s pumps and smell one another’s cooking. They know the same flood marks and wake to the same whistles. Their town does not offer privacy from the systems that sustain it. Beneath every room is shared water; above every roof is shared shade. What lives there is therefore not quite a collection of separate households, nor a factory settlement wrapped in vegetation, nor a garden that happens to contain machinery. It is a population of people, plants, and working structures trained into mutual adjustment—restless in small ways, stable in the aggregate, and always listening for a change in the flow.
