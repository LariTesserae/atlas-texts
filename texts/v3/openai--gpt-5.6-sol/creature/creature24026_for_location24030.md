What lives here lives slowly.

At first the basin’s inhabitants seem to be the obvious plants: the round-crowned trees in the courts, the broad-leaved crops standing in flooded beds, the moss on shaded walls. Yet none behaves as vegetation does in a country of animals. Nothing armors itself with thorns. Few fruits are sweet or brightly colored. Flowers are small, pale, and nearly scentless. They open beneath the clouds and close again untouched.

Many fertilize themselves before their petals unfold. Others cast fine pollen into the saturated air, where it settles only a short distance away. Ferns release spores that drift across pools like dust on glass. The marsh plants reproduce by runners, extending joint by joint through the mud. What appears to be a meadow may be one plant repeated thousands of times, its buried stems crossing old property lines and passing beneath causeways.

The trees are likewise less solitary than they look. Their exposed roots descend into cracks, but finer roots travel sideways through the thin, placed soil, joining buried grafts left from an earlier age. A court containing seven trees may hold only one living organism, divided above ground into seven quiet crowns. When one trunk rots, another thickens. Saplings rise not from seeds but from roots following old garden walls.

Water is their chief carrier. Seeds are flat, sealed, and scarcely heavier than the pools that hold them. Spores cling to droplets. Broken stems remain alive for weeks and root wherever a channel strands them. The city’s conduits have become veins of dispersal: each slow overflow bears fragments from one terrace to the next. The plants still follow the hydraulic plan of vanished gardeners, so that cultivated forms persist in rectangles while wild forms collect beyond the final sluices.

In the clear cisterns lives a skin too thin to see except when the light lowers. Green cells coat the stone below the waterline; colorless filaments occupy the pores above it. They feed on mineral seepage, dissolved light, and the remnants of leaves. Slender fungi thread through old roof turf and the dark stains left by mats. Their fruiting bodies are soft gray cups that emerge after prolonged wetness and collapse back into paste without ever being grazed.

Decay is correspondingly gentle. There are no jaws to tear a fallen leaf, no larvae to open timber, no worms to churn soil. A leaf lies whole for months while films spread through it from the veins outward. Wood softens from within. Clay jars hold sediments made not of trampled refuse but of pollen, mineral dust, and the transparent husks of algae. Life dismantles things molecule by molecule, preserving their shapes long after their substance has begun to change.

The old crops remain, though their usefulness has become meaningless. Thick corms multiply beneath flooded beds until they press against the clay lining. Gourds trail over low walls and produce hard, bitter vessels filled with a few viable seeds. Fruiting shrubs carry dull green clusters that fall directly beneath them. These plants bear signs of former selection—oversized roots, straight fibers, leaves too broad for their stems—but without hands to divide and replant them, they are gradually becoming smaller, tougher, and more capable of continuing alone.

Nothing contests them except place. Moss ends where stone receives too much light. Reeds stop at water a finger too deep. Trees cannot cross the bare basalt because no soil forms quickly enough before their seedlings fail. Thus every living patch has a clean boundary. The openness of the basin is not maintained by grazing or fire but by the youth of the ground. Much of it is still simply unavailable to roots.

If the city itself has an inhabitant in any larger sense, it is this dispersed green continuity: not a forest, not a garden, but an old cultivation slowly teaching itself to live without cultivators. It occupies walls, beds, channels, roof turf, and deposited soil while leaving the architecture legible. It does not conquer the city. It uses it.

The most active life is hidden at the meeting of water and stone. There, minute colonies draw substances from fresh basalt and leave faint pale margins around their cells. Over centuries they roughen a surface enough for darker films to adhere; the films catch dust; moss follows. They are the basin’s first soil-makers, proceeding across the young mountain at a pace almost indistinguishable from stillness.

At dusk the visible plants become silhouettes, but these submerged and buried lives continue. Roots exchange water through old grafts. Spores swell in masonry seams. Algal skins release tiny bubbles that cling beneath the rims of pools. Fungal threads advance through a wooden peg by less than the width of a hair.

No single creature presides over the empty city. Its inhabitants are colonies, clones, films, and patient networks—lives without footsteps, hunger cries, nests, or eyes. They have inherited every street and court, yet they scarcely move beyond the exact conditions that sustain them. Their presence is measured not by motion but by persistence: a green edge on black stone, a root following a channel, a pale flower opening into air that will never answer it.
