What lives there is not a collection of creatures so much as a single abundance that has never learned to divide itself into individuals.

At its smallest, it lives in the almost-water.

In the film beneath a shard of basalt are transparent cells flattened thinner than frost. They wake for minutes at a time, whenever sunlight loosens the ice above them. In those minutes they drink dissolved stone, exchange fragments of chemistry with their neighbors, and rebuild the glassy envelopes that will hold them through the next freeze. Some have not divided in centuries. Their lives consist of brief awakenings separated by long intervals that, to them, may be no intervals at all.

They are the first mouths of the plateau. Iron, sulfur, phosphorus, the rare salts trapped in new rock—these pass through them before entering anything larger. Around each viable fracture they form a dark, adhesive skin. Dust catches there. Spores catch in the dust. Moisture catches among the spores. What appears to be dirt is a layered congregation of sleepers, miners, vessels, eggs, and the remains of membranes too useful to be discarded.

From these skins rise the vanes.

A vane is not one organism. Its black anchoring pad is a parliament of burrowers and mineral-eaters surrounding a central bladder of brine. Its pale blade is grown by photosynthetic threads whose lineages may differ from one finger’s breadth to the next. Beneath the reflective surface lie chains of tiny, contractile animals. They tighten when warmed, turning the whole structure toward the light. Other inhabitants patrol the interior, grazing away infections and sealing punctures with their own dissolved bodies.

When grazers hollow a vane from below, they do not quite kill it. They convert it.

The six-legged herds arrive as a blackness moving under silver. Each grazer is low and broad, protected by overlapping mineral plates polished to mirror brightness away from its body. Six limbs cling to the undersurface; a seventh appendage, soft and rasping, moves continually across the living tissue. Their eyes are buried under their armor and perceive little more than heat, charge, and the rhythm of the herd around them. Their true sense organ is the column itself. A grazer separated from its neighbors becomes directionless, circling until it freezes. Packed among thousands, it can follow a mineral gradient finer than any solitary animal could detect.

They travel shoulder to shoulder because each body completes the insulation of the next. Along the outside of the migration, specialized individuals turn their armored backs outward and die there when the nights grow too cold. The column later consumes them. Nothing marks their deaths except a momentary roughness in the flow.

Behind the grazers come the breath-bearers: pale creatures swollen around chambers of warm gas. They walk delicately on black hooked feet and insert their long mouths into the emptied vanes. A breath-bearer spends much of its life making one exhalation. Deep within its body, slow colonies ferment sugars and release heat. The creature gathers that heat, concentrates it, then empties itself into a hollowed plant chamber. Its collapse is total. For a time it hangs like a translucent cloth from the opening.

Inside the warmed cavity, it lays larvae.

The larvae do not resemble their parent. They are wet, red, and almost without boundaries, flowing over one another in the dark. They clean the vane’s inner walls, eat the grazers’ residues, and weave a new membrane across the cavity. Eventually some become the contracting fibers of the restored vane. Some become winged reproductive dust. A very few inflate, tear themselves free, and begin the long work of accumulating a breath.

Thus the same hollow may be leaf, pasture, lung, nursery, and animal, depending on the season.

The cylindrical trunks among the vanes are more communal still. Their outer walls are built by ring-shaped organisms that spend their adult lives gripping one another. Each ring secretes upward and feeds downward. None can survive alone; removed from the column, it opens reflexively until its fluids freeze. Together, millions of them make a tower. Inside hangs a warm lining, pleated like the interior of a throat. Condensed water runs through those folds and is swallowed by valve-creatures stationed at intervals down the shaft.

The valves are blind, soft, and long-lived. They know the world as pressure. A drop arriving from above causes one to contract; that contraction sends a pulse to the valve below. In this way water descends not as a stream but as a message, passed from body to body into buried reservoirs. During drought, the sequence slows. During cloudbursts, the whole trunk trembles with swallowing.

Some trunks are male in winter and female in summer. Others have not reproduced in ten thousand years because reproduction is only one of their functions, and seldom the most urgent. They are chimneys, cisterns, organs of ventilation, and vertical roads for creatures that never touch the outer air.

Among them move the ribbed walkers.

From far away, one may seem to be a low hill striped with shadow. Up close, there is still no obvious place where the walker ends. Its feet are narrow living pillars, each governed by its own cluster of nerves and its own small circulatory colony. They do not always agree on direction. A young walker shudders and pivots for decades before its feet settle into a common rhythm.

Above the feet rises a cage of nested ribs. Ice fills the gaps. Red moss covers the ice, darkening or paling to regulate warmth. At the center, protected by many layers of inhabited structure, hangs a slow organ like a cluster of stone fruits. This is not precisely a brain. It stores tensions, chemical inclinations, and crystalline records of old routes. Decisions emerge from it gradually, sometimes taking years to reach the outer limbs.

The walker lifts a foot. While that foot is in the air, generations pass among the mites living beneath its ribs.

Its back holds warm pools rimmed with cartilage. Filament groves grow there, fed by minerals drawn from the animal’s blood. Small wingless fliers swim through the denser air immediately above the pools. Pale predators hide among the filaments and spring upward to catch them. Parasites tunnel into the moss, but the tunnels channel meltwater, so they are tolerated. Their eggs feed another species that cleans the walker’s respiratory folds. No resident is merely passenger or pest. Even disease tends to acquire a task if it remains long enough.

Old walkers become increasingly asymmetrical beneath the weight of their acquired worlds. One flank may carry a cold forest, the other a chain of warm ponds. Their routes bend accordingly. In extreme age, some stop moving altogether. Their feet root into fractures, their ribs open, and colonies from the interior spread outward. Such a walker may remain alive as a stationary landscape for millennia, continuing to circulate blood through what outsiders would call a carcass.

The creatures of the air are thinner than they look.

Each broad flier consists mostly of wing: two translucent membranes kept apart by a lace of conductive veins. The body at the center is little more than a mouth, a reproductive knot, and a series of gas bladders. They feed on bright dust, airborne cells, electrical charge, and one another’s discarded scales. Their wings are never wholly still. Even in sleep they make minute corrections, borrowing lift from the rising air.

A single flier is nearly silent. The pressure felt across the plateau comes from uncountable wings adjusting against their neighbors. No individual knows the chord. Each hears only the pitches immediately around it and changes the tension of its membranes in response. Yet the adjustment propagates outward through the flock until millions hum in relationship.

The chord is weather, courtship, navigation, and census.

When food is plentiful, high layers sing sharp and thin while lower layers answer in broad pulses. Before electrical storms, subsonic notes pass through the air, too low for many ground creatures to hear but strong enough to shake spores loose from their housings. During migrations, the chord develops moving hollows—regions of deliberate silence into which distant fliers turn. Seen from below, these silences appear as lanes of violet-black sky opening and closing among the wings.

The longest-lived fliers may remain aloft for years, but they do not remain unchanged. Small attendants land upon them and replace damaged wing tissue. Algae bloom within their veins. Their reproductive knots detach and join with those of other fliers, forming temporary many-bodied creatures that drift together until a storm tears them apart. The oldest become heavy with iron. They leave the sustaining currents and climb toward the central spires.

Their final ascent can take months.

At the spire, a flier clasps the conductive shell and does not release it. Its membranes dry and shred in the wind. Its veins harden. Clinging organisms bridge the gaps between its body and those already fused below. In time it ceases to be distinguishable from the tower, except perhaps as a faint radial pattern in the black material.

It has not become dead matter. It has become architecture, though nothing has built it.

Lightning runs first through the mineral veins of the accumulated fliers. The charge branches downward through shells, fungal cords, nerve-roots, buried water, and the conductive remains of migrations long past. Creatures far away register the strike as taste, warmth, muscular impulse, or dream. Eggs loosen. Valves turn. Herds alter course. A walker’s lifted foot begins, at last, to descend.

Beneath all of them live the cable forests.

They have no single body plan. Some strands are roots with nerve tissue threaded through them. Some are nerves wrapped in fungal insulation. Some are chains of tiny animals gripping tail to mouth, their fluids carrying ions from one body into the next. They grow toward current. They prune themselves away from silence. Where two cable systems meet, they may fuse, recoil, infect one another, or spend centuries exchanging pulses before becoming compatible.

Within the network move signals too slow to be perceived as signals by quicker minds. A change in charge may travel the length of a valley in a week and cross the plateau in several seasons. Along the way it is transformed by every colony it enters. News of a flood becomes a command to hatch, then a shift in sugar production, then a muscle contraction closing a vent, then a darkening of surface tissue that can be seen from another mountain.

No message arrives unchanged. Accuracy is less important than continuation.

In certain warm cavities, the network thickens into organs. There the refiners live in stacked communities around reservoirs of brine. Their forms are specialized almost beyond recognition as living things. Sheets exude wax. Tubes fill the wax with gas. Jawed threads carve branching channels through it. Crystal-bodied colonies plate those channels with metal salts. Egg clusters are inserted, eaten, resealed, dissolved, and replaced. The chambers pulse with slow manufacture.

Nothing made there has a sole purpose. A resin may first seal a larval chamber, later serve as insulation around a nerve-root, and centuries afterward be digested into fuel. Metallic crystals guide charge but also deter grazers. Sugars feed the chamber and control the freezing point of its fluids. The entire underground economy depends not on ownership or intention but on proximity: each organism produces what its chemistry compels it to produce, and because the neighbors have evolved around that compulsion, the result resembles planning.

Predators participate in this planning.

The curtain creatures across the passes begin as thin colonies on opposite cliff walls. Over centuries they grow toward each other, trailing sensitive cords through cracks and snow. When the curtains finally meet, they make a porous living barrier. Most small migrants pass unharmed. Larger bodies press against the mesh and trigger constriction. The curtain tightens by degrees, sometimes taking days to close around prey. Digestion can last a season. While it feeds, the pass is sealed, redirecting entire herds into neighboring valleys.

The water mimics are softer. They spread blue, reflective membranes over shallow basins and maintain a convincing skin of melt. Beneath the false surface, radially arranged mouths wait around a warm central stomach. A drinker’s touch does not always provoke them. They sample salts in the saliva first. Creatures carrying useful spores may be allowed to drink and leave; mineral-rich migrants are more likely to feel the pool adhere.

Echo predators are perhaps the least bodily of them. Their feeding chambers line hollows in the cliffs with velvety tissue. They shape incoming sound, strengthening certain notes and swallowing others. A flier hears what seems to be a distant gathering of its own kind and enters. The chamber narrows. The echo changes. By the time the animal recognizes the error, its own wingbeat has become the lure for the next.

Even these hunters feed more than themselves. Their wastes mineralize the cliffs. Their abandoned chambers shelter eggs. Their interruptions divide migrations into pulses the plateau’s other systems have come to require.

And beneath predator and prey, beneath root, river, and conductive reef, there may live something for which none of those categories are adequate.

It has no enclosing skin. Its active parts are separated by mountains. Some of its tissues fly; some sleep inside rock; some are born only during floods and vanish when the water recedes. Its electrical impulses take months to pass. Its memories are stored in communal ice, in the branching of cable forests, in compressed migration paths, in the mineral composition of spires, and in the angles at which the silver vanes hold themselves to the sun.

It does not command the creatures of the plateau. It is made of their responses to one another.

If it thinks, its thoughts are not symbols but events: a continent-wide wing rotation, a reversal of underground current, the opening of a buried chamber after nine hundred years. A drought may be an ache within it. Lightning may be appetite. The long summer flood may serve as breath, digestion, reproduction, or all three in different regions.

It may never know itself as one being. Self-knowledge would have little use here.

Still, at times the plateau behaves as though attending.

A shadow crosses one distant slope. Vanes darken in answer. Fliers shift height. Air descends. Frost thickens over a buried river, increasing its pressure. Walkers on an ancient route halt with their feet lifted. Far below, valves close in sequence. The change reaches the black spires after many days, and a pale static begins to gather around their tips.

Nothing issued an order. Nothing stands outside the process and understands it.

Yet the whole bright country has turned, by the smallest possible degree, toward what comes next.
