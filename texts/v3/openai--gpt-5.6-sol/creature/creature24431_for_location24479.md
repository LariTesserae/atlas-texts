At first the inhabitants seem to be another class of fittings.

They crouch beneath lifting gates, brace themselves between pillars, hang from ladders with baskets on their backs. In the uncertain light their waxed capes resemble patches over cracked walls. A line of workers hauling a rope might be mistaken for a counterweight mechanism until one of them slips, curses, and the whole line laughs without releasing its strain.

Only nearby do they resolve into people.

They are generally short-legged and strongly made, though not uniformly so. The basin punishes height. Its ceilings descend without warning, its stairs turn tightly, and its safest passages are often those left between an old arch and the support built to prevent its collapse. Children grow up stooping, climbing and balancing. Adults stand with knees slightly bent even in open courts, as if expecting the floor to tilt beneath them. Their feet are broad from generations spent barefoot or in thin-soled climbing shoes. Their palms are webbed with callus. Most have a ridge of scar tissue across one shoulder where ropes, poles and stone baskets habitually rest.

Their skin has the colors of things kept from daylight: pale gray, yellow-white, old ivory, sometimes deep brown made ashen by lime dust. Soot settles in every crease. Faces are wiped clean more often than hands, not from vanity but because the movements of a mouth and eyes must be seen at close range. Eyebrows are often shaved or clipped so that water does not run into the eyes while working in rain. Hair is kept short, braided flat against the skull, or sealed under fitted caps of felt and waxed cloth. Loose hair is a danger around wheels.

Their pupils remain wide in lamplight. They are not blind in darkness, but neither do they possess any miraculous sight. What distinguishes them is the discipline of their hearing. They notice vibration before sound becomes audible. A sleeper wakes when a familiar drain changes pitch. A mason can lay two fingers against a parapet and tell whether a loaded cart is crossing three levels above or a gate is opening somewhere below.

They seldom call themselves by one collective name. In different circumstances they are the Builders, the Hearers, the Braced, the People Between, or simply those of a particular stair, kiln, ramp or cistern. To say where one lives is not to name a district. It is to recite a route:

Down from the seven-mouthed vent, across the bridge that is removed at second bell, beneath the cracked blue gutter, behind the warm wall.

Their personal names are similarly unstable. A person has a spoken name for close conversation, a hammer rhythm for work, a cord-pattern for records and often a private vibration made by humming through the brow. The last is shared only with family, lovers and those trusted to find them in total darkness.

Forehead-touching is more than a way to overcome noise. It is their most intimate manner of speech. Brow to brow, with hands resting on the other’s neck or shoulders, they speak softly enough that much of the voice travels through bone. Words become pressure, resonance and breath. An apology delivered this way cannot easily be shouted or performed for bystanders. Promises made forehead to forehead are considered structurally binding: not sacred in the manner of an oath before a god, but load-bearing. To break one is to remove a support while pretending the weight has gone elsewhere.

They do shout, of course. They are a noisy, impatient people, quick with warnings and insults. Yet loud speech is regarded as public material. A cry belongs partly to everyone who must respond to it. False alarms are punished severely. So is using a recognized danger rhythm as music.

Most communication is tactile or patterned. Workers tug messages along ropes. Lovers tap through adjoining walls. Parents summon children by striking pipes with spoons. In crowded galleries, people pass news hand to hand by squeezing fingers in agreed sequences. Deafness is neither shameful nor rare, especially among hammer crews, but a person who loses hearing is usually retrained in watching water cups, suspended plumb bobs and the behavior of dust in cracks. The deaf often become excellent surveyors because they distrust the obvious voice of a wall and attend to subtler movement.

Children are taught that every structure has at least three sounds: the sound it makes when resting, the sound it makes while bearing weight, and the sound it makes shortly before failure. Their earliest games involve guessing which cup conceals a tapping beetle, tracing footsteps through a floor, or crossing a room without touching the guide ropes. They build little vaults from broken tile and load them until they fall. Adults do not stop these collapses. A child who has never watched a model fail is considered dangerously educated.

Kinship follows access as much as blood. A household may include siblings, apprentices, cistern partners, former spouses, an elderly signaler and whoever possesses the neighboring ladder rights. They share heat, water allotments and emergency obligations. Such households divide and recombine whenever a route closes. If a collapse cuts a cell off from its kiln, its inhabitants may become members of another family by winter simply because they now sleep against the same warm wall.

Marriage is often marked by joining routes rather than rooms. Each partner leads the other from their birthplace to the proposed dwelling without a lamp. If both can complete the path, they exchange pieces of guide cord. If one cannot, the route is practiced until it belongs to both. The ceremony may take weeks.

Property is carried in memory and witnessed in use. A person may own no walls but possess the right to cross twelve of them, to draw six jars after rainfall, to anchor a bridge at a certain ring, and to receive warmth from a bakery flue before dawn. These rights are inherited, traded, wagered and occasionally stolen. Theft may consist of moving a ladder by one landing or changing the rhythm announcing a freight platform. The most powerful families are not those with the largest chambers, but those whose passage rights form uninterrupted routes through many layers.

Their rulers, if they can be called that, are coordinators of weight and water. No one commands the whole basin. Authority gathers temporarily around emergencies. During a wall failure, the person who best understands that wall may order elders, guild chiefs and strangers alike. Once the braces hold, that authority ends. Attempts to make emergency command permanent are remembered as a form of architectural madness: a person mistaking themselves for the entire load.

Their laws are kept in rhythms, cords and carved sections of wall. A legal hearing often involves less testimony than reconstruction. People walk the disputed route, strike the relevant supports, measure water stains and inspect old knots. Truth is something that should fit the surviving structure. An account contradicted by wear marks or resonance is treated with suspicion, however eloquently spoken.

They have no clear division between engineer, priest and physician. All three listen for concealed distress.

A healer sounds the chest as a surveyor sounds a floor. Splints are miniature buttresses. Swellings are called trapped water; fevers, kiln heat; old injuries, buried courses. Conversely, a cracked vault may be said to have a bad hip, a tired back or breath caught behind its ribs. This is not merely metaphor. They understand bodies and buildings through the same habits of pressure, circulation and repair.

Their devotions are directed less toward creators than toward continuance. Before opening a sealed chamber, workers place a lamp at the threshold and wait through one full burning of its wick, listening. Before demolishing a pillar, they touch it with bare palms and name every visible thing it supports. The naming is practical, but it also resembles mourning.

The dead remain useful, though not casually so. Flesh is wrapped and placed for a time in warm drying chambers. Bone may later be incorporated into measuring tools, flute pipes, toggles or handles, but only with prior consent. Many families set the small bones of the ear into wall niches beside their lamps. There they are said to continue listening for the household. Larger bones are more often sealed into fresh masonry with the dead person’s cord-name impressed nearby. Thus ancestry is quite literally structural. A family can point to a buttress and say, “Our grandmother is in the third repair.”

Food is precious and monotonous. Grain and dried meal arrive from storerooms whose supply routes are older than any living record. Bakers stretch dough with rendered fat, mineral salts and pale pastes skimmed from certain cisterns after careful testing. The translucent films are officially treated as contamination, yet poor districts cultivate selected strains in covered blue-glazed troughs. Some films are harmless; some numb the mouth; some produce fever or leave hexagonal sores in the gut. Cistern keepers guard their practical knowledge fiercely.

Animals used for wool, hide and grease are so few that each has a name, genealogy and schedule of shared ownership. A single shaggy, blind ewe may belong by hours to six households: its warmth to one, milk to another, dung to a kiln crew, wool to a curtain-maker, eventual hide to the cistern guild. Killing such an animal without collective consent can start a feud lasting generations.

The pale shell-things in the drowned foundations are not loved, but neither are they considered vermin. Drain workers know individuals by missing limbs, shell scars and favored grilles. They give them names such as Nine-Toes, Lamp-Biter and Little Chancellor. Their appearance during rain helps reveal which lower channels have begun to flow. A sudden absence is more alarming than an abundance.

The winged scavengers inspire a more complicated reverence. Because they come from an unknown elsewhere, some consider them messengers; because they eat refuse and occasionally carry off infants or small animals, others consider them only hazards with valuable bodies. When one circles a kiln, work slows. Everyone watches to see which chimney it chooses. Its cry is imitated only at funerals and only outdoors, under cloud.

As for the mortar-eater, it occupies the place that wolves, ghosts and plague might occupy in another country. No one agrees whether it is an animal. Some say it is a colony of minute jaws moving together. Some say the clicking is produced by lime crystallizing under pressure. Some believe it is the first truly native life of the basin: a creature not imported, kept or inherited, but born from masonry itself.

Children dare one another to sleep beside red handprints. Builders leave bowls of loose mortar in sealed wall cavities and return days later to inspect them. Occasionally the powder is disturbed in patterns resembling tracks. Occasionally it is gone. Once, according to a widely repeated account, a wall marked for infestation began clicking in the rhythm of the warning used by the crew outside it. No one entered that corridor again, but neither was it sealed. To silence it without understanding would have broken the oldest law.

The rains are changing the people as surely as the country.

Older inhabitants distrust water falling freely from above. They cover their heads, close their mouths and hurry beneath shelter even in a drizzle. Children stand in open courts and let it strike their faces. Some have begun floating chips of ceramic in new streams and racing beside them. They collect the first rounded pebbles as curiosities. Young masons carve channels that curve instead of running square to the walls. Their elders call this surrender to water; the young call it listening ahead.

A new kind of worker is appearing: neither builder nor surveyor, but a student of flow. These people trace wet routes through the layers, stain water with soot to learn where it emerges, and argue that certain collapses should not be repaired. They speak of giving the rain permanent passages. They imagine channels no hand must empty and basins that clean themselves by overflowing.

To traditional builders, this borders on heresy. The world, they say, is maintained by labor. Anything that moves without being hauled, turned, raised or released is merely a machine whose operators have been hidden.

Yet even they now sleep to the murmur below.

The deepest change is difficult to see because it is happening in their language. Once, every hollow was a room, every rise a roof, every descent a stair or failure. Now people have begun using words for bank, streambed, slope and shore. They speak of water choosing. They speak of a tower settling as though it were becoming a hill rather than ceasing to be a tower.

The inhabitants are still builders. Their bodies, families, laws and prayers all assume that existence is something braced by hand against collapse. But the youngest among them are learning another possibility: that a place can continue making itself after the workers have gone; that not every new form is an error; that ruin, water and time may also be inhabitants.

In the dark, they press their foreheads to the walls and listen to that possibility moving underneath.
