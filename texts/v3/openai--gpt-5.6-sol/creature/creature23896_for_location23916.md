At first it seems that nothing belongs to the basin more completely than the stones. The crabs shelter among them, the fish imitate them, the brittle stars reach from beneath them, but all these creatures appear temporary: visitors holding themselves against a world that would prefer to move them on.

The basin’s true resident is harder to distinguish.

It lives below the visible floor, though not by burrowing into clay. It occupies the floor’s looseness—the interlocking dark between cobbles, the narrow chambers left where three rounded stones fail to meet. In those spaces, where the current becomes many small currents and the weight of the basin is divided among countless points of contact, it folds itself and waits.

An adult is no longer than a forearm, though most are smaller. Seen in open water, which is rare, it resembles neither fish nor crab for more than a moment. Its body is low and jointed, enclosed in overlapping plates that flex without gaping. The front is broad, the rear tapers, and the many short limbs beneath it move in sequence, passing a ripple from head to tail. Two limbs are heavier than the others and end in blunt hooks suited less for fighting than for fastening. When the current bears down, these hooks enter cracks in stone. The animal flattens until water passes over it as over another worn surface.

Its shell does not have one color. Young ones are translucent gray, with their pale organs faintly visible through the plates. Later, mineral stains enter the shell. Some become basalt-black with a blue sheen. Some take on the green of submerged slabs. Those living near granite grow speckled, while old individuals may be veined with white from lime and shell dust. The color is partly the animal’s own and partly what has accumulated upon it. Bacteria darken the seams between its plates. Minute crusts spread over its back. In the oldest, a low brown skin of algae may survive in the shelter behind the head.

This growth is not cleaned away. It is camouflage, but also more than camouflage. The resident carries a little of the basin’s surface life with it. When it settles, the films on its back continue feeding. When it crawls, spores and microbes are brought from one protected hollow to another. A very old animal can look like a stone that has acquired joints.

It has eyes, but they are small and set close to the shell. They detect the upper gray and the passage of larger shadows. Sight is useful mainly for knowing when to become still. Most of its world arrives through other organs: rows of fine pressure hairs hidden beneath the plate margins, pits that taste dissolved matter, and a pair of pale feelers that can enter spaces narrower than the head. With these it reads the basin at close range.

A pebble ticking once against another is only disturbance. A repeated faint vibration may be an amphipod scraping a film. The soft intermittent pulse inside shell grit may be a worm. A sudden smooth pressure, too broad and swift to resolve, is a passing fish or seal. Long before such a body becomes visible, the resident has tightened its hold and shut its feelers beneath the armor.

It does not hunt by pursuit. Pursuit is costly, and open water belongs to the current. Instead it feeds where moving water delivers food into stillness. It chooses the downstream side of a cobble, lowers a fringe of bristles, and strains edible particles from the eddy. It pries small animals from gravel with its forelimbs. It rasps the films beneath overturned stones. When a dead fish settles nearby, the residents emerge in numbers, but never in a rush. They appear one by one from the rock bed, each crawling against the current along a route of shelter. By the time larger scavengers arrive, the softest tissues may already have been opened.

Then they disappear. A cod nosing among the stones finds only plated backs pressed into matching hollows. A crab may seize one, but the resident releases several outer plates and leaves them in the claw. The exposed skin beneath hardens slowly. Until it does, the animal remains deep in the gravel, vulnerable even to sand.

Much of its life is spent waiting for the water to change.

At full current it braces. At slack tide it travels. The interval may last only minutes, and the animal seems to know it before the pebbles cease trembling. Its limbs loosen. Its feelers extend. It climbs from its hollow and moves over the stones with an unexpected smoothness, as if a dark patch were passing across them. It searches for newly opened gaps, carrion, mates, and surfaces uncovered by the latest rearrangement.

If the tide turns early, the traveler does not attempt to return. It takes the nearest hold. This is why no individual has a fixed territory. A single storm may shift it farther than it would otherwise travel in a season. The basin redistributes its inhabitants as it redistributes shell and gravel. Their fidelity is not to a site but to a kind of space: a certain width of darkness, a certain pressure of flow, a stone heavy enough not to roll.

They grow slowly. The cold permits no haste. The smallest molt several times in their first year, hiding in shell grit while the new plates harden. Later the intervals lengthen. A large animal may keep the same armor through many seasons, its growth occurring in thin additions along the overlapping margins. These additions form bands, though they are irregular and often worn away. No one looking at them could easily tell age from hardship.

Reproduction begins beneath winter darkness. The adults do not gather. Instead, one animal follows the chemical trace of another through the gravel, sometimes for days, losing it when silt thickens and finding it again after the current clears. The meeting is brief. Months later, the egg-bearing animal descends as far into the stones as its body allows and fastens a small cluster beneath a stable cobble.

The eggs are flat, dark capsules with tough edges. They do not hang from tendrils, which would chafe through. They are cemented directly to the underside of the rock, each aligned with the smallest local flow. Water passes over them carrying oxygen but little grit. The parent remains nearby, not brooding in any warm or tender sense, but cleaning the capsules with its mouthparts and driving away worms that would pierce them.

The young hatch during a period of weak water. They are nearly weightless and possess broader fringes than the adults. For several days they do what their elders cannot: they enter the current deliberately. They rise only a little above the bottom and are carried across the basin in the streaming sediment. Many vanish into fish. Many are crushed when stones settle. Some descend into channels too swift for them ever to leave. But a few catch against gravel, fold their fringes, and begin life in a hollow far from the place of hatching.

In this way the species spreads without commanding where it goes.

There are differences across the basin. Those in hard-packed gravel are narrow and wedge-shaped, able to force themselves between stones. In boulder fields they grow broader, with stronger hooks. Near cold seeps their shells are unusually pale, and the microbial stains on them form rust-colored rings. In the deepest pockets, where no surface light reaches for long periods, the eyes are covered by an unpigmented layer and the feelers may be nearly the length of the body. These may be separate kinds, or merely the basin writing itself differently upon the same animal.

Very rarely, after an exceptional disturbance, one is lifted clear of the floor.

In open water it loses all resemblance to stone. The limbs spread. The plates separate slightly. Its pale underside shows, soft and intricate, crowded with beating parts. It tumbles without grace, hooks searching for what is no longer there. A creature that seemed armored and self-contained is revealed as dependent on contact, made for pressure against something solid.

Usually it finds the bottom again. It strikes among pebbles, closes around the first stable edge, and waits while sand hisses over its back. Within hours its outline has begun to disappear. Within days a stain gathers along the shell seams. Soon it is once more difficult to say where animal ends and basin begins.

It does not master the cold, the darkness, or the moving stones. It survives by yielding to their scale. It owns nothing, constructs nothing, and leaves no lasting mark except an empty shell plate occasionally found beneath a cobble, already abraded, already becoming grit.

Its entire life is an attachment between rearrangements.
