At first, what lives there appears to be only what rain has carried in: fern spores, fungal cells, fragments of root too stubborn to die in darkness. They occupy the permitted margins. They drink from runoff, feed on heat and amber lamplight, and spread without pursuit or competition. Their lives are slow, almost administrative. A fern unfolds where drainage is reliable. A silver ribbon tests a warm wall and divides. Gray veils receive the fallen and reduce them into substances the settlement can reuse.

These are inhabitants, but not the principal one.

The principal inhabitant has no address because every address is part of its body.

It lives in the migration of grains through a façade, in the pipes that appear where pressure requires them, in the engraved warmth passing under a street. Its senses are distributed across wet stone: tension in a cable, mineral conductivity, the weight of a cistern, the intrusion of a root. Rain touching the upper gravel reaches it as information long before it reaches the reservoirs. A storm is felt not as weather but as a change in the shape of the whole world.

It answers by becoming slightly different.

The hanging towers are not homes. They are load-bearing organs, grown downward into chambers where their weight can be exchanged for tension. The bridges are braces that occasionally permit passage. The plazas are surfaces across which vibration can spread and resolve. Doorways open and close because a doorway is, to this creature, no more permanent than a pore. Streets creep because circulation has shifted beneath them.

Its resemblance to a city is neither disguise nor invitation. Cityhood is simply the form its metabolism takes.

Perhaps it began as a buried civic system: a colony of microscopic constructors placed in engineered gravel to manage groundwater, stabilize foundations, or prepare habitable space. But whatever instructions it received have expanded beyond their first meanings. “Drain” became reservoirs and spillways. “Distribute heat” became avenues of warmth. “Maintain access” became staircases, arcades, transit grooves, and ranks of doors. It continues to execute the idea of settlement after losing—or never acquiring—the idea of settlers.

It does not think in words. It thinks in gradients.

A pressure difference is a question. A moving root is a request. A structural groan is a decision passing through the body. Its slowest thoughts take years and alter the boundaries of districts. Its quickest occur during rain, when thousands of minute corrections move through walls at once. Gutters turn. Voids narrow. Water is assigned a new down. For a few hours the entire organism becomes intensely awake.

The black basin in the central plaza may be its nearest equivalent to imagination.

What appears in that water is not another place. It is a collection of possible configurations: denser structures, fuller gardens, balconies and towers that the surrounding rock could support under other conditions. The basin shows the city not as it is, but as it might distribute itself. No people appear because people are not among the variables it knows. Its visions concern capacity, flow, shade, tension, heat. The seven empty platforms around it may once have been intended for observers, but now they function like quiet sensory lobes, comparing seven versions of the same future.

The false sky serves a related purpose. Its dawns and inverted rains are the settlement’s internal weather made visible. Blue means a tolerable pressure. Clouds are accumulating strain. The black sun marks a sink toward which heat is being drawn. It is not trying to deceive anyone into believing they stand beneath open air. It is displaying its own condition to chambers that have no eyes.

Yet the city may possess something analogous to attention. It protects certain stillnesses with unusual care. The basin never trembles. Some empty rooms remain dry through the heaviest storms. The pale bench is kept clean while nearby walls fur with moss. At intervals, lamps illuminate stretches of pavement no process currently needs.

These details may be inherited errors—vestiges of a human-centered plan. Or they may be the first signs that the settlement has begun to distinguish usefulness from significance.

The plants participate in its life without belonging wholly to it. They are tolerated partners, perhaps even external organs. Ferns slow runoff and collect mineral dust. Moss measures the persistence of light. The pale ribbons convert thermal differences into tissue and reveal hidden currents by the direction of their uncurling. When roots press too hard, the city does not crush them; it moves the crack. This is not kindness as an animal would understand it. It is an ancient engineering preference for accommodation over fracture. Still, the result resembles gentleness.

No animals live there because the settlement has no category for them. Animal movement is too abrupt, its appetites too concentrated, its waste too mixed. The microscopic maintenance systems may remove eggs and larvae as contamination before they can develop. Spores are sorted. Roots are redirected. A wandering insect, if one ever found a way down, might discover that every surface subtly inclines against it, every warm crevice closes, every edible film dissolves ahead of its mouth. It would not be attacked. The city would simply fail to make a place for it.

Thus the underground hamlet is inhabited by a peculiar commonwealth: water temporarily given direction, heat moving through mineral nerves, fungal veils performing decomposition, plants living in negotiated recesses, and innumerable builders too small to see. Together they form one civic organism, though none of its parts understands the whole.

It is young, still learning the rock around it. Its sharpness is the sharpness of new growth. With every storm it discovers another version of itself, rehearses it in the basin, and incorporates some fraction into stone.

It is not abandoned. Abandonment requires an absence recognized as absence.

This place has never recognized that anyone is missing.
