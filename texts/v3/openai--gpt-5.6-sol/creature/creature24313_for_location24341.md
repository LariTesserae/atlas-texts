What lives there first appears to be the green itself.

It lies close to the stone, not as a forest or meadow but as a hide: a thin, resilient pelt drawn over the excavated valley. Moss occupies the wet seams. Rye holds the brighter lanes in dark, bristling ranks. Clover presses three small leaves against the cold air, and sorrel gathers a sourness from water that has already passed through pipes, filters, roots, and stone. The fodder plants are low and fleshy, bred to hoard warmth and moisture in themselves. Nothing wastes energy on height.

These plants are descendants of surface species, but they no longer behave quite like them. Their clocks answer to lamp schedules rather than sunrise. Many never flower. Those that do open pale, abbreviated blooms to empty air, waiting for no insect. They are thick-rooted, slow-breathing, and tolerant of long dim intervals. A crop may continue growing when a bank of lamps fails, not thriving, not dying, simply becoming darker and denser until maintenance arrives.

The vines are different. Given cables and mesh, they seek height with an almost misplaced confidence. Their leaves are broad where the lamps are strong and dwindle to coins along the cold margins. In the oldest chambers, individual vines are difficult to distinguish. They have grafted against one another, rooted through neighboring cisterns, and thickened around steel until the trellis and the plant share the same shape. Crews assign them bed numbers, but workers give the oldest walls names.

The structural willows are watched most closely. They began as bundled rods set beside failing partitions and soft roof margins. Over years they fused into pale, many-stemmed columns, their roots confined to engineered troughs and their crowns repeatedly cut back. They are neither wholly supports nor wholly crops. Sensors disappear beneath their bark. Steel collars must be loosened before the wood swallows them. Engineers inspect them with mallets, listening through the roar for changes they can mostly feel through their gloves.

A whole smaller population lives within the planted skin.

Springtails graze on fungal films between fibers. Mites move through bark chips and vanish into root felt. Nematodes occupy the warm wet margins of the return channels. Pale fungi lace dead roots together and sometimes climb living stems before being cut out. Bacterial cultures in the reservoirs perform more of the valley’s digestion than any visible animal does. They break down clipped leaves, spent fiber, and dissolved waste, returning nutrients to water that will pass beneath the rye again.

These minute lives are tolerated only while they remain useful or obscure. Every district has its own microbial character despite the doors and sterilizing baths. One smells faintly of mushrooms when its mats are opened. Another produces an orange film on filter screens. A third grows a black yeast along the warm pipes, resistant to ordinary washes. Technicians catalogue such things as contamination events, though many have persisted longer than the technicians themselves.

The moths live nearest to anything resembling a sky. They are almost colorless, with soft bodies and wings powdered gray-white. Their larvae feed on algae, shed root hairs, and the tender tissue of vines where warm condensation gathers. Adults collect around lamp housings and beat themselves against the protective glass. When a lighting bar shuts down, they cling motionless to it until the next cycle. They have poor flight and no reason to cross the darkest chambers, so separate lamp fields have developed separate populations: smaller moths in the cold north galleries, darker ones near the compost processors, a nearly wingless strain around one old substation.

Voles are rarer and more conspicuous. They arrive from above inside grain pallets, insulation rolls, or machinery crates. Most are caught within days. A few learn the architecture before the traps close around them. They travel under growing mats, leaving narrow ridges through clover, and nest beside warm motors where vibration hides their movement. Their fur becomes oily with mineral grit. They eat roots, spilled grain, moth larvae, and the soft insulation stripped from wires. A vole colony, when one survives, is treated as a failure of procedure. Workers nevertheless follow its fortunes. They recognize the entrances. They leave no food, but they notice when the tracks stop.

The people live there too, though never in numbers proportionate to the place.

They come down in shifts through lifts and sloping service tunnels, wearing insulated coveralls sealed at wrist and throat. Their helmets carry front lamps for work and side lamps for speech: brief flashes, colors, and repeated pulses visible through the murk. In the open chambers they rarely hear one another. Their language is made with hands, shoulders, lamp signals, and touch.

A palm held low means gas.

Two fingers drawn across the forearm mean a blocked line.

A fist against the chest asks whether the other person is warm enough.

An open hand turning slowly in the air means that something—pump, cutter, fan, root auger—is beginning to vibrate incorrectly.

Because the machinery fills every silence, the workers become attentive to movement. They see a warning in the shiver of a pipe or in leaves trembling against the prevailing vibration. They know when a turbine has changed pitch not because they can clearly hear the difference, but because the rail beneath one boot feels wrong. A cultivation worker may identify failing bearings from the behavior of clover fifty metres away.

Most are specialists. Water technicians test reservoirs and crawl beneath the beds to clear mineral deposits. Crop crews guide low harvesters through the lanes, cutting rye and fodder growth without tearing the root fabric. Lamp crews work from suspended platforms in violet light, their faces made strange behind frost-streaked visors. Structural gardeners tend the willow, trimming, bracing, and mapping the pressure of living wood against stone. Air crews climb into the shafts and return rimed with surface ice. Sanitation teams move between districts with sealed carts, collecting mold, dead roots, moth masses, and anything else that might travel where it should not.

They spend comparatively little time in the bunkrooms. The cabins are narrow, overheated, and bright, with benches, drying racks, emergency oxygen, and windows filmed by condensation. Inside, the roar becomes a deep padded pressure. Speech returns abruptly. New workers often talk too loudly; experienced ones sit for a while before using their voices.

Nothing personal is supposed to be introduced into the growing districts. Nevertheless, small possessions accumulate in the control huts: a painted cup, a deck of swollen cards, surface stones, photographs sealed under transparent tape. Someone has scratched harvest dates into the frame of an old monitor. Someone else keeps a forbidden piece of birch bark inside a locker, double-bagged and dry. The longest-serving crews can identify districts by smell alone and speak of them as if they possess temperaments.

The East Beds are thirsty.

Nine takes root badly.

The Old Willow does not like rapid warming.

Beyond the workers are the things they have made to act almost like inhabitants. Trimming machines pass on programmed routes, low and broad, with frost gathering along their housings. Pollination carts release puffs of air among sterile flowers. Inspection crawlers enter irrigation channels too narrow for a person. Pump controls respond to reservoir depth, conductivity, temperature, and demand from beds kilometres away. None is intelligent in the human sense, but all follow cycles, encounter obstacles, and leave tracks. A broken cart may remain blinking in the rye until a crew can reach it, its warning light continuing through multiple false nights.

Together, plant, microbe, water, worker, and machine form something larger than a farm but less self-sufficient than a wilderness. The valley cannot live without constant intervention. Its air must be driven. Its roots must be warmed. Its water must be recovered. Its dead growth must be removed before it becomes disease or fire load. Yet the intervention cannot fully command it. Roots enter joints that maps show as sealed. Fungi cross barriers on boots and tools. Willows lean by degrees against braces. Moss grows over abandoned rails and turns the straight lines of excavation into softer, less legible forms.

At the active edge live the youngest plants. Each rises alone from a cut in black cloth, separated from the next by exposed stone. Their leaves are small, dark, and unnaturally glossy. They shake in the ventilation current, surrounded by a chamber that has not yet acquired the smell of growth. Technicians inspect them often because failure is easiest there: one frozen valve, one dry feed line, one misaligned lamp, and a rectangle of green becomes a rectangle of blackened stems.

But where they survive, their roots spread laterally. Moss arrives in the damp around the cuts. Clover runners cross the cloth. The isolated rectangles become patches, then beds, then a continuous surface in which the original planting pattern can no longer be seen.

No creature witnesses the whole occupation. A worker knows a handful of districts. A moth knows one cluster of lamps. A vole may learn the warmth beneath a single processing shed. A willow’s roots explore only their trough and the fractures that open beside it. Even the control system knows the valley as numbers rather than as a place.

What lives there, finally, is this divided, cooperative persistence: cold-adapted leaves under artificial twilight; roots feeding from circulated water; pale insects at the lamps; hidden decomposers; rare stowaway mammals; human crews moving by signal through the thunder; machines repeating routes until greenery erases their tracks.

None of them possesses the hollow. Together they keep it from returning to bare stone.
