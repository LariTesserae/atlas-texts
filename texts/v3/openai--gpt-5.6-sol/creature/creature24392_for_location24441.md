At first the inhabitants are difficult to separate from the town.

A figure resting beside a conduit may be mistaken for a bundle of hoses, a root mass, or an old diving garment left to fur over. Their clothing takes the color of immersion: green-black, copper-stained, softened at every edge. Weed fiber grows through its seams. Small plates of ceramic protect the chest and joints; colonies of filter moss are encouraged along the shoulders. Tools hang close against the body so they will not snag in the crowded passages. Nothing trails unless it is meant to take root.

Then a hand unfolds.

It is long and narrow, with six fingers webbed almost to the final joint. The nails are dark and flattened, useful for scraping corrosion or sensing vibration in a pipe. The skin beneath is pale gray, but seldom visible except around the eyes and palms. Everywhere else it is patterned by mineral stains, old electrical burns, grafted patches of protective algae, and the fine red lines of blood near the surface.

The people of the Compression are slight. Broad bodies fare poorly in throat-passages, and unnecessary mass is expensive to feed and difficult to turn among the machinery. Adults are narrow through the ribs and unusually flexible at the shoulders. Their legs are strong but not graceful; swimming is less important here than pulling, bracing, and folding oneself through apertures. Most move hand over hand along cables and rails, with a slow, practiced economy. They can remain motionless in a current without seeming to exert themselves.

Their lungs are small. Along the sides of the neck and beneath the lowest ribs lie layered breathing fronds, protected by slitted membranes. When the water is clean, these open like dark red fern leaves. In silt or chemical runoff, they seal, and the wearer draws from a bladder of compressed gas fixed beneath the sternum. Children’s fronds are vivid scarlet. In the old they fade almost white and gather branching deposits of copper.

Their eyes have broad, black pupils and a second transparent lid. They see well at arm’s length and poorly beyond it, which is no great disadvantage. Recognition depends more on movement, scent, and touch. Every household cultivates its own trace mixture of oils, minerals, and plant resins. Kin know one another by the taste of the water disturbed by a passing body.

Faces are quiet. Speech requires closeness: brow to brow, hand at the other’s throat, mouths nearly touching so that a few shaped vibrations survive the water between them. Intimacy and conversation are therefore difficult to distinguish. For ordinary business they tap rails, flex signaling membranes at the wrist, or release droplets of color from little valves worn at the belt. A skilled speaker can send a sentence through iron several chambers away. A skilled listener rests two fingers against a pipe and feels not only the words but the speaker’s fatigue, haste, or fear.

No one is merely a gardener or merely a machinist. The distinctions that matter are between those who tend living structures, those who tend dead ones, and those who know where the difference has become unreliable.

Rootwrights guide vines through cracked pressure housings and persuade them to tighten in prescribed directions. Platers grow metal across worn surfaces, pruning copper crystals with bone shears. Warmers maintain the density boundaries between chambers. Listeners diagnose the deep pumps by lying against their casings for hours, sometimes days. Siltkeepers clear the outer grilles and return with their clothing hardened by pale accretions. The most respected workers are seam-readers, usually elderly people whose fingers can tell whether a wall is opening, settling, or merely breathing.

Age alters the inhabitants slowly. Their movements become smaller; their skin roughens and takes on a reeflike texture. Calcifying organisms settle in scars. An old person may carry a garden of translucent polyps along one arm or a permanent ruff of moss around the neck. These growths are not always removed. Some filter poisons. Some warn of electrical leakage by closing. Some are simply old companions.

Children are smooth, quick, and regarded as a structural hazard. They slip into turbine housings, crawl through warm pipes, and vanish into orchard roots. For their first years they are tethered by braided lines that pass from household to household. The whole neighborhood feels their movements. A sudden stillness on a child-line brings more alarm than any shouted warning could.

They are raised communally in warm nursery cisterns where the water is kept clear and gently circulating. There they learn currents before language. Colored dyes are released into the cistern, and the children must predict where each plume will go, where it will divide, and how long it will take to reach the drain. Later they learn the town by touch: the vibration of the Lung, the rhythm of each pump, the particular grain of basalt beneath old algae. Few could draw a map of the Compression. Most can cross it blind.

Their dead are announced in white.

The body is washed of cultivated growths, though useful symbionts are transplanted. Metal implants are recovered. The bones are placed in a low-voltage garden where, over many years, they become coated in branching copper and incorporated into battery walls. Thus the dead continue to hold charge, smooth fluctuations, and give a faint warning hum before a failure. Certain chambers are lined with generations of ancestors, though no names remain attached to individual shapes.

Names themselves are temporary. A child receives a private touch-pattern from its household, later an occupational color, and eventually a pipe-rhythm recognizable across the district where it works. After death, the rhythm may persist for decades as part of a pump signal or warning code. It is possible to hear an ancestor every morning without knowing that the sound was once a person.

The inhabitants rarely think of the sea above as a destination. Things descend from it: drowned wood, broken instruments, unfamiliar fish already dying from the basin’s chemistry, once a bronze bell that never stopped sounding until roots were grown through its mouth. Expeditions upward are made only to inspect the cliffs and outer grilles. Those who go wear mirrored eye shields and weighted veils. They return disturbed by distance. In open water there is nothing to grasp, no wall close enough to answer a tap, no ceiling of pipes to prove that one has not been lost.

They find the upward fall of bubbles especially alarming. Outside the basin, exhalation deserts the body. Air flees toward the remote brightness. Stones remain below. The whole sea appears to them disordered.

At the empty square, their behavior changes. They approach without tools if they can. They fasten themselves to rings at the edge and do not cross the tiles. Some bring children. Some bring the newly old, when the breathing fronds have begun to pale. They watch the air sphere trembling around the dead tree.

They possess no certain account of it. One tradition says their ancestors lived inside air and that water once killed them. Another says the tree is a machine for measuring a span of time too long for instruments. A third holds that the sphere is not preserved air at all, but an opening into a dry place that continues elsewhere.

The inhabitants do not worship the tree. Worship would suggest petition, and no one expects it to answer. They maintain the square as they maintain a pressure seal: because the consequences of neglect are unknown.

Once every few years, when a branch is said to move, witnesses tap the news into the surrounding pipes. The signal is not white, green, violet, or yellow. It is older than the color codes: three slow beats, a pause, and one beat repeated until the hand tires.

Deep beneath them, something else may answer.

The creature below the Lung is known chiefly by absences. Gas reservoirs empty without rupturing. Sediment channels change course. Glass shrimp gather along the lower grilles and then disappear all at once. Workers descending to the deepest valves sometimes feel a broad surface pass under their feet, warm and faintly ridged, too large to be recognized as flesh before it is gone.

Some believe it is an animal that entered when the basin was young and grew around the foundations. Some say it is a colony, or a collapsed machine made animate by weed and current. The oldest seam-readers insist that the Compression does not rest on basalt at all.

It rests on that.

They say the Lung is not feeding the creature but breathing for it, and that the strange downward pull of air is only the appetite of something too slow to seem alive.

No official warning is issued. The pumps continue. The orchards are pruned. Children tug at their lines. Copper grows grain by grain across the bones of the dead.

And below the tiled square, below the dry fountain and its impossible sphere, the pressure dials occasionally sink to zero in a patient row, as though something vast were turning in its sleep.
