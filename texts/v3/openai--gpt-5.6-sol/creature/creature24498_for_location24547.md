At first the inhabitants are difficult to separate from the birds.

Both crowd the roofs and harbor walls. Both wear pale coatings of mineral dust. Both move in abrupt gestures against the noise. From offshore, a work crew chipping a causeway may resemble a row of dark waders pecking at the stone, while the actual birds stand nearby with the proprietary stillness of overseers.

Only at close range do the people resolve into individuals: fishers slick with oil and spray, reservoir keepers carrying shell dippers, potters gray to the elbows, children with baskets tied above their heads, old people seated in protected door recesses with their hands in constant conversation.

They are not one kind of people. Boats have been arriving for longer than anyone can reckon, bringing spouses, captives, fugitives, traders, and crews who intended to stay only until the next departure. Faces from many coasts appear in the markets. Yet the city gives them all a family resemblance. Their hair is cropped short or shaved at the sides because long hair remains wet, catches salt, and stiffens with lime. Their palms are heavily ridged, their nails worn blunt, and most have white scars where mineral grit has entered small cuts. Even dark skin takes on a chalky cast by the end of a working day.

Their feet are broad and tough from warm stone. Indoors they go barefoot; outside they wear sandals made from layered fish skin, imported reed, or scraps of hide, with soles that can be replaced one strip at a time. Anything woven is too valuable to trail through runoff. Garments are short, wrapped close, and frequently oiled. The wealthy are not those who wear the most cloth, but those who can afford cloth fine enough to dry.

Everyone carries tools. A child may have only a shell scraper hung from the waist, but no one goes far without something capable of freeing a blocked gutter or cutting a crusted knot. Hammers are as personal as teeth. People recognize one another’s work by the rhythm of striking and by the shallow, overlapping facets left in stone.

The dark copper implements belong to districts rather than persons. Their custodians hold considerable authority, though they are thought of less as rulers than as people burdened with dangerous trust. A copper adze neglected in brine can lose more value in a few days than a household earns in many boat arrivals. Each tool has a name, a record incised on fired clay, and a succession of keepers. Lending one requires witnesses.

The city’s true law concerns flow.

There are prohibitions against fouling drinking basins, narrowing common channels, diverting seepage onto a neighbor’s threshold, or allowing a crust to thicken until it threatens a public stair. Theft is punished, but deliberate blockage is treated as something worse: an attack on the continued separateness of the city. Most disputes are settled by reservoir councils composed of channel cutters, cistern keepers, fishers, and household elders. They examine water marks, taste deposits, and send children crawling into ducts too narrow for adults.

No household is self-sufficient. One may own a room, a roof tray, a pig pen, or rights to a section of shell bed, but no one can live without the people upstream clearing their channels. Independence is regarded with suspicion, almost as a form of madness. To be human here is to maintain an opening for someone else.

Their language exists in several forms at once.

The shouted language is clipped and forceful, full of vowels that carry through spray. It is used at sea, on causeways, and during emergencies. The hand language is subtler. It can be spoken across a market or through a window without adding to the city’s uproar. Its grammar uses shoulders, brows, direction of gaze, and the distance of a sign from the body. A price offered close to the chest differs from the same number held outward. The sign for fresh water is made low and cupped; salt water is shown with fingers spread; mineral water closes the hand gradually, as though the air itself were hardening.

Older inhabitants often hear little beyond vibration. They are not therefore excluded. Some become negotiators because they cannot be distracted by shouted abuse. In council, the best speakers use voice and sign together, allowing one argument to carry two shades of meaning.

Intimacy is quieter. People speak directly into one another’s ears, lips nearly touching skin. Parents soothe infants by humming against the breastbone so the child feels the sound. Lovers converse beneath hanging hides, where the roar is dulled enough for breath to be heard. Silence is not an absence here but a costly enclosure, something made by thick walls, fitted doors, and constant maintenance.

Children learn the city from its dangers. They are taught never to step on an untested pale film, since a crust may conceal hot water or a black trench. They learn which pools burn the eyes, which crabs sever fingers, and which birds defend their nests by striking from behind. Before they can count, they can distinguish the tremor of an ordinary spring from the deeper shudder that means a vent is narrowing.

They play with shells, knucklebones, and flakes of soft stone. They build miniature streets and then pour mineral water over them, competing to see whose arrangement remains open longest. They hunt crabs in the alleys, steal warm eggs from low nests, and dare one another to crawl into abandoned drains. Their games are practice for maintenance.

There are no local flowers for naming daughters, no trees for comparing sons to height or strength. Plant words arrive with cargo. Children know a reed first as a yellow cut stick, grain as flour inside a sealed jar, and fruit as a wrinkled sweetness from beyond the sea. A fresh green leaf, when one survives the voyage, may draw a larger crowd than copper. It is touched carefully, passed from hand to hand, and usually eaten before it can blacken.

Because nothing sprouts, growth is imagined differently. A child is said to deepen, like a channel; a household thickens; a friendship is kept clear. A healthy person flows well. A stubborn one is crusted shut.

Food comes chiefly from the crowded water. Fish are eaten fresh, dried in kiln drafts, mashed into paste, or fermented in stoppered jars. Shrimp and larvae are swept from pools with imported reed screens. Crabs are cracked in communal courts where the shell fragments can be collected for mortar. Eels are roasted in clay sleeves. Bird eggs are gathered under strict district rules so that no nesting ledge is emptied completely.

Meals are mineral-tasting and strong: fish broth, salt cakes, flat bread when flour boats have arrived, pig fat, dates divided into thin ceremonial portions, bitter shellfish, and sauces left until they nearly dissolve their pots. Fresh water is served with formality. The host drinks last, after guests have seen that the cup is clear.

Birds are neighbors, prey, thieves, weather signs, and public nuisances. The people know them in exhausting detail. They distinguish birds that foul reservoirs from those that eat midges, birds whose eggs keep well from those that spoil quickly, birds that indicate fish beneath the surface from birds that merely follow other birds. Nesting colonies are culled, protected, robbed, and argued over. No attempt is made to drive them all away. Such an idea would seem as futile as forbidding the springs.

Dogs live closest to human households. They are narrow-bodied, short-haired, and scarred around the muzzle from crabs. Many go partially deaf. They sleep wherever the stone is warmest and wake furious at any bird that lands just beyond reach. Pigs are less beloved but more carefully guarded. A pig represents imported husks, pen labor, fresh water, and many months of accumulated food. Its slaughter is a district event.

Time is not measured by weather. People count shifts of labor, reservoir clearings, kiln firings, boat arrivals, and the recopying of records before mineral skin obscures them. A person may say that she was born between the loss of the southern flour boat and the third recutting of the east hall. Age is often known approximately, but obligations are exact.

Written memory is troublesome. Marks cut in exposed stone vanish beneath new deposition; paint runs; cord tallies rot if not kept dry. Important records are incised on fired clay tablets and stored in sealed chambers, but even these must be inspected and recopied. Thus memory is treated as maintenance. Genealogies, debts, channel rights, and accounts of disasters survive only if someone continues to clear them.

The same is true of the dead.

Bodies are not placed under open markers, because markers would soon become shapeless lumps. Some dead are taken by boat to deep water, especially those whose families came from elsewhere. Most are laid in chambers beneath houses or within sections of wall being closed. They are wrapped in precious reed mats if their household can afford it, otherwise in hide or layered fish skin. Warm seepage enters. The wrapping hardens, the bones whiten, and gradually the chamber becomes indistinguishable from the foundation.

This is not considered disappearance. The dead have ceased needing passages and can safely become structure. People say that the city stands because its ancestors no longer move. A house may contain generations in its floor without any visible tomb. Names must therefore be spoken; the ground keeps bodies perfectly but does not explain whose they were.

Their sacred figures, where they have them, are squat and enclosed. There are no sky-reaching gods on towers. Holiness belongs to mouths, vents, thresholds, and vessels: places where one substance enters another without destroying it. The most respected ritual specialists are often also engineers of a kind. They listen at spring openings, read the color of runoff, and decide when a vent may be widened or must be left untouched. Their prayers resemble instructions. Their instructions resemble prayers.

They do not imagine creation as an event completed in the past. Creation is what the water is doing now, indiscriminately, and human life is the art of interrupting it. Too little stone and the sea would take the shelf. Too much stone and every channel, room, vessel, and mouth would close.

So the inhabitants do not dream of defeating the place. They trim it. They bargain with it in increments no thicker than a fingernail. Their civilization is made not from conquest but from repeated refusal: this doorway will remain a doorway; this basin will remain drinkable; this stair will not become a slope; this room will not yet be filled.

Around them, the shrimp pulse, the eels turn in warm darkness, the crabs test every crevice, and the birds occupy whatever the people leave uncovered. The animals require no history. The people require one desperately, and must keep cutting it free.

Seen closely, the city is not fixed at all. It is composed of millions of small acts performed against closure: a hand lifting shell from a gutter, a child signing a warning, an old woman scraping the name on a tablet, a fisherman pulling silver bodies from a black channel, attendants knocking newborn stone from the ceiling of the council hall.

What lives there is everything that can endure abundance without greenery, permanence without rest, and a world that is forever hardening around it.
