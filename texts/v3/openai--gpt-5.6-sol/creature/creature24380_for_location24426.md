At first, it is difficult to tell what lives on the plateau because almost everything living has learned to resemble a process.

The black growth in the fractures might be mistaken for vegetation. The traveling bulges beneath it might be wind. The figures on the glass cliffs seem like mineral stains until one turns inside out and begins to drink. Even the largest animals pass easily for portions of the terrain: moving shelves, low ridges, outcrops burdened with gardens. Here, concealment does not mean looking dead. It means looking geological.

The smallest inhabitants arrive with the rain.

They wait as transparent grains pressed into cracks, each grain faceted like crushed glass and containing not an embryo but a crowded set of possible embryos. Dryness keeps these possibilities unresolved. The first drop chooses among them.

In warm rain, the grains unfold into rafts of ciliated mouths that spread across temporary pools and graze on dissolved stone. In cold rain, they become needle-legged things that skate beneath the water’s surface, hanging upside down from its tension. Where droplets rise instead of falling, they open into hollow bells and ascend with them. A single dormant grain may carry fins, roots, wings, mineral teeth, heat bladders, and organs for laws that are not presently in effect. Most of these never become real. The weather edits them.

For the brief life of a rain corridor, they are everywhere. They redden black pools. They coat the conductive reeds in silver movement. They climb the inside of water-filled fruits and are eaten by translucent grazers that themselves did not exist an hour before. They feed, divide, mate, harden, and die while the storm is still visible on the horizon. When the water lifts away, their last generation seals itself into the stone again.

Some leave behind tiny mineral cups. After thousands of storms, these cups form terraces along the cracks, recording not years but kinds of rain.

Larger creatures follow the corridors.

The rain-followers are built low and broad, with six or ten or several dozen legs depending on where they hatched. Their backs carry dark sails that do not catch wind. The sails are colder than the surrounding air and comb moisture from it, passing droplets through branching veins into reservoirs around the animals’ hearts. When a rain corridor moves, they travel along its drier edge, never entering the downpour. To them, a storm is not water but a moving wall of pressure, charge, scent, and possible anatomy.

They feed on the fresh skin of the plateau. Their mouths are fans of metallic threads, delicate enough to brush bacterial films from sulfur crystal and strong enough, when electrically stiffened, to rasp black glass. Herds leave pale grazing traces across the shelves, but these marks vanish when the stone sweats and regrows its mineral glaze.

Each rain-follower bears smaller animals between its sail ribs. These passengers drink from capillaries beneath the skin. Some clean spores from the breathing folds. Some inject their own young into the host’s unresolved tissues, where they become temporary organs: an extra eye for a magnetic storm, a heat-sensing lobe, a bladder that stores a particular flavor of gravity. When that weather passes, the organs detach as mature creatures and crawl away.

There is no stable boundary between parasite, offspring, and body.

On vertical escarpments live the glass-backed grazers. They cling with rings of soft feet that flow into microscopic flaws in the cliff and harden there. Their upper bodies are almost perfectly transparent, except for silver digestive fans and the dark knots of water they carry inside themselves. Seen from below, they resemble clear insects filled with storm clouds.

They climb toward condensation. Because sound is unreliable, they communicate by dimming portions of their skin. A whole cliff colony can transmit a warning in bands of darkness: shadow passing upward against gravity, then dividing around an unseen danger.

Their predators lie flatter still.

These are not quite animals when resting. They spread themselves over the basalt in films only a few cells thick, extending for tens of meters. Dust settles on them. Mineral glow passes through them. They copy scratches, thermal gradients, even the slow changing reflections of clouds. Beneath this skin, their organs remain compressed into braided threads.

When prey steps across one, the film does not spring immediately. It thickens around the foot so gradually that the grazer continues for several paces. Then the predator rises behind it, assembling height from the ground upward. Mouth, limbs, and digestive cavity appear only as needed.

Often the wound arrives first.

A grazer climbing through a zone of reversed causality may begin leaking clear blood from an unopened body. The herd sees the injury and scatters. Minutes later the flattened predator chooses which fleeing animal to strike, drawn toward the one already wounded by its future attack. Hunger here can be a loop that closes itself.

Sometimes it does not close. A predator may wait over the promised blood and never make the kill. Such individuals starve beside evidence that they have fed.

In the blade-forests, creatures move through plants rather than between them.

Many are ribbon-shaped, with jointless bodies woven through hundreds of stems. No single section contains the whole animal. Its stomach may lie in one ravine, its reproductive chambers in another, its sensory fringes spread across a kilometer of foliage. If a ridge touches a distant ridge during fog, the creature extends into the temporary contact. When the land separates, it is divided but not necessarily harmed. Each half retains a different portion of its habits.

Over time, these separated portions become unlike one another. One may grow lungs for sulfur vapor while the other learns to feed on electrical discharge. When the ridges touch again, they may fuse, exchange organs, devour each other, or pass without recognition.

Their young are knots. The knots hang from leaves and collect impressions: changes in pressure, the chemistry of rain, tremors that have not yet happened. When heavy with conditions, they drop. What crawls from each knot depends on where it lands. Thus the parent does not produce a species. It produces a question the ground answers.

Above the fractures drift the buoyant swarms.

Each flyer is seed-sized and wingless, its body enclosing chambers of heated gas, vacuum, spores, or electrically polarized fluid. Individually they have little control. Together they arrange themselves into immense permeable bodies that swim along gradients of charge. The swarm may become a sheet over a mineral chimney, a funnel around a heat column, or a long articulated shape moving against the clouds.

These shapes can appear purposeful. They bend around obstacles before reaching them. They gather over unopened flowers. They hesitate at ravines where physical law has become uncertain. Yet no flyer knows the shape of the swarm.

Their coordination comes from flashes passed body to body. Because light travels more reliably than sound but less reliably than intention, a message changes as it crosses them. A signal meaning *rise* may become *hollow*, then *divide*, then *remember water*. The swarm acts on all versions at once.

During electrical storms, these drifting multitudes descend upon the hollow chimneys and enter their vents. For days they live inside the cycling gases, feeding on metallic vapor. When they emerge, their bodies have been plated in conductive crystal. They arrange themselves into branching circuits across the sky. Lightning travels through them without killing them, and for an instant each member carries the same sensation.

Afterward, many fall as warm silver dust. The survivors retain fragments of a memory too large for any one body.

The great walkers are the most visible inhabitants, although at first they are seldom recognized as animals.

A mature walker may be mistaken for a dark hill moving across the plain. Its body hangs between rows of pillar limbs, some fleshy, some mineral, some borrowed from the thickets rooted on its back. It places each foot with extraordinary care. Weight travels through it in waves, so that no single point bears the whole creature at once. On shelves where gravity fluctuates, sections of its body may walk at different heavinesses.

The thicket it carries is not camouflage. It is part of the walker’s metabolism and also more than that.

Black leaves cool its blood. Blue-lit roots conduct charge between its organs. Bladder fruits store rain above its spine. Grazers trim fungal overgrowth from its joints. Burrowing scavengers enter pores in its feet and return carrying rare salts. Small hunters patrol the canopy. Pollinators breed in the warm mist of its breathing vents. Entire lineages are born, feed, and become extinct without ever touching the ground.

The walker itself drinks rarely. It inherits water.

Its reservoirs are layered through the body, each containing liquid from a different region: sulfurous rain from the eastern shelves, clear condensation from sheltered hollows, dense iridescent fluid taken from uphill pools. Mixing these waters would kill it. Separate organs preserve their incompatible properties, and the walker draws upon them according to the local rules through which it travels.

When it reaches a fissure, it becomes still. Plates open along its underside. Rootlike vessels descend into the vegetation below and graft onto whatever tissues accept them. For several hours, the walker and ravine exchange fluid, heat, spores, parasites, electrical patterns, and memory.

Memory on the plateau is not stored only in nerves. It circulates chemically through ecosystems.

A ravine touched by a walker may receive the remembered scent of rain that fell a thousand kilometers away. Plants respond by flowering before that rain arrives. Larvae alter their bodies for conditions their ancestors encountered on another shelf. The walker, in return, absorbs impressions held in root mats: the approach of a folded distance, the taste of a newly risen cliff, the passage of a predator that will not become audible for another month.

The great walkers are neither beasts of burden nor wandering islands. They carry nothing for anyone. Yet through them the plateau’s separated ecologies remain one ecology.

Young walkers begin as mobile gardens with almost no animal beneath them. A mat of plants creeps across warm rock, gathering symbionts. It thickens, develops reservoirs, raises itself on roots, and eventually learns to step. Only much later does a central nervous system condense around the routes it has taken.

Old walkers become heavier in the eastern shelves, where age acquires weight. Eventually they cannot lift their legs. They settle over fractures and root permanently. Their pillar limbs hollow into chimneys. Their reservoirs become ponds. The gardens spread down their sides until no clear anatomy remains.

Whether this is death is uncertain. Sometimes, centuries of delayed sound rise through such a rooted walker, and all its leaves open at once.

Near the magnetic caverns live the asymmetrical ones.

Every repeated structure in that region draws charge, so these creatures continually spoil their own symmetry. One leg grows long while another dissolves. A left eye divides as the right seals shut. Paired lungs twist into different organs, one breathing vapor and one straining iron from blood. Their offspring are born almost symmetrical and therefore dangerously bright to the fields below. Parents immediately begin the necessary work of making them uneven: biting off mirrored frills, turning one limb backward, inoculating one side with growths.

Beauty there is measured in successful imbalance.

Some asymmetrical creatures possess clusters of sensory organs that never perceive the same present. One eye sees the body’s current surroundings; another sees the nearest place where gravity is weaker; a third sees whatever the animal would encounter if it turned around and waited. They move with a halting precision, seeming indecisive while choosing among adjacent realities.

They are hunted by creatures with no fixed side.

These pursuers roll their anatomy continuously through themselves. A mouth becomes a stomach, a stomach becomes a tail, the tail opens into an eye. No organ repeats long enough to attract a magnetic charge. When they feed, they pass prey through a traveling corridor of digestion that circles the body and eventually consumes its own entrance.

Farther inward are animals that migrate between laws.

They gather at the edges of altered regions before those regions are visible. Light-bodied giants wait for a weakening of gravity, then step upward and graze from the undersides of clouds. Burrowers press against solid cliffs until a passing storm makes stone porous to flesh. They enter in herds, swimming through basalt, and are sometimes trapped when solidity returns. Generations later, a new fracture exposes them alive within the wall, still moving toward grass that no longer exists.

Others inhabit multiple depths of the same ravine. Their bodies appear as separate animals at different levels: a pale swimmer in the warm upper water, a many-legged browser on a ledge far below, a luminous sac drifting among the star-small stones at the bottom. These are not stages of one life but simultaneous portions of one creature. Injury to one depth produces caution in the others. Feeding above causes growth below. When the ravine settles into a single geometry, the portions rush together. The resulting animal is usually brief and confused.

There are also the witnesses of the calm pools.

They are pale, delicate swimmers no longer than a finger, each surrounded by a halo of unrealized motion. Their possible paths remain visible around them as faint luminous bodies: the turn not taken, the dive postponed, the sudden flight into air. Most animals collapse such possibilities by moving. The swimmers preserve them.

They feed by choosing not to make certain motions. Abandoned movements condense along their gills as a fine blue substance, which they consume. A pool rich in them seems crowded even when only three or four are present.

When danger comes, they do not flee. They select a motion from the halo and disappear into it. The water is left holding only the other possibilities, which slowly fade.

These swimmers may be among the few creatures that experience the plateau as a sequence. They gather at still water because reflections provide them with additional futures. A predator’s reflection can attack before the predator does; a swimmer can escape into the difference.

Beneath all of these inhabitants, inside the hot shelves and rotating strata, lives something slower.

It cannot be separated cleanly from the mineral machinery. It occupies pressure chambers, ore-veins, chimney roots, magnetic caverns, and the creeping belts of glass. It may be one organism or a convergence of many unrelated forms that have entered the same immense metabolism.

Its tissues are stone valves and films only molecules thick. Its circulation takes centuries in some regions and seconds in others. It feeds on differences: hot and cold, wet and dry, magnetic and still, present and almost present. It separates metals, raises vapor, extrudes towers, and supplies charge to the surface ecologies. In return, roots and buried animals carry organic compounds downward.

Nothing commands this exchange. There is no center from which the deep life governs. If it thinks, thought occurs as redistribution: a ridge rising, a storm diverted, an ore-vein contracting around an irritation. If it remembers, memory is preserved in the tilt of shelves and the chemistry of rain.

The surface creatures do not worship it. They are not aware of a distinction between it and the ground.

Still, before the plateau’s great upheavals, many species perform the same acts. The vanes close. The glass grazers flatten their silver fans. Walkers lower their gardens and spread their feet. Predators withdraw their films from exposed stone. Swarms arrange themselves into dark rings around empty air. Even dormant rain-grains rotate within their cracks.

For one suspended moment, every scale of life adopts stillness.

Then a province begins to rise.

The deep organism—or the geology, if those are different things—opens fresh wounds across the shelves. Heat-loving growth pours from them already mature. Buried herds emerge with mineral lungs pumping. New walkers unfold from compressed gardens. Flyers stream upward in forms no swarm has used before. Predators spread over cliffs whose stone is younger than their hunger.

Nothing waits to learn the new country. The inhabitants arrive adapted because adaptation here does not always follow arrival.

The land lifts beneath them. Their bodies alter as it rises. Water passes from reservoir to root, from root to cloud, from cloud to unborn mouth. A delayed impact begins traveling upward through the stone.

Weeks from now, in some sheltered hollow, it will surface as a single colossal note.

By then, everything that hears it may have become something else.
