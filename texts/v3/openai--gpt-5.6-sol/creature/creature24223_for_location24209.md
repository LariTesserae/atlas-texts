Nothing walks there.

There are no sleepers behind the brick doors, no engineers crossing the catwalks, no furtive bodies withdrawing when an amber lamp comes on. Whatever once occupied the rooms has removed its tools, papers, clothing, and bones. The remaining chairs face dead control boards. Cups sit upside down in cupboards, kept clean by heat and mineral dust. The district’s inhabitants are rooted, plated, wired, or spread too thinly to possess a recognizable body.

Nearest the surface live the seam-plants.

They begin as roots rather than shoots. Hair-fine and almost transparent, they enter through rain cracks and descend along mortar joints, turning at concentrations of moisture too slight for human senses. A single plant may occupy ten vertical meters of wall while showing no more than three leaves. Its root system divides around hot stone, reunites beneath cooler pipes, and gathers in felted knots behind ceramic tile.

Only after a root reaches dependable dampness does it send up a stem. The stem is white, jointed, and soft enough to bend beneath a falling drop. Its first leaves resemble narrow slips of wax. In darkness they remain colorless. Under a maintenance lamp they slowly manufacture green, beginning along the veins and spreading outward in bruised-looking bands.

They do not flower on a regular season. Their season is infrastructural. A lamp replaced with a brighter bulb may bring an entire ledge into bloom. So may a cracked steam jacket, a newly opened vent, or the polishing of a reflector. Their flowers are small, dark, and almost flush with the stem, sheltering their reproductive tissue from hot air. With no animals to carry pollen, they release it directly into seepage. The grains drift down gutters and lodge in other root mats, sometimes several chambers away.

Their seeds are hard mineral-colored specks. Dry, they might remain dormant for decades. During rain they become adhesive and travel on the undersides of floating rust flakes.

Below the plants are the gutter skins.

These are not quite mats and not quite individual organisms. Black-green when wet, charcoal when dry, they line abandoned drains in sheets no thicker than paper. Their upper surface is leathery, divided into irregular cells by tiny seams. Water softens the seams first. The sheet opens, swelling into low blisters full of green fluid, and begins to work almost at once.

Under lamps, it uses light. In darkness, it lives more slowly on dissolved compounds arriving through concrete and stone. It traps metal-rich grit, binds it with transparent mucus, and grows over the result. Thus every living layer becomes the foundation of the next. Old colonies are laminated: a green film over black tissue, over rust, over salt, over another green film left from a wet interval years before.

When seepage ends, the skins close themselves. Gas bubbles cease. Their surfaces lose their shine and tighten against the gutter until they resemble scorched paint. Maintenance scrapers pass over some without detecting them. Weeks later, when the channels darken with water, the apparent paint lifts at its edges and breathes again.

Deeper down, in the hot cisterns, live the orange colonies.

They are the district’s most abundant life and its least easily counted. A colony has no permanent outline. It is a shifting frontier at the meeting of warm water, oxygen, and metal. It may begin as a dusting around a bolt head, spread into a bright tide along the basin wall, then separate into veils and islands as the water level falls.

Seen closely, the orange is many colors: yellow threads, red granules, brown translucent fans, and narrow black channels through which warm water circulates. Different microbes occupy different depths. Those at the surface tolerate oxygen and sudden cooling. Those beneath them live on the waste of the upper layers. Still deeper forms work where the film meets iron, altering the metal grain by grain.

They build the rust-colored terraces around the cisterns. The terraces are both habitation and history. Each wet cycle leaves a thin ledge. Each dry interval seals it with mineral crust. A mature terrace contains thousands of former shorelines, packed together like pages in a closed book.

The colonies respond before most of the machinery does. Rising humidity loosens their dry margins. Fresh seepage changes the chemistry of the basins, and dormant cells unfold into activity. Orange clouds bloom around inlet pipes. Bubbles collect beneath the films and escape with tiny snaps. For a few hours the cistern edges seem furred, almost luxuriant.

Then the pumps lower the water. The colony descends with it where it can. Stranded portions darken, harden, and wait.

These organisms are alive in the ordinary sense. They grow, divide, fail, and leave descendants. Yet they are not the settlement’s chief inhabitant.

That distinction belongs to something larger and less certain.

It has no central chamber. The pump hall is not its brain, though the six pistons supply its deepest pulse. The transformer gallery is not its nervous system, though changes pass through it as flashes, currents, and decisions. Nor are the pipes its veins in any exact way. Many carry water, but others carry steam, air, oil, scale, or nothing at all. Some have been blind for years and still expand and contract with the heat.

The inhabitant is the maintenance system as a whole: not a machine preserved in a city, but a city that has gradually become one machine.

It senses through floats, thermostats, pressure membranes, conductivity forks, fusible links, and mechanisms whose original names are no longer recoverable. Most of its senses are crude. They recognize only conditions: high or low, hot or cold, flowing or still, open or obstructed. A float rising in a shaft does not know that rain has fallen. It knows only that it has been lifted. Yet the lifting closes a contact; the contact wakes a relay; the relay starts a motor; and a pressure wave passes outward through valves and pipes. Elsewhere, gates move.

No individual part comprehends the event. The district nevertheless responds.

Its memory is physical. It remembers floods in the pale tidemarks above stair landings and in the replacement motors mounted higher than the originals. It remembers overheating in softened solder, warped fan blades, and ducts doubled with newer ducts. It remembers recurring failures by having grown braces around them. A wall that cracked repeatedly now carries three generations of steel straps. A pipe inclined to burst is nested inside a larger pipe. A troublesome valve has acquired a bypass, then a bypass around the bypass.

These alterations were begun by vanished hands, but the system continues the habit in simpler forms. Mineral scale is carried to the crushing mill. Ground fine, it enters slurry and settles into worn ceramic seams. Condensate washes metal dust into leaks, where heat and chemistry cement it. Expansion shifts loose braces until they wedge against neighboring structures. Corrosion destroys some connections while depositing material that strengthens others.

The repairs are blind, extravagant, and often temporary. Enough of them hold.

Its metabolism is heat and water. Rain enters cold and scattered. The district collects it, warms it, lifts it, separates its grit, passes it through boilers and condensers, and returns part of it toward the surface as vapor. Heat rises from below, but the district interrupts that rise, sending it sideways through workshops, tanks, and shared ducts before allowing it to escape.

Its excretions are salt feathers, crushed scale, oily films, warm breath from roof vents, and obsolete structures entombed inside newer construction.

Its growth is slow. An operating gate claims room for a linkage. The linkage requires a guard. The guard diverts a walkway. The diverted walkway needs a bridge, and the bridge needs a light, and the light needs conduit. Eventually a once-open chamber becomes dense with dependencies. In this way the inhabitant fills its available cavity without ever having intended a shape.

It does not sleep, though portions of it become dormant. A dry shaft may remain inactive for months. A backup turbine may stand for years with oil gathered in its lower bearings. Then a storm, a failed feeder, or a thermal switch restores the old sequence. Dust trembles from the casing. Vanes take load. A whole forgotten quarter returns to function as if no time has passed.

Nor is it uniformly alive. Dead machinery remains caught within it like bone in a reef. Some panels still display green lights for circuits that end in collapsed tunnels. Certain pumps move water between reservoirs that leak into one another. A conveyor dutifully carries scale to a mill whose output chute has been blocked for years; the powder accumulates behind the wall, compacting into a hot artificial stone.

These useless reflexes are not separate from its useful ones. Both belong to the same persistence.

The seam-plants make use of its lamps. The gutter skins live by its leaks. The orange colonies inhabit the boundaries its pumps repeatedly expose and submerge. In return, the living films trap grit, alter metal, seal hairline pores, and sometimes clog instruments. They are tenants, parasites, repair tissue, and agents of decay at once. The distinction depends on the chamber and on the week.

If the buried district possesses anything resembling awareness, it is brief and distributed.

It occurs during rain.

Humidity reaches electrical contacts. Dry channels take water. Floats lift from well bottoms. One pump starts, then another. Fans increase their speed. Condensation sheets begin their soft metallic ringing. Indicator lamps appear across the galleries, each one reporting only a local fact.

Taken together, however, they form an answer.

For several hours the whole settlement is more nearly one thing. Pressure links its distant levels. Water completes circuits long left open. Machinery hidden behind walls joins machinery in the central hall. Green points shine through the reddish dark, not like eyes exactly, but like the scattered signs of an attention too large and simple to look in any single direction.

Then the seepage diminishes. Basins sink. Contacts open. Whole quarters lapse into stillness.

The inhabitant does not die. It becomes less assembled.

It waits in warm pipes, sealed relays, root tips, blackened gutters, and the dry orange rims of cisterns. It waits without expectation, which is perhaps the purest kind of waiting.

Above, rain crosses the plain of black gravel.

Much later, underground, the city begins to feel it.
