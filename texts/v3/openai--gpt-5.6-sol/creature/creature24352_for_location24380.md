What lives in the basin is not adapted to water alone. It is adapted to water under instruction.

The most abundant lives are the least animal-like: transparent laminations suspended where unlike waters touch. They have no fixed outline. In still conditions they resemble flaws in glass, broad as a hand and thinner than skin; under a pressure change they pleat themselves into ribbons, cups, and many-layered fans. They grow along differences—heat, salinity, dissolved charge—and vanish when those differences disappear. A warm rust-bearing seep entering a cold rain pool may clothe itself in them within minutes. Once the waters mix, only a faint gelatinous dust remains.

These veils are the basin’s first mouths. They do not eat substances so much as the passage of substances from one state to another. Iron oxidizing, salts diffusing, a weak current escaping ceramic insulation: each transition allows them to thicken. They leave no green stain and require no light. Their waste is a fine mineral grain, often metallic, around which much of the basin’s later life builds its shells.

The clear swimmers feed on the veils.

At rest, a swimmer is easy to mistake for a splinter of ice. Its body is an elongated bladder, jointless and nearly invisible, with a black visceral knot held near the center by radiating threads. Along one side runs a blue luminous organ, not bright enough to cast light but sufficient to mark the animal’s position in deep water. It swims by changing the pressure inside a series of chambers. The resulting motion is smooth, with none of the paddling or undulation expected of a small aquatic creature. It simply inclines and is drawn forward.

Their light brightens near active cables and fades in rainwater. Large schools form around outlets moments before water begins to flow, suggesting that they feel changes traveling through the pipes long before the machinery announces them audibly. When alarmed, they descend together. A pool full of stars becomes, in an instant, black.

The intake grilles are not hazards to them. They are passages in a migration.

Young swimmers enter narrow enough to pass through mesh. They disappear into conduits where there should be little oxygen and less food. Those that return are broader, darker, and weighted with grains arranged in neat lines along their bodies. Some carry iron, others copper or pale flakes of ceramic. The grains may be nourishment, ballast, or the beginnings of another form. Mature swimmers eventually settle against stone, empty their luminous chambers, and harden around themselves.

Thus some—perhaps all—of the shell animals begin.

A newly fixed shell is translucent and flexible. Over time it incorporates whatever the surrounding water provides: calcite, rust, silica, traces of old alloys. No two colonies are quite the same color. Those around iron are amber or brown; those on ceramic are milk-white; those near electrical leakage acquire smoky violet margins. Each animal possesses several overlapping valves, closing with the clean click of porcelain.

A colony behaves less like a crowd than a slowly deliberating tissue. Individuals attach in rings around a leak, their shells angled so that the escaping water passes through them. They feed from the flow while narrowing it. As mineral matter accumulates between their bodies, the ring becomes a gasket. The animals at the most forceful point grow thickest first, diverting pressure toward their neighbors until the joint is sealed evenly.

Then, deprived of the gradient that fed them, they depart.

They do not crawl. They release themselves one after another during a pressure pulse and are carried away like chips of bone. A colony may take days to migrate a distance of several feet. Yet its next settlement is rarely accidental. The shells gather at another faulty seam, another outlet, another place where two conditions meet. Across years, they move through the machinery as maintenance moves: closing some paths, causing pressure to seek others, following the consequences of their own repairs.

The flat grazers travel behind them.

These are low, many-footed animals with bodies divided into thin interlocking plates. Their undersides are soft and crowded with mouthparts. Their backs imitate the surfaces on which they feed—black on wet slate, orange beside rust, chalky under calcite—though the match comes from embedded mineral rather than pigment. A large one is no wider than two fingers, but some are longer than a hand, their age visible in the number of added plates.

They rasp newly exposed metal and fresh deposits. What they remove is often too thin to see until their feeding tracks overlap into pale arcs. They do not appear to consume the metal itself. They harvest the invisible communities living on the electrochemical boundary, along with the energized compounds forming there. A polished surface is, to them, an exhausted field.

Their trails have consequences. By stripping corrosion from one side of a fitting and leaving the other untouched, they alter where current passes. By opening a clean patch on an alloy, they create a new gradient, and the transparent veils gather there. The grazers cultivate without knowing that they cultivate—unless the distinction between instinct and engineering has no meaning in the basin.

In the paving joints live the wire-worms. Most of each worm remains hidden below the stone. The exposed portion may be no thicker than a hair, colorless except for a dark, wedge-shaped head. When pressure knocks through the buried system, hundreds emerge at once and lean in the same direction.

They do not merely sense vibration. They seem to read it.

A short pulse brings them partway out. A falling pressure draws them farther. Certain widely spaced knocks cause neighboring worms to touch heads and form temporary meshes across a runnel. Swimmers shelter behind these meshes; mineral grains lodge against them; veils bloom in the slowed boundary water. When flow reverses, the worms dismantle the net and withdraw before it can tear.

Their buried bodies may be much larger than their visible threads suggest. Occasionally a paving slab lifts by the breadth of a coin, and beneath it can be seen a pale braided mass running from joint to joint. Whether this is a colony or one divided creature is unknown. Cutting a strand would not answer the question. The severed ends seek each other.

Predators live among this patient abundance.

The needle-mouth is made for stillness. Its body consists of rigid wet spines arranged around a central opening, with six or eight finer limbs concealed beneath. It wedges itself among bolts and mineral spikes, orienting according to current. Its resemblance to debris is nearly perfect until a swimmer passes. Then the spines fold inward faster than the eye can follow. A moment later they reopen, empty, while the blue light of the prey dims inside the predator’s central sac.

Needle-mouths grow by replacing their spines. Cast-off spines remain upright in the mineral crust, so a thriving hunting ground gradually fills with false predators. Swimmers avoid both living and dead forms. In this way the animal’s discarded parts continue hunting by directing prey toward the places where it waits.

The shadow-disks are less mechanical in appearance and perhaps stranger. A disk spreads itself over the bottom of a pool until it resembles a patch where illumination simply fails. It has no stable edge. Its circumference frays into lobes that enter tiny cracks, tasting changes in brightness and charge. It does not pursue prey when prey moves. It moves when the light moves—when a cloud passes above, when swimmers brighten, when a cable’s field wanders through the water.

Anything caught beneath it is digested slowly. For hours afterward, points of blue may remain visible through the dark flesh, drifting toward the disk’s center.

The creatures of the droplets are quicker than either. They spend their entire adult lives inside beads of condensation. Several can inhabit a droplet no larger than a pinhead, skating along its inner surface without breaking it. They feed on charged particles gathered from the air and reproduce when the droplet begins to lengthen before falling. One body divides into dozens of silver motes during the descent.

Impact is their dispersal.

Most burst or dry at once. Survivors enter pools, where they remain active only briefly before encysting on ceilings, ledges, and the undersides of rails. They wait there as metallic dust until humidity encloses them in water again. Their generations are measured not in days but in drops.

Above all these smaller lives walks the six-footed animal.

It is rarely seen complete. One first notices a foot placed soundlessly on the side of a pump housing, then a second far beyond where the body ought to reach. Each foot has six narrow toes radiating from a central pad, leaving star-shaped prints in condensation. The legs are long, hinged, and almost black, though their joints are pale and semitransparent. They rise toward a body held high in the mist.

That body changes according to posture. Seen between pipes, it appears narrow as a cable bundle. Crossing open paving, it seems broad, hung with folded plates. There may be more than one creature, or a single creature with limbs capable of uncoupling and taking separate routes. Its gait supports both possibilities. Three feet sometimes remain motionless on one pump while the other three proceed to the next, linked for a moment by filaments too fine to see except when droplets form on them.

It walks chiefly during periods of system instability: heavy rain, prolonged electrical leakage, an impending draw from the deep shaft. It visits leaking joints and shell colonies but does not eat them. Instead it lowers a structure from beneath its body—part mouth, part tool—and removes selected shells. Sometimes it carries them elsewhere. Sometimes it crushes them against exposed metal, releasing a milky fluid that hardens under current.

The night walker also tends the cables. Where one twitches against stone, the animal braces it with two feet until the movement passes. Where insulation has split, it wraps the opening in a translucent secretion that gradually blackens. Its behavior resembles repair, but not repair toward any human design. It preserves flow, pressure difference, and the possibility of leakage. A pipe sealed too perfectly may be opened again by one precise puncture.

Close observation would likely reveal that the walker is not one species but an assembly. Its plates resemble mature grazer shells. Blue points pulse within its joints. Fine worms extend from its feet to test the stone ahead. What seems to be a large animal may be a mobile treaty among many smaller ones, gathered temporarily around a framework of scavenged metal.

Perhaps the basin builds it when needed.

Beneath everything is the shaft-dweller.

Nothing definite can be said of its shape. It announces itself through displacement. The water in multiple pools lowers at once, though the channels between them may be too shallow to account for the change. Cables draw taut. Warm bubbles rise carrying red oxide and small empty shells. After several minutes—or sometimes an hour—the water returns from below.

The draw is too regular to be collapse and too forceful to be feeding as ordinary animals feed. It resembles inhalation, except that it may be the machinery inhaling through an animal, or an animal operating the machinery as a lung.

Fragments occasionally rise from the deepest shaft: curved plates pitted with bolt-shaped impressions; lengths of pale flexible material with copper filaments embedded like nerves; translucent membranes stamped by old grille patterns. None remains intact for long. Grazers reduce them, shell colonies cover them, or the six-footed walker carries them back to the water.

The shaft-dweller may be immense. It may also be distributed through the lower chambers as the wire-worms are distributed through the paving. Its body could include flooded reservoirs, piston cylinders, rootlike colonies inside pipes, and the long hanging cables that twitch when it shifts. The low sound heard once or twice a day may be a valve closing in response to it—or part of it closing.

The surviving machinery belongs among the inhabitants as surely as any shell or swimmer. It has metabolism without autonomy, organs without a single body. Corrosion opens pathways; animals close them. Current attracts veils; grazers expose current. Shells seal pressure into one conduit until an old piston moves and sends pressure elsewhere. Each reaction becomes the condition for the next.

No individual rules this system. Yet the system shows preferences.

It preserves differences. It resists complete mixing, complete drainage, complete stillness. It permits failures that create new gradients and corrects failures that would end them. The amber indicator in the control booth may measure water level, load, pressure, charge, or something for which the makers had a narrower name. To the basin’s creatures, its needle is season, tide, and command. They array themselves around its position because generations that did not do so have vanished.

In the end, the most accurate answer may be that the basin is inhabited by a single life whose parts have not agreed to share a skin.

Its blood is water of several kinds. Its smallest tissues are the transparent veils. Swimmers carry material through its vessels. Shell colonies clot its wounds. Grazers reopen useful sores. Wire-worms transmit pressure through the joints. Predators prevent any one moving part from multiplying without limit. The six-footed assembly serves as a wandering hand. Something in the lower chambers breathes.

And the old machinery—corroded, intermittent, purposeless by the standards of its builders—provides the skeleton, the valves, perhaps the memory.

When the deep water shifts and every creature becomes still, it is not necessarily fear that stops them. It may be attention. For that brief interval the scattered inhabitants align: shells closed, worms extended, swimmers suspended, walker braced above the shaft. The basin becomes aware across all its separate bodies.

Then the signal passes. The awareness loosens. Each thing returns to being itself.

A droplet falls.

A valve moves.

Life resumes the work of keeping the ruin unfinished.
