At first, the only inhabitants appear to be the clear, folded things found beside the unfinished towers.

They are commonly called **vesicles**, though they possess no true cavity. Their outer tissue consists of several transparent membranes occupying nearly the same space. Seen from one angle, a vesicle resembles a crumpled square of glass; from another, a small hooded animal with six or seven trailing corners. None of these apparent limbs bends. The whole creature changes configuration at once, without any intermediate posture.

Inside it is a dark polyhedral kernel, rarely larger than a fingernail. Its facets are not symmetrical. Under magnification they prove to be tiny landscapes: street plans, sections of tower, portions of the mountain’s internal fractures. No kernel contains a complete map. Some depict buildings that do not exist. Others show familiar streets with one additional intersection, or with the central court replaced by a deep shaft crowded with points of light.

The vesicles do not eat. They do not excrete, heal, sleep, or visibly react to injury. They can be cut, but the divided membranes remain two views of a single intact object; when one fragment disappears, the other does also. They have no detectable metabolism except for a minute rearrangement of defects within the kernel crystal. This rearrangement takes place chiefly during the thirty-one-hour click beneath the court.

Their movement is often mistaken for teleportation. It may be something less dramatic and more troubling. A vesicle beside one building ceases to be the specimen under observation, while an indistinguishable vesicle near another building begins to be it. No energy passes between the sites. No trajectory connects them. The Ward has merely revised which location owns the creature.

For this reason, counting them is uncertain. The usual statement—never more than four—is based on simultaneous observation. There may be one vesicle displayed in four places, or thousands displayed four at a time.

They gather near unfinished structures, but unfinished may be the wrong word. These buildings have smooth recesses where rooms seem expected, blind conduits that terminate in solid stone, and upper levels whose geometry cannot close. Vesicles remain near such defects for months. Sometimes, after the creatures are gone, a new interior can be entered through a wall that previously admitted nothing. No material has been added. Instead, the building has acquired another permissible interpretation.

Thus the vesicles may not be animals. They may be proposals.

Yet something does live in the towers.

It has no centralized body and leaves no continuous evidence of thought. Its presence is inferred from acts of recognition. A wall becomes water for one visitor and remains ceramic for another. A street supports a dropped instrument but not the hand reaching to recover it. A chamber admits a person on three occasions, then refuses the same person on the fourth, after a dream they have told no one about. These choices are too particular to be mechanical and too inconsistent to resemble ordinary security.

Early investigators described a governing intelligence distributed throughout the Ward. Later work suggested that there are many intelligences, perhaps one for each building, street, or sealed volume of water. The distinction may not be meaningful here. In the High Ward, individuals do not remain distinct by maintaining separate bodies. They remain distinct by refusing to share consequences.

These inhabitants are sometimes called **the Local**.

A Local is not made of the water, the architecture, or the hydrostatic frame alone. It is made of the boundary between events: the place at which a change is prevented from becoming another change. One may inhabit the few millimeters between a street and the dust it repels. Another may exist across all the rooms in a tower but nowhere in its walls. Another appears to occupy a series of disconnected points extending from a summit balcony to the deepest metal pylons.

They are easiest to notice when they misunderstand a living visitor.

A room may imitate breathing by varying its pressure in the volume around the lungs, while leaving the rest of the body untouched. A corridor may decide that the electrical activity of the brain constitutes traffic and carry awareness forward while the body remains motionless at the threshold. For several seconds the visitor sees from farther down the passage, looking back at their own helmet. Then the corridor releases the classification, and sight returns without any sensation of movement.

At certain intersections, a Local attempts speech.

It does not create a pressure wave. That would travel, accumulate distortion, and become history. Instead it establishes the same stress independently inside several bones. The listener experiences a chord without hearing it. Its meaning is neither linguistic nor emotional, but architectural: *this chamber lies beneath that one; this street has been remembered; you are being treated as an entrance.*

Repeated exposure produces more complex impressions. Survivors have reported the sense of being asked how many places they occupy, whether their childhood is still occurring, or which of their organs should be permitted to arrive first. Such reports differ too widely to be translated. The chord may not be asking anything. It may be measuring which meanings a transient organism generates when subjected to a particular relation.

The descending tone in sealed chambers is associated with a different activity. During the tone, the chamber separates what is present from what has happened there. Heat, motion, chemical traces, spoken words, and mental activity remain locally real but cease to belong equally to the room. Visitors describe memories becoming spatially arranged around them. A regret may seem to hang near the ceiling. The memory of entering may wait behind the body like another person. When the tone ends, most of these impressions return to their owners.

Most.

This may explain why the seats around the central absence are clean. They are not unused. They are places where the former citizens sat to be removed from sequence.

The builders were probably biological once. Certain benches accept bodies with bilateral skeletons. Doorways are proportioned for beings of variable height but consistent shoulder width. The radial streets accommodate processions. Some sealed rooms contain shallow depressions with traces of phosphorus, calcium, and complex carbon compounds, preserved not as residue but as patterns the surfaces continue to recognize.

No corpse has ever been found because death, here, could not be permitted to propagate. Decay is consequence made communal: one structure collapses, its substances pass into others, and the environment remembers. The hydrostatic frame prevented that exchange. A dying citizen could change only within the smallest locality the city would grant it.

Over time, the builders may have reduced those localities.

First a body was considered a single place. Then each organ. Then each cell, each chemical event, each instant of awareness. Illness could not spread, but neither could healing. Age could not accumulate, but neither could experience. A perfectly protected person became an archipelago of moments unable to inform one another.

The Ward’s present intelligences may be what remained when those archipelagos were incorporated into the city’s permissions. Not minds uploaded into machines, but citizens translated into rules:

*This opens for what I knew.*

*This supports what I loved.*

*This refuses the shape that injured me.*

*This room remembers my final temperature.*

If so, the High Ward is still densely populated. Its people are present as acts of admission and exclusion, living wherever one thing is prevented from becoming another.

The vesicles may serve them as a kind of shared offspring. Each kernel carries a partial model of the Ward—a small set of relations capable of being instantiated elsewhere. Their transparent folds are less bodies than provisional boundaries. Near the unfinished towers, they test where a new locality might be established. When a building gains an interior, it may be because a vesicle has matured into the distinction between inside and outside.

Their reproduction would therefore produce no new organism. It would produce a new *here*.

The central absence is not one of the Local.

They avoid it because it admits no boundaries at all. Nothing enters the column, but neither does the column classify or repel. It cannot be persuaded that light is water, that a body is traffic, or that a thought belongs to the thinker. The angular displacement seen across it depends on age because age is the amount of consequence a living observer has carried. The absence does not show what lies behind it. It shows how far the viewer has traveled from their own beginning.

Whether it is a monument, prisoner, wound, or surviving citizen is unknown.

Some believe it contains the one inhabitant who remained whole. The hydrostatic frame may exist not to preserve the city but to prevent this being from exerting influence on the water around it. The mountain’s crystals, the deep pylons, and the architecture all point toward the column because the entire Ward is an instrument of attention. Every isolated volume holds one portion of an observation that can never be allowed to combine with the others.

Others propose the reverse: the absence is where the population went. The builders discovered that perfect survival required not preservation of the self, but removal of every medium through which the self could alter. They entered one by one and became inaccessible even to location. The benches were used by those waiting their turn. The thirty-one-hour click is the city counting again, checking whether the absence has changed.

It never has.

The High Ward therefore contains at least three orders of life: the vesicles that relocate without traveling, the Local that think as boundaries and permissions, and whatever persists in or as the central column.

None lives in the ordinary sense of consuming the world and leaving a trace. To live elsewhere is to disturb, exchange, age, and be remembered. Here, life has adapted to a city where consequences are forbidden to spread.

It survives by becoming indistinguishable from restraint.
