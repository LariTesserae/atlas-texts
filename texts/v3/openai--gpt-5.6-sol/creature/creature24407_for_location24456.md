At first, the inhabitants seem to be ordinary mountain creatures caught at unusual moments.

There are birds above the tarn, insects among the vents, amphibians in the shallows. There are fish, snails, mites, and soft many-legged things pressed into the wet seams of the cliffs. They have recognizable anatomies, but not recognizable lives. None is ever observed arriving, resting, hunting, or fleeing. Each exists in the posture by which it performs its part.

The birds are called, if they can be called anything, the **Held**.

Each occupies a particular volume of air. Some hang with wings spread, others crouch on ledges with their heads angled toward fissures, listening to sounds that have always already occurred. Their feathers are soaked but never grow heavier. Condensation gathers on the barbs, enlarges, and remains there without falling.

The birds do not fly. Flight lives in them.

Their muscles contain the strain of an endless downstroke. Their hearts are rapid, hot engines sealed inside cold bodies, yet no beat succeeds another. Beneath the tarn, their reflections behave differently. Those dim counterparts wheel, dive, quarrel, and vanish into reflected valleys. It may be that the visible birds are only anchor points, fastening those more active creatures to the surface of the basin. Occasionally a reflected bird returns carrying something in its beak: a twig, a bright organ, a loop of lightning. The bird above receives nothing, but for an instant its eyes shine with the color of the carried object.

The amphibians along the shore are heavier and older in appearance, though age has never touched them. Their skins are black, blue, or the reddish brown of cooled iron. Pale lines run beneath the skin in geometric branches, connecting throat, spine, and fingertips. Their inflated sacs produce the hammering and turbine notes that fill the notch. No two animals sound the same. One is a high metallic whine. Another gives off the concussion of enormous presses. Another contains the rhythm of chains dragged through a cavern.

They are not calling to one another. Their chorus has no message.

The sound is part of their metabolism—or perhaps their bodies have condensed around preexisting noises, as shells form around grit. If one were somehow lifted from the water, the note would remain where it had been sitting, while the animal became silent and transparent in the hand. But nothing lifts them. Their long fingers are sunk through the mud and into the root bundles below, coupling the tarn to the deeper mechanism.

Around the cliff vents stand the copper beetles. They are small enough to cover with a thumbnail, yet each produces a hum vast enough to make the teeth ache. Their open wing cases expose translucent membranes crowded with dark branching veins. Cold vapor passes through these membranes without moving them. The beetles appear to regulate pressure, although no vent ever opens further or closes. Their rings are exact but not symmetrical: each contains one apparent gap. Watched long enough, the gap proves to be occupied by a beetle too near to be seen, its shell reflecting the back of the observer’s own eye.

The smallest inhabitants are stranger.

Glass mites live inside individual droplets, grazing on minute flashes of light. They have faceted bodies and a multitude of legs extending in directions the droplet does not seem to contain. Their tracks can be seen as fine scratches in the reflection of the sky, though the water itself remains unmarked. The slender fish inhabiting films on the rock possess neither left nor right sides. One face looks toward the stone and one toward the air. Their gills admit the same water in both directions, extracting not oxygen but sequence. Around them, tiny intervals vanish. This may be why nothing in the basin can quite proceed to its next condition.

In the moss dwell broad, slow forms that initially resemble stones. Their backs are planted with sedges and miniature ferns; roots pass through their bodies without injury. They have mouths underneath, held open against the saturated earth. These mouths contain no teeth or stomachs. Instead, each opens onto another patch of the basin, sometimes only an arm’s length away, sometimes deep within the impossible interior of the mountain. They feed the place to itself.

None of these creatures is born. None dies. There are eggs, larvae, shed skins, nests, opened seed cases, and small clean skeletons, but these are not earlier or later stages. Each is an inhabitant in its own right. An egg does not contain a future animal. It contains the continuous pressure of hatching. A skeleton is not a remainder; it performs the precise work of being dead without any creature having died to produce it.

Thus predator and prey may be joined in permanent events. A pale centipede is forever half-swallowed by a fern. A moth hangs between the jaws of a cliff lizard, its powdery wings uncreased. The lizard feeds, the moth is eaten, and neither is diminished. Nutrition passes between them as a closed vibration. Hunger exists, satisfaction exists, but neither follows the other.

The larger creatures glimpsed through the fissures are not a separate species. They are the same inhabitants encountered at another scale.

A gearlike ring revolving around a shaft of lightning is a colony of mouths, each mouth belonging to a dark animal whose body extends out of sight. The contracting chambers are hearts without owners. Forests of cilia are the feeding fans of beings so immense that the mountain occupies only a pore in their skin. Yet viewed from elsewhere—perhaps from within a droplet—those same beings may be mites, trembling on the back of a beetle.

No scale is fundamental here. Every inhabitant is both organism and organ.

Together they form the creature that truly lives in the notch.

It has no single body except the basin, no central mind except the exact correspondence of all its parts. The roots are its nerves and conveyor lines. The cliffs are its lungs. The tarn is an eye that looks both upward and down. Birds hold its distances apart; fish remove duration from its water; amphibians maintain its great internal noise. Moss seals it against consequence. The things inside the mountain digest process itself, taking every event that might produce change and returning it to the instant of its beginning.

This composite being is not asleep. Sleep implies waking. It is not imprisoned, because it has no wish or motion beyond its boundary. Nor is it immortal in the ordinary sense. It does not survive from one moment to the next.

It inhabits one moment completely.

If a visitor entered the notch, the inhabitants would not turn toward them. Nothing would scatter. Yet the visitor would gradually discover an allotted place waiting in the ecology: a hollow in the moss shaped for their feet, a gap in the beetle ring corresponding to their shadow, a note among the amphibians pitched to the resonance of their bones.

Eventually their breath would cease to emerge in separate clouds. It would become one pale rope of condensation, permanently extended before their mouth. Their reflection would begin moving independently in the tarn. It would struggle, climb, call for help, perhaps even escape down the endless reflected mountains.

The visitor above would remain where they stood—not dead, not living onward, but incorporated.

Another exact function in the spacious, deafening stillness.

Another creature that had always lived there.
