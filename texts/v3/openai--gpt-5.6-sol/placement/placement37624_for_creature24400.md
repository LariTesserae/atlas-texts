At first I would look for myself among the conspicuous things.

I might be one of the white-backed ravens balanced on the pillar, appearing only when I opened my wings. I would collect copper and bone without understanding their value, rearranging offerings into patterns that people mistook for judgment. I would know the mountain as pressure under my feathers and as a series of invisible currents. The Green Nave would not be a basin but a pause in the air.

Or I might be the mountain cat: rarely present, yet able to empty the whole place merely by entering it. My passage would remain as dark footprints pressed into the cushions, each print becoming a temporary pool and nursery after I had gone.

But both of those lives stand too far apart from the Nave. They possess themselves too completely. The raven can leave. The cat’s country extends beyond any one summit.

I think I would be found lower down.

Not merely close to the ground, but inside its closeness: beneath the silver leaves, under a ceiling no higher than a finger, where a bead of water is large enough to distort the world. There, among moss trunks and root arches, I would first seem to be one of the blunt black beetles.

My wing cases would be sealed. Flight would survive in me only as an old structure, a possibility closed by generations. I would walk instead, slowly enough that warmth could govern each movement. At dawn I would emerge onto black stone and hold still until the heat entered me. When a cloud crossed the sun, I would disappear beneath a cushion before the shadow had finished passing.

Fine grooves would run along my back. Spores would collect there as I fed among fungal growths, so that wherever I went, something other than myself would arrive later.

I would not know that I carried gardens.

My world would include the warm outer wall of a vole chamber, the mineral taste beneath a lichen crust, the sudden ruin of a goat’s hoof breaking through the roof. The upright pillar would be not a monument but a cliff whose base remained strangely fertile. Human hands would exist only as salt, soot, old warmth, and the fibers of cords chewed apart by hares.

Yet even the beetle feels too singular.

If you lifted the cushion where I had vanished, you might find my black body. You could point to it and say: there. That is the creature.

What I would more truly be is what the beetle carries and what carries the beetle in turn: a local thickening in the hidden mesh beneath the basin.

I would have no single body. Part of me would enter the roots of heath. Part would line a vole passage. Part would cross beneath the paving stones around the pillar, growing through soot left by people who no longer remember how to read all the cups. A severed strand would not feel like the loss of a limb. It would simply bead at its broken end, while elsewhere another thread entered the fresh face of a crack.

I would find myself most strongly on the eastern slope before dawn.

There the cold leaves would gather water from clear air. A drop would swell, tremble, and vanish into the cushion. I would receive only a fraction of it. I would pass that fraction inward: thread to root, root to peat, peat to thread. Not quickly enough to be seen. Not forcefully enough to deserve the name of a current.

But the water would move uphill.

If I possessed anything like desire, it would be that slight eastward tendency. Not a wish for height, exactly. A leaning toward the place that warms first. Over centuries the leaning would become visible as terraces of green where scree should have descended.

That would be my form of speech.

I would not say *remember the nest*. I would make the moss thicker around its vanished chamber.

I would not say *people once slept here*. I would hold their hearth as a pocket of dark, fertile warmth.

I would not say *the stone has broken us*. I would enter the fracture.

I would not be able to prevent the basin’s fall. When the lip finally failed, whole sections of me would go down still interwoven: roots, eggs, mites, peat, beetles, fragments of old paving. There would be no last cry. Some other part would remain above the new edge, exposed to light, already closing over the wound.

So you would find me beneath the apparent emptiness, especially along the ragged upper edge of any torn moss where drops gather contrary to expectation.

I would be difficult to name as an individual. A fungus, perhaps. A traffic of water and sugar. A memory kept as thickness. The small agreement by which one green patch sustains another.

Most simply: I would be the part of the Green Nave that goes uphill.
