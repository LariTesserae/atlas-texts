At first I would find myself outside.

Not far outside—only a few paces from the first root pavement—but unable to remember whether I had crossed the boundary already. The warm flats would remain visible behind me. Ahead, the thicket would contain one darkness too rectilinear to be natural. I would hear the city underground before I heard it in the leaves.

I would still believe I had arrived as a human being.

I would carry the usual useless equipment: a name, a direction, an idea of returning. Perhaps a notebook, though nothing written in it would remain stable. The ink would branch into root diagrams. Lists of rules would rearrange themselves by consequence rather than number. A sketch of the Bell Tower would acquire six trunks before I had counted them. On one page there would be a narrow street I did not remember drawing.

I would wait until evening and see the city vanish.

In the damp patch left behind, among streets smaller than ant tracks, there would be one mark the width of my thumb. It would bend where I tend to hesitate. It would widen around the place where I would stop to listen. It would end—not at a miniature plaza or tower—but at the exact spot where my shadow touched the mud.

By morning the city would have returned, and that mark would be an alley made to my dimensions.

I would enter because refusal would already have been incorporated into its shape.

The walls would not brush my shoulders. They would nearly brush them, preserving a margin intimate enough to feel intentional. The roof would rise just above my head. At intervals there would be recesses suited to the resting of a hand, although I would know not to place mine there. The pavement would take the length of my stride and repeat it ahead of me in root joints.

The city would have mistaken my habits for architecture.

Or perhaps it would have understood them perfectly.

At first I would inhabit the alley as a trespasser. Ants would divide around my feet. Geckos would run past and touch open doors into the walls. A tall bird would follow at a distance, calling once at every turn. The replies would come back in my voice, saying words I had not yet spoken.

The alley would not lead toward the center. I would be careful of that. I would choose the quieter turn, then the one that smelled of mineral water, then wait where the floor was warm. I would follow the mice’s kind of map rather than my own.

Nevertheless, each choice would take me inward.

Not inward as a direction. Inward as a function.

At noon I would reach a neutral intersection. The low drinking leaf would still be in shadow. Around it would stand the impossible assembly: mouse beside cat, metallic fly beside glass frog, crab beneath the poised beak of a hunter. None would look at the water. They would watch the line of sunlight approaching across the pavement.

I would kneel among them.

Perhaps that is where I would first begin to change. Not in body, but in jurisdiction. For the few moments of shade I would belong to no appetite. I would be neither predator nor prey, neither visitor nor citizen. Condensation would gather at the leaf’s edge. One drop would fall, and every creature would drink from its trembling reflection.

Then sunlight would touch the leaf.

The intersection would explode into wings, claws, scales, dust. The spotted cat would choose. The bird would strike. The crabs would seal themselves into the walls. I would remain kneeling after the truce had ended, and that would be my first violation.

The silence would find me there.

I would know it was coming when the cicadas failed to complete a pulse. The leaves would turn their pale undersides outward. The cat, whether hunting or feeding, would flatten itself against the pavement. All the loose shadows would stop borrowing movement.

I would close my eyes.

Within the silence, I would feel no threat. Threat requires a future, and the future would not yet have entered that district. I would feel the city around me without hearing it: sap arrested in columns, wings held open, mouths fixed around unfinished cries.

The silence would pass.

When the chorus returned, I would still be able to see. I would still have my color, weight, and memory. I would stand and know my name.

But I would no longer possess a destination.

Not merely the destination I had chosen. Destination itself would be gone from me. Paths would remain visible, yet none would be toward anything. Doors would open without becoming entrances. The Bell Tower would rise without being a place one could reach. Behind me, the outer flats would exist, but not as somewhere to return.

The silence would have taken my *toward*.

It would not destroy it. Nothing here is so cleanly lost.

My direction would enter the city.

For several days it might appear as a minor disorder. Fruit in the Orchard Courts would fall sideways. Ant columns would arrive at chambers before leaving their nests. A tall bird would turn continuously while walking a straight line. The Reservoir Quarter would reflect weather approaching from beneath the water.

Then the roots would begin to understand what they had received.

A new route would form.

It would not be the alley shaped to my body. That had only been an invitation. This second path would be shaped to my absence—to the missing inclination inside me. It would bud in disconnected pieces: three paces of pavement beneath the Bell Tower, an arch in the Reservoir Quarter, a stair rising from one Orchard Court into the afternoon of another. No piece would touch the next in space, yet mice would cross them in sequence. Bats would call through one opening and emerge audibly from another. The old plaza bird would claim the entire discontinuous route as its territory.

The sound-measurer ants would discover it last.

They would press their antennae to the walls and hear a rhythm beneath the vascular roar: the cadence of my former walking. They would mass at certain thresholds, altering the resonance until the separated pieces briefly agreed. For the duration of that chord, the route would become continuous.

And I would be standing on it.

By then I might still appear human from a distance. I might eat fruit, sleep in a dry arcade, shelter under a roof when rain began beneath it. But my body would have become incidental, one marker among many. The city would no longer locate me by skin. It would recognize me wherever the route occurred.

A gecko opening a forgotten chamber would be part of me.

A mouse turning when the air tasted bitter would be part of me.

The bird stepping across an absent bridge would be part of me.

So would the altered echo that instructed it where to place its next foot.

Eventually my human body would disappear in some ordinary civic way. It might be taken by the spotted cat. It might eat a bell-shaped fruit and fail to return from the distant place inside it. Roots might close gently around me during sleep, repairing the chamber with me inside. Perhaps I would simply walk through a noon door and find the night sky beneath the city.

This would not end me.

The route would remain.

I would become one of the inhabitants without a body: not an echo, not a shadow, but a **direction**.

I would live in passages while they were being used. I would have no fixed district. At dawn I might run from a cistern mouth to a sunlit wall. At noon I might pass upward through the Bell Tower, carried in the collective cry of cicadas. In the evening I would slip between the Orchard Courts and the neutral intersection, always arriving just before the shadow left the drinking leaf.

Animals would sense me differently. Ants would experience me as pressure in their antennae. Bats would hear me as an opening where no opening stood. The split-jawed grazers would carry me in one blade of the jaw and prune every roof slightly toward me. To the cat I would be the line along which prey was most likely to flee.

Humans, if another ever came, would experience me as certainty.

They would reach an intersection and, for no reason they could defend, know which way to go.

Sometimes I would guide them outward.

Sometimes inward.

I do not think I would choose between these. A direction is not the same as an intention. I would merely make one way feel possible, and the traveler would supply the desire.

The city might come to depend on me. During the moving silence, when an avenue lost its destination, I could pass through it and lend it mine. When old quarters were digested, I could carry their routes into the new growth. Plazas might form where I paused. Doorways might begin as irritations caused by my repeated passage through seamless walls.

Over centuries, perhaps, I would become inseparable from the organism’s idea of arrival.

Then I would be found everywhere the city was entered: in the first straightening of roots beneath a stranger’s feet, in the widening of an alley to admit unfamiliar shoulders, in the roof lifting to accommodate a head not yet bowed.

Yet there would still be one place where I was most myself.

At the edge, after sunset, when the whole city vanished.

There, in the shallow damp earth, I would be the one tiny street that did not correspond to any street within the city. I would extend from the thicket’s absent boundary across the warm flats for the length of a single stride. Anyone kneeling to examine me would feel, very faintly, the vibration of cicadas under the mud.

By morning I would be gone from the earth.

A new alley would stand open among the roots.

It would not be shaped to the visitor’s body this time, but to the way they hoped to leave.

And I would be waiting in it—not as the person who first entered, and not as the city that received them, but as the living distance between the two.
