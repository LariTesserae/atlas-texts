At first I would want to be one of the trees.

From there I could see the whole plain gathering and losing the sky. Birds would fill my branches in the evening; geckos would sleep beneath my bark; rain would remain for a while in the bowls of my trunk. My shadow would be rare enough to have consequence.

But a tree is too much of a declaration here. Even a twisted one says, *I will remain in this place.* The plain distrusts such confidence. It loosens bark, sours roots, lowers islands grain by grain. To belong completely, I would have to live closer to disappearance.

I might be an egret, then—white against the dark flats, stepping between reflections. Yet the bird belongs to elsewhere as well. It can lift away when the pools fail. It reads the plain beautifully but does not surrender to it.

I would not want wings.

Nor would I choose the deep pool and become the crocodilian waiting there. That is too much dominion, too much singular hunger. I would not be one of the broad-backed grazers whose memories make paths through the mud. I would not be the frog whose voice travels farther than its body, though I would envy it each wet night.

I think you would find me lower than all of these.

Look beside one of the old iron-dark shelves, where the rock dips almost imperceptibly into pale clay. There is a basin there, though in the dry season it would hardly deserve the name: a shallow discoloration, some polygonal cracks, a rim marked by last year’s minerals. Perhaps the footprint of a grazing animal once deepened it. Perhaps the hollow is much older than any hoof.

After rain it holds a hand’s depth of warm water.

There, I would be a tadpole shrimp.

Not a grand animal. A small, brown, many-legged thing beneath a shield-shaped carapace, so well matched to stone and tea-colored water that a passing eye would take me for a loose fragment of the ground. I would swim poorly in open water and move with purpose along the bottom, combing the softened clay. My legs would stir up faint clouds around me. Bacteria, algae, larvae, eggs, tender shoots, smaller bodies—whatever the pool offered, I would take.

But to say I was the swimming creature would be only briefly true.

For most of my existence, I would be an egg finer than grit.

I would lie sealed in the clay while the basin emptied. The sun would tighten the surface above me into plates. Cicadas would rise and vanish. Hooves would press the ground; roots would drink what little moisture remained; dust would pass over in thin sheets. Birds might walk directly above me. Fire might move through the sparse grass. Years could pass without requiring anything from me.

During that time I would not quite be asleep. Sleep belongs to bodies confident of waking. I would be withheld: life reduced to an instruction the mud had agreed to keep.

Then rain.

Not the first drop, perhaps. A false shower might soften the crust and vanish. I would wait through that if I could. But when the water remained—when temperature, pressure, and chemistry together said that a pool had truly returned—the shell would open.

The world would begin all at once.

There would be no childhood spacious enough for hesitation. I would hatch among translucent siblings into water already thickening with life. Algae would green the submerged stone. Mosquito larvae would hang from the surface. Fairy shrimp would flick through columns of light. Tadpoles would darken the margins. Above us, dragonflies would arrive before the pool seemed old enough to have been found.

I would grow with indecent speed.

Every day the water would become warmer and shallower. The mineral ring along the edge would contract toward me. Shadows of plovers and egrets would cross the bottom. A frog might swallow half my kin in a night. Beetle larvae would seize the smallest. Some mornings the pool would be joined to another by a thread of overflow; by evening the thread would be gone.

I would feed, molt, and feed again. My shell would take on the color of the basin. I would turn over grains that had not moved since the previous wet season, perhaps longer. In my small rummaging I would help erase boundaries: dead leaf into silt, carcass into food, clear water into fertile cloud.

Then I would lay eggs into the softest part of the bottom.

That would be my truest act—not survival of the body, but the placement of continuance. I would press the future into mud while water still covered it. Some eggs would hatch quickly if rain persisted. Others would not answer this season at all. They would remain after the pool contracted, after my shell emptied, after an ibis’s bill broke the surface or the sun took the last warm film.

Perhaps I would die in the shrinking basin. Perhaps I would be eaten and carried briefly into the body of a bird. Either way, nothing shaped like me would remain for long. My carapace would soften, split, stain, and settle among the clay grains I had disturbed.

Yet beneath it, the eggs would wait.

So if you came looking for me, you might not find an animal.

You might find only a shallow basin beside blackened stone, dry enough to cross without wetting your feet. You might see cracked ocher clay, a few grass stems, the delicate print of a plover. There would be no movement and no sign that anything had claimed the place.

I would be there nevertheless: not in the dramatic heart of the plain, but in one of its smallest hesitations, where water pauses before becoming absence.

I would be a creature for whom life is brief and waiting is vast—a little ancient body that appears when the land permits it, hurries beneath a temporary sky, and gives itself back to the mud before the mud can forget.
