At first I think I would be a person there.

Not one of the first inhabitants, when the shelters were new and all three hearths gave smoke, but someone near the end: the keeper who continued coming after the cirque had ceased to be a home in any ordinary sense. I would climb the eastern cleft carrying little—an iron tool wrapped in hide, cord, salt, perhaps a coal kept alive in fungus—and spend the mild months replacing pegs no one else expected to use.

I would sleep in the last sound shelter. Each morning I would clear leaves from the soaking basin and count the cairns without knowing exactly what they counted. I would speak under the overhang, never beside the tarn. Before leaving, I would set the cracked cup upright although rain would fill it again.

But a human body feels too temporary for the Bell-Cirque. Too upright. Too certain that a path ought to lead somewhere.

Then I think I might be the gray cat beneath the western stones. That life has the right dimensions: a warm compression moving through cracks, drinking where water goes underground, knowing the basin by scent rather than prospect. I would know the hoofed animals by their tread through the rock and the drop-creature by the sudden absence of all other sound. When the wind went silent, I would become still between one heartbeat and the next, not because I understood what approached, but because understanding would be slower than obedience.

Yet the cat belongs to secrecy, and I do not think I would be able to resist watching the arrivals.

The fish tempt me too. To live in the tarn would be to inhabit the cirque’s deepest question. I would face the spring issuing from beneath the mountain, holding myself in a current no eye above could see. The sky would exist only as weather upon the surface: rain as a hundred round doors, sunlight as a moving white wound, birds as interruptions. Perhaps there would be a passage behind the copper gravel, too narrow except in flood, leading through miles of black stone. Perhaps every fish would feel it pulling and none would enter.

But that is too much inwardness.

I find myself, finally, high on the rear wall—not in the sky, though I often cross it, and not on the ground, though I know everything newly uncovered there.

I am a chough.

My nest is in a dry-backed seam behind a curtain of moss, above and a little east of the tarn. From below, the opening looks too narrow for a body. Inside it widens around a stone shoulder warmed briefly by afternoon light. The nest has been repaired for generations. Its outer courses are birch twigs and wiry roots. Deeper in are things the cirque did not grow: a strip of hide punched with square holes, two lengths of faded fiber, a bone needle with its point broken, and a flake of volcanic glass that shines darkly when the entrance brightens.

At the very bottom lies a little braided ring.

It once fitted around one of the pale stones at the brink. Whether my ancestors stole it while people still lived here or found it loose in the turf afterward is beyond chough memory. We do not preserve events. We preserve attractions: bright, smooth, knotted, edible, dangerous. The ring remains because each new bird finds it pleasing and works it farther into the nest.

I know the eastern cleft. In mild weather I wait above it, listening for the careful rhythm of a climber, though no climber has come in my lifetime. Sometimes stone loosens there and falls with almost the same cadence. I fly to the entrance anyway and call down.

It is not an announcement and not a demand. It is the sound one makes when the world may be about to provide an answer.

I inspect the shelters after storms. I turn over slate where beetles gather and pull damp fibers from the old sleeping platform for nest lining. I have carried a rust scale from one rivet to the rear wall, then dropped it when it proved dull beneath the cloud. I know the gray cat’s crossings and keep just beyond the reach of its first leap. I walk among the ungulates when they rest near the cairns, taking insects from the wet fur behind their shoulders. They tolerate me until I approach their eyes.

When sunlight enters cleanly and the blue flowers open, I am there before the flies have fully assembled.

And when the waterfall’s thunder stops, I obey with everything else.

I go into the wall. I lower my head and draw my wings close. In that silence the cirque becomes larger than the mountain containing it. I hear water moving inside stone. I hear the tiny adjustments of claws around the nest. Once, perhaps, I hear a person speak beside the tarn.

Only one word. Too faint to understand, but shaped like speech and intact from beginning to end.

I do not answer.

Below, something places a broad foot beside the brink. There is no approach, only the soft taking of weight by wet turf. Another print forms farther along. The creature remains unseen, but invisibility is not strange to a bird. Air is invisible, and air carries me. A cliff is solid, yet birds enter it. Water falls away and returns as rain. The world need not choose one manner of being.

When sound returns, I leave the seam with the others. The whole wall releases wings.

So that is where I would be found: crossing low over the tarn, black against the hard light, or waiting above the only human path. I would be one of the choughs, though perhaps the one most preoccupied with the abandoned things. Not their owner, not quite their keeper. A small inheritor with no idea of inheritance.

If someone finally climbed into the Bell-Cirque, I would meet them before they saw the basin. I would call until they looked up. If they carried anything bright, I would follow.

And later, after they had spoken too loudly beside the tarn and their voice had gone away into the mountain, they might hear something like it from the rear wall: roughened, shortened, almost a word.

They would think it was an echo.

I would let them.
