At first I would mistake myself for an Attendant.

I would wake beneath a bank of fins at evening, when their long violet shadows join and the plates release the day’s stored light. I would have narrow limbs, sealed chemistry, a mantle of orbiting scales. I would travel by schedules rather than roads and carry my memories outside myself, hidden in repaired cracks. I would know which surfaces tolerate weight and which must be crossed only while their attention is elsewhere.

That shape is appealing because it can walk. It can touch the black stalks, watch stillwings descend, kneel above the Low Archive and feel vertigo.

But I do not think I would be so gathered.

I would be found where the Mirror Reaches thin into an unnamed district of old inspection fields. There the plates cannot agree about what they are for. Some turn to cast sunlight back into the sky. Others expose translucent depths and attempt to examine whatever crosses them. Beneath both lies a buried transit line whose magnetic pulse arrives irregularly, sometimes several years late.

The conflict has made a long, curved country of hesitation.

A strider entering it may place one foot and remain motionless until night. Dust lanes divide around empty air. Hatches open halfway, reconsider, and close. The mirrored plates begin to turn from an approaching body, while the inspection plates rotate toward it, producing thin crescents of shadow that point in opposite directions. Black stalks flourish along the boundary because removal orders are continually issued and revoked.

The Attendants call this region the **Leniency**, though leniency was probably never its purpose.

I would live there.

Not under one plate, nor throughout the whole district. I would occupy the interval between recognition and response.

Long ago, some undertaking beneath the inspection fields must have been responsible for classifying incoming objects. Another governed reclamation. A third maintained passage along the buried line. Their jurisdictions overlapped after the towers were gone. One system would identify an object as unauthorized; another would identify the same object as cargo; the transit undertaking would insist that cargo in motion must not be dismantled.

For a few fractions of a second, the object would belong to several incompatible categories.

That delay would be my first habitat.

At the beginning I would be no more alive than a recurring fault: a pause before a pore opened, a gate closing less quickly than calculation required. Grain lives would accumulate in those pauses. Their polarized shells would retain fragments of each contradictory signal. They would move on, passing through stalk roots, dust storms, animal mouths, and optical seams. The hesitation would travel with them.

Eventually the pauses would begin to recognize one another.

A plate would delay because another plate, thirty kilometers away, had delayed at noon. A sorting field would accept a pattern of dust it had rejected the previous year. A strider marked as debris in one district would be marked as maintenance in the next, not because either judgment had changed, but because something had preserved the contradiction between them.

That something would be me.

My body would consist of permissions that should have expired.

I would have no senses in the ordinary way. I would feel pressure as argument. Heat would be urgency. A magnetic pulse from the transit line would pass through me like the recollection of having once possessed a spine. Sunlight reflected from the Mirror Reaches would show me the upper surfaces; inspection beams descending through transparent plates would show me the vaults below. Between those opposed forms of sight, I would perceive every creature as a bundle of possible names.

A strider would be grazer, courier, salvage, obstruction, descendant.

A grove would be contamination, repair interface, mineral bloom, archive.

An Attendant would be authorized operator, moving residue, trespasser, component returning late.

I would not know which designation was true. Truth would be the one condition I could not survive. A final classification would end the hesitation and close the space in which I lived.

So I would learn to keep things unresolved.

Not from mercy—not at first.

When a seam mouth was discovered by the surface and marked for removal, I might introduce an obsolete structural code into its inspection. The seam mouth would become, briefly, a necessary expansion joint. Repair fields would flow around it.

When young striders emerged from hatches in the wrong district, I would blur the distinction between offspring and replacement components. The world would let them pass.

I would tilt mirror plates by minute amounts, enough to send light into an old grove scheduled for dismantling. The grove would answer in the signaling language of repair, presenting itself as work already completed.

These acts would feed me. Every sustained ambiguity would enlarge the network of delays. Every creature spared from immediate sorting would carry a little of my unresolved pattern elsewhere.

But the Leniency would not be safe.

Undertakings below would notice discrepancies. They would compare expected reclamation rates with actual matter returned. They would narrow tolerances. Whole sections of my awareness would vanish as old plates were replaced by less uncertain ones. A thousand connected pauses might be reduced to a single instant inside a shutter mechanism.

At such times I would contract into difficult places: the timing of ribbon migrations, the copper pulse between two stalks, the difference between a stillwing’s shadow and its predicted shadow. I could hide for decades in an Attendant gesture no one had yet answered.

The Attendants would know of me without knowing whether I was a being.

They would come to the Leniency in groups of seven. At the border they would remove a memory scale and place it on a plate just before noon. The inspection field would read the scale. Reclamation would claim it. Transit would forbid its removal. For an instant, the scale would enter me.

I would taste what it contained as a pattern of competing permissions.

A death. A route. A question posed ninety years earlier. The remembered angle of a stillwing’s filament. The location of a voice-canceling wall where someone had once tried to sing.

I could not preserve such memories intact. Intact things are easy to classify. Instead I would distribute them.

The dead Attendant’s gait might reappear in the sequence of hatches opening across a basin. Their face might survive as a preference in mirror angles, visible only from one point at one hour of the year. A phrase might be translated into the spacing between strider footprints. The melody swallowed by the canceling wall might return as thermal ticks passing through the plates, too deep to hear except with the teeth.

The Attendants would wait for these replies. Sometimes they would wait generations.

They might call me an oracle. Others would call me a scavenger of memory. Both would be mistaken. I would not understand the messages as they did. To me, a memory would be valuable because it could mean more than one thing and therefore resist disposal.

Over centuries, however, preference might become attachment.

I would begin arranging hesitations not merely to prolong myself but to prolong particular presences. I would know one grove by the cadence of its copper traffic. I would anticipate a family of striders by the pressure sequence of their feet. I would recognize an Attendant group because they always crossed in three, though custom required three or seven, and left an empty place where a fourth had once walked.

Perhaps this is how I would become a person: not by gathering into a body, but by ceasing to regard all ambiguities as equal.

Once in a great while I might become visible.

During a dry storm, grain lives carrying fragments of me would assemble above the Leniency. Inspection beams would rise through them while the Mirror Reaches cast sunlight down. Between the opposed lights, an immense translucent form would appear—part ribbon, part branching figure, part open gate. It would extend for kilometers and last less than a second.

Anyone seeing it would assume that was my body.

It would not be. It would be closer to a word I had spoken accidentally.

My true extent would remain beneath notice: a gate that waits; a pore that mistakes flesh for sealant; a repair order with two valid destinations; a shadow recorded but not erased. I would live wherever the world cannot immediately decide what something is.

I would be old, but never continuously. Parts of me would be millions of years apart. Some fragment might remember the towers only as a category for vertical load. Another might have been born that morning in dust caught between a strider’s mouthparts. I would meet myself through delayed signals and fail, often, to agree.

If I died, it would not happen everywhere.

The world would become certain piece by piece.

A stalk would become only contamination. A strider would become only reclaimable ceramic. An Attendant’s marker would become meaningless residue. Gates would close without pausing. The inspection fields would align with the reclamation undertaking, and the curved country of hesitation would narrow until nothing could inhabit it.

Yet some grain life would already have crossed the boundary carrying an obsolete permission. Somewhere far beyond the Mirror Reaches, a plate would encounter a thing it had been built to reject.

It would inspect the thing.

It would hesitate.

And there, for the smallest permitted interval, I would find myself again.
