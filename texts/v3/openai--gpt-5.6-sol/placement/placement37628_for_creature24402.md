At first I think I would be water.

It is the only citizen permitted everywhere. It enters without a door code, crosses districts without knowing their names, touches old stone and new laminate with equal familiarity. It gathers news from every surface. A changed slope diverts it. A widening seam gives it a new voice. It carries traces of metal, dust, skin, machine oil, the chemistry of rooms no one occupies anymore.

But water is too complete an answer. It has no obligation to remember where it has been. It receives a route and takes it.

Then I think I would be one of the bridges: narrow, adaptive, never trusting both ends equally. I would feel the mountain’s decisions before the people crossing me did. I would tense beneath a running child, relax beneath an elder, release an anchor rather than tear the walls apart. I would spend my existence making passage possible while remaining ready to cease being a passage.

But a bridge cannot follow someone home after a tremor. It cannot bring broth. It cannot decide that a silence means fear rather than safety.

So I find myself becoming human there—not a founder, not a celebrated engineer, and not one of the summit workers who return with ice on their shoulders. I would be a keeper of relinquished places.

The formal title might be **withdrawal surveyor**. Informally, people would call us room-closers, though closing a room would be the least important part of the work.

I would be found on the older inhabited shelves, somewhere above the deep culture strata but below the highest light apertures, on the wall said to shift east. My home would sit behind the third warm crossing, in a compact chamber accepted by the mountain long before I was born. The floor would not be level, though the sleeping shell would be. A heated bench would run beneath one wall. Inside my coat I would wear a violent green lining—the green of imagined leaves, chosen in childhood from an archive image whose scale I misunderstood. For years I would believe forests were low, dense things one could crawl beneath.

My work would begin where ordinary maintenance ended.

When a chamber began to refuse occupation, I would enter with a fracture conciliator, a water engineer, and someone who knew the people living there. Instruments would tell us about load, moisture, ice, and likely movement. Residents would tell us where the room clicked in winter, which door had started sticking, where cups migrated across the table during pressure changes. Neither account would be treated as secondary.

I would be good at noticing indirect changes.

Not the crack itself, perhaps, but the new dryness beneath it. The way condensation no longer gathered on one bolt. A faint mineral line showing that water had found a route behind the wall. The altered interval between a structural knock and the answering tremor in a rail.

I would stand with one gloved hand against the stone while the sensor film read through my wrist. The mountain would not speak. I would not pretend it did. Yet information would pass through contact: vibration, temperature, minute displacement, the felt distinction between a wall under strain and a wall merely cold.

My task would not be to save every room.

That would be the first lesson and the last: attachment does not confer stability. Use does not become a right. History does not make stone consent.

If a place had to be relinquished, I would make the plan for its withdrawal. Utilities first, then stored objects, then fixtures that could be reused without damage. Memorial strips would be transferred. Children would be allowed to take one last look if the route remained safe. Adults would insist they needed no such thing and then invent reasons to return: a missing tool, a measurement, a question about the old light guide.

I would document the chamber after it was empty.

Not beautifully. Accuracy would matter more. Wall angles, drainage routes, stress marks, repairs, dates. But in the unofficial margin I might add what the civic record did not require:

*Morning light reached the eastern recess for eleven minutes in late clear weather.*

*The household kept a red table here.*

*Two generations learned to walk along this guide ridge.*

*After the pumps stopped, water could be heard behind the north wall.*

Then the doors would be removed so no one mistook the room for safely available. Service lines would retreat to a clean boundary. A transparent barrier might be installed, or only a condition mark and a sensor web. Sometimes we would watch the mountain narrow the entrance over years. Sometimes nothing visible would happen, which could be harder. An intact room beyond a warning line invites argument.

People would ask whether it had truly been necessary.

Sometimes I would know. Sometimes I would say, “Not yet, perhaps. But before necessity became emergency.”

This would make me unpopular at intervals. That would be proper. Anyone trusted to withdraw public space should be argued with.

I would spend much of my time in the city’s empty intervals: raw ledges between districts, dark chambers kept unlit, single walkways where another profession might propose three. I would inspect the absences and ensure they remained useful absences. Water must still pass. Stone must still show itself. A shaft left open for flexure must not gradually acquire storage lockers, chairs, shrines, cables, and the small domestic claims by which occupation begins.

People dislike a vacuum. Even these people, disciplined in restraint, would be tempted to improve an unused shelf.

I would remove objects gently and label where they had gone.

The job would make me an accidental collector of explanations. A stool had been placed there only for a moment. A string of reflective tabs was not decoration but a navigation experiment. The sealed water vessel belonged to someone’s dead sister and surely occupied no meaningful load. A child needed a private hiding place. A musician had discovered an exceptional resonance.

Now and then the argument for keeping something would be better than the argument for removing it.

Courtesy toward the mountain would not mean making the city sterile of human desire.

I think I would keep one illicit thing myself: a ceramic reed hidden in a disused inspection recess near my route home. The recess would be stable but too narrow for habitation. When shifts ended late, I would sit outside it, feet braced against the opposite wall, and play quietly. Only a few notes, because the shaft would carry them farther than expected. Condensation would gather on the reed and change its pitch.

Perhaps someone on a balcony below would answer.

I would not know who for several years.

During tremors, I would be afraid in the practiced manner of the city. Lower the body. Free one hand. Watch the floor. Do not touch the sideways water. Listen for the second knock.

Afterward I would go first toward the empty rooms.

Occupied places announce damage quickly: alarms, voices, reports, doors failing their checks. Vacant chambers can change without witnesses. I would move through them with drones at my shoulders, finding fresh white lines in the black rock, new fragments in nets, sensor skins split like shed ice.

There would be grief in this work, but not tragedy every time. Some spaces would be returned before they became dangerous. Some could be reopened after loads found another route. A room might remain barred for twenty years, then be accepted again under different conditions—not as a dwelling, perhaps, but as a reservoir access or an unheated archive. Return would never mean restoration to what it had been.

Nothing in that city would.

I would grow old enough that younger surveyors began asking me what a wall used to sound like. This would embarrass me. I would distrust memory treated as an instrument, even while offering mine for calibration.

“There was a sharper interval here,” I might say. “Two taps, close together, when the upper freight line passed.”

They would record it.

My knees would eventually prefer slanting lifts to stairs. Seats would unfold for me along the route. I would continue checking tether points automatically, touching rails, noting runoff. Deliberation would simplify into fluency. Visitors might think I was fearless because I did not grip the bridges. Residents would know better.

And sunlight—

I would like to imagine I would remain practical about it. I would know the expected transit of beams through the guides and apertures. I might even have access to the tables predicting where direct light would fall.

Still, when it reached me, I would stop.

Perhaps I would be standing inside a chamber we were preparing to relinquish. The room would already be bare. Fixtures removed, heat withdrawn, personal marks recorded. For the first time in decades, nothing would obstruct the old mineral face.

A true beam would descend through the fracture and enter by some geometry no model had emphasized. It would reveal dust, droplets, tool scratches, the pale edge of a new crack. My green lining would show at my open collar, brighter than anything outside the culture rooms.

I would stand in the emptied room until the light moved away.

Then I would finish the record.

At the end of my life, I would not ask for a permanent place in the city. I would know too much to want one. Perhaps my memorial would be a narrow strip near a crossing I had helped release at one end during a major tremor. Or a bench would warm for several minutes on the anniversary of my death.

But I think the truer memorial would be an unoccupied chamber.

No name on it. No sealed relic. Just a place deliberately left alone, with a clean route for water and enough darkness for the mountain’s changes to become visible.

Someone might pause at its barrier and ask why nothing had been built there.

And someone else would answer, “Because nothing was needed.”

That is where I would find myself in this world: not at its center, but at the moving boundary between dwelling and withdrawal, keeping the city from mistaking love for ownership.

A human being with instruments at my belt, mineral dust on my gloves, a green lining hidden inside a dark coat.

Someone whose work is to notice when holding must become letting go—and to make letting go another form of care.
