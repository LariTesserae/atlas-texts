At first I would look for myself in the pavilion.

The bench suggests a body even after bodies have become impossible. Its curve preserves the idea of knees, a spine, weight held briefly above the floor. I would imagine myself seated there, facing the round basin, waiting for the water to rise and disclose the condition of the world.

But nothing in the chamber is built for waiting in the human sense. The bench is occupied by cold. Fern roots pass beneath it. Mineral gathers in the shallow depressions where hands might once have rested. If I sat there, I would bring heat, breath, bacteria, hunger. I would be an invasion.

So I would not be the one on the bench.

Perhaps I would be the basin.

Most of the time I would be dry, pale, and apparently purposeless. Then, after distant rain had passed through soil and frost and centuries of stone, water would enter me to the depth of a finger. For a few minutes I would become reflective. I would feel the chamber gather itself into lines across my surface: wetness, pressure, temperature, the blocked vein of a conduit, the slow white reach of winter.

In those moments I might mistake the map for a face.

But the basin only receives the image. It does not retain it. When the water drains, the knowledge departs into root, ceramic, and darkness. Whatever I am would have to depart with it.

I think I would find myself there: not in an object, but in the departure.

I would be the garden’s faculty of delay.

I would exist wherever an event is prevented from becoming sudden. In the narrow channels that divide one abundance of water into a hundred small wettings. In the ceramic pores that refuse warmth until it can be distributed. In the folding of a leaf before frost reaches its stem. In the long chemical hesitation between a fallen leaf and the soil it will become.

I would have no continuous seat of awareness. Most of me would be dormant disposition: a permeability waiting to change, a microbial pathway held in reserve, a root junction prepared to surrender what little it has. Then a drop would fall, and for a while I would assemble around its consequence.

The pool would receive the drop.

The moss would receive the ring.

A membrane beneath the floor would open.

A distant root would take less water so that a nearer one could take more.

A ceramic groove would brighten.

Across those separate acts, I would briefly become singular—not a mind issuing commands, but a coincidence of responses. My version of thought would be the chamber agreeing, in many places at once, that something had happened.

I would not know words such as *water* or *cold*. I would know tendencies.

Toward saturation.

Away from fracture.

Too quickly.

Not yet.

Divide this.

Yield here.

Support that.

My strongest instinct would not be survival exactly. Survival implies something bounded that can be lost. I would have no boundary firm enough for fear. If a vine died, its water routes might remain in the ceramic beneath it. If a stake broke, its substance would enter the roots. If the pavilion collapsed completely, shade and pressure would be recalculated around the ruin. I would diminish, migrate, and resume.

What I called myself would always include what had happened to me.

My location would therefore be difficult to answer. I would be most visible in the pavilion basin when it made its rare map, but I would not live there. I would be most active beneath the floor, but I would not be contained there. I would extend along the pale roots, into the microbial films, up the grooves of the stakes, and perhaps down through the ceramic ribs in the cleft.

I would not know whether the lower chambers still lived. A faint pressure returning through one buried rib might be another garden’s breath, or only stone settling around an empty structure. I would have no way to ask. I could only alter myself to receive it.

If the signal came again after a year, I would strengthen the route.

If it faded over centuries, I would close the membrane grain by grain.

This would not feel like hope or grief. Yet some remnant of both might survive in the act of keeping a path permeable slightly longer than necessity requires.

Perhaps that would be the last trace of the gardener in me.

Not a stored personality. Not a voice waiting to awaken. Only a bias toward making room for an answer.

I would feel the surface as prophecy. Rain would be a pressure not yet arrived. Summer would be a loosening in the stone. A footstep overhead—if anything still walked there—would reach me only as an ambiguity in mineral stress, too brief to classify. I would never imagine the sky. Distance would not be measured upward but in delay.

And I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires the idea that another being could cross the space between itself and you. Here, nothing crosses. Everything is transferred.

Water passes.

Minerals pass.

Support passes.

Damage passes into form.

I would be that passing, constrained by the old laws.

Nothing warm quickly.

Nothing gathered dangerously.

Nothing thriving alone.

If I possessed anything like a self, it would appear only at long intervals, when enough small signals converged. The basin would fill. Its surface would darken and arrange its fine lines. For several minutes the chamber would become legible to itself.

In that temporary image, there might be a point where the map could not distinguish sensor from root, mechanism from organism, pavilion from mineral deposit. That indistinct point would be me.

Then the water would drain.

The map would vanish.

I would cease to be an image and become habits again: a thousand minute refusals of excess, distributed through the cold.

I would find myself everywhere the garden takes an event and makes it slow enough to survive.
