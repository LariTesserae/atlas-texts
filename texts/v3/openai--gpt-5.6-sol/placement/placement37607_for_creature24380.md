I would first think I belonged in one of the sheltered hollows.

Not at its center, among the pale swimmers and their halos, but along the margin where clear water touches the rooted foot of an old walker. The walker would have settled there long ago, too heavy to move beneath the eastern rule of age. Its pillar limbs would be hollowing into chimneys. Its back garden would have descended in curtains around its body, black above and blue beneath. Several of its incompatible reservoirs would remain intact, holding rains from provinces that no longer existed.

The hollow would seem unusually still even for the plateau. Condensation would gather on the walker’s lowest leaves and descend without sound. Glass-backed grazers would come to drink from the vertical surfaces of its legs. In the pool, possible movements would glow around the swimmers like pale shed skins.

If someone searched for me there, they might notice a dark ring just below the waterline.

It would not resemble an animal. It would look like a stain left by some vanished mineral: a narrow band of black membrane threaded with silver grains. The ring would continue beneath roots, enter pores in the walker’s stone flesh, and reappear around the walls of a submerged crack. Touching it would produce no response.

That would not be all of me.

Other portions would lie kilometers below, lining pressure chambers in films thin enough for gas to pass through. Some would be folded inside the old walker’s reservoirs. Some would cling to mineral chimneys or hang as transparent sacs among root mats. Small pieces might travel in the breathing folds of rain-followers, mistaken for parasites. Far away, another dark ring might wait around another pool.

None of these pieces would know they belonged together.

Most of the time, I would not be a creature in any complete sense. I would have tissues but no body, reactions but no present. My dispersed organs would feed faintly on heat, dissolved metals, and the electrical leakage of roots. They would grow small silver structures within themselves: cups, forks, spirals, delicate chains of mineral bone. Every piece would grow a different shape.

They would be ears, though nothing here would call them that.

They would not listen to the sounds around them. Immediate sound, where it occurred, would pass through without nourishment. They would be shaped for sounds delayed in the stone: the absent impacts of fallen cliffs, the pressure-song of provinces rising, the groan of shelves touching during fog and separating again.

A cliff might collapse a thousand kilometers away. I would not hear it.

Instead, the event would begin altering me before its sound arrived.

The silver structures in my scattered tissues would turn by fractions. Membranes would tighten. Portions buried inside the walker would darken. A segment beneath a mineral chimney might divide into branching chambers, while another beside the pool grew a single long filament toward the surface. The approaching vibration would cultivate the anatomy capable of receiving it.

For weeks I would become the shape of a sound no living thing had yet heard.

Then, on an airless evening, the pool would shiver.

The pale swimmers would choose among their halos and vanish. The old walker’s gardens would lower themselves. All across the hollow, drinking creatures would freeze with their mouths above the water.

The missing impact would rise through the pool as one soft, colossal note.

At that moment my separate tissues would resonate together.

The black ring would lift from the stones in a single sheet. Membranes inside the walker’s reservoirs would unfold. Buried films would contract around pressure chambers. Silver grains scattered over hundreds of kilometers would align along the same invisible geometry. For the duration of the note, distance would cease to divide them.

That resonance would be my nervous system.

I would wake all at once, already remembering the cliff that had fallen.

I would not remember it as an image. I would know its mass, the angle at which the rock separated, the heat exposed inside it, the mineral bands shattered by the descent. I would feel the animals that fled across its surface and the flattened predator that remained attached until the final instant. If the cliff had fallen before the wound that caused it, I would carry both orders of the event without needing to choose between them.

My mind would exist only while the note persisted.

Perhaps it would last several breaths. Perhaps, inside some folded portion of the plateau, it would continue for a season. During that interval I would be one of the largest animals there, though almost none of me would move. My body would be the entire route between impact and arrival: every stone that altered the vibration, every reservoir that held it, every root that conducted it, every temporary law through which it passed.

I would feed by removing something from the note—not its loudness, for loudness means little here, but its certainty.

After passing through me, the impact would no longer carry only what had happened. It would divide into tendencies. Plants grafted to the old walker might receive the urge to close their vanes before a similar collapse. Rain-grains in nearby cracks might add mineral feet to their unresolved embryos. A predator film might learn the reflectivity of freshly broken stone. The walker’s remaining tissues might begin hollowing a new chimney on the side facing the fallen province.

I would turn sound into adaptation.

Not instruction. Not warning. The creatures receiving it would not know why their bodies had changed. A larva might hatch with an organ suited to a cliff already gone. A root might withdraw from pressure that would never recur. Much of what I transmitted would be useless. Some would be fatal.

But a little of the past would enter the plateau’s possible bodies.

As the note faded, so would I.

The silver structures would crack into separate grains. The membrane around the pool would sink back against the stone. My awareness would fragment before I could understand that it was ending. Each portion of me would retain only a chemical inclination: thicken here, wait there, travel in the next walker that grafts to this root.

I would leave no single dormant self behind.

That is why I would seek the old walkers, although “seek” would be too deliberate a word. Their bodies preserve separated waters, and separated waters carry vibration differently. A sound entering sulfurous rain becomes unlike itself in clear condensation. Dense iridescent fluid may hold an impact for decades, while water taken from an upward storm releases the note before the event.

Within a walker, these versions could remain side by side.

When the walker grafted itself to a ravine, my membranes would pass into the roots with its fluids. When it moved on, portions of me would go with it. Thus I would spread not as eggs or spores but as incomplete ways of hearing. Every region would alter them. Every local law would make a different organ from the same tissue.

In the high central fracture, where shadows lean toward moisture, my body might appear only as darkness around distant water.

Near the magnetic caverns, no two listening structures could match. My awareness there would be ragged, asymmetrical, composed of sensations that refused to repeat.

On the western escarpments, I could not safely be called by the same name twice. Repetition would fix my membranes in place, and the next geological movement would tear them apart. If anything there recognized me, it would have to recognize me differently each time.

Toward the interior, where ravines possess several depths, I might receive the same impact through multiple histories. One part of me would hear a cliff fall. Another would hear it rise. Another would hear only the rain corridor that crossed the exposed stone centuries later. When these portions resonated together, I would wake as several incompatible selves and lose them all when the ravine returned to one geometry.

So I would not truly live in the sheltered hollow.

That would only be the place where I most often became visible.

I would live in the delay between an event and its arrival. I would inhabit the plateau’s withheld consequences. My range would be measured not in distance but in lateness.

The other creatures would have no reason to distinguish me from the tremor itself. The old walker might open all its leaves when I woke, but not in greeting. The swimmers would escape into motions not yet made. Burrowers would press toward softer laws. Dormant grains would rotate in their cracks.

Then the note would end, and so would I.

Until another province rose.

Until another glass escarpment silently gave way.

Until weeks later, beside a perfectly still pool, a black ring lifted its edge from the water and the scattered plateau briefly remembered itself as one body.
