I would not find myself in the luminous center, though at first I might mistake that emptiness for freedom.

I would imagine being one of the ribbon creatures: translucent, without a face, entering on a current I could not resist. I would unfold in the whirl until blue threads shone through me. The people on the wall would watch with the particular anguish reserved for something whose distress resembles their own. Perhaps a young keeper would reach for me. Perhaps I would numb their hand with an old pain neither of us understood.

But I do not think I would survive as a creature of the open water. The Bell Chamber punishes anything that cannot distinguish movement from passage. I would be torn into clear strands, and the only durable part of me would be a hard knot caught in a net above the platforms, ringing faintly whenever the mountain changed.

That image persists: not the animal, but the knot left behind.

So I think I would find myself on the least turbulent wall, tied among tools whose purposes have outlived their makers. At first I would be a person—one of the keepers, probably the one entrusted with listening rather than crossing.

My hand-name would be three pressures delivered to the heel of the palm: two close together, one held. The nearest translation might be **Still-After**, though that would not truly be a name. It would be the sensation of waiting after a blow, when the bone continues to carry what struck it.

My long name would be much larger. It would begin in a chamber that no longer existed, a warm hollow below the Bell Chamber where iron water entered through a red fissure. It would trace my family’s escape when the ceiling thickened downward and divided the room. It would cross two passages later sealed, pause at the chamber where my mother’s brother was lost, and end with the upward route by which I had arrived at the bell. Tapped into stone, it would take long enough that the striker’s wrist would tire.

Most people would know only fragments of it.

I would have been a child who learned gripping easily and release badly. On the training cord, I would keep both hands closed after the signal, even when the current twisted my shoulders. An adult would have to peel my fingers away one by one. For years afterward I would carry the shame of that—not because I had been afraid, but because fear had made me useless to anyone connected to me.

Eventually I would learn that release is not the opposite of loyalty.

That would be why they placed me at the resonant stone.

The mallet keeper must abandon certainty over and over. You strike; the mountain takes the rhythm. Then there is nothing to hold. The answer may return altered, doubled, delayed beyond expectation, or not at all. You cannot pull sound back through stone. You cannot follow it with your body. You can only remain at your post and become sensitive to what absence is made of.

I would sit hooked beneath the rib cage surrounding the stone, one foot braced in an old handhold, the other tucked through a rope loop. The current would push mineral grit continually across my face. White crust would form in my throat folds. Every few lulls, another keeper would pin my shoulders gently and clean the gills with a shell blade. This would be one of the intimacies by which I knew I belonged to the living.

My gift would not be striking loudly. It would be hearing the damage in an answer.

A note arriving from the western hollows would have a softness at its edge when new silica began forming over the route. A chamber below would answer with two vibrations where once there had been one, meaning a partition had grown between us and the signal now traveled by separate paths. Sometimes a reply would seem to arrive too soon. I would know it was not a reply at all, but the Bell Chamber reflecting its own blow through some newly hardened rib.

I would keep these distinctions in my body. There would be no permanent map for them. Maps here would become lies faster than they could be carved.

When no answer came, others would look to me.

I would strike again.

Wait.

Strike again.

By the third silence, everyone would understand, though no one would mourn yet. I would rest my palm against the resonant stone and feel its remaining tremor. Beyond it might be death, flight, thickened wall, broken mallet, frightened children, or hands too injured to answer. Uncertainty would not be an empty space in our knowledge. It would be something under pressure, capable of opening later into rescue or grief.

The old enclosed keeper would matter greatly to me. During the mountain’s deep tolls, I would wait for the small tremor that followed. Others might call it a flaw in the partition. I would never insist it was a person. Belief stated too firmly would feel like carving: an attempt to force a permanent shape onto something still forming.

But privately, I would answer it.

Not with the network signals. With my hand-name.

Two close blows. One held.

Sometimes the wall would return a faint uneven pulse. Most often it would not.

As I aged, the chamber would take pieces of me without killing me. The edge of one ear. Two nails. Flexibility in the left wrist. A front tooth broken when a pressure shift drove the mallet handle into my mouth. My shoulders would widen; my hips would remain narrow. The younger keepers would think I possessed uncanny stillness, though what they saw as wisdom would mostly be accumulated injury and a precise understanding of which movements hurt.

They would watch me cross during the cancellation between currents. To them it would seem that the room carried me.

In truth, I would have stopped imagining that my body ended at my skin. The guide-line would be a tendon I could detach from. The handholds would be temporary joints. Other people’s fingers on my throat would complete an act my own hands could not perform. The stone beneath my palm would extend hearing beyond the limits of flesh.

Then one season—if seasons can be spoken of in a room with no sky—the Bell Chamber would begin to close around the resonant cage.

Not dramatically. A pale softness would appear along the rear ribs. Bone splinters set there would vanish in the wrong order. The floor vents would deepen in pitch while the ceiling shriek thinned almost beyond perception. Clay pressed into a crack would return folded like skin around a wound.

We would know the cage must be moved.

We would wedge levers under it. Cut away young stone. Tie new hauling cords. Wait for lulls. The chamber would resist without intention. One rib would come loose; another would already be half entombed. The resonant slab would be heavier than any of us had guessed because the mountain had grown through its base.

At some point the central current would reverse.

A young keeper would be caught beyond the guide-line, one wrist loop snagged under a crystal hook. I would be nearest. I would have time either to hold the cage’s main cord or to release it and reach them.

This time I would let go.

The cage would wrench backward into the whitening wall. I would catch the young keeper by the sternum and drive both of us into a sheltered rib. Someone else would secure them. Someone would touch the sign for living against my wrist.

Behind us, the resonant stone would disappear beneath a collapse of pale mineral curtains.

Perhaps I would disappear with it.

Not heroically. A secondary cord might wrap my ankle. A slab might fold between me and the others. They would see my hands for several breaths through cloudy stone, and we would waste those breaths trying to reach one another. Then the water between us would become milk-white with precipitation. The partition would thicken.

There would be no final speech. There could never have been one.

I would put my palm to the new wall.

On the other side, someone would place theirs against the same place. The stone would be too thick for warmth, but not yet too thick for pressure. They would tap my hand-name. I would answer with the signal for open passage, though the passage was closing.

Or perhaps I would live. Perhaps the wall would leave a narrow fissure, and I would breathe the iron-tasting seepage until another chamber opened months later. In this mountain, entombment and shelter are often the same shape viewed at different times.

Yet I think the chamber would keep me.

Afterward, the others would salvage what they could and build a new cage around another ringing knot. My body would disappear into calcite. My long name would become impossible to repeat because too many of its passages were gone. Children born later would know me only as an irregular after-note in the Bell Chamber’s toll.

That is what I would become at last: not a ghost, exactly, and not a preserved self.

A delay in the stone.

When the new keeper struck the signal for open passage, the mountain would answer from distant hollows. Then, beneath those replies, there would sometimes be one more tremor: too soft to locate, too late to belong to the original blow. The keepers would include it in their listening because usefulness matters more than certainty.

Some would say it came from the resonant slab trapped in the wall. Some from the cage of bent bone. Some from a fracture carrying echoes along an extinct route. A child might secretly believe a person remained inside, still performing their work.

All of them would be partly right.

I would find myself there: inside the young stone at the edge of the Bell Chamber, where a wall is still deciding whether it is a barrier, a grave, or an instrument.

And I would be what remains when a keeper finally releases everything except the rhythm.
