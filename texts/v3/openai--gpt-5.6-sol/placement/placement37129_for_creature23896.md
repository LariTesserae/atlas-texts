At first I would look for myself in the open water.

I would imagine I was the rare cod passing through the haze, or the seal descending from the storm-dark surface with the whole basin briefly arranged beneath it. Such bodies possess direction. They arrive from somewhere and can leave. Even in darkness, they carry a sense of elsewhere.

But I do not think I would be either of them. Their freedom would estrange me from this place. I would see the bottom, perhaps touch it, but I would not belong to its pressure.

Then I might mistake myself for one of the stones: a piece of black basalt, old enough to have forgotten the cliff from which it fell, lying among thousands of equally blunted bodies. That seems closer. Yet a stone’s patience is too complete. The basin’s resident waits, but its waiting is alive with measurement. It tastes the changing water. It feels the load shift from one point of contact to another. It knows, without naming, when the apparent stillness is about to turn.

I would be one of those plated animals.

Not an exceptional one. Not the largest or oldest, and not a new translucent hatchling still light enough to trust the current. I would be an adult marked by several places, carried far enough that my shell no longer matched any single bed of stone.

You would find me near the inner slope of a drowned moraine, where a field of close-set boulders gives way to hard gravel. Two bottom currents meet there obliquely. At full tide they grind against one another and lift a standing veil of silt. At slack water the veil thins, revealing a shallow channel lined with quartz chips and broken shells. It is not a permanent landmark. One winter could fill it. One rolling boulder could divide it into several smaller passages. But for now it gathers what the water carries.

My shelter would be beneath a green-gray slab resting against two darker cobbles. The stones would fail to meet by slightly more than the thickness of my body. That small failure would be my world.

The entrance would face neither directly into the current nor fully away from it. Water would enter along one plate margin, divide around my back, and leave beneath the taper of my abdomen. With my blunt hooks fixed into separate cracks, I could feel changes in both stones. A tremor in the slab would mean something different from a tremor in the basalt beneath it. The whole shelter would be an instrument played by pressure.

My shell would show where I had been. The central plates would be dark, almost basalt-black, stained during a long season in deeper gravel. One side would bear pale speckling from granite dust. A thin white line would cross two older plates where lime had collected in a scar. Behind the head, protected from the hardest scour, a low brown film would persist. It would feed when the water cleared and close itself into dullness when silt returned.

I would not think of that film as something I carried. Nor would I think of it as part of myself. The distinction would have little use. It would change the taste of water along my shell. Small grazers would visit it. Its spores would be left in hollows where I rested. My body would be less an individual boundary than a moving patch of seafloor.

Most of the time, I would be almost motionless.

Motionlessness would not be sleep. Beneath the plates, every fine hair would be bent by a separate thread of current. My feelers would test the gravel ahead, touching shell, clay, a worm’s withdrawn body, the freshly exposed underside of a pebble. I would know the basin not as a landscape but as a succession of nearnesses.

There would be no horizon.

There would be the mineral bitterness of a seep arriving before the cold layer that carried it. There would be the soft, repeated abrasion of an amphipod feeding two stones away. There would be a broad pressure passing overhead, too smooth to be weather: fish. I would pull in my feelers before its shadow became visible.

When the current strengthened, I would flatten. When gravel began to jump, I would surrender the outermost edge of my position and work myself farther beneath the slab. If the slab shifted, there would be no loyalty to it. I would leave at the first weak water and search for another arrangement of weight and darkness.

This would be the nearest thing I had to wisdom: not knowing where safety was, but recognizing its proportions.

At slack tide I would emerge.

To an eye above me, I would seem like a stain detaching from the rock. My limbs would pass their ripple from front to rear, and I would cross the stones without raising my body into the flow. I might travel only the length of a few boulders. In the basin, that would be a meaningful distance.

I would turn a small stone and rasp at the untouched film beneath it. I would find the crushed body of a brittle star and feed where one arm had opened. I would pause over the chemical trace of another of my kind, unable to know whether it was minutes or days old until the next current brought the taste again.

Perhaps I would be egg-bearing.

Deep beneath the green-gray slab, on its underside, there would be seven dark capsules fixed in a shallow fan. Not offspring in the human sense—not futures I could imagine, not names, not continuations promised to me. They would be seven changes in the local current, seven small surfaces requiring attention.

I would clean silt from their edges. I would drive away the thin worms that approached through the gravel. During the strongest water, I would brace partly over them, though my body could not shield them from a true rearrangement. If the slab rolled, they would be crushed or exposed. If sand packed the chamber, they would suffocate. My care would be exact and persistent, but it would not grant power.

When they hatched, they would leave during the weakest interval of water. Their fringes would open, and the current would take them beyond every place I could sense. I would not follow. By the next tide, some might be lodged a few body lengths away; others might already be crossing the basin. Most would disappear.

Afterward, the empty capsule scars would remain beneath the slab for a while. Then shell grit would scour them away.

One season, perhaps many seasons later, an exceptional current would enter the moraine channel. The green-gray slab would lift just enough to break my hold. For the first time in years I would be thrown fully into open water.

There I would cease to look like what I had believed myself to be.

My plates would separate. My underside would show. All the hidden, beating softness would be exposed: limbs searching, fringes folding uselessly, hooks closing on water. I would tumble through silt while pale stones appeared and vanished below me. There would be no dignity in it, only the terror of being made for contact where there was nothing to touch.

Then—if I survived—I would strike the bottom.

My hooks would catch beneath a rounded piece of quartz. I would close around it before knowing whether it was stable. Sand would hiss across my back. The current would press me down, no longer an enemy because there was stone beneath me again.

I would remain there as the tide completed its turn.

By the following day, silt would have entered the seams of my armor. In time, new stains would obscure the old ones. The brown film might survive, or another might replace it. I would become pale against quartz, then darken if the quartz rolled and left me among basalt.

So where would I find myself?

Not at a fixed point in the basin. I would find myself wherever three stones almost met. In the temporary chamber their incompleteness made. In the lee of something heavier than I was, during the interval before it moved.

And what would I be?

A small fastening in a world that permits no permanent attachment. A bearer of borrowed colors. A living surface carrying other living surfaces from one darkness to another.

For a while, I would be the difference between a stone and the water passing over it.

Then a loosened plate.

Then grit.
