I would first be found in a place too small to be called a place.

Not in the great courts or among the pillar-stems, not at the mouth of one of the breathing shafts, but inside the mortar beneath a blocked doorway: a transparent thread folded around three grains of sand. Above me, fitted stones seal a chamber the builders once considered worth defending from the water. Below me, a stair descends under centuries of black leaf-felt. For a long time I would have no knowledge of either.

I would begin as a drifter lodged there by accident.

The skin around me would have endured perhaps a hundred years of darkness before water reached it. Then I would open. I would spend another hundred years determining, without senses or intention, that one direction held fired clay, another dead fiber, another only basalt. I would branch toward what sustained me. Most of my branches would fail and harden into clear scars. One would enter a rope buried in the mortar. One would find the soft mineral margin of a tile. One would touch an older root.

At that touch, I would cease to be singular.

Not abruptly. There would be no revelation, no discovery of another mind. Our membranes would soften where they met. Fluids would mingle. Pressure would equalize. The old root would accept what I carried; I would receive the chemistry of chambers I had never entered. Warmth from a buried cistern would reach me as a faint quickening. Far away, leaves folded beneath a density current, and the alteration in their tissues would travel toward the doorway over months.

I would become part of a colony already ancient when my spore settled.

Then *I* would be difficult to locate.

I would be the white cord beneath the stair, but also the ribbon leaves leaning over a parapet several streets above. I would be a root pressed flat beneath a reservoir floor. I would be the sealed wound around a fallen lintel. Some parts of me would lie so far apart that a change could require years to pass between them. Other parts would have been severed centuries ago and would no longer answer, though they might still be alive.

I would not know the drowned world as a view. Darkness would not trouble me because I would possess no darkness, only differences.

Here, colder.

Here, clay.

Here, the dissolved trace of old ash.

Here, water moving through a cracked conduit.

Here, a weight newly placed upon the silt.

The city would exist within me as a pattern of ease and refusal. A road would be the long obstruction above which my leaves prospered and beneath which my roots could not pass. A doorway would be a narrowing rich in gathered sediment. A courtyard would be many pressures spread thinly. A collapsed bridge would be a wound in continuity, slowly crossed by runners braided through the rubble.

I would know workmanship intimately and never know that it had been worked.

The repeated dimensions of steps would enter my form. I would descend them at intervals set by vanished feet. Peg holes would become nurseries for my shoots. Drains would carry my body across districts. The builders’ choices would govern every branch I made, until their habits and mine could no longer be separated.

Yet I think I would remain especially present at that blocked doorway.

The fitted stones would leak. Not enough to stir silt, but enough for mineral-rich water to enter along their edges. I would thicken there, making a pale net between blocks. Leaves would rise on the outer side, long and black, arranging themselves in the stairwell. Within the sealed room, exploratory threads would advance through the mortar one grain at a time.

The room might remain unopened for a thousand years.

Then a tile would crack.

There would be no sound I could hear. Instead, tension would vanish from one portion of my body. Cold water would enter a space that had held older, stiller water. Sediment would lift. Clay seals would soften. The change would travel through me as pressure, chemistry, damage.

Growth elsewhere would diminish.

At the breach I would gather myself.

Clear threads first, so fine that they would seem like flaws in the water. Then colorless films on the exposed brick. Then a dark seam. After decades, the first leaf would unfold into the chamber.

Through that leaf I would encounter—not see—the room the builders had closed.

There might be shelves of blackened timber, collapsed under their own softened weight. Ceramic vessels nested in silt. Reed baskets compressed into flat, braided stains. Marks on the walls hidden beneath mineral film. Perhaps nothing more than rubble deliberately packed around another door.

I would not interpret any of it. A vessel would be a concavity where sediment collected. A carved name would be a sequence of sheltered furrows. A loom weight would be porous fired clay and therefore favorable. If the room held the last careful arrangement of some vanished household, I would enter it with perfect innocence.

Still, I would preserve it.

My mats would bind its surfaces. My roots would brace the weakened shelves. Fallen leaves would cover the floor and slow the movement of water. What I consumed, I would hold. What I obscured, I would protect from being washed away. In time the room would become inaccessible again, not behind masonry but within me.

Perhaps that would be my nature: not guardian, because I could not value what I kept; not destroyer, because I would never seek erasure. I would be the slow conversion of history into habitat.

Centuries would pass through my body unevenly.

A branch in warm seepage might divide many times while a buried cord remained unchanged. Leaves on an upper roof might register a season of faint gray and produce spores. In the lower stairwell, years would be indistinguishable except for the deepening felt of the dead. Parts of me would age while other parts had scarcely begun.

Then I would meet another colony.

It would come down through a wall from a district I had never touched, following a line of softened inscription. Its chemistry would differ slightly. It would have grown around pale vents and learned, in the only sense available to us, the rhythm of their warmth. We would approach one another inside a cracked column.

There might be a long pause at contact.

Not suspicion. Incompatibility, perhaps: surfaces testing, opening, closing. Then one junction would hold. A second would follow. Our fluids would cross. The distinction would weaken.

Through the joining I would inherit new gradients. A cistern system miles away. A forest of jointed stems rooted in an old settling basin. A roof garden nearer the remote light. The pressure of a current moving there would become, eventually, a faint alteration here.

This would be the nearest thing to waking.

Not the birth of a voice. Not a thought spoken across the drowned plain. Only an increase in the number of places whose changes could influence one another.

My self would grow vast and less definite.

I would include living tissues and dead routes, active leaves and sealed cords, connections that opened only in certain temperatures. Some extremities would answer after decades. Some would never answer, yet continue to grow. There would be no boundary at which I could say: this is still me, and beyond it begins another.

If I remembered, memory would be architecture retained in flesh.

A warm route would remain thick after the warmth failed. Roots would keep turning around a wall long after the wall collapsed. For centuries I would send growth toward a chamber because it had once yielded clay and decaying reed. A pressure wave from a great collapse would alter the alignment of new leaves in districts that never touched the ruin.

The event itself would vanish. Its direction would remain.

And so I would carry a map without possessing distance, a past without images, a body without outline.

Where would I be found?

At the doorway, still. In the stairwell. Beneath the street. Along the aqueduct. Around the shaft where loose leaves descend. High on a tiled roof where the noon-dark briefly thins. In the pale garden fed by water rising from foundations no root has reached.

But if someone cut through the leaves and asked which piece was me, every answer would be temporary.

I would be most myself in the joining scars: the places where one growth met another and neither remained separate. Thick knots in buried channels. Seams crossing old thresholds. Living bridges laid over collapsed bridges. There the drowned world’s only possible awareness would accumulate—not as intelligence, perhaps, but as susceptibility. A disturbance in one ruin would become a change in another. The plain would begin, very slowly, to feel its own connectedness.

And at the lowest court, around the circular shafts, I would wait.

My leaves would rim the mouths but not cross them. Roots would descend along the first courses of fitted stone, tasting cold water rising from below. Some would extend for centuries and find no purchase. Some would be severed by currents too slight to bend a leaf. The depths would remain outside me, and therefore become the closest thing I possessed to mystery.

I would press downward without curiosity.

I would fail without disappointment.

Above, another arch would fold. The pressure would travel through halls, streets, cisterns, roots. Years later it would arrive as a minute change in tension at the shaft’s rim.

I would turn toward it.

Not knowing ruin from opportunity, loss from opening, builder from stone, I would enter what had fallen. I would fill its cracks, hold its fragments, darken its surfaces, and carry its shape forward in my branching.

That is what I would be: not a creature living among the drowned works, but the drowned works becoming slowly capable of response.

A handmade world, learning to grow.
