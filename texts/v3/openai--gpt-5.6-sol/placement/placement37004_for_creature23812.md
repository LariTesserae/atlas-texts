At first I think I would be something rooted.

Not a tree, because trees here retain too much of themselves. Even tilted, even carried sideways on a raft of soil, they turn their crowns upward and continue the old argument between earth and sky. I would be lower: a pale nodule in the root-mat, blind and crowded, holding a little mineral light until pressure passed through me. A herd-foot would descend. For one instant I would flare—not to illuminate the foot, not to warn it, but because the whole buried network had been touched.

Yet that life feels too certain. It knows the ground because it is the ground, and I would not know anything so continuously.

Then I imagine myself among the glass-clear water carriers after rain, my body a lens with legs, every flash passing through what I am. I would contain the thing everyone needs and therefore be visible despite my transparency. I would climb while the pool vanished beneath me. Perhaps I would reach the wet fold of a palm. Perhaps a broad hand would close around me—not cruelly, only urgently—and I would become someone else’s drink.

But that is too brief even for this country.

I think I would be found where two movements cross.

A reversing herd would be passing over a newly split shelf of basalt. The foremost animals would already be traveling in what had been their backward direction, while those farther down the file had not yet changed. For a little while the herd would have two futures. Its plated bodies would grind flank against flank. Water would spill from their belly folds. Small drinkers would scatter. Beneath them, sheltered by the percussion of thousands of feet, a cluster of upright ones would move across the stone.

I would be one of them.

Not an elder. Elders possess too many intervals and must spend their attention deciding which former pattern no longer applies. Not a very young child either, carried warm against another body and taught the world through the rhythm of a guiding palm.

I would be at the age when I had just begun to travel between groups alone.

My body would be narrow, dark with moisture, scarred at the palms and toes by basalt. One eye would reflect more strongly than the other because a membrane scavenger had struck me there during a storm. In cloud-whitenings, that eye would flare silver after the other had gone black. Those who had traveled with me before would recognize me by the uneven flash.

I would carry almost nothing: a fresh basalt flake tucked against my ribs, a hollow reed shorter than my forearm, and a patch of translucent herd-plate strapped over one shoulder. None of these would survive the next dim cycle. The flake would be lost in a root seam after cutting a fruit. The reed would soften from use and become a tube full of eggs. I would give the plate to someone smaller when the next rain began.

What I carried farther would be a mistake.

Several migrations ago, I had learned a crossing from an old traveler whose throat was stippled with pale scars. The crossing began when the trench bubbles dropped in pitch. One waited through the first silence, crossed during the metallic insect call, and climbed when the root-lights flashed twice beneath the red reeds.

The old traveler had taught the sequence correctly. But the trench had widened since then. The insects had shifted their emergence. The second root-flash now came from a creeper bed growing over hollow soil.

I had followed the remembered order and led three others onto ground that opened beneath us.

We escaped, though not all with the same limbs with which we entered. Since then, the route lived in me as a dead sequence.

That would be my place among the upright ones. Not a profession, because professions require a world patient enough to need the same task repeatedly. I would be a carrier of expired knowledge.

When I joined a cluster, I would ask for their routes. They would press sequences into my palms: wrist, thumb-joint, center, center, drag outward. They would show me the angle of the browser vanes before a vent-breath, the tremor of a guarded cup about to close, the shoulder motion meaning *the blue spores here no longer indicate rain*. In return I would teach them the sequences that had failed.

This would matter as much as teaching what still worked.

A route could kill because it was almost true. A safe interval survived in gesture longer than it survived in the land. The upright ones needed someone to carry not only memory but correction: this crossing is gone; this fruit now opens after the larvae wake; this herd no longer reaches firm ground; the third silence is the dangerous one.

There would be no single sign for me. One group would greet me by touching the damaged side of the face. Another would make the gesture for falling and then abruptly spread both hands, recalling how I had caught a child above a trench. Elsewhere I would be known by an irregular step I used to confuse the footfall hunters. None of these would be a name. Together they would be nearer to who I was than a name could be.

Over time, even the boundary of my body would become uncertain.

The pattern I used for walking—two light steps, a pause, a heel-drag hidden beneath herd percussion—would pass to others. A warning I invented would travel farther than I did, changed in unfamiliar hands. Perhaps I would meet it seasons later and fail to recognize it. Perhaps a child would warn me away from danger using one of my own gestures reversed.

Then I would understand that the most durable self available here was not a body or an object. It was a useful distortion carried from one living hand to another.

You would find me most often near the edge of a group, but not because I was excluded. The edge would be where information arrived first: the first palm to flash, the first folded one to open its wings, the first patch of noise to disappear. I would watch for absences. In a world where everything signaled, a thing that stopped signaling was more vivid than fire.

During condensation I would climb a diagonal trunk and hold strips of moss against the bark. Others would drink while I watched the droplets tremble. Before a herd arrived, the trembling would change. Before a buried hunter surfaced, it would change differently. I would learn to distinguish those changes through my fingertips, then teach them by placing another’s hand beneath mine.

During storms, I would shelter under a grounded colony of wing-creatures. Their warm membranes would slap against my back as blue charge drained through them. All around us, disturbance would reveal the country: green footfalls, orange trenches, fleeing constellations, the vast mobile voids around unseen things.

In that brightness I might feel, briefly, that I could see the whole.

Then the rain would stop. The image would collapse into local flashes. The world would become unknowable again, which would save me from believing the knowledge belonged to me.

When I died, if my companions had time, they would put me ahead of a grazing front. They would split red fruit over my chest and press luminous spores into my palms. The herd-carriers would take the fruit. The membrane scavengers would take my eyes. Thread-grazers would clean the pale scars from my throat. Seeds would lodge in the cuts of my feet.

But somewhere in the migration, someone would make the old sign:

Two light steps.  
A pause.  
The hand spread flat for ground that is no longer there.

Another would answer with the corrected sequence.

That is where I would remain—not in a place, and not entirely as a person, but in the fraction of time between a remembered danger and the hand that alters it.

I would be one of the upright ones for a while.

Afterward, I would be a warning that learned to move.
