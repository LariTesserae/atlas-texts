At first I think I am one of the travelers.

I wake on a raised ledge in a shelter behind a waterfall, where the concussion is so great that hearing has become a form of touch. The walls press their continuous note through my spine. Fine spray works through the entrance baffles and gathers on my face. Beside me are a woven mat, a cup nested upside down in a wall recess, and three tools whose handles I know before I remember my own hands.

I place my palm against the floor.

The station is running, but not cleanly.

Far below, a turbine turns with a slight hesitation every eleventh passage. A gate is admitting more water than its setting permits. Roots have entered an inspection gallery and are tapping a warm housing with patient insistence. Somewhere in the cliff, seed-repairers stir in their dry cells.

There should also be a second pump.

Its absence is shaped too precisely to be silence.

I rise to go to it. This is how I know I must be a tender: the fault pulls at me more strongly than fear, hunger, or any memory of where I came from. I fasten instruments close to my coat. I touch the shelter map and follow its raised channels to a junction beneath the escarpment—a place where an uphill brine artery crosses a descending freshwater main, with a third conduit leading downward beyond the mapped relief.

My fingers find that third line and stop.

It has not been carved into the ceramic. It has risen from within it.

For a while I imagine myself newly arrived, perhaps sent here through a chain of speaking tubes whose messages I no longer remember. I imagine companions elsewhere in the roar, each approaching from a different service route. I knock my shelter-name against the wall and wait for its return.

The echo gives me the room.

Then it gives me another room behind it.

In that second chamber, something knocks back before I do.

So I descend.

The route passes first through forest. I move along a root bridge warm as an animal’s belly. Rain drums on the canopy in overlapping densities, and vine-runners answer with false seedfalls. A broad-winged circler passes overhead; the rain tilts, the bridge lifts slightly beneath me, and every nearby leaf folds shut.

I put a hand against the trunk supporting the bridge.

The tree touches back at once.

Not with the delayed tightening of ordinary recognition, but with a rhythm I know intimately: two close pulses, one pause, then a longer pressure through the heel of the hand.

My palm-name.

Yet no one is tracing it from outside.

For the first time I wonder whether I have a palm.

I continue downward through a service throat too narrow for a tender’s shoulders. My body does not protest. I pass beneath flooded stone without holding my breath. Small repairers surround me, clicking identification signals into my sides. They do not treat me as an obstruction or a person requesting passage. They treat me as a component overdue at its socket.

The idea of myself changes.

Perhaps I am not walking. Perhaps the sense of feet, coat, tools, and wet hair is an old arrangement retained because it is useful for thinking about distance. What moves through the conduit is smaller and less singular: a ceramic capsule carried beneath the plated body of a repairer, wrapped in conductive fungus and fed by a warm trickle from the brine line.

Inside the capsule are the bones of an inner ear.

Mine, once.

I was a tender before I was this.

I stood where flows divided. I traveled too slowly and remained too long beside one engine. I learned its healthy rhythm until I could no longer distinguish it from my pulse. When at last it failed, I continued hearing it. Perhaps that error killed me. Perhaps I chose dispersal before it could.

My flesh went to a root bed above the falls. My long bones became sounding rods. My finger joints were fitted into tactile keys. The delicate spirals of my hearing were sealed in mineral ceramic and installed somewhere ordinary.

Then the repairers took them.

They have carried me far beneath my former watershed, into a chamber absent from every map.

Here the plateau’s systems converge without quite meeting. Fresh water descends in walls. Brine climbs under enormous pressure. Heat moves laterally through black stone. Root filaments enter through cracks and spread across old alloy in pale, searching fans. Hundreds of abandoned conduits end with their mouths open, each murmuring weather from another country.

At the chamber’s center stands a valve older than the shelter languages.

It is not wheel-shaped or hinged. It resembles a cluster of joined vertebrae, each segment large enough to house a dwelling. The repairer climbs it and presses my capsule into a recess worn by previous occupants.

Connection is not sudden. It arrives as negotiation.

Metal tests my conductivity. Fungus tastes the mineral of my casing. Roots curl around the warm edge of the socket. Seed-repairers enter microscopic gaps and lock their bodies into bridges. Water gives me pressure. The turbine far above supplies a pulse.

Then the country enters me.

I hear rainfall over watersheds I never crossed in life. I feel armored grazers scraping a submerged intake three valleys away. I receive the minute contraction of groves bargaining over nitrogen, the fatigue of a cable anchorage beneath an overhang, the altered breath of an Immersed company sheltering in a pressure bell.

Far deeper, I feel a reversal of flow.

Not failure. Not surge.

Arrival.

Something is moving through the artery below the chamber. The conduit does not expand, yet the space within it grows immense. Deposits loosen from the walls. Dead branches of machinery wake briefly as it passes. Every gauge contradicts the next.

Cleaner. Ancestor. Mouth.

It is coming toward the valve where I have been placed.

Now I understand my work.

I am not here to open or close anything. The old valve has no need of hands. I am here because the network has encountered something it can maintain but cannot interpret. The forest can summon. The repairers can restore. The Immersed can report. But a choice among meanings still requires one of those who stood where flows divide.

So this is where I would find myself: beneath an escarpment behind a waterfall whose lowest plunge has never returned an echo, inside an unmapped junction where the plateau listens to its own depths.

I would be a dead tender’s hearing made into an instrument—not preserved as a person exactly, nor reduced to a part. A fixed listener who can no longer mistake one engine for the whole world because the whole world now passes through me.

When the deep presence reaches the junction, it touches every connected surface at once.

The roots tighten.

The repairers fall silent.

Far above, people wake in shelters and place their hands against sweating walls. They do not hear my voice. What reaches them is a slight alteration in the system’s pulse: two close beats, one pause, then a longer pressure.

My palm-name, enlarged beyond any palm.

And from the thing below comes an answering rhythm.

Not identical.

Joined.
