At first I would look for myself in the center of the basin.

That is where a self accustomed to being singular would stand: on the compacted earth, able to see the shelves, the three enclosures, the seep, the separate plants. I would imagine I was the one taking the place in, and that the basin existed around me as a complete, quiet scene.

But nothing living here occupies the center. The center is dry. It is only the point from which everything can be seen at once, and sight belongs to a world this basin does not need.

So I would move toward the water.

Perhaps I would be the green in the deepest pool, dim beneath the old-linen sky. Yet the green is only the visible fraction, the briefest declaration of something mostly concealed. It is what the inhabitant looks like where weak light and clear water happen to meet. To call that patch the creature would be like calling a single warmed stone the summer.

I would have to go under.

I would find myself just inside the limestone at the pool’s northern margin, where the visible stain fades into pores too fine for roots. Not in one chamber, and not as one thread. I would be the exchange passing among threads: a little water admitted through a membrane; a dissolved mineral handed inward; a trace of carbon moving back toward the light. My body would be discontinuous by ordinary measures. Stone would lie between one living part and another, yet the damp would connect us. A distance of a finger’s width might be a continent. A seam beneath the shrubs might still be part of what I called myself.

Even then, “I” would be too strong a word.

There would be no place in the colony where the whole basin was known. The cells near daylight would know daylight only as a change in what could be made. The deeper cells would know the seep as chemistry, not as flowing water. Those beneath the ruined walls would know the walls as pressure, porosity, and rain arriving from altered directions. No part would remember human hands stacking limestone. The memory of that event would persist only in the routes moisture now took.

That would be my kind of memory: not an image retained, but a condition changed.

A wet year would remain in me as a wider fissure.  
A long drought as an extra mineral skin.  
The fall of one wall-stone as a new dark contact between surfaces.  
The death of a grass root as a brief richness descending through a crack.

I would not experience centuries as waiting. Waiting requires an imagined arrival. In dryness I would close, and the world would become nearly eventless—not unbearable, not lonely, simply reduced. Metabolism would narrow until existence and suspension were almost the same thing. Then condensation would gather. A membrane would soften. Somewhere beyond any single cell’s awareness, the colony would begin again.

Awakening would not happen all at once. It would pass through me as darkening stone.

If there were anything like sensation, rain would be a loosening. Light would be a pressure near the surface. The mineral-laden seep would have the intimacy of breath, though no rhythm regular enough to count. I would not hear droplets striking the pool. I would register the consequences later: dilution, movement, a channel briefly joined to another channel, a handful of cells carried farther than their lineage had traveled in centuries.

Most of those travelers would die in the gravel.

I would not mourn them. Mourning belongs to creatures for whom each body is a gathered and irreplaceable center. Here, division is quieter. A thread pinches apart; both portions continue if they can. A fragment becomes isolated by drying stone. It may remain dormant beyond all contact with the rest, carrying no message back. Is it still me? The basin offers no reason to decide.

Perhaps that is what I would be here: not the colony itself, but the uncertainty of its boundaries.

I would be present beneath the green pool and absent from it. Present under the shrubs through mineral work done before their roots arrived. Present in the pale rims as discarded substance. Present in the ruined enclosures where lichen and hidden threads make the lifted stones ordinary again. Some of what I had been would no longer be alive, yet would continue to govern where water paused. My dead layers would shelter my living ones. My smallest acts would outlast the cells that performed them.

From above, no one would see a body.

They would see spacing: herbs here rather than there, a grass clump lodged in a softened crack, moisture retained beneath one ledge after the others had dried. They might attribute each fact to stone, chance, or the shape of the basin. They would not be wrong. I would not stand apart from those things strongly enough to deserve sole credit.

So I would find myself along the northern seam, but also under the western walls, around the pools, and beneath the remote shrubs. I would be most alive at interfaces: light and limestone, water and air, root and fissure, ruin and ground. Never the object on either side, but the slow transaction between them.

And if some impossible visitor knelt, broke open a stone, and searched for me, the exposed threads would fade before they could make sense of what they saw. They would hold a pale fragment and think they had missed me.

They would be right too.

I would be what remained in the unbroken basin: a life without a viewpoint, making room for other lives by imperceptibly changing what can hold water. Not the keeper of the place, because the place requires no keeping. Not its mind, because nothing here needs to be represented to itself.

I would be its long, distributed habit of becoming habitable—so slow that the work resembles stillness, and so thoroughly given to the stone that no clear boundary survives between inhabitant and world.
