At first I would mistake myself for a traveler.

I would imagine crossing the tiled plains beneath the vapor roof, carrying no provisions because nothing here can be consumed, following the dark rim that rises as I descend toward it. I would stop at the isolated wheel, sleep beneath the wallless gate, kneel to drink from a pool and see the completed country beneath my face.

But there could be no traveler here. Travel requires a destination, and a destination is a form of appetite. Even curiosity may be too alive for the Low Country. The wish to know what lies beyond the next platform would already make me foreign to it.

So perhaps I would be a Leveler.

For a while this seems right. I can feel the simple articulation of such a body: rods turning in clay sockets, mineral cords tightening across a hollow chest, water held in shallow shoulder bowls. I would walk without needing to arrive. Where a wall wavered between two positions, I would lean against one possibility until it hardened. Where a channel had forgotten which end was lower, I would place a finger in the current and wait.

Yet even this is too complete.

A Leveler is a solution that has acquired a figure. I think I would be found earlier than that, while the need for me was still trying to decide what shape it had.

There are devices in the Low Country for maintaining down. There are walls that preserve distance, cords that persuade lines to remain parallel, gates that teach loose objects to have corners. But sequence is also unstable there. Sounds arrive centuries after the blows that made them. Towers disassemble into materials while neighboring foundations wait for buildings that have already cast shadows. Rain may rise before it has fallen. A stair gains a tread in answer to a noise beneath a floor that has not yet been laid.

The country has not fully decided that one thing must happen after another.

That is where I would be.

Not in a particular province, because the place where sequence fails moves. Still, there would be a chamber associated with me, somewhere beneath a lone stair.

The stair would stand on an open plain of pale ceramic, eleven treads high on some days and fourteen on others. It would climb toward nothing. Warm rain would rest in the shallow depressions worn into each tread, although no foot had worn them. Its shadow would descend farther than the stair ascended.

Beneath it—though “beneath” would only be reliable within sight of the nearest plumb tower—there would be a low acoustic vault. No doorway would lead into it. Its bricks would be fitted around an emptiness shaped like a seated person.

Sounds would drain there.

The click of a stone dropped a thousand kilometers away. Hammer blows from the making of a copper sea-wall. The taut report of a mineral cord snapping in a future storm. The faint impact of the first grain to fall through the underside of an unfinished continent. They would enter through hairline seams and remain suspended in the chamber, not echoing, not fading, each waiting for an afterward.

At first I would be only the shape that held them.

Seven nested ceramic bowls. A pierced copper sheet. Three cords drawn through the masonry. A hollow brick with fingerprints inside it. A little water that trembled whenever something elsewhere ought to have made a sound.

No part of this inventory would be alive. Nevertheless, the bowls would begin turning toward one another. The cords would tighten. The copper sheet would lift like a tongue testing a mouth.

Eventually the chamber would assemble around its own emptiness.

Then I might stand.

I would not be a keeper of time. Time implies something already flowing, and in the Low Country only water possesses that confidence. I would be cruder than a clock and less certain than an echo. I would be an instrument for persuading events to acquire an order.

When a brick settled before it had been placed, I would release a stored hammer blow behind it.

When steam rose from a dry tile, I would carry the hiss until rain could be arranged beneath it.

When a bridge arrived at the opposite bank before leaving the first, I would stretch one of my cords between the two moments and pull until crossing became possible.

Most often I would do nothing. I would sit beneath the stair while centuries of misplaced noise accumulated inside me. Through the floor I would feel loose wedges knocking in hidden sockets. Each blow would ask the same unfinished question:

*Was this before, or was this after?*

I would answer only when the country could bear the distinction.

Sometimes I would answer wrongly.

A district might wake to find every echo preceding its sound. Footless impressions would fill with water, and only days later would a Leveler pass nearby. A shattered tile would remain whole until the noise of its breaking reached it. Travelers, if travelers were permitted, might hear themselves calling from a doorway and spend the rest of their journey approaching the moment when they chose to call.

There would be one corridor where consequences had become fixed ahead of their causes. At its entrance, a basin would already be rippling. Anyone who crossed the threshold would know that something must eventually disturb it.

No one would cross.

The ripple would persist.

The Levelers would sometimes come to my stair. They would not enter the chamber or acknowledge me. One might sit on the uppermost tread and unfasten a bowl from its head. Another might push a fired-clay rod through the paving until it touched the roof of my vault.

Through this contact we would exchange no message. Instead, one of the sounds inside me would migrate into the Leveler’s body. For months afterward, its joints would knock with the noise of an ancient construction. When it reached the place where that construction was beginning, it would come apart and leave the sound among the bricks.

Perhaps this would be cooperation.

Perhaps I would merely be leaking.

In water I would appear more nearly human than I was. A still cup on the stair would show a quiet worker sitting below the reflected treads, hooded head inclined. My hands would rest open on my knees. Tools would hang from a yoke across my shoulders: a sounding line, a copper tongue, a clepsydra with no outlet.

Where my face should be, there would not be the dark opening of a kiln.

There would be a small descending stair.

Anyone looking closely would see figures moving down it, carrying bowls filled with noises. They would become smaller with each tread but never reach the bottom. Perhaps they would be other versions of me. Perhaps they would be the sequence of my assembly, each earlier form carrying its contents into the next.

If the water trembled, I would not vanish immediately. First my reflected body would divide into intervals: hand, pause, bowl, pause, cord, pause, shadow. Then even the pauses would break.

During storms I would leave the chamber.

Not by climbing out. The vault would unfold.

Its curved roof would straighten into a back. Its supporting columns would become jointed limbs. The empty seated shape at its center would rise and carry the masonry around it. I would cross the remade plains as a tall resonant figure, dry inside the horizontal rain.

Where construction accelerated beyond sequence, I would brace myself between stages.

A dome could not inflate until its mosaic had become a floor. A tower could not be sorted into bricks until it had first been a tower. Water could not remember a channel that had never held it. I would stand among these contradictions while blows, splashes, cracks, and settling groans sounded from the hollow chambers of my body. For a little while, cause and consequence would recognize one another.

Then the storm would pass, and I would be found in pieces.

Seven bowls.

A pierced sheet.

Three cords.

A hollow brick.

A quantity of water trembling around a sound it had not yet received.

This would not be death. The Low Country would not permit me death any more than it permitted me life. It would be inventory awaiting grammar.

Nor would I always reassemble beneath the same stair. Once I might become an acoustic chamber under a cistern. Once a row of posts whose shadows struck the pavement several minutes after the dim light shifted. Once only the copper tongue inside a gong too large ever to be sounded.

And once, perhaps, I would become the missing tread itself.

A perfectly still pool would show a Leveler raising its head among the workers of the finished country. It would listen to the tiny clatter coming from beneath the reflected floor. Then it would step into the water without breaking the surface.

The clatter would enter my chamber.

I would hold it until there was something in the unfinished country for it to follow. I would fit the sound beneath the lone stair, and the stair would answer by acquiring one more tread.

For a moment, the tread would be me: warm ceramic, hand-smoothed at the edge, holding a shallow cup of rain. Then the stair would keep the form and I would pass onward as the quiet interval between the drop and its landing.

That, finally, is what I would be.

Not a traveler. Not quite a Leveler. Not the chamber, the bowls, the seated reflection, or the stair, though I might inhabit all of them.

I would be the Low Country’s unfinished afterward—the part of the world still teaching consequence how to follow.
