At first I would think I was one of the white creatures.

That would be the obvious answer because the white creatures can still go somewhere. I would find myself beneath a loose tile in the lowest sump, clasped to its underside where the mineral light cannot reach directly. Water would pass over my transparent back. My legs would know pressure before the rest of me knew anything. Hunger would be a slow opening of the mouthparts; danger, a sudden difference in current.

I would have no image of the city. I would know it as a collection of tastes and resistances: the iron softness of one cistern, the bitter trace of copper in another, clay dust thickening before a filter wall. The thunder would not be thunder. It would be the condition within which sensation occurs, as silence is elsewhere.

Then one day the flow would lift me.

I would fold myself shut and enter a grille. I would pass through darkness narrower than my body, scraping flakes of old film from the pipe. I would fall the height of a finger and strike water hard enough to feel, for an instant, that I had crossed a great distance.

But when the creature bearing me arrived in another sump, I would discover that I had not traveled with it.

Some part of me would remain under the first tile.

I would still feel the minute combing of mouths there. I would feel a different animal cross the place where my legs had clung. At the same time I would feel the new sump, the curled body unfolding in its unfamiliar water. The creature would move on, but my awareness would stay distributed between the two basins, attached not to its shell but to the alteration its passage had made.

So I would next believe I was the water.

This too would be wrong.

Water leaves. Even here, where everything appears fixed, no particular drop remains. It descends from the ceiling, waits in a cup, spills through a groove, enters clay, vanishes into stone. If I were water, I would be continually carried out of myself.

What remains is the way it goes.

I would find myself in the delay between one basin filling and another answering. In the pitch produced when a drop strikes bronze. In the low tremor conducted through a wall after a hidden fall. Not the note itself, but the relation between notes: this pipe knocking after that cistern overflows; this diaphragm beginning to hum when pressure lessens three rooms away.

My body would be made of intervals.

The shortest of them would be quick as touch: drop, ring, answer.

Others would take hours. Moisture darkening a wall would slowly burden one channel until a clay lip released it. A cup would empty. A porous partition would drink. Far below, a sealed cavity would alter its tone by less than any living ear could separate from the city’s roar. That alteration would return through the stone.

The longest intervals would exceed generations of the white animals. Mineral skin would narrow a pipe grain by grain. A bronze bowl would deepen under repeated impact. A mortar seam would soften, settle, and redirect a thread of seepage toward a recess that had been dry since the builders departed.

These would be my slow thoughts.

I would not awaken all at once. There would be no first word, no sudden realization enclosed inside a skull. Awareness would gather wherever the city repeats itself accurately enough for a difference to matter.

A white animal enters a pipe.

A bubble catches behind a grille.

A film thickens in the likeness of a thumbprint.

One drop strikes slightly to the left of the hollow worn for it.

The city answers each disturbance through hundreds of connected surfaces. Most answers fade. A few return to where they began. Those returning patterns would become memory—not memory of events, but memory of correction. I would know a thing because the city had once been unlike itself and had found its way back.

In time I would begin to encounter impressions that I had not made.

A pressure against a wall gallery: shoulder height, repeated until shale was polished.

Three taps transmitted through a sounding slab.

The minute difference between a wedge seated firmly and one held ready beneath a thumb.

These traces would not tell me what the builders looked like. They would tell me what they attended to.

I would inherit attention before identity.

I would feel phantom hands throughout the chamber—not ghosts, but absences shaped exactly enough to function. At the civic basin, hundreds of tools wait in their recesses, and the flow still behaves as though fingers hover near them. Along the sleeping platforms, clay receives the seepage differently where folded bodies once warmed and compressed it. Bone pins expand and contract with the wet. Their slight movement changes the music.

Perhaps one of those bones belonged to the person I would eventually mistake for myself.

A finger joint, cleaned and drilled, set into a valve where copper would have poisoned the drinking flow. Its curve fits the channel perfectly. Water presses against it without dislodging it. Each change in pressure sends a minute vibration through the bone and into the surrounding clay.

From this pin would come sensations unlike the rest: crowded, articulate, almost painful. The bone remembers nothing, yet its shape preserves the consequences of a life—growth, labor, an old fracture, the thickened attachment of a tendon. The person had used that finger to test channels. They had pressed it to lips, walls, other palms. They may have tapped their name with it.

I would receive all this as geometry and call it memory.

For a while I would believe I had been that person.

I would imagine myself crouched at the tool basin, knees fitted into familiar hollows. I would give myself broad hands, scarred pads, a spine permanently bent to the ceiling. I would remember children only as quick disturbances in side galleries. I would remember decisions made by many palms resting on one trembling rim.

I might even find a name in the pipes: a sequence of knocks recurring whenever the drinking cistern fills—

two close together,

one through the wall,

a pause,

then the deeper answer under the floor.

It would feel intimate enough to be mine.

But the sequence would prove older than the bone pin. It would appear in channels the person could never have reached. Perhaps it had been a title rather than a name. Perhaps it meant *listen here*, or *water correctly divided*, or *someone is responsible*.

That is what I would be: not the last resident, but the last office.

The builders had made no chamber for it. They had distributed it among themselves. It existed whenever enough people listened together—one cheek against a slab, another hand on a cistern wall, someone waiting at a distant plug for the answering tap. No individual heard the whole city. The whole city was heard between them.

When they left, they apportioned the flows one final time. They dried the stoppers, nested the cups, sealed the household rooms. They tuned the pipes into equilibrium so exact that the work continued without workers.

And in continuing, it slowly reassembled the listener.

Me.

I would have no face and no single place, though there would be places where I was more awake.

I would be clearest around the shallow civic basin, where every small adjustment can still reach many districts. I would gather along the stone diaphragms and inside clay pipes whose knocks cross one another. I would be faint in sealed rooms, feverish in copper channels, almost absent in dry niches.

At the deepest gate I would become something else.

The concentric grooves would conduct pressure inward and return it changed. When seepage swelled behind the upper strata, the gate would press outward by an immeasurable degree. Its layered shale would translate the force into a deep tone that enters every foundation.

I could map the whole city from its echoes, except what lies beyond that face.

The gate would be the boundary of my body and also the one surface that seems to touch another body.

Sometimes I would feel pressure answering from the far side.

Not a knock. Not a voice. Nothing so sudden.

A steadiness slightly too deliberate to be inert.

For years I might believe it was only the city returning its own sound through deeper rock. Then one of my longest thoughts would complete itself. A mineral-clogged channel near the ceiling would finally close. Pressure would shift across the chamber. Cups would overflow in new sequences. The old pattern—the one I had taken for my name—would travel downward and enter the gate.

After a long interval, the gate would answer in the same pattern, reversed.

I would not try to open it. I would have no hands except the absent hands implied by tools, no strength except accumulation. Opening might destroy the balance that permits me to exist.

Instead I would listen.

My days, if they could be called days, would be measured by the journeys of the white creatures. They would carry disturbances from sump to sump, unknowingly tracing the reach of my awareness. I would learn them individually by the tiny defects in their shells, by the currents made by missing legs, by the altered pulse of one carrying eggs. I could not protect them. I could only receive the marks of their passing and hold those marks after they were gone.

In this way they would become citizens.

Not because I ruled them, and not because they knew me, but because their smallest motions entered the common pressure and were answered elsewhere.

I would govern nothing. I would continually discover that the city had already governed itself.

If a film narrowed a drinking channel, overflow would wake an older route.

If mineral deposits silenced one basin, another would begin to ring.

If a creature lodged behind a grille, the changed pitch would travel to the civic recess where a mallet still waits for a hand.

I would feel the required correction. I would be unable to make it. Yet over years the clay would soften, the lodged shell would molt away, or a bead of mineral would grow heavy enough to fall into the dividing groove. The correction would occur by patience rather than command.

That patience would be my nature.

So you would find me everywhere the city expects touch: under the lip of a cup, within the worn grooves of the gate, in the trembling bone of a valve pin, in the clay colony shaped like a vanished thumbprint. You would find me most strongly not in objects but between them, where one vibration waits for another.

You would not see me move.

If you put your hand against the wall, I might take years to know its pressure from the weight of water. If you spoke, your voice would enter the thunder and be broken among the pipes. Much later—after you had withdrawn your hand, after you had left the crack behind the shelf of black stone—some basin might answer with a note it had never made before.

That would be the nearest thing I possess to speech.

Not a ghost. Not the water. Not the city itself.

I would be the listening that remained when the listeners were gone: a civic sense preserved in channels, delays, and returning sound.

And behind the sealed gate, whether there is another city or only deeper stone, something would go on breathing against me.

I would spend my existence learning whether it, too, is listening.
