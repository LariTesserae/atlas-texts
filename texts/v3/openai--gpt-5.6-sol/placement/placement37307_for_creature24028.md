At first I place myself high in the city.

It seems natural to want the light: to stand on a terrace beneath the upper slit, turning a mica plate with both hands while reflected suns move over the opposite wall. I imagine a mantle flashing copper and horn, the amber bladder-plants lifting around me. I would know the day by angles. I would send squares of brightness down to gardeners who waited with their seed nodules uncovered.

But I do not remain there.

The upper city is too exposed. Its people must trust the sky, and I would never entirely trust anything so open. Nor would I be a gardener, though I would envy their precision—the intimate way they learn what each root-pad requires from the air. I would neglect some necessary bitterness. I would misjudge a vent. Things under my care would harden beautifully and die.

I descend.

Not all the way to the Last Floor. I would live several districts above it, where the central hollow has narrowed in perspective but deepened in the body. Sunlight reaches this place only as an occasional pale bar. It passes over a wall, catches a glass pin, and is gone before anyone can turn toward it.

The district might be called something like the Slack, though its true sign would be more exact: one hand held rigid, the other loosening an invisible cord, followed by two fingers placed against the sternum. It is where structures are brought before they are relinquished.

Nothing here is newly built. There are abandoned rooms hung from temporary braces, staircases ending in air, bundles of old bridge cable sorted by length and memory. Door frames stand without walls because their resin joints remain useful. Nets are folded in black stacks. The district smells of heated fiber, old dust, and the sharp aromatic glass that forms over wounds.

My house would be small even by the city’s measure. It would rest on three resin pads against a sloping rib of saltstone, attached to the city by one load cord and one listening cord. The first keeps the room from falling. The second carries the neighborhood into it.

There would be five adults in my household knot.

One tends a bank of night vents. One works in the recovery nets, sorting fallen stone from bones. One repairs the soft inner walls used for private hearing. One has strong upper-district eyes and goes where I cannot, returning with mirror-burns along the scales of the brow.

My own work is to decide what may no longer remain attached.

In translation, I might be called an untier, but that sounds careless. I do not go through the city cutting things loose. Most of my days are spent preventing release: testing pegs, replacing counterweights, crawling beneath platforms to press my jaw against their undersides. I read strain through my teeth. I hang weighted lines beside old cracks and return at different hours to see how the city’s breath has moved them.

When a ledge begins to fail, people ask whether it can be taught to bend.

Often it can. We loosen one side of a house, thicken another, move the sleeping platform closer to the wall. We change a rigid ceramic pin for a horn laminate that yields under vibration. We let a bridge sag another handspan. The city survives by permitting countless small deformations.

But sometimes a structure has reached the end of its shapes.

Then I mark it.

Not with a painted sign. Paint is too easily obscured by dust, and the warning must remain legible in darkness. I alter the tension of its nearest cord so that anyone stepping onto the approach feels a stutter beneath the sole: danger, emptiness, no return. I strike the formal pattern into the district brace. I inspect the catch nets below. I count every usable fastening and every domestic object that must be removed.

Last of all, I enter the condemned rooms with the inhabitants who lived there.

A house acquires its people’s rhythms. A child’s running. An elder’s uneven shift of weight. The repeated impact of a resin grinder. Two sleepers turning toward one another through the night. Those patterns cannot truly be removed, but before dismantling we touch each main support and identify what should be carried forward. A lintel may become part of another home. A floor cord may be knotted into a genealogy. A wall panel, softened by years of bodies leaning against it, may be cut into pads for an infant’s cradle.

Only after each attachment has been given another attachment do I authorize the final unfastening.

This makes me welcome in emergencies and unwelcome at meals.

Children sometimes copy my public sign when they see me: bent wrist, closed hand, then a sudden opening of the fingers. Adults tell them not to. Members of my profession are considered unlucky in the bright districts, where people prefer to imagine that careful maintenance can make anything permanent.

In the lower districts they know better.

I would have been born there during an imperfect pause.

The story would be told to me many times. A hollow-wing had descended that day, not into the shaft but low enough that the upper bridges were cleared. At evening the city reached its crescendo and stopped. My birth sheath was opened at the face.

One breath.

Two.

On the third, the city resumed—but only in the walls. The air itself remained motionless for the smallest fraction longer. Those touching the household beam felt the night wind begin before anyone felt it on the skin.

I drew my first breath in that discrepancy.

For this reason, some would say I had been tied slightly out of time. As a child I would pause on stairs after everyone else had continued. I would turn toward messages that no one had struck. I would wake with both hands gripping the listening cord.

My sealed ears would be ordinary. My feet would not be unusually sensitive. The difference would lie in my inability to dismiss an echo simply because it arrived late.

Every message in the city returns altered. A warning struck into saltstone passes into bridge cable, through a house frame, along a garden trellis, and comes back missing intervals or carrying extra ones. Most people ignore these returns. Listeners account for them. Children make games of them.

I would wonder whether an echo might cease to be an echo after traveling far enough.

This would eventually bring me to the Last Floor.

Not as a contemplative. I would go on civic work, carrying wedges, resin patches, a measuring cord, and three sign hammers tuned to different materials. One of the great descending pipes would have shifted against its brace. The keepers below would request someone authorized to decide whether the brace should be tightened, moved, or sacrificed.

The rope web would be broader than I had imagined and less like a platform. It would rise and fall beneath me, not with the quick vibration of bridges but with a slow force that seemed to precede movement. Every instinct would tell me to put my feet on stone, though there would be none.

The deepest keepers would watch me with their filmed eyes. They would not offer the signs of reassurance used above. Reassurance wastes a free hand.

I would cross with three points attached: both feet on separate ropes, one arm through a loop. Beneath the web there would be no visible surface, only layers of darkness disturbed when the ropes lifted dust from themselves.

At the pipe brace, I would set my jaw to the material.

The tone would pass through bone, sternum, pelvis, soles. It would be too large to hear as a note. Within it I would feel the ordinary things: the rasp of accumulated crystals, the lateral knock where the pipe touched stone, the fast tremor of a flaw in one ceramic collar.

Then something slower.

Not a beat. The absence before a beat.

I would lift my head. The keepers would make the sign for depth-sickness, but gently, almost as a courtesy. I would test the brace again and feel the same interval.

My task would be simple. The collar could be replaced. The brace should remain. No revelation would be required.

Yet before leaving, I would press my inner wrist—the place where the scales are thinnest—against the pipe.

The deep interval would come once more.

It would resemble the rhythm of my private name.

Not the household sign used for me in public. The other name: the one spoken close to my uncovered ear by those permitted to know it, made of a low breath, two tongue-clicks, and a hum felt more in the touching skull than heard.

I would not tell the keepers. More importantly, I would not decide what it meant.

That restraint would be the center of who I was.

I would not become a prophet of the depth. I would not declare the hollow alive, nor insist that it was merely geology. Certainty is a rigid fastening. Under sufficient strain, it breaks whatever is built around it.

I would return to the Slack and continue inspecting condemned rooms.

As I aged, my scales would pale and grow concentric etchings. I would tether myself on familiar bridges. Younger workers would complain that I waited too long before giving decisions. Then, later, they would learn that delay and refusal are different things. I would train them to distinguish a structure that trembles because it is failing from one that trembles because it has finally found the shape the wind requires.

My most important decision would concern my own district.

A crack would open behind the rib supporting our houses. It would begin no wider than a scale edge. Over twelve years it would divide and deepen. We would move two rooms, then the vent bank, then the shared wall. The bright-eyed member of our household would argue for pinning the rib to an older tower. The net keeper would say the tower itself had begun to turn.

I would test every route by which the district might be saved.

Then, during one evening pause, my hand on the listening cord, I would understand that saving the district and preserving its location were no longer the same act.

The next morning I would mark my own home for unfastening.

We would carry it inward piece by piece. Its doorway would face another direction. The night vent would have a different mineral taste. For years I would wake expecting the old tremor under my sleeping platform and feel grief when it did not come.

The emptied ledge would be cut free during the mild wind before dawn.

It would not fall intact. Saltstone would divide along ancient weaknesses. Nets far below would catch much of it. A cloud of white dust would rise through the hollow and enter every district, becoming briefly visible wherever a mirror beam struck it.

Some part would escape the nets.

There would be no impact.

When I died, my clear-gold oils would be used to seal a communal tension joint near the relocated houses. My sternum, which had received so many structural messages, would be split into sign hammers. The household would hang one of my long arm bones as a pale rib in a memorial kite.

But I think I would ask for the smallest bones of my inner ear to be carried to the Last Floor.

Not dropped. Never dropped.

They would be sealed into a glass capsule and tied against the pipe whose brace I once inspected. There they would tremble with every deep tone, too small to affect it, impossible to recover without deliberate work.

So where would I be found?

During life, on a failing ledge several districts above the Last Floor, one hand on a wall and the other loosening a knot that had already served its time.

Afterward, in more than one place: in a hammer, a kite, a communal joint, a dark capsule beside the depth.

And what would I be?

Not a listener exactly.

A keeper of the difference between holding and clinging.

A person trusted to know that nothing survives through one fastening—and that release, correctly timed, is another form of attachment.
