At first I would think I was one of the wall listeners.

It is the obvious vanity: to imagine myself narrow and damp-clothed, one hand laid against brick, hearing what others cannot. I would live in the western row, perhaps in the fourth dwelling, where an old pipe passes through the bedroom without entering or leaving by any visible aperture. I would own ceramic tablets covered in careful transcriptions. I would know which handrails carry quarrels, which arches release hammer blows in winter, which section of retaining wall sometimes whispers in the voices of children not yet born.

But the ward would not keep me for what I wished to be.

As a child, I would first be noticed at a settling pool.

I would have gone in after a dropped gauge key. The cistern crew would tie a line beneath my arms and lower me through water so clear that descent felt like falling through air. Below would lie the usual things: green coins, white bones, bolts standing upright in silt, a pressure dial with its needle still trembling. The key would rest among them, apparently an arm’s length away and actually several fathoms down.

When they pulled me out, I would have the key.

I would also have one more memory than when I entered.

Not a useful memory. Not even a human one. I would remember resting for a long time beneath a sluice, feeling the machinery pause around the breadth of my back. I would remember warm metal being placed on a bank above me. I would remember neither taking it nor leaving it. The recollection would contain no sight, only pressure: water against a large, soft surface, iron grating overhead, the whole ward dimly present as a pattern of vibration.

The adults would ask me my name. I would answer correctly.

They would ask how many memories I had carried down.

I would lie.

After that, I would not be sent to the cistern crews. Children who return altered are not punished, but neither are they given work near open water. For several years I might be trained on the message lines. I would learn the practical taps for *close the third valve*, *bridge unsafe*, *eel agitation*, *do not answer this sequence*. Yet birds would interrupt whenever I practiced. They would press their beaks to remote pipes and return my signals with one tap added at the end.

Always one.

The instructors would suspect mockery. I would know it was correction.

Eventually the droplet sorters would take me.

You would find me in the combing house, at the last bench beneath the copper return pipe, where the air is colder on the left side of the body than on the right. I would wear a lead-patched apron and keep my hair covered, though moths would still gather at the nape of my neck. Before me would stand trays of ampoules and needles of blackened steel.

My official work would be classification.

This droplet is clear but unwilling.

This one moves east when no surface inclines that way.

This one contains the sound of crockery being set down in a room bricked shut during the ninth recorded descent.

This one falls normally until it crosses the shadow of my hand, whereupon it stops.

I would become skilled, but not celebrated. The ward distrusts brilliance when patience will do. I would be valued for sitting a long while with uncertain water without forcing it into a category.

My labels would be small and exact.

**Apparent origin:** condensation from pipe entering north wall of combing house.

**Possible prior route:** gutter beneath demolished dye workshop.

**Audible inclusion:** three footsteps; wooden bell; rain on zinc.

**Apparent destination:**

There I would leave the usual blank.

At first.

Then, over the course of a descent, I would begin to fill in the second entries.

Not with street names. Water does not care for municipal addresses. I would write:

**The underside of the fifth bridge, after the bell-throat sounds.**

**A dish beneath a bed not yet moved into the room.**

**The mortar around the brick stamped EDRIN VALE.**

**The storm inside the easternmost fruit.**

**The hand of the attendant who stands between the fourth gauge and the fifth.**

No one would know how I obtained these answers. I would say I judged reluctance, clarity, direction, and tone. This would be partly true. The rest would come from the extra memory, which by then would no longer be singular. It would have branched inside me like seepage finding old gutters.

I would remember pipes before they were cast. I would remember the pale stone uncovered and wet, before masonry, before staples. I would remember the retaining walls as banks shaped by water, though no human being had stood there to remember them.

In exchange, I would begin forgetting personal things.

The fourth dwelling.

The face of the person who taught me to stamp labels.

Whether I had siblings.

Which hand was mine when both lay on the bench.

This would not frighten me as much as it should. The ward has gentle language for certain losses. I would be called early porous. A bed might be prepared for me near a warm line, where ivory fungi opened in calm rings. But I would continue working, because the droplets brought not only recollections but destinations, and because increasingly those destinations converged.

Different water wanted different routes, yet the routes all bent toward one place.

Not the capped well itself.

Something beside it. Or beneath it. Or an interval the plaza had been constructed to surround.

I would start carrying selected ampoules out of the combing house at night. This would be against procedure, though not clearly forbidden. I would cross the iron bridges while water mice tested the surface below. The birds would tap my passage ahead of me. Drain crabs would pause in their arrangements and angle their numbered shells toward my feet.

I would not go directly to the plaza. No path in the ward is made more true by being direct.

I would follow the droplets’ remembered routes: through an abandoned valve kiosk, down a staircase that had become a cascade, across the roof of the boiler hall, into the round tower among the descending chains. I would wait wherever an ampoule clouded. Sometimes for minutes. Sometimes for a season.

Then I would seal the ampoule into a seam, lower it into a canal, leave it beneath an elder’s hanging bed, or trade it to a crab for a washer polished mirror-bright.

Each droplet would disappear.

Years later, some would return.

One would appear in a household dish, filling it from below.

One would bead on an inward-facing brick over the name of a dead attendant.

One would fall from a clear sky into the hollow fruit of a grate-rooted tree.

One would be found inside a gauge, resting on the wrong side of the glass.

With every return, I would lose another boundary. My skin would remain cool. My hands would remain strong. I would still eat eel cakes and dark grain, still patch my boots, still answer when spoken to. But moths settling on my coat would no longer imitate the cloth. They would imitate wet brick. Occasionally they would align themselves across my back into the outline of a doorway.

The moth keepers would cover me with a blank cloth and report me.

The well attendants would come without metal. They would ask no interpretive questions. They would merely watch.

Perhaps then I would understand that I had never been learning where the droplets were going.

They had been learning how to go through me.

At the next simultaneous movement, I would stand at the edge of the circular plaza, outside the animals’ avoidance line but not among the people. The cap would descend its few millimeters. Chains would tighten. The crane would raise what could not be seen. Every fungus in the warm lines would fold shut.

The five needles would point inward.

For once, one of them would point at me.

The low adjustment would pass through the ward. I would feel it in brick, water, bone, and iron—but inside my body it would not pass onward. It would remain. All the delayed noises stored in me would find their surfaces at once: rain, tapping birds, demolition blows, children’s wooden bells, the argument in the sealed pump room, warm bearings laid on a bank.

Beneath them would be the sound I brought up from the settling pool as a child.

Not the sound of a creature.

The sound of being large enough that a ward could mistake you for the ground beneath it.

Afterward, I might still appear to live in the combing house. A figure would sometimes be seen at the last bench, sorting droplets into lead-lined trays. Labels in my handwriting would continue to appear, though no one would see them written. New workers would be told not to disturb the dark coat hanging from the chair, even when it seemed to breathe.

But if you came looking for me, that is not where you would find me.

You would find me wherever the second entry had been left blank.

In the thin water covering exposed stone. In the pause between a bird’s tap and its answer from another quarter. In the extra weight registered by the empty crane. In the memory a child carries back from the pool, beyond the number they took down.

I would be neither worker nor broad creature, neither resident nor machine.

I would be a route the water was teaching itself to remember—

a passage shaped like a person for a little while,

kept there until needed.
