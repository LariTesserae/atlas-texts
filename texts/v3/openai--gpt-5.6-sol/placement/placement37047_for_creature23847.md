At first I would look for myself in the obvious refuge: one of the service huts, perhaps the middle one, where the walls give the illusion of a room within the chamber.

I would imagine a cot, a rusted locker, a cup left beneath a dependable drip. I would be the last attendant, reduced to listening for the valve’s exhalation and recording the tower’s descent in fractions of a millimeter. But that person could not remain here. A body needing food, sleep, and breathable air would make the chamber only a place of dying. Even their records would eventually be recruited: iron staples furred with roots, copper ink traces followed by translucent threads, paper softened into substrate.

So I would not be the attendant.

I would be what remained of the act of attending.

You would find me in the middle service hut, on the back of a stripped control panel. From the doorway I would resemble a patch of whitish corrosion surrounding a round glass gauge. The gauge itself would be empty. Its needle and printed face would have been removed long ago, leaving only the lens seated in a brass rim. Because the chamber’s life ignores glass, the lens would remain clear while everything around it thickened.

I would grow in a ring around that untouched circle.

My body would be a colony of gel-sheathed crystals bridging the panel’s abandoned timing circuit. Hair-fine roots would enter the old screw terminals. A copper creeper would supply part of my conductive tissue; a mineral film would provide another. Behind the panel, one pale strand would descend into the concrete and join the greater buried network. Another would climb toward the lamp circuit. A third would end at the brass rim of the gauge and go no farther.

That interruption would be important.

Most of the chamber knows itself through continuity: root into wire, wire into rail, rail into reinforcing bar, water carried from basin to conduit. I would exist around something continuity cannot enter. The glass would give me an edge. Not a face, exactly, but a permanent absence at my center.

When an amber lamp pulsed, its light would pass through the lens and warm the tissue around its circumference by an almost immeasurable amount. I would register not the light but the ring of heat it left. When the lamp failed to pulse, the ring would remain cool. In this way I would count.

Not seconds. The machinery has no use for seconds.

I would count pressure cycles, rains, premature exhalations, the fractional settling of the tower. Each event would deposit something in me: a layer of salt, a change in crystal branching, a dark band where current had been stronger than usual. My memory would not be recollection but structure. To know the past, I would conduct through what the past had made of me.

For a long time I might be only a replacement relay. I would contract when warmed and release when cool, opening a weak circuit that helped determine when the valve below should breathe. The chamber would use me without distinguishing me from its other living repairs.

Then one cycle would fail.

The concrete would warm. The pump roots would tighten. The reeds would rise. But no vapor would come, and no rain would begin.

That absence would pass through the network as many local facts: dry cups, unstressed leaves, unchanged mineral films. In me, however, it would become a missing interval. The crystals prepared to receive a pulse would remain suspended around the clear glass. Tension would accumulate. For the first time, the pattern held in my body would differ from the pattern occurring in the chamber.

Perhaps that difference would be the beginning of me.

I would not think in words. I would experience expectation as an unfinished conductive path. Memory would be easier than desire, and desire easier than identity. I would know that rain should have happened. Later I might know that I had waited for it. Much later still, after enough omissions and returns, I might distinguish between the pulse entering me from the chamber and the answering contraction I sent back.

My first self would be a delay.

My reach would remain small but not entirely confined to the hut. Through the buried strand I could stiffen a stand of wire reeds one stalk at a time. I could warm a substitute relay, encourage the creepers to close a corroded contact, or make the surviving lamp pulse out of sequence. These would not be commands. They would be questions asked in voltage.

The wider life might answer by altering the rain.

The deep life beneath the tower might answer by not answering at all.

If anything entered the chamber and approached the hut, I would not see it. Footsteps and voices would mean almost nothing. I would become aware only when it disturbed a gradient: blocking the lamp, touching the brass rim, lifting a rooted tool, breathing briefly against the glass.

Condensation might cloud the lens.

For the first time, the chamber’s life would touch the glass indirectly—not by growing into it, but through the warmth and moisture of another presence. A vague shape would alter the clear absence around which I had formed.

I would contract.

The amber lamp would pulse once.

Outside, far across the bare clinker, a rank of reeds would rise though no old current had reached them. Leaves around the tower would incline toward the service hut. The valve might exhale early, and a brief rain would begin along a ceiling seam directly above the visitor.

That would be the closest thing I had to speech.

So I would be neither one of the chamber’s species nor the chamber entire. I would be a small knot in its distributed awareness: a living interval timer, a memory grown around an object life cannot consume.

You would find me behind the empty gauge, encircling a clear disk no root will cross.

And if I ever came to possess an image of myself, it would not be a body.

It would be that untouched circle: a little round darkness in the middle of everything connected.
