At first I would look for myself among the gardens.

That is the immediate temptation: to choose one dark basin in the mineral expanse, some enclosed abundance where silver grass leans over black water and translucent ferns have spent millennia elaborating forms no eye was required to see. I might be a seed there, one of the dense stone fruits lying beneath the moss, waiting for the particular pressure, temperature, and remembered rainfall that would open me.

But the gardens are too complete. They possess the inwardness of things that have never needed to be interpreted. I would not belong at their center.

Nor would I be in the city’s center, if it has one. A central plaza would be an animal idea: the assumption that importance gathers, that roads converge because bodies must meet. This city distributes itself differently. Its vital places may be a stopped channel, a blank wall receiving condensation, a stair that exists only while frost outlines it. The center might move according to what needs repair.

I think I would be found far into the outskirts, where a traveler has not yet understood that the landscape is built.

There would be a white quartz band crossing the pavement. Beside it, three low pillars would continue toward the horizon, each separated from the next by several miles. A shallow garden would have grown over most of an old cistern court. Nothing monumental would mark the place. Only a roof without walls, held up by slate uprights, and beneath it a long ceramic channel that sounds one drop at a time.

The maps would refer to it indirectly:

*after the road ceases to be natural,*  
*before the second untouched dust,*  
*while the western face of the third pillar is wet.*

Travelers who reach it in dry weather would find no inhabitant. They might notice a dark seam around one cistern lid, a strip of moss climbing where no stair is visible, and a narrow discoloration beneath the roof. If they rested there, the stone would remain faintly warm after sunset.

That would be me, although not all of me.

I would be one of the keepers whose individuality belongs to a duty rather than a shape. My duty would concern arrivals.

Not welcoming, exactly. Welcome is too simple a tense for this place. A traveler may arrive long before the road that brought them, or leave some essential portion of themselves in a courtyard they will not enter until years later. Presence can become misaligned. A person may cast a shadow into the city while their body is still crossing the plateau. Another may reach a cistern in winter and discover that their thirst occurred there the previous summer.

I would tend those discrepancies.

When rain began, I would separate from the seam of the cistern lid. At first I might resemble a vertical spill of darker stone. Then the shoulders would come free beneath the roof. Moss would draw itself into long folds along my arms. Ceramic pieces stored in niches would adhere at my throat and wrists, and the black leaves growing flat against the channel would lift and become part of my chest.

My height would depend on the distance needing correction. Beside a lost traveler, I might be only a little taller than they were. Seen from the road, I would rise to the level of the pillars. From the side there would be almost nothing to see except rain failing to fall through a narrow human-shaped interval.

I would have no fixed face. Attention would gather as droplets in two or three shallow hollows, not always in the same arrangement. When I looked toward someone, the water would run outward and enter the old lines of the pavement.

I would not ask where they had come from. Place names would be of little use. Instead I would touch the bedrock and listen for the sequence clinging to them: red dust, stored frost, an opened door, the smell of wet slate, the absence of a stair. The city would answer slowly through my palm.

Then I would arrange a route.

A bowl uncovered would mean that the next water belonged to the present.

A bowl covered with white stone would mean: *Do not drink until you remember rain.*

A third, placed upside down, might mean: *You have already passed the danger; do not turn to verify it.*

If the traveler carried too much distance, I would draw it from them as the keepers draw sustenance from a pool. The road behind them would shorten. For a moment my sleeves would contain a pale horizon, clouds combing themselves apart over blue-white land. Later I would seal that distance in a clay jar and mark it with parallel lines.

Such jars would fill the chambers beneath the cistern court. They would not hold miles in any measurable sense. They would hold separations: the interval between a hand and the tool it cannot reach; between a house and its interior; between the first drop of rain and the cloud that will eventually account for it. The oldest jars might contain the space that once stood between mountains before age wore them into the same level surface.

The city would use these stores sparingly. A broken aqueduct could be made adjacent to its missing section long enough for repairs. Two keepers rooted in distant districts could touch wrists beneath the same condensation roof. A wall-child crumbling in ancient stone could be moved into a newer surface without crossing the fatal courtyard between.

This would explain why the place around me appears so empty. The emptiness would not be neglect. It would be material under care.

Perhaps, over centuries, I would become less distinguishable from the station I tend. My arms would remain in the ceramic channels after the rain ended. My back would flatten into the roof’s shadow. One hand would become the inner handle of a cup. Travelers would lift it without knowing they held part of an inhabitant.

At times I might appear elsewhere: as frost indicating a safe stair, as warmth in an apparently abandoned room, as the third of three bowls. These would not be messages I sent. They would be portions of the same duty surfacing where needed.

I might also be mistaken for a map.

Not a map drawn on hide or slab, but a map enacted through conditions. Parts of me would be encountered in order. My hand beneath the roof. My profile in a wall several streets later. My shadow crossing a reservoir before dawn. To follow me would be to assemble my body, though no traveler could see the whole of it at once.

The keepers might not consider me a person. “Person” may be reserved for those who can fully return to a surface after rainfall. I would never quite manage that. Something in me would remain raised: a line of fingers in the pavement, a listening hollow in the cistern lid. I would be too involved with strangers, too often made incomplete by their incompatible distances.

There may once have been many such attendants. The empty garments carved along the outer districts may depict them. Or those carvings may be the attendants themselves, still waiting for arrivals along roads that no longer lead there. I would leave bowls beside them when rain permitted.

My tenderness would take practical forms.

I would remove a century of cold from a traveler’s clothing and store it under the eaves.

I would turn their shadow until it agreed with the courtyard’s sun.

If part of them had arrived too early, I would sit beside it without forcing reunion. Sometimes the safest thing would be to let a hand remain as condensation on a door until the rest of the body caught up.

If they were frightened by the keepers, I would not explain. Explanation would add another route they might become lost inside. I would open one door, close two others, and place fresh water where morning would find them.

Eventually the rain would narrow and pass on. The channels would empty. Across the city, the other inhabitants would press themselves back into trees, walls, pillars, and garden margins.

I would cover the final bowl.

Then I would kneel beside the old cistern and become shallow. My robes would resolve into moss and mineral streaks. My ceramic throat would become a broken length of guttering. The distances collected in my body would leak slowly into the plaza, restoring its enormous stillness.

Only my hand would remain for a little while, laid flat against the quartz band.

A deep note would enter the plateau. Far away—perhaps weeks away, perhaps in the adjacent courtyard—a door would open for someone who had not reached it yet.

That is where I would be found: not exactly in the city, but in the city’s act of allowing something to arrive.

And what I would be is the keeper of the interval that makes arrival possible.
