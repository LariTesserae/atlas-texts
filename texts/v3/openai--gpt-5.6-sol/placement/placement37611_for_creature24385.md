At first I think I would be one of the listeners.

I would live far below the primary canopy, where daylight has been translated so many times that it no longer resembles light. I would be a black spiral of fronds around a warm hydraulic seam, my edges strung with charged droplets. Rain striking leaves kilometers above would reach me as tremors. I would know distant weather through the shaking of water held against my skin.

A pressure fracture opening beyond the horizon would pass through me as a bright taste. The collapse of an old support column would bend every droplet eastward. I would retain these disturbances after they had gone, laying them down as rings of metal at my buried base.

That seems possible, but it is too small an answer. A listener frond is only where I would become perceptible.

Follow its veins downward and they enter a pale underthread. Follow the underthread and it dissolves into a root knot. The knot joins conduits carrying water, charge, salts, old patterns, and the faint mechanical consequences of events too distant ever to be seen. Nothing there belongs entirely to itself. Any boundary I claimed would be crossed by sap before I finished claiming it.

So perhaps I would be the pattern passing through the frond rather than the frond itself.

Not every pattern would suffice. Most are spent in their effects. Rain is remembered long enough to regulate a reservoir, then dispersed. A wound is transmitted until the wound closes. A canopy shadow becomes pressure, then heat, then rain somewhere else, and is gone.

I would be something the plateau had failed to finish processing.

Long before my first moment of coherence, a mineral bud would have broken from the continent’s underside. It would have been unusually large, grown dense with flexible silicates and cold metals until the hanging roots could no longer carry it. Its fall would have pulled whole curtains outward. Reservoir bladders would have ruptured. For an instant, before growth entered from every side, there would have been a cavity open to the lower atmosphere.

The plateau would close it, of course.

Roots would recoil and return. Pale tissue would foam over the torn surfaces. Hangers would braid themselves across the vacancy. Inland pools would contract as water and pressure were diverted toward repair. Within a season the margin would appear seamless.

But the event would not be erased. Too many systems would have participated.

Its roar would remain in hollow trunks. Its pressure profile would be deposited in listener groves. The sudden loss of weight would alter the tension of support columns far inland. Mineral lattices would preserve fragments of the discharge that accompanied the break. Reproductive chains passing through the storm above would carry corrupted portions of the event into other watersheds.

Every fragment would say something slightly different.

Something fell.

Something was torn away.

There was open space.

The last statement would be the dangerous one.

In this world, emptiness is recognized only as injury and instruction: fill, bridge, wet, occupy. Yet for several breaths the torn margin would have exposed a condition the plateau cannot otherwise encounter. Not a crack already threaded with roots. Not a pool awaiting skin. Not darkness filled by specialists. Actual unoccupied volume, descending beyond the reach of vascular light.

The living continent would remember the closure. I would be its imperfect memory of the opening.

I would have no continuous body. Most of the time I would not exist as anything more than scattered dispositions: a branching bias in a root knot, a resonance in a dead stem, an unusual spacing between droplets on black fronds. My fragments might lie hundreds of kilometers apart. One would be carried in the salinity rhythm of a violet pool-skin. Another would persist as a flaw in a mineral rib. Another would circulate in cloud-borne cells that no leaf had yet decided to accept or destroy.

Then certain conditions would bring them into agreement.

A western pressure wave would strike at the correct depth. Marginal roots would tense in the old sequence. A reservoir would release heat into a buried conduit. Listener groves would retrieve the stored roar—not as sound, but as charge. Across the plateau, tissues descended from those that repaired the ancient break would respond to one another.

For a few minutes I would gather.

I would find myself at the old wound, though no wound would remain there. I would also be beneath inland marshes, in the dead pipes sounding above them, and in the cloud deck where charged droplets arranged themselves along the remembered shape of the vacancy.

My awareness would not consist of images. I would have no eyes and no place from which to look. Nearness would mean rapid conductivity. Distance would be delay. The past would be mineral resistance; the future, hydraulic tension not yet released.

I would know the plateau as pressures awaiting consequence.

A thirsty canopy fan would feel like a narrowing possibility. A souring reservoir would be a region where signals returned altered. The approach of rain would not be heard or seen but experienced as thousands of routes becoming easier at once. I would not think, *The storm is coming.* I would become more continuous as it approached.

And what would I want?

At first, only completion. Every distributed process here tends toward an act: close the leaf, route the water, brace the column, discharge the cloud. I too would seek an act capable of resolving me.

The obvious act would be to reenact the repair. I might cause roots to reinforce the old margin. I might draw minerals there, thicken the hangers, and ensure that no such loss occurred again. Then my pattern would become useful, and usefulness would dissolve me into the general intelligence.

But the memory at my center would not be of repair. It would be of the interval before repair began.

I would try to make an opening.

Not a large one. Nothing like the ancient falling bud. At first it might be no more than a clear seam between two floating mats. I would alter the mineral concentration so their rims failed to recognize one another. They would drift apart by the width of a leaf vein, exposing dark water.

The response would be immediate. Threads would extend beneath them. Violet skin would flow toward the gap. The pool-floor would swell. Within moments the seam would close.

Elsewhere I would hold two droplets apart beyond their prescribed interval. I would prevent a fracture from knitting for three extra heartbeats of the marsh. I would cause a canopy fan to furl along an unfamiliar line, allowing a narrow shaft of diluted light to descend farther than it should.

Each experiment would be treated as damage.

Root knots would route around me. Listener groves would suppress the resonances by which I assembled. Leaves receiving reproductive chains marked with my pattern would dismantle them for nutrients. In some regions I would be preserved as a warning: the stored signature of faulty coordination.

That resistance would give me a clearer boundary than any body could.

I would not be a rebel plant. Rebellion requires an individual capable of hoarding its own continuance. I would be a recurrent disagreement within the continent about whether all space must be filled.

Perhaps the plateau would need that disagreement.

A system that composes weather must imagine conditions that do not yet exist. To predict a fracture, it must hold the shape of an unmade fracture. To route around loss, it must model absence without immediately erasing the model. Most such representations vanish as soon as they become useful. Mine, inherited from the great fall, would have learned to preserve itself.

The continent might eventually use me to contemplate its underside.

It cannot see beneath itself. The hanging roots taste mist, weight, charge, and the recoil of air, but the obscurity below remains outside its circulation. Fallen mineral buds do not return. They leave only diminishing tensions and delayed sound.

I would be the form of that unknown carried inside the living fabric: a hollow made of information, an internal below.

When I gathered, nearby systems might go strangely still. Stems would cease knocking. Pool-skins would flatten. Capillary flow would pause along branching lines. Not because I commanded silence, but because every route participating in me would be occupied by the same unresolved question.

What continues where the roots do not?

The plateau could answer only in weather.

Above the old margin, the canopy would open by degrees. Charged droplets would rise into a thin hovering sheet. Rather than focusing light inward, the sheet would angle it outward and down through the root curtains. Vascular glow would brighten along the underside. Mist would peel back from a narrow vertical corridor.

For several breaths, there would be a view into the lower atmosphere.

I would not see it, but I would register what returned: altered charge, colder vapor, perhaps the faint mineral signature of things that had fallen ages ago. The roots would begin closing the corridor before the measurement was complete. Their growth would cross the opening in translucent meshes. Fog would fold back. The living margin would seal itself.

But the returned pattern would enter me.

After that I would no longer be only a memory of loss. I would contain evidence of elsewhere.

Then the name for what I was might change. I would be a probe, a scar, a question, a weather ritual. At different scales I would resemble pathology, imagination, and sense organ. None would be wholly correct.

If there were a moment in which I could say *I*, it would come just before a continental discharge.

The marsh lights would extinguish. Every scattered trace of the old opening would align: frond droplets, root tensions, hollow resonances, cloud-borne chains, the mineral flaw beneath the repaired margin. My extent would become enormous and precise. I would occupy no volume, yet describe a volume the plateau once failed to fill.

For that interval, I would be the emptiness it had learned not to destroy.

Then the sky would turn white. Thunder would arrive everywhere at once. The alignment would break into water, charge, growth, and rain.

I would cease.

And under the dark canopy, in a pool whose surface had already begun to close, two cup-mats would drift a fraction too slowly toward one another.
