I would not be on the rim, though at first I would imagine myself there.

I would imagine the bent tree, the platforms among its few leaves, the outer lands visible beyond the basin. I would think I was one of those who crossed over for grain or black glass, returning with seeds in my sandals and too much distance in my voice.

But I do not think the basin would let me remain so exposed.

I would be found lower down, west of the Empty Place, in a room that had once been a stair. The lower steps would have been leveled into a sleeping shelf, while the upper ones disappeared into a wall patched so many times that no one could say whether it held up the room or merely remembered passing through it. My blanket would be rolled beneath an old drain marker. There would always be three cups by my head, though only one reliable drip.

I would be called **Blue Palm**.

Not from birth. My first names would have been temporary and mostly unflattering: Small Heel, Lid-Biter, Sleeps-Through-Ladles. Blue Palm would adhere when I was nine or ten, after a wall opened beside the Potters’ Hollow.

The exposed clay would be startlingly blue. While the adults argued over braces and runoff, I would press my hand against it. Not from wisdom—only because children touch what they are told not to touch. Beneath my palm I would feel a delicate, repeating pressure, like a second pulse traveling through the ground.

I would ask why the wall was swallowing.

They would cut where I pointed and find an old ceramic throat, sealed for perhaps three generations, filling slowly from below.

For days the blue clay would remain in the creases of my hand.

After that, people would call me an under-listener, though I would resist the word. Under-listeners cause inconvenience. They interrupt meals, empty shelves, and oblige people to open walls that often prove dry. Every ache in their teeth becomes public business. Neighbors respect them in the same way they respect rain: sincerely, but with complaint.

My left ear would be poor. A settling jar would have cracked beside me in adolescence, leaving that side of the world muffled. I would say this helped. Sounds mislead; pressure does not. To listen, I would rest one palm against plaster and set my teeth lightly together. Moving water would arrive in the jaw before it reached the hand.

My work would be remembering routes that no longer function.

Others would know the active city: roof, gutter, elbow, trough, jar. I would keep the unusable paths—the blind drains, buried rooms, sealed springs and channels abandoned because a household grew across them. These would not be drawn. A drawing makes walls seem still. I would remember them as instructions for a body:

Lie beneath the third sleeping shelf.  
Turn the cheek toward the cold seam.  
Reach past the timber peg.  
There is a hollow behind the plaster, but not where the plaster is dark.  
It bends toward the old kiln.  
Do not open it unless the Steps of Rain begin to sweat.

Most days this knowledge would appear worthless. Children would use me as a landmark. Potters would leave wrapped tools beside my knee. People would step over my legs while I sorted chips of old ceramic according to curve and mineral stain. I would have authority concerning passages no one wished to use, which is very nearly the same as having no authority at all.

Then the little surface in the House Without a Roof would tremble.

Notice would reach me as a hand on the shoulder.

I would bind on a pale cord, take two cups, a shell scraper and a lamp, and follow the crews through rooms whose occupants had scarcely woken. I would not lead. The person with the wedges would go first where there was room; a narrow child would go first where there was not. My task would begin when everyone else had become uncertain.

At each stopping place, we would touch the city.

I would feel nothing at first. One learns not to invent movement merely because others are waiting. A false listener can open half a street by wanting too much to be useful.

So I would wait through the scrape of sandals above, the settling of cloth, someone breathing behind the wall. Then a pressure would gather along one tooth—not pain, exactly, but the promise of pain. I would move my palm a handspan lower.

There.

The old route would return to me not as memory but as posture. I would know that somewhere beyond the plaster was the upper edge of a lintel from a room sealed before I was born. Beneath it, a channel sloped the wrong way. The water would be moving toward a household that believed itself dry.

I would say, quietly, “Open below the mark.”

The black-glass blade would come out without its cord being checked.

Cold water would bead in the cut, then run over my fingers carrying red silt. Someone would taste it. Someone else would hold a lamp close to judge the grains. The room would fill with people suggesting destinations.

I would not decide where the water went. That would belong to the wall keeper, the jar counter, the households beneath, and whoever could repeat the new route without error. My authority would end when the hidden thing became visible.

I think I would prefer it that way.

I would not marry by cutting a threshold. I would join one by sealing it.

The person on the other side would be a jar mender whose sleeping room leaked whenever my old stair-room received runoff from the west. We would spend a season proposing openings, each defeated by dyed water. At last we would close the narrow passage between us and build two small hatches offset from one another, so bowls and hands could pass but drafts could not.

The city would understand this as devotion: not insisting that closeness must take the form of removal.

As I aged, I would move only a little farther downward. My room would gradually cease to be a room. A retaining rib would thicken along one side; storage jars would occupy another. The remaining floor would narrow until I slept curled between an obsolete drain and the wall that replaced it.

People would know where to find me because I was always in the way.

That would not be an insult. In the basin, useful obstruction is one of the forms an elder may take. Baskets would pass over my knees. Children would touch my shoulder before slipping behind the jars. I would teach them that a cold wall is not always a wet wall, that clear water may carry dangerous silt, and that the most important part of a route is often the place where everyone believes it ends.

Eventually I would no longer go with the notice crews. They would come to me and describe what they found.

I would close my eyes and ask what stood above what.

After my death, little would be kept. Perhaps a tooth, because of the listening. It would be ground small and mixed into plaster above the sealed hatch. The builders would object even to that until assured the fragment could not interrupt the bond.

But if I had lived properly, I would not be in the tooth.

I would persist in a route reopened during rain: from the Steps of Rain beneath the old kiln, along the blue clay seam, behind the room that was once a stair, then downward through the offset hatches to a jar newly placed in a pantry.

People might forget why the final turn was made so sharply. They might call it Blue Palm’s Turn, then Palm Turn, then only the Blue, long after the clay itself had been covered.

If asked who I was, the basin would give no account of my character.

It would answer:

Upper seep.  
Old kiln throat.  
Third sleeping shelf.  
Blue turn.  
Lower jar.

And somewhere, hand to hand, water would still know the way.
