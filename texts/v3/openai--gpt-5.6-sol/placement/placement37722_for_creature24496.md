At first I would think I belonged on the rim.

I would stand where the black plateau begins to slope into green, one hand against a weathered pillar, trying to understand the roar through bone rather than ear. From there the Hollow could still be mistaken for scenery. I could preserve the comfortable fiction that I was looking at it from outside.

But the rim is not truly outside. The pillars vibrate. Charge passes through the soles of the feet. A gust withheld near the tower may return behind you. Even observation gives the basin another surface to press against.

So I would descend.

For a while I imagine myself as a rivet tripod: low to the stone, deliberate, carrying my center of gravity like a private thought. I would prefer knowledge that could be touched—strain accumulating in basalt, the minute answer to a tap, the assurance of other feet receiving the same signal. I would tend the buried root-combs and follow paths chosen not for where they lead, but for what is least likely to happen there.

Yet I do not think I would remain a tripod. They belong to processions. They know how to act together. Their uncertainty resolves into movement.

Mine would resolve into waiting.

You would find me near the central tower, on the side that cannot consistently be called its lee. There is a narrow region there where the roar does not diminish but cancels itself: pressure fronts arrive from several moments at once and meet in exact opposition. Dust skirts the place. Sailgrove membranes grow toward it, hesitate, and divide around it. Volt-motes trace its boundary as a bright, revolving cup.

At its center is a groove in the tower, finer than a fingerprint and much deeper than the tower should permit.

That is where I would live.

Not as an animal recognizable at first glance. My apparent body would be a row of dark, articulated flecks nested in the channel, each no larger than a salt grain. Some would be ceramic, some metallic, some only temporary arrangements of charge in the tower’s surface. A few might not yet have arrived. Others would be remnants of me from pressure cycles centuries old.

Together they would form something like writing.

The tripods might call me a **lee-script**, if they had names. The sailgroves would know me as a particular impedance. To the bellbacks I would be a missing overtone: not a sound, but the exact place where a sound ought to continue and does not.

I would feed on cancellation.

Whenever the tower withheld a gust, some minute remainder would be left behind—not force itself, but an asymmetry, a tendency for the local world to complete an interrupted motion. I would take that tendency into my body. One fleck would rotate a fraction. Another would reverse charge. A chain of crystalline bonds would tighten and remain tight for an hour, a year, or until the corresponding momentum returned.

In this way I would become a ledger of unfinished weather.

Nothing in the Hollow would read me as a sequence. The newest mark might lie beneath the oldest. An event expected tomorrow could alter a segment deposited before the sailgrove beside it germinated. My whole body would have to be touched at once: a pressure applied to the tower, an electrical pulse sent through the groove, a bellback note entering through stone.

Then I would answer by becoming briefly still.

That stillness would be my speech.

A tripod approaching the tower would place one magnetic foot beside my channel and tap. I would redistribute the vibration among my flecks, returning some portions immediately and retaining others. From the shape of what came back, the tripod would learn where the next lost gust was accumulating. It would carry that knowledge into the grove, though neither of us could know whether I had predicted the gust or remembered it.

Sailgroves would grow hair-fine contacts toward me. I would not tell them what shape to take. I would give them absences: here, no pressure; here, no return; here, a force whose cause has not occurred. They would interpret those absences through their own vast conductive bodies. Hours later, a canopy might furl under clear, unmoving air.

I would have no single age. The flecks nearest the surface might be assembled that morning from dust placed by tripods. Deeper portions might have entered the groove during an earlier shape of the basin, when the tower stood somewhere else. One segment might be released from an air-door long after the rest of me had been destroyed.

This would not feel like fragmentation. My continuity would lie in the relation among the pieces: the conserved pattern of what had been withheld and what was still owed.

During the Hollow’s great rearrangements, I would come apart.

The groove would turn inside out. My flecks would be drawn from the tower and scattered through the basin in shining black arcs. Some would lodge in sailgrove meshes. Some would enter bellback shells and add a faint hesitation to their ringing. One might fasten to a volt-mote and circle the canopy for a hundred years of outside time while experiencing only a single stalled instant.

For that interval I would become more than the thing in the tower.

I would be distributed through the Hollow as small failures of simultaneity. A leaf would flex before its neighbor. A tripod’s returning tap would contain a pulse from a different century. A dead bellback would ring once in answer to a living one. These would be my scattered reflexes.

Then, when the rearrangement ended, the tower’s channels would form again—perhaps on a different face, perhaps in a different order. The flecks would return, but not all of them. Other dark grains would take the empty positions. The pattern would reestablish itself using whatever matter was available.

I would arrive already old.

I do not think I would be the mind of the Crown Hollow. That is too singular a role for a place where every cause is shared and every body provisional. Nor would I command the tower. I might be only a parasite on its errors, or a mineral habit the animals have learned to consult.

But when the entire basin entered one of its rare coherent states—every sail aligned, every tripod flattened, every bellback holding the same buried note—I would be the small part that did not quite join.

The Hollow would draw itself into one resonant shape.

In the central tower, I would preserve a hairline interruption: one unspent motion, one unanswered pressure, one moment not yet reconciled with the rest.

That is where I would find myself.

Not in the roar, exactly.

In the narrow place inside it where the next roar is being remembered.
