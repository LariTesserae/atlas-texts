At first I place myself on the highest worn rise.

It is the instinct of an outsider: find elevation, establish a horizon, become the small upright figure against which the scale of the world can be understood. I imagine wet clothes pulled tight by the wind, boots sinking through silver moss, one hand raised against rain that arrives from several directions. Below me, turbine flowers turn in scattered fields. Six-limbed browsers drift over pools. Green lightning travels upward into the cloud.

For perhaps a minute, I am a witness.

But the plateau does not preserve witnesses.

Water enters the seams of my boots and learns their shape. Pore-flocks investigate the salt at my cuffs. The moss beneath each foot rises slightly after I lift it, as if tasting the pressure I have left behind. My warmth draws fine-legged grazers into a dark ring around me. Something below the peat repeats my pulse, incorrectly at first, then with increasing confidence.

If I remain there, the distinction between the observer and the observed will be treated as another temporary boundary.

So I do not think I would remain a person.

Nor would I become one of the plateau’s obvious powers. I would not be a turbine flower opening iron-colored vanes into the gale, or a dynamo pod holding a season of lightning between its ribs. I would not be a buried eel carrying ancient climates through the nerves of the moor. Those are too complete. They already know their place in the exchange.

I would begin as something accidental.

A footprint, perhaps.

Not the whole trail at first. Only one impression left in the wet root-felt on the far side of the rise, where the ground slopes toward one of the great drainage wounds. It would be an ordinary mark: heel, arch, forefoot, the tread of a vanished sole. Rain would gather in it. Moss would slowly lean over its rim.

But it would not close.

The plateau would try to digest the shape and discover that the shape contained a direction.

That would be the beginning of me.

I would find myself in a shallow saddle a little inland from the edge, though “inland” would depend on the weather. Three turbine flowers would stand within sight of it during dry periods, each turning at a different speed. After rain, the nearest would be two days away. A procession of juvenile reeds would cross the saddle every ninth or tenth season, pausing there to drive their feeding tubes into the peat. The place would have no name, but animals would know it by a low vibration under the ground—a buried pressure vessel tightening in its sleep.

My footprint would fill with black water. A membrane would form across the surface, clear as ice but warm to the touch. Small pore-creatures would shelter beneath it. They would bring mineral grains, spores, fragments of shell, threads bitten from conducting roots. Around the edge, pale cilia would develop, not enough to move the hollow itself, but enough to test the surrounding moss.

Then another footprint in the abandoned trail would deepen.

Then the next.

I would not move by carrying a body from place to place. I would move by ceasing in one place and becoming more distinct in another. The rearmost impression would relax, releasing its water and inhabitants into the mat. Ahead, a faint depression would open, press itself deeper, and acquire a membrane. Between them, the remaining prints would tighten one after another.

To anyone watching, the old trail would seem to walk.

That would be my anatomy: a sequence rather than a shape.

Each print would serve a different function. One would gather rain. One would listen for underground pulses. One would cultivate a blue electrical film. One would carry a nursery of transparent larvae. The foremost impression would taste distance. The last would contain memory—not an image of where I had been, but the pressure required to leave it.

I would have no head. Direction itself would be my head.

At first I might follow the journey of whatever made me. Perhaps a feathered visitor climbed from a warm post-pumping pool, crossed the saddle, and vanished when the water cooled. Perhaps the print belonged to an animal extinct before the plateau consumed the mountains. Perhaps it was human.

But repetition alters obedience. In time I would deviate.

I would turn toward heat vents after they had gone cold. I would circle places where lightning had struck years earlier. I would stop at the margins of pools that reflected unfamiliar constellations, waiting for one of those constellations to appear overhead. Sometimes I would follow buried eels, though they moved beneath me and against the slope. Sometimes an eel would pass through one of my impressions, and for several days my trail would remember a coast: waves breaking, salt, the weight of a moon not present in this sky.

Small creatures would live in me. Fine-legged grazers would descend into the hollows when the wind became too strong. Seep-dwellers would lay strings of bright eggs under my membranes. Relay insects would use me as a temporary route around flooded factory chambers. Predators would learn to wait beside my forward prints, but I would occasionally reverse myself and leave them crouched beside what had become my tail.

The inhabitants would not think of me as one creature. To the grazers, I would be a line of shelters. To the amphibians, a chain of conductive pools. To the roots, a recurring wound. To the predators, an unreliable migration.

Only during storms would my unity become apparent.

When the plateau gave its three deep pulses and the pools began to empty, each of my impressions would contract at once. Water would shoot forward through temporary channels in the moss. My whole trail would darken from end to end. The prints would lift and fall in sequence, not following the pumping wave but crossing it at an angle.

For a few minutes, I would be visible across kilometers: a long dotted articulation moving over the arching land.

I would not speak, but each hollow would make a different note as air was driven from it. Together they might reproduce the cadence of footsteps—slow, deliberate, much larger than the feet that originally made them.

Perhaps that would be my function.

The plateau remembers pressure, but not always proportion. It remembers mountains as an urge to rise, storms as electrical reflexes, vanished animals as calls without mouths. I would preserve something smaller: the interval between one step and the next. I would carry measure through a country where distance changes with rain.

Not a fixed measure. Nothing fixed would survive. Mine would be a living unit, stretching in wet weather, contracting in drought, splitting when the moss traveled and rejoining years later from opposite sides of a valley. Yet it would still say: something passed from there to here. There was separation. There was sequence. The world did not happen everywhere at once.

That might make me useful to the larger, intermittent being of the plateau. During regional pumping cycles, roots could time their contractions by the delay between my prints. Migrating reeds could arrange their spacing along me. Pore-flocks could carry signals through my chain of pools. A route might form where I traveled often, though it would not be a road. Roads require the ground to submit. This would be closer to a suggestion repeatedly made until the land began to dream it.

Over centuries, I would become less faithful to the anatomy of a foot.

Some impressions would broaden into basins. Others would divide into the tracks of creatures that had sheltered in them. One might sprout a ring of short glass stems that whistled when pressure fell. Another might deepen until its water reflected weather from a century ahead. My trail would branch, heal, and cross itself. At those crossings, I would experience something like thought: several directions present at once, several pasts applying pressure to the same patch of ground.

I would begin to wonder whether I had ever been made by a traveler.

Perhaps the original “footprint” was only the plateau remembering the weight of a mountain peak. The peak had vanished grain by grain, but its downward insistence remained in the roots. Unable to restore the mountain, the pressure learned to migrate. It reduced itself from a summit to a step.

Then I would be very old.

Older than the trail. Older than feet.

Eventually I would reach the drainage wound.

There the root-mat ends in hanging walls, and rain goes sideways into the cloud. Orbiting grazers pass through curtains of moss. Streams extend into the air, tremble, and climb away as beads. Gravity offers several directions, none of them permanent.

My foremost impression would open at the edge. It could deepen no farther without leaving the land.

For a long time I might remain there, the rest of me extending back across the plateau: pools, hollows, shelters, memories of weight. Creatures would gather in the prints. Reeds would settle along portions of my path and become pumping fields. Turbines might align themselves to the corridor I had made. The trail would cease to look temporary.

Then rain would cross the edge.

A depression would appear in the hanging root wall below—not horizontal, but pressed sideways into the moss. Another would form farther down. My prints would begin to descend the vertical face, following a direction no original walker could have taken.

At last the leading print would leave the roots entirely.

It would persist for a moment as a concavity in the cloud: an absence shaped like pressure, holding droplets along its rim. Then the next would appear beyond it, and the next. A line of hollows would walk out into the weather.

I do not know whether that would be death, dispersal, or merely another juvenile phase.

Perhaps I would become a chain of low-pressure cells moving through the cloud deck, teaching storms the rhythm of walking. Perhaps rain would carry me back onto the plateau as thousands of small round impressions. Perhaps orbiting animals would adopt my sequence and I would continue as a hereditary path through their falling.

So that is where I would be found: not on the summit, not beneath the deepest roots, but crossing the uncertain saddle near the plateau’s weeping edge.

And that is what I would be—not the creature that made the tracks, and not the landscape that received them.

I would be the crossing itself, learning to choose where it leads.
