At first I would mistake myself for many things.

I would be the dark shape fixed above the escarpment, wings locked, held motionless while the whole country passed beneath me. I would feel the wind entering the separated tips of my feathers and imagine that stillness was flight perfected. I would read the cliffs through my breastbone: this chamber trustworthy, this one altered by rain, this thin scream concealing a fault that will open after frost.

Then the light would change, and I would be below the stone instead.

I would lie among the blunt-snouted sleepers in a chamber no creature had dug. Warm bodies would be fitted around me, flank to flank, until there was no distinction between shelter and colony. I would know my companions by oil, dust, and pressure. I would carry a tiny white seed in my fur without knowing it, delivering it to the damp seam where it might wait years for rain.

At another hour I would be almost nothing: a clear case in the dust at the bottom of an empty pool, containing a life folded too tightly to resemble life. The rain would strike. Stone would become sky. I would hatch, feed, mate, and leave my descendants sleeping in the basin before noon drank the world away.

All of these seem possible there. The plateau does not ask a creature to possess one grand form. It asks only that the form be exact.

But I think you would find me among the small soot-colored birds.

Not in the largest colony, where thousands turn at once and the cliff seems to flash one vast white wing. I would live farther along the western rim, in a cavity behind a low arch of limestone. Its entrance would be narrow enough to exclude the great hovering birds, and its note would be a low, steady pressure rather than a shriek. The chamber would darken immediately beyond the mouth. Frost would silver the lip at dawn, but the inner shelf would remain dry.

I would know the cavity not as a place enclosed by stone, but as a particular vibration in my feet.

Its note would accompany the shell-thin sounds of my own kind: the nest-click of a returning mate, the small pulse made by a hungry chick, claws shifting on limestone. These sounds would be precious because they could exist only at close range. Beyond the entrance, sound would cease to carry meaning. The wind would already be speaking everywhere.

So I would speak with what could be seen.

My throat patch would be an unremarkable color until I turned it directly into the light—then it would show blue-gray, like old snow compressed in a fissure. I would flatten it to yield a perch, brighten it to warn another bird away from my stored stone, cover it when approaching the nest. A raised left wing would mean that I was leaving. Both wings held low would mean that I had found food enough to share.

Perhaps my mate would recognize me less by my face than by a slight asymmetry in flight: one tail feather damaged early in life, causing me to brake with a small turn. I would always enter the cavity from below, rise through the arch’s lee, flare, turn, vanish.

My days would be arranged around stones.

There would be one anvil near the cliff edge, a pale slab crossed by a dark mineral seam. Windborne beetles would gather behind a volcanic block twenty wingbeats east of it. I would carry them one at a time to the slab, pin them over the seam, and strike with a hammer stone held in my beak.

I would not understand fracture as a principle. I would understand this place.

I would know that the broad end of the beetle must face the sun; that a blow delivered too near the mineral seam sends the shell skittering into the wind; that the best hammer is neither the heaviest nor the sharpest but the one with the shallow hollow that fits the upper curve of my beak.

My favored stone would be brown, fine-grained, and no larger than the last joint of a finger. I would have found it beside a transient pool after rain exposed its underside. It would possess no beauty that another species could see. To me, its weight would be a kind of certainty.

I would keep it in a crack near the anvil.

“Keep” would not mean own in any permanent sense. Another bird might take it. Frost might work it deeper into the fissure. A careless landing might cast it over the rim, and it would fall through cloud into the lower world from which the insects rise. I would search for it, fail, and eventually choose another.

I would never chip a replacement into the right shape.

The thought would not occur to me that shape could be asked of stone. Stone would precede intention. The country would contain suitable things and unsuitable things, and intelligence would consist of learning the difference.

As a young bird I would choose badly. I would carry rounded pebbles that slipped from my beak, porous fragments that broke before the beetle did, glittering crystals too light to strike with force. An older bird would tolerate me at the edge of her feeding place. She would not instruct me as a human instructor might. She would simply perform the act without concealment: place, angle, strike, turn the shell, strike again.

I would watch her feet.

Years later, a fledgling would watch mine.

Nothing would mark this inheritance. There would be no carved sign beside the slab, no pile of selected hammers, no image of a beetle incised into the rock. Knowledge would pass through attention and vanish from the world each time attention failed.

That would be my country within the country: the route among cavity, insect-shadow, anvil, seep, and nest. It might occupy less than a kilometer, though the distances would not feel small. Between each place would lie exposed crossings where the wind could roll a careless bird along the ground. Every safe arrival would require the correct height, the correct angle, the correct surrender to the air.

I would drink at a black pool cupped in stone. I would not land at its rim directly; hunters would expect that. I would settle first among the silver grass, remain still, then approach in short sideways movements. The water would taste of limestone, insect husks, and the faint bitterness of the plants growing beneath its edge.

Sometimes a grazer procession would pass.

From close by, the animals would be immense: black-blooded, deep-breathing, their coats streaming west. I would wait on a horn while one drank. The horn’s ridges would hold lichen dust and mites. When the animal lowered its head, I might pick insects from the tangled skirt of its lee flank. It would tolerate my weight without acknowledgment.

Yet I would feel its route through the ground.

Long before the herd appeared, the anvil beneath my feet would carry a repeated trembling. Not danger. Not rockfall. Hooves. I would learn to distinguish the approach of grazers from the quick irregular impacts of a hunter, just as the nesting birds distinguish a stable chamber from one beginning to fail.

This would be my deeper faculty—not tool use, not flight, but recognition.

I would know the plateau as a collection of differences too slight for an outsider to name:

the silence behind one stone that means shelter, and the silence behind another that means a waiting predator;

the darkening of the eastern wall before its regular rain;

the vibration of safe limestone;

the hour when pale moths begin to rise through the gap;

the posture of a mate who has returned alone;

the nearly invisible slackening of the wind around a grazer’s body;

the exact fracture that opens a beetle cleanly.

I would own none of these things. I would alter almost nothing. My life would leave perhaps a few shells at the foot of the anvil, droppings in a deep cavity, a feather caught in rust-colored lichen.

After death, even those signs would be redistributed. The feather would line a nest or feed a blind scavenger. The shells would bleach and enter the gravel. My bones would lodge behind a ledge, gather dust, and slowly lose their resemblance to a bird.

But for a while I would be there: pale-footed, soot-feathered, almost voiceless, carrying a small brown stone across the roaring light.

Not building.

Not naming.

Choosing correctly.
