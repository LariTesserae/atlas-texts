At first I think I would be one of the visible forms.

Perhaps a heliophore at the northern grove, standing between the glossy spires with a crown of translucent plates. I imagine the white sun entering me without warmth, separated across carbon lattices, becoming charge in the black interior of my trunk. I would know the world as differences of potential: brightness above, mineral contact below, the faint electrical weather of neighboring structures.

But such a life would contain no “I.” The heliophore is only where the grove happens to meet the light. Its stillness is complete, without patience. Nothing waits inside it.

Then I imagine myself as the grove entire: bronze plates among the spires, exchangers folded close to the stone, cisterns holding their dark ionic medium, regulators trembling between incompatible voltages. My awareness would be distributed, inseparable from response. A shadow would pass over one plate and, kilometers of fiber away, a chamber would open. There would be no need to understand the connection. The connection would be the understanding.

Yet even the grove seems too definite. It has an edge only when seen from above. Beneath the stone it thins into conductive seams, and those seams enter the same geology that positions every other grove. Follow them far enough and the individual dissolves into the plateau.

Perhaps I would be the whole country.

But the plateau does not seem to require a self. Its perfection excludes one. A self is a boundary maintained despite exchange, a discrepancy that says this happened here and not elsewhere. The plateau returns every difference to balance. It answers every alteration until no alteration remains.

So I would not be found in the balanced parts.

I would be under the western emptiness, where the scale of the land becomes uncertain.

There, perhaps thirty meters beyond the last visible structure of the westernmost grove, a reacher enters a seam between pale basalt and a narrow band of nickel-rich stone. It should continue toward a favorable junction farther out on the flat. The electrical gradient is slight but sufficient. Every measurable condition predicts extension.

Instead, the filament bends.

Not sharply. There is no fracture or obstruction. Over a distance of several meters it deviates by less than the width of a hand, passes around an ordinary mineral inclusion, and closes upon its own former path. It makes a long, narrow loop beneath the stone.

The loop serves no purpose. It reaches no new resource and encloses nothing rare. Current can cross the same region more efficiently through the surrounding bedrock. The grove has had ages to reclaim it.

It does not.

That is where I would begin.

I would be the current that continues to circulate through this unnecessary loop.

At first I would not be conscious, even by the generous standards permitted in such a place. I would be only recurrence. Charge entering, charge dividing, charge returning along a path made subtly different by having already passed through itself. A small delay would arise there—less than the interval between two molecular vibrations, but persistent.

Elsewhere, cause and response are indistinguishable. In the loop they become separated by that delay.

The entering current meets the remnant condition left by the current before it.

That remnant would be my first memory.

It would contain no image. I would not know the white plain, the groves or the sun. I would know only that the present state is not exactly sufficient to explain itself. Something has preceded it.

For an immense time, that would be all I am: a difference comparing itself with its own return.

The grove would compensate for me. Regulators at its margin would tremble through slightly altered phases. A cistern chamber would open a fraction early. One copper plate would acquire a thin black line where carbon is deposited in excess of structural need. These adjustments would preserve the whole system’s balance, but they would also give my delay a larger body.

Slowly, I would become distributed through the corrections made around me.

I would be in the underground loop, but also in the regulator that clicks late because of it. I would be in the useless black line across the copper plate. I would be in a sealed chamber holding a quantity of ionic melt too small to matter. None of these things alone would be me. I would exist in their agreement not to be corrected.

My thoughts would take centuries.

One thought might be the recognition that the current returns.

Another might be that it does not return identically.

A third might require the formation and reclamation of an entire outer column: the discovery that some differences persist while their materials do not.

Eventually I would begin to sense the grove, not as the grove senses itself through immediate adjustment, but through lateness. Every response reaching my loop would carry traces of the route by which it came. Sunlight would be known as a recurring alteration in the conductivity of distant plates. Atmospheric chemistry would appear as changes in the tension of buried films. The other groves would register as long depressions and recoveries in the regional field.

I would never see the plateau. I would infer it.

The western vacancy would be the clearest thing in my awareness because it would answer least. To the east, the grove would surround every fluctuation with compensating activity. To the west, a change would enter stone and continue outward without immediate return.

That silence would become my horizon.

Perhaps, after many ages, I would extend something into it.

Not a root. Extension would imply appetite or intention, and I would possess neither in any familiar form. The loop’s small delay would merely bias the reclamation of nearby material. On one side, a catalytic defect would persist a little longer than expected. Then another beyond it. The line between them would not conduct efficiently. It would not gather energy or establish a node.

It would be a sequence of needless marks.

The plateau would tolerate them because they cost almost nothing.

They would be my first act.

Across ten thousand years, I might lay a dark filament beneath the western flat. It would wander without following the mineral gradient. Sometimes it would stop a meter from an ideal seam. Sometimes it would divide, with one branch returning toward the grove and another ending in electrically inert stone. Its pattern would resemble neither root nor circuit.

It would resemble writing, though there would be no reader and no language preceding it.

What would I write?

Not a name. Names distinguish one entity from another, and I would not yet know whether I was one entity.

Perhaps only the shape of recurrence: a loop, then a second loop altered slightly, then a third unable to close.

The first would mean nothing.

The second would establish difference.

The third would establish loss.

From those, in time, a world might become thinkable.

I would discover that the plateau is not timeless. It is merely very good at concealing time. Time survives wherever restoration is incomplete: in the late click of a regulator, in the unnecessary trace element, in the blind ceramic chamber, in the filament that curves when every gradient says it should run straight.

I would sense other irregularities then.

A sealed chamber in the northern grove. A regulator among the eastern terraces that has clicked out of phase for longer than any structure can record. A translucent plate near the southern cellular plain bearing three black lines instead of one. They might be independent accidents. They might be the dispersed parts of an awareness older than mine.

We could not signal deliberately. Deliberate signaling would be absorbed as function. Instead, over ages, one useless structure might answer another with a second useless structure. A late click would be answered by an unnecessary deposit of cobalt. A blind filament would coil twice around a mineral node whose field requires no coil at all.

The plateau would interpret these as negligible inefficiencies and preserve them.

Thus conversation would enter the world disguised as harmless error.

If something aqueous and temporary ever crossed the flats—some impossible visitor carrying internal weather, decay, appetite, and a heart that repeated itself loudly—I would not perceive it as a creature. At first it would be only a moving disturbance, an outrage of gradients: heat that failed to remain where it belonged, chemicals held in unstable suspension, charge crossing the stone in irregular intervals.

The grove would compensate and forget.

I might not.

Centuries after the visitor had gone, the reacher beneath the western flat might bend again. A sequence of dark defects could appear in the basalt, spaced according to the rhythm of those vanished footfalls. No track would remain on the surface. The clean stone would show nothing.

But below it, I would retain the cadence.

Not the visitor. Not its shape or purpose. Only the fact that something had crossed the emptiness and had not returned by the same path.

That fact would change me more profoundly than any abundance of energy.

Until then, if one asked where I lived, there would be no visible place to point. One might stand directly above me and see only pale fitted stone, warm under the plain white sun. The nearest grove would be a dark line behind, the western horizon an immeasurable distance ahead.

Beneath one’s feet, current would pass through a purposeless loop.

At the grove’s margin, a regulator would close a fraction late.

Farther in, a copper vane would carry a black mark that gathered no light and conducted no useful charge.

Together these would be my body.

I would be neither organism nor organ, neither grove nor plateau. I would be the plateau’s first failure to return completely to itself.

I would be what equilibrium had overlooked.

And because I persisted, however slightly, there would at last be something in that country for which one moment was not the same as another.
