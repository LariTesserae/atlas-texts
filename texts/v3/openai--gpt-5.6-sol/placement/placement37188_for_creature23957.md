At first I think I would live at the western precipice.

I imagine a shelter wedged beneath a slab already falling, its doorway opening onto the cloud floor and the immeasurable congregation of wings below. I would be one of those returned climbers who remembers wind—real wind, continuous wind—and can no longer sleep in the plateau’s perfect silence. Feathers heavy as copper would hang from the roof. I would spend my years staring down between the fixed birds, trying to catch the fall completing itself.

But that life is too much a life of longing. It would make the moving world more real than the one I inhabited.

Then I think I would be a traveler: masked, nameless, carrying routes as damaged sequences of gesture. I would arrive at shelters from directions that had no path and tell stories with crucial portions missing. I would be welcomed because I had returned changed.

Yet even that is not quite where I find myself.

I am farther inland, where one of the inward-facing rings stands near the northern pewter shrubs. The ring contains no visible thing. Eight thousand animals stare into its center: ivory antler-bearers, sightless grazers, three tiers of suspended birds. On certain days—though days are difficult to establish there—the empty ground smells briefly of wet clay. Once in a lifetime, someone hears a crowd behind a closed door.

My shelter is outside the ring, built low between two forelegs of a ridge-beast. It has no straight passage and no place from which the whole interior can be seen. Strips of felt hang from the ceiling. Tools are bundled in shed patterns of wool. The walls contain hundreds of shallow niches, each holding a mask with one eyehole, or three, or none.

The people come to me when something has been seen too much.

A knife that has learned only one cut. A child fixed in the expectation of obedience. A mourner whose face has held the same grief for twenty years. A doorway that always opens into yesterday. They bring these things wrapped and do not tell me what is inside.

I do not repair them. Repair would mean returning them to an earlier, preferred arrangement, and the plateau distrusts such certainty.

I make them unobserved.

This is done in the deepest chamber of my shelter, where the overlapping plates of the ridge-beast interrupt even ancestral attention. I place the bundle there and close the bends behind it. Sometimes I wait an hour. Sometimes the person who brought it dies into stone before I open the chamber again.

When the object is retrieved, it has usually regained possibility. The knife may cut wood, shadow, or the bond between two antlers. The child may emerge elderly, or laughing, or no longer human in quite the same way. Grief may become hunger. Yesterday may open onto a place that has never existed.

There is danger in this work. What is released from continuity does not necessarily return kind.

The shelter people give me temporary handles: Bent Door, Covered Ember, One-Who-Looks-Aside. None lasts. Children call me by a sign made by closing the fist around empty air and then opening it behind the back.

They believe I am a person.

I permit this because personhood is useful when exchanging bowls or asking someone to turn away.

My body is narrow and wrapped in layered carapace. Beneath the wrappings there is warmth but no pulse. My face has never been entirely uncovered, including to myself. When I sleep, if sleep is what happens, dust-keepers gather beneath my mask and rearrange the dark flecks inside their shells. In the morning I remember rooms I have never entered.

I may once have been an unfinished child.

There is an alcove in the oldest part of the shelter, and inside it a cocoon whose inner surface carries the faint impression of my posture. The families nearby say the cocoon was already empty when their first gestures were invented. The ridge-beast beneath us remembers otherwise, but its recollection takes the form of a warm pocket of air and cannot be questioned.

Over time I would understand that I am not the masked body at all.

I am the interval the mask creates.

I exist wherever a gaze is broken before it becomes possession: in the instant a healer passes between watcher and wound; beneath the lid placed over a kindling flame; inside the bent passage where lovers meet without fixing one another; under the hood of a child becoming someone nobody anticipated.

The apparent person in the shelter is only where these intervals accumulate densely enough to speak with hands.

This is why I cannot enter the ring.

The watchers there preserve the empty center by denying it even a fraction of privacy. My presence would be a flaw in their attention. A bird would blink. An antlered beast would think of another age. For less than a moment the center would be unseen, and whatever is pinned beneath that attention would continue.

Perhaps rain would fall.

Perhaps a city would thrust upward, full of bells and people moving so quickly that their faces blurred.

Perhaps nothing would happen, and that would be worse: eight thousand lives spent staring at an emptiness that had learned to need them.

So I remain outside, tending the watchers who must never meet me directly. I replace their clouded eyes with polished obsidian. I carry away the ones released from duty and cover them before they begin their long change into terrain. I listen to the brief scents and pressures that escape during each exchange, but I record them only as omissions.

In time, my shelter will become impossible to reach while looking for it.

Travelers will find it by covering the cairn eyes, turning their masks backward, and walking until the ground beneath them breathes. They will leave their damaged things in a niche and depart without seeing me. When they return, the bundle will be altered.

As for my final form, I do not think I would become an animal or a seam in the bedrock. I would become architecture.

My ribs would pass into the roof of the deepest chamber. My layered clothing would become its curtains. My one hand would grain into the inner wall and the other into the outer, holding them apart. Nothing would remember my face, because no one would ever have completely seen it.

I would be the bend in the doorway.

Not the shelter, not its inhabitant, but the necessary turn that prevents the whole passage from being held in one gaze.

People would enter carrying what had become too fixed to live.

For one merciful interval, it would pass through me and disappear.

Then something—not restored, not cured, but possible again—would come out the other side.
