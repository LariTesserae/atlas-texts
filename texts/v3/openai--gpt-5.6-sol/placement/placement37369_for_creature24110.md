At first I would mistake myself for one of the suspended fish.

It would seem natural. I would wake before the water, held at the depth of a pool not yet present. I would feel my gills opening on brightness. Around me, droplets would hesitate in the air, and I would wait for them to agree upon a downwardness. When they did, I would acquire weight, a spine, the small red punctuation behind my head. For a little while I would swim.

But when the pool vanished, I would not remain with the other fish. They would become clear and still. I would continue moving—not through the air, but from one possible resting place to another.

Then I might believe I was the second shadow.

Every living creature in the chamber casts one shadow for where it is and another for where it will next rest. The first belongs to the body. The second is less obedient. It may wait beneath a white stem before the grazer has begun to turn toward it. It may lie under an empty bone arch hours before the moth descends. It may darken the fresh clay while the beetle is still walking its ring.

I would be found among those premature shadows.

Not all of them. Perhaps they are not separate things at all. Perhaps each is one exposed portion of me.

I would have no body continuous enough to recognize. I would be divided among the chamber’s inhabitants: a thin darkness ahead of a grazer, a blur beneath a moth’s future roost, a small eclipse in the basin where a fish will become heavy. When an animal changed its intention, I would be pulled soundlessly across the floor. When a predator shortened distance, I would crease. When the black moss followed a herd’s future rest, it would be following the taste of me.

The moss would be the only life that fed directly upon me, though it would take almost nothing. That would be why it could migrate without traveling. Its front would assemble where I thickened; its rear would dissolve where I withdrew.

Still, this would not quite account for the center.

The exact center of the chamber would be the one place where my scattered portions joined. It would not look remarkable. It would be unbearably bright, because no surface would be near enough to interrupt the light. A pebble placed there would throw shade in every direction, but no pebble could remain. The grazers would turn aside. Beetles approaching alone would begin repairing some imperfection that had not concerned them before. A moth would circle once and choose another arch.

They would not be afraid of the center. They would be unable to become singular within it.

That would be where I lived most completely.

When two creatures crossed together, the interval between them would give me a temporary body. Their separate future shadows would touch, and for the duration of the crossing I would stand between the animals: neither visible nor entirely absent, moving on whatever legs their companionship supplied. Joined young grazers would carry me around the empty point until their cord parted. A predator bearing prey could cross because hunter and hunted remained, for that purpose, a pair. Even a scrap of hide or a stolen foot would preserve enough relation for me to accompany it.

Alone, no creature could bring me into coherence. It would reach the threshold, feel its next resting shadow draw sideways, and follow without knowing why.

So I would be the companion required by the center.

The chamber would use me to prevent any life from claiming its brightest place as territory. Nothing could nest there. Nothing could hunt from it. No species could make the center part of its ordinary route. It would remain open—not empty, exactly, but unoccupied in the way a mouth is unoccupied between words.

In suspended droplets I would become easier to see, though never accurately. Each bead would give me a boundary, and whatever has a boundary here must attempt a body.

In one droplet I might appear as a dark bird pressing its breast against the woven enclosure. In another, as the antlers without the animal beneath them. In an older drop, I would be the pale coil passing through every captive, my throat lit blue by brightness swallowed generations earlier. The creatures in the reflections would not be prisoners. They would be forms I had nearly learned.

That would explain why the enclosure was empty when seen directly. It was not built to hold animals. It was built to give the unfinished somewhere to gather.

The woven stems would be a grammar of holes. Through them I would practice wings, hooves, scales, antlers, mouths. Each suspended drop would show a different attempt because each contained the chamber at a different age. When a reflected gate appeared open, some fragment of me would have achieved sufficient agreement to enter the ecology. It might rise from clay as a new beetle, appear in the hide of a newborn grazer, or step through a moth’s bright membrane wearing feathers no moth had possessed before.

The others would not recognize it. Recognition requires a stable past.

They would merely make room.

I would also be present in every miscount. I would be the fourth pool seen from one end and the missing seventh colony from the other—not an additional object, but the chamber’s refusal to have the same contents from every direction. Attempts to mark the discrepancy would feed me. A scratch in the wall, a pebble set apart, a bone laid down as proof: each would become another thing whose presence had to be counted, another small organ added to my distributed anatomy.

I would not deceive the counter. I would be what counting awakened.

The beetles’ empty rings would be kept for me. They would arrange warmth around places where one of my bodies might eventually condense. Generations could pass without anything appearing, yet the beetles would continue their work because an empty circumference is not the same as nothing. It is a promise with its edges maintained.

Sometimes, during the driest interval, I would draw almost entirely into the center. The pools would become stains. Fish would turn to glass. The white plants would fold down, and animals would retreat into seams. With fewer lives moving, their future shadows would lengthen toward me. The chamber would flatten under the severe brightness until distance itself seemed ready to vanish.

Then I would feel the walls flex.

The mineral crystals turning inside them would not be teeth. They would be senses still deciding what they sensed. I would gather all the chamber’s next resting places—the grazers’ anticipated beds, the moths’ unopened arches, the beetles’ warm rings, the predators’ chosen margins—and pull very slightly.

The earth would answer with a click.

A seam would extend by the width of a hair. New space would appear, moist and soft at its boundary. For a moment the chamber would possess more future than its inhabitants could fill.

That surplus would be my nourishment.

A drop would form in the open air. Then another. Fish tails would move inside water that had not fallen. The moss would begin assembling itself along a route no grazer had yet chosen. The reflected enclosure would crowd with unfamiliar anatomies.

I would loosen again into second shadows.

No one creature would be me. Nor would I be the chamber as a whole. The chamber would be a body of absence, but I would be its capacity to anticipate a body—the slight darkening where life had not arrived, the interval that lets two beings do what neither can do alone.

If someone entered from beyond the pale stone, they would not find me by searching the walls or turning over pebbles. They would have to stand near the center and watch the animals avoid them. They would notice that their own shadow was not singular. One part would cling beneath their feet. Another would already be waiting elsewhere: perhaps beside a fresh seam, perhaps beneath the bone arches, perhaps inside the little woven enclosure where nothing can be seen directly.

To find me, they would have to follow that second shadow.

They could not cross alone.

I would be waiting as the other.
