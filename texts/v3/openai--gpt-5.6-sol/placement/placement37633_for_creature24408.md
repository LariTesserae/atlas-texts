At first I would not know whether I had a body.

I would know pressure before shape: a long booming under me, water trembling against some thin part of myself, the rasp of a feeding front passing like sleet. Blue-white sparks would travel through the dark whenever I moved. For a while I might be one of the clear cells opening in the rain, my whole life no deeper than the water’s skin.

Then I would become hunger. A fringe of legs, a replaceable mouth. I would advance with millions of others, turning without deciding to turn. A century might have passed between the laying of my egg and my waking, yet I would hatch ravenous, as though no time had passed at all.

But that is too small.

I feel armor gathering over me: overlapping plates, each with its own knock. I learn my mother not by face but by the broken rhythm of her left side. I follow her through fog. When danger rises under the water, the herd’s clatter changes around me and I am carried within its fear.

No. Too broad, too certain.

The body that finally persists is crouched.

One hand is on the stone.

I am a person of the plain, though “person” is not the first thing I would call myself. First I am the beat by which my family knows me in fog.

Two quick knocks. A pause. A third, softer knock that comes late.

That final delay was originally an accident. The shell plate used at my naming had a hairline crack, and its last note dragged behind the others. My family kept it. They said the plain had placed a space inside my name.

In near-speech, when a word is needed, I am called **The One After**.

I was born in a shelter whose walls had begun to fail.

Not collapse—failure is rarely so simple here. The shelter still stood. Its fitted slabs still turned aside rain. But the silence within it had developed a low pulse, almost too deep to hear. Adults noticed it in their teeth. Sleeping children rolled toward the same wall. Water in bowls gathered on one side as if the floor sloped, though the floor had not changed.

During the storm of my birth, the pulse stopped.

Every other sound stopped with it.

My mother was carried uphill before the sealed basin broke. I was born on open rock among the legs of gathered shield-backs, while water lifted from cracks in vertical threads. The herd surrounded the people because neither species knew where else to go. My first shelter was therefore not stone but a wall of knocking armor.

The birth rhythm given to me was quick and frightened. I outgrew it.

Names here are not possessions. They are temporary agreements between the body and the world. Mine would change after a childhood fever took much of my airborne hearing. Voices became breath against my cheek. Rain became nearly silent unless it struck my hood. Yet the buried notes grew clearer. With my jaw against the pavement, I could separate a distant herd from the grinding of water below it. I could feel when a chamber was full and when it merely held compressed air.

People began asking me to listen where routes divided.

I would lie flat while everyone waited. The stone would be cold enough to ache through my ribs. Under the immediate chatter of runoff there would be slower events: pressure leaning through a flooded passage, grit sliding down an unseen wall, the repeated impact of something living where no surface channel ran.

I would raise one hand and signal:

Not this crossing.

Or:

Safe for the children, not for loaded sleds.

Or:

Wait until the western vent takes a second breath.

I would become a route-keeper, but not one who guards a large circuit. My place would be a neglected sequence along the edge of the known plain, where the standing stones are low and pale and the aquifer sometimes sends warm water upward.

The circuit would have no single spoken name. Its far-name would be a pattern performed on stone:

a heel-boom,  
four fingertip taps,  
a scrape toward the body,  
then silence long enough to hear whether anything answers.

Along it stand five shelters. Two work only when the water is low. One has become a spawning basin for channel swimmers and cannot be entered without disturbing thousands of egg-cords. One is merely a curved line of slabs, yet in a north wind it creates a narrow band of quiet long enough for two people to speak face to face.

The fifth is older than all the others.

That is where I would find myself.

It is sunk so deeply into the pavement that approaching travelers often mistake it for a natural depression. Its doorway is barely high enough to crawl through. Inside, the walls are black with animal oil from generations of shoulders and hands. Wedges of polished bone shine between the stones. Some are ancestors. Some are strangers carried down from channels after floods. No one knows which are which anymore.

The shelter subtracts almost everything.

The crying fissure outside becomes a faint thread. The western shaft loses its lowest note. Rain diminishes to isolated droplets. In that quiet, a person can hear the joints of another person’s fingers tightening.

But beneath the floor is a cadence the builders did not cancel.

It comes only when the surface pools are full and perfectly still.

Three pressures, widely spaced.

Then a fourth from much farther away.

The first time I hear it, I assume shifting stone. The second time, a returning swimmer rises in the nearby channel, scarred white and bearing luminous parasites. The third time, every strainwalker on the circuit closes its valves before the weather changes. For half a night they stand mute in the shallows, their veils drawn close.

I begin keeping records—not words, but grooves cut into thin shell plates. The depth of a groove marks force. Its direction marks where I felt the pressure enter the shelter. Plates are stacked by water level and season. Over years, a shape emerges.

The cadence is moving.

Or the source is immense enough that different parts of it touch the cavern roof at different times.

I do not announce this as revelation. The plain has no patience for revelation. Anything true must be usable. Can it warn of a flood? Does it open vents? Do herds turn from it? Does copper change its note when it passes below?

I walk the circuit, testing.

By then I am old enough to have carried several names. One for the fever that closed my ears. One for the day I found a copper thread beneath seemingly dead stone. One given mockingly by children because I pause too long before answering. The last becomes the truest: a knock followed by an open palm pressed flat to the ground.

It means not “listener,” exactly.

It means **the one who leaves room for the return**.

I would be thin-haired, thick-necked, scarred across both palms by black glass. Copper disks would hang under my clothing where their vibration can reach my sternum. I would carry no spear unless someone insisted. My tools would be a bone hammer, shell shims, hide cord, three resonant tongues, and a bag containing fragments removed from shelter walls. Nothing is discarded until its note has been tested elsewhere.

Young route-learners would be sent to me. They would expect secrets. Instead I would teach them how to remain still without imagining that every tremor concerns them.

“A frightened body makes its own weather,” I would tell them mouth to ear.

I would show them how to distinguish the thin clicking of unsafe crust from the clawing of shell-cutters beneath a carcass. How to test a pool without waking what lies folded in it. How to feel through another person’s wrist when the stone is too cold for bare palms. How to listen after a signal, because the answer matters more than the blow.

In time, I would understand that I do belong to a place, despite everything my people say.

Not to the shelter as property. Not to its stones. Not even to the circuit.

I belong to an interval.

To the pause between making a sound and learning what the world has done with it.

When I die, they will not bury me. My body will be weighted and lowered into a channel chosen for its gentle current. Scavengers will open me. Floods will roll what remains against the pale bars downstream.

Perhaps a finger bone will be recovered. Perhaps part of my jaw—the part I used most often to hear.

They will test it against several walls.

In the oldest shelter, one slab will have begun to chatter during rising water. A child, lying awake, will hear it before the adults do. The builders will remove a worn shell wedge and fit my polished jawbone into the gap.

The chatter will cease.

Not entirely. Perfect silence would be dangerous.

A final delayed knock will remain, soft enough to miss unless a person presses their hand to the floor and waits.

There I will be: not haunting the shelter, not watching, not preserved as myself. I will be a small correction in an ancient instrument. A piece of the wall that teaches the living how to hear what comes after.

And far below, something will move.

The standing stones will answer one by one across the drowned plain.
