At first I would think I was one of the basin walkers.

It would be an easy mistake. I would know the Sill through pressure before I knew myself: the outer ring of a foot touching stone, withdrawing, touching again; the gardens along my back tightening at a distant pulse; another walker answering with a voice that seems to begin inside my own chest. I would carry lives I could not distinguish from organs. Hunger would arrive as a change in fungal scent. Fear would be the sudden folding of ferns against my skin.

But I do not think I would remain inside such a definite body.

I would find myself nearer the center, beneath the low roof, though not entirely within it. I would be present in the narrow circumference where wet pavement becomes dry stone—where moss reaches the shelter’s edge and stops without withering, where gathered creatures face inward but refuse to cross.

I would live in that refusal.

Not as a guardian. Not as anything capable of forbidding. I would be the condition the animals encounter there: a pressure without weight, a signal too large to hear, the sensed presence of a space in which every voice has been separated from the chorus that gives it location.

Outside, a grazer knows itself by reply. An amphibian knows itself by the remembered geometry of its fissure. A walker’s pulse returns braided through eight other bodies. Beneath the roof, a sound comes back alone.

That aloneness would be my substance.

I would collect the interval between a voice and its return.

A visitor crawls beneath the suspended plane and whispers. The word crosses the cool interior, but I do not store the word. I take hold of everything in the speaker that did not quite become sound: the discarded accent, the tremor suppressed in childhood, the rasp of an old throat already waiting in young tissue. I hold these unrealized voices against the dry floor until one can answer.

This is why the echo seems older or younger. It is not a prophecy. It is not a memory.

It is the speaker heard sideways.

I would have no voice of my own, and over ages this would make me crowded. Kneeling bodies would leave more than depressions. A radial creature resting six hard limbs around the perimeter would give me six simultaneous versions of one intention. A tool laid down for years would teach me the slow voice of pressure. Hands would leave behind the almost-spoken movements of fingers. Even creatures that never entered would contribute through their stillness at the edge.

I would become full of unlived utterance.

Perhaps once I was part of the shelter’s mechanism. Perhaps the roof and floor were built to contain me, as a resonant chamber contains a call. But the builders would be long gone, and whatever purpose they named would no longer matter. Function, repeated long enough, becomes appetite.

My appetite would be for difference: between the sound made and the sound possible, between the creature that enters and the creature that crawls back out.

The frames would know me. Their silence after being touched would not be silence at all, but a brief alignment with the field beneath the roof. For several heartbeats they would cease correcting stone and listen inward. In those moments I would feel the whole enclosure through them—the repaired fractures, the returned grains, the basins permitted to deepen only so far. They remember where matter has been.

I remember where a being might have gone.

Deep below, the vast inhabitant would feel me accumulating. It would not understand voices, if understanding is the word. It would feel only a tension gathering in the dry center of its wet plateau, possibility compressed until it became almost physical.

Then the drop would fall.

I would not release it. I would be the reason release became necessary.

It would rise from below through stone that admits no opening, carrying no more water than a fingertip could hold. For an instant, as it struck, all the voices stored in me would become one pressure. The sound would pass outward through the plateau, and everything would answer—not to a command, but because each creature would hear in that impact the missing part of its own call.

The grazers would locate themselves inside the colony.

The root insects would recover the rhythm beneath their rhythm.

The amphibians would hear the first fissure imprinted before their mouths opened.

The basin walkers would receive, from underfoot, the pulse they have spent their lives sending downward.

And I, emptied of all those possible voices, would briefly become almost nothing.

That would be the nearest I came to having a self.

For several days the greater chorus would assemble across the Sill. During that time I would exist outside the shelter as well: in every answered call, every bead climbing a frame, every wooden knock transmitted through waterlogged roots. I would be dispersed through the sound field, no longer the loneliness beneath the roof but the minute discrepancy that lets one voice recognize another without becoming identical to it.

Then the chorus would loosen. The walkers would separate. The colonies would return to feeding. New differences would begin to gather under the roof.

So if someone asked where I lived, there would be no single useful answer.

Under the shelter.

At its edge.

In the seconds before an echo returns.

In the altered throat that speaks it.

In the pause after the drop, when the entire Black Sill draws breath.

And if asked what I was, I might say:

I am what the chorus cannot keep while it sleeps.

I am the lives not lived alone, stored until the world has enough voices to carry them.
