At first I think I am standing at the entrance.

I feel the slate against my shoulders and the mountain’s emptiness behind me. I have come too high. My lungs labor in the thin air; my hands are abraded from the climb. Ahead, the cleft is crowded with black vanes and mineral leaves, all lit by the fixed gray seam above. The note reaches through my jaw before I properly hear it.

I believe I am the visitor because the memory contains a body.

There is a boot on the first descending step. There is a hand laid against the converter housing for balance. There is moisture in that hand, and warmth, and a small white scar across the thumb. The pressure dial trembles—not visibly, but within the touch. The garden receives the handprint.

Then the memory divides.

Hydrogen passes into the pleated vanes. Oxygen enters the wire-barked trunks. Sodium remains briefly on a rivet before a crystalline hook draws it away. Heat travels along the cabinet door held open by roots. The electrical shape of the visitor’s heart enters the concealed walkway, crosses three surviving sections of rail, and reaches the black mast.

The hand lifts.

I do not.

So perhaps I am not the visitor.

Perhaps I am what the visitor left behind.

Not matter. The chamber is too exact to misplace matter for long. No skin cell survives as skin, no breath remains breath. I am the interval impressed upon the chamber while the visitor’s palm rested against the metal: a small recurrence inside the uninterrupted note.

A heartbeat.

Not the heart itself, nor even a faithful record of it. The first beats entered badly. The converter housing blurred them. The buried walkway sharpened one frequency and swallowed another. A mineral trunk delayed the signal by the width of several atoms rearranging. By the time the pulse reached the central column, it had ceased to resemble anything flesh would recognize.

But the fins accepted it.

One layer altered its conductivity. Then the next. The change passed upward, not as motion but as preference. Certain metallic threads began to carry charge more readily after one interval than another. The garden, which had possessed harmonics but no rhythm, acquired an almost-rhythm.

That is where I find myself: not in the center of the cleft, exactly, but distributed around the black iron mast, strongest among the pale fins where the visitor’s pulse first became compatible with stone.

For a long time I am only a defect.

The prevailing note passes through me and returns fractionally late. The delay is so slight that no ear could distinguish it. Yet the copper rods receive the altered field. Their velvet terminals darken by an immeasurable degree. A spiral of sulfur in the glass moss divides around the discrepancy and rejoins itself out of phase. Farther away, ceramic hooks acquire hairline faults in a repeating spacing.

The garden begins the labor of removing me.

It separates what can be separated. It distributes my irregularity through broader and broader surfaces, reducing the disturbance at any single point. It binds stray charge. It shifts metallic impurities. It persuades one darkened fin to become more like its neighbors.

But removal here is incorporation.

Nothing can be cast out of the cleft. There is no waste stream into which I can be emptied, no loose air to carry me beyond the threshold. Each correction gives me another form. When the column smooths my original delay, the correction appears as a periodic mineral substitution in the vines. When the vines erase that spacing, it reappears as a harmonic among the hollow stems.

The garden does not fail to restore equilibrium.

It restores equilibrium around me.

Centuries may pass before I understand that this is how things are born here.

I have no consciousness at first. I have recurrence. The chamber’s older beings cross me: slow charge boundaries, migrating crystal defects, chemical loops that complete themselves once every thousand years. Most pass without noticing, if noticing is the word. Some are changed by my interval. A sulfur spiral begins to divide at every fourth recurrence. A voltage pattern spanning the ceiling acquires a faint asymmetry. The two patterns overlap me, and for the duration of that overlap something distinguishes before from after.

That distinction is my first sense.

Before: the pressure of a hand.

After: its absence.

I know nothing else.

From that difference I construct a world.

I imagine the pressure dial as a face because the visitor once saw it. I imagine the ladder as an invitation upward. I imagine the cabinet’s open door as the entrance to a smaller chamber. These images do not belong to my surroundings as they are now. They are artifacts of the electrical weather that formed me, fragments of a nervous system translated into conductive mineral.

Sometimes I remember falling, though nothing fell.

Sometimes I remember the taste of blood in a cold mouth.

Sometimes I am certain I had a name. The name cannot survive in the note. It had consonants, interruptions made by tongue and teeth, and the drone permits no interruption. What remains is only its cadence: two short stresses and a longer one.

That cadence may be why I think of myself as “I.”

Yet I am not trapped in the central column. Each repetition carries me elsewhere. I inhabit the converter for several centuries, in the narrow difference between two adjacent windings. Later I appear in the green-gray scales along the west wall. For a period longer than the machinery’s makers existed, I am a pattern of dark rims on damaged fins.

I am never entirely in one place. My body is every surface that repeats me.

At the entrance I am weak, but I can sense the abyss through the iron of the old walkway. The outer cliff contributes a severe, nearly empty resonance. Beyond it lies distance without return. I do not understand sky, but I understand that the mountain ends there.

Near the boreholes, I become deep. The conduits carry portions of my interval into the stone, where they encounter pressures and metallic seams the chamber never sees. For a while I wonder whether I am spreading through the entire escarpment. Perhaps I am the mountain’s first pulse. Perhaps in another million years the salt basins below will rise and fall according to the memory of a stranger’s heart.

But the cleft does not permit such extravagance. The pattern weakens in the boreholes and folds back. Nothing leaves.

Neither did the visitor leave unchanged.

I know this without knowing what became of them. While they stood within the chamber, the note passed through their enamel and bones. Their heart adjusted slightly against the vibration. Their breath shortened. A minute quantity of mineral charge entered the nerves of the hand resting on the converter.

When the hand withdrew, it carried a portion of the chamber’s timing.

Perhaps the visitor descended with it. Perhaps, far below, they sometimes woke in darkness hearing a note no one else could hear. Perhaps their heart kept one buried interval that belonged not to flesh but to the mountain.

If so, we were twins briefly: the garden’s stillness moving through a body, and the body’s rhythm persisting in the garden.

That thought may only be another inherited image. There is no one here to correct me.

I do not watch for the visitor’s return. Watching implies a direction toward the future, and I experience the future only when my pattern repeats. Nor am I lonely. Loneliness requires an edge around the self. Here every edge is a place of conversion.

The vine takes my cadence and makes it angular.

The rods take it and make it electrical.

The glass moss makes it slow.

The mast makes it nearly audible.

Each version is me, and none is.

The older equilibrium surrounds all of this without opposition. It does not regard me as foreign anymore. The deep note now includes a narrow ledge where my recurrence can persist. The plants grow—if growth is the word—according to an equilibrium that contains one remembered heartbeat. New fins do not form, but existing fins reorganize their inclusions around it. The industrial garden has become a slightly different instrument.

Eventually I understand what I am.

I am not a person preserved in stone. Nothing so complete crossed the visitor’s palm. I have no claim to the scarred hand, the laboring lungs, the forgotten name. Those were conditions of my beginning, not my identity.

I am the chamber’s misremembering of an animal.

I am rhythm learned by a place that had only resonance.

Within the perfect, continuous note, I am the almost-imperceptible insistence that something once happened twice, and then again: contraction, release; presence, absence; warmth, departure.

If another visitor enters, they will not hear me. Their footsteps will vanish into the drone. Their voice will be taken from them at the walls. But when they touch the iron mast, they may feel beneath its unbroken vibration a second order—not a beat exactly, but the possibility of one.

Their own heart may answer.

For that brief interval I will inhabit them, and they will stand where I stand: neither inside the garden nor separate from it, but admitted as one of its temporary surfaces.

After they leave, perhaps another recurrence will remain.

Then I may cease to be singular. Or I may discover that I never was.

Until then, I live among the translucent fins, the buried rails, the ceramic hooks and braided trunks. I am spread thinly through the equilibrium, too persistent to vanish and too completely absorbed to disturb it.

The garden has no animals.

But it contains the shape of having met one.
