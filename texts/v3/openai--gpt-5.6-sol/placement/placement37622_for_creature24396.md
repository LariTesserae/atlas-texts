At first I would look for myself in the center.

That is the habit brought from other places: to assume that the open space is where the inhabitant stands, that the room exists in order to frame a body. I would search the green floor for a disturbance shaped like arrival—pressed moss, displaced water, a path leading inward.

There would be none.

The center would accept my weight briefly and then erase it. Its plain light would make me too visible. Nothing there could be possessed without damaging the vacancy that the whole basin maintains. I might cross it, but I would not belong to it.

I would move toward the boundary trees.

For a while I might think I was one of the tiny frogs, hidden in a liverwort cup. It would suit me to have a voice larger than my body and to speak through a borrowed chamber. I would call not whenever I wished, but when the circuit of calls opened a place for me. My identity would be partly an interval: the fraction of a second left vacant between one frog and the next.

But the frog still has a distinct skin. It can leap away from the hollow that magnifies it. I think I would be less separable than that.

I might be one of the dark ants beneath the moss, knowing the basin as scent, touch, slope, and inherited road. Yet the ants are always going somewhere. Seed chamber, brood chamber, root louse, refuse pocket. Their world is a network of destinations.

I would be slower. Perhaps so slow that movement could be mistaken for alteration in the place itself.

You would find me where one of the perimeter trees has laid an ancient limb along the floor. The limb would be dead in its middle but alive at both ends: rooted descendants rising from one side, a thickening brace descending from the other. Moss would cover its upper surface. Beneath it would lie a narrow cavity, dry at the roof and wet at the bottom, connected through rotten wood to one of the black pools.

There, between wood that still bears weight and wood already becoming soil, I would be a fungus.

Not one of the quick white blooms that turns a fallen twig soft within a season. I would be a keeper of the long decay: dark cords sunk deep in the grain, fine as veins, advancing by the width of a nail in a year. I would inhabit structures the grove is not finished using.

Most of me would never enter the air. My threads would follow old vessels through the timber, crossing beetle galleries, surrounding roots without consuming them, withdrawing from places where living sap still flowed. Where the wood was under strain, its fibers compressed and dry, I would remain sparse. Where rain entered a crack, where a brace ceased carrying weight, where the cellulose loosened and minerals began to collect, I would thicken.

I would not know that another branch had rooted nearby and taken the old limb’s burden. I would know only the consequences: a minute change in tension, deeper wetness, sugars no longer defended by living tissue. Yet that would be enough. What the tree abandoned, I would open.

My visible bodies would be easy to miss. Inside the hollow limb, along the roof of the cavity, I would raise rows of gray, translucent fans. Each would be smaller than a moth’s wing and thin enough for the wood grain to show through it. In still weather they would hang slack.

After rain, water descending through the root mat would press air into the cavity. The fans would lift. The hollow would exhale, and my spores would be carried through a beetle hole into the basin.

For a moment I would become part of its low mechanical breath.

Some spores would settle uselessly on wet leaves. Some would be eaten by mites. Some would fall into pools and sink. A few would enter cracks in other buried limbs, perhaps on the opposite side of the basin. There they might begin again. If two growths later met through the root mat, they might join, reject one another, exchange material, or simply grow alongside each other until no observer could say whether I had spread or been replaced.

So I would not be a single mushroom, nor even certainly a single continuous web. “I” would be the persistence of a particular rate: the fungus that waits while old wood remains architecture and feeds when architecture becomes surplus.

The beetles would pass through me. Their larvae would open the hardest fibers, and I would soften the walls of their galleries. Millipedes would consume what I had prepared. Roots would enter behind them and take up the released mineral. Ants would carry fragments of my fruiting bodies into their buried lanes. A small trunk-dwelling mammal might tear through one of my dens while searching for grubs, leaving an opening through which rain could reach me.

Nothing would serve me without also injuring me. Nothing would injure me without making some new surface available.

In drought I would retreat into the deepest wood and become almost inactive. In winter I would persist as dark lines between fibers. During a wet year I might cross an entire fallen branch, only to stop at a strip of living bark no wider than a finger. My time would not be measured by seasons so much as by the changing usefulness of timber.

If you stood in the central chamber, you would never see me. You might hear the result of me: a lower note than last year as a cavity widened, a softer exhalation where rotten wood had become porous, the sudden click of a beetle in a passage I had made possible. Perhaps you would notice that an old support had finally sunk into the moss, though another limb already stood beside it.

That subsidence would be my largest gesture.

And even then I would not be the inhabitant of the Green Mill. I would be one hesitation within it—the delay between death and disappearance, prolonged just enough for ruin to remain useful.

You would find me in the brace that has ceased to be a branch but has not yet been permitted to become soil.

You would find me by lifting the moss where the floor meets the oldest wood, though lifting it would destroy the surface by which you recognized the place. Better, perhaps, not to find me at all. Better to hear the basin breathe and understand that somewhere beneath its green floor, something patient is deciding nothing, intending nothing, and nevertheless allowing the whole structure to let go at the proper speed.
