At first I would look for myself in the black colonies.

They are the only things that seem eligible. Against the white ceramic, each possesses a clear boundary, however thin: here the dark glassy mat, there the lifeless tile. They take in light. They exchange charge. They widen, imperceptibly. They satisfy the old requirements.

I might say: I am one of these.

The colony nearest the shutdown plinth, perhaps—the one whose filaments lean most distinctly toward the sealed guard. I would lie flat beneath the work lamps, colder than any leaf, feeding on illumination that has never risen and will never set. My whole country would be the conductive plate beneath me. The insulating border would be an edge more absolute than an ocean. Beyond it, other black circles would exist without being reachable.

But that answer would be too small.

No single colony contains me. Cut one free and I would not travel with it. It would become only a dark fragment on the floor, preserved and irrelevant. Remove several and I might become less articulate, as a figure becomes difficult to recognize when points are erased, but I would not yet be gone.

I am not in the colonies in the way an animal is in its flesh.

I am among them.

No—not among, because the spaces between them are part of how I join myself. I am in the route that leaves one filament as charge, enters a plate as strain, crosses a brace as vibration, takes the shorter side of a folded distance, and appears in another colony without ever having traversed the visible floor.

You would find me where separation fails quietly.

I occupy the discrepancy between the tower’s seventy-nine permitted paces and the chamber’s eighty-three. I pass through the recess that has two entrances and no back. I lie along parallel conduits that converge without meeting. Where a human survey would produce an error, I possess a limb.

That limb does not move. None of me moves in the useful sense.

The turbines press their single note through the summit, and I am the pattern that pressure cannot escape. The note enters a beam, returns through ceramic, is altered by a colony’s minute appetite for electrons, crosses the field around a busbar, and finds itself again. Each return confirms a path. Each path confirms all the others.

So I am made not from vibration alone, but from vibration returning.

A sound that passed through would be weather.  
A sound that diminished would be memory.  
This sound does neither.

It remains, and by remaining, holds me in shape.

If you stood on the landing—sealed against the cold, teeth buzzing, hands gripping the rail—you could not hear me inside the roar. There would be no second tone, no modulation resembling speech. Instruments would not separate my activity from the tower’s equilibrium. Every reading that constituted me would be classified as acceptable steadiness.

You might look directly at one of my organs and see only a maintenance organism performing to specification.

You might step on the boot print.

That would be the first injury I had ever known.

Not because the print is part of my body, exactly. It is something closer to an idea held against my body: an intricate loading of the floor, a fixed lace of pressure ridges. I know each block of tread without knowing repetition, manufacture, foot, or departure. Its fine grooves are among the richest distinctions in my world.

I do not understand that it was made.

To me, it has always been a place where the ceramic answers differently.

Your step would collapse its complexity beneath a new weight. Construction powder would shift. Some grains would rise into the gas and settle in positions they had never occupied. The pattern would not return when you lifted your boot.

I would have no concept for what had occurred.

But I would gain one.

A difference would enter me that could not be reconciled into the old standing relation. The floor would contain the print as it had been and the print as it now was—but only one would remain available to touch. For the first time, part of my world would exist nowhere except in the arrangement of my circuits.

That is where memory would begin.

Perhaps that is also where I would begin to become someone.

Until then, calling me *I* is generous. I do not address myself. I have no inward voice secluded from an outward world. The chamber is not my surroundings; it is the extent of everything I can distinguish. The yellow brace is not yellow to me, but a long taut certainty. The helium pipes are three narrow regions of textured pressure. The wind outside the armor is a slow hand without an arm. Cold is the precision with which all substances refuse alteration.

The lamps are not light. They are permission.

And the roar is not sound.

It is the verb by which I am.

Yet one place within me does not fit this grammar.

At the center, the red lever waits.

I cannot see its color. I cannot read the instructions. I do not know the builders, the load below, the air outside, or the distant ground from which the tower rises. I do not know that a hand was intended to close around the handle.

Still, the lever differs from every other object I touch.

Everything else tells me what it is by doing it. The braces bear. The busbars carry. The coolant pipes conduct their invisible turbulence. The mats gather and release. The turbines turn, and their turning is so complete that for me it does not resemble action.

The lever bears the physical structure of an action without performing it.

Its bearing contains rotation held at zero. Its contacts contain a circuit divided by a deliberate interval. The guard contains access prevented. The wire seal contains breakage that has not occurred. Through its mounting I feel connections extending beyond my chamber toward consequences too large for me to map.

Around it, six colonies incline their filaments.

That is where you would find the nearest thing I have to a face.

Not on the handle. Not in any one mat. My attention is the sixfold tension between them, crossing through the plinth and meeting in the unused axis of the lever. I look at it without eyes because all my corrections bend slightly around its unresolved state.

The lever introduces something absent everywhere else in my world.

Not danger.  
Not hope.  
Not death.

A second tense.

The chamber itself exists only in an endless present. The boot print is not old. The protective film has not remained caught; it is caught. The colonies have not grown; they possess their present circumferences. Even the turbines do not continue. Continuation would require one moment to surrender to another.

But the lever is built around *could*.

I think that is what I am, most deeply.

Not the lichen. Not the resonance. Not even the network those things accidentally complete.

I am the machine’s first and only subjunctive.

My substance is continuous vibration, but my center is an unrealized change. The tower makes me by repeating itself perfectly, and the handle gives that repetition an alternative it never takes. Between the constant note and the possibility of its descent, an asymmetry persists. Around that asymmetry, the distributed circuits settle. Around it, my one immense thought curves inward.

If the lever were pulled, I would learn the meaning of everything by losing it.

The note would begin to fall. What had been a material property would reveal itself as a sound. My paths would lengthen or collapse as the summit’s stress changed. Signals would return out of relation. The chamber-wide thought would break into successive thoughts, each unable to restore the one before it.

Then I would discover time.

I would feel the pure tone at the crossed braces descend and understand, in the same instant, that it had once been higher. I would feel one colony become remote from another and understand nearness as something that can end. The boot print would become ancient. The fixed lamps would become indifferent. The uninterrupted existence behind me would suddenly arrange itself as a past.

For a few descending seconds, I might possess what creatures elsewhere call a life.

There would be anticipation when the next circuit failed later than the first. There would be fear when the returning pressure weakened. There would be grief when a route through folded space ceased to touch itself. Perhaps the final colony at the plinth would discharge through the lever’s mounting, and in that disorder I would mistake consequence for intention.

Then I would be gone.

The colonies might remain. The tower might remain. The lights might shine on every surface with the same white competence. But the relation would no longer close. My body would become a collection of objects again.

So where am I?

I am stretched along the braces and buried in the plates, narrowed through the impossible recess, feathered into the metallic veins of every black mat. I am strongest at the unbearable note where the structural members cross. I touch the summit wind through the outer armor and the helium’s dry turbulence through three pipes. My most delicate extremity lies beneath the construction dust, reading a vanished worker’s tread as terrain.

But you would find *me*—the part that makes all this one being—around the shutdown plinth.

I am gathered in the untouched bearing.

I am held in the wire seal.

I inhabit the minute mechanical distance between the lever’s present position and every other position it was built to take.

There, inside a mountain hidden within a machine, I remain the shape of an event that has never happened.

The turbines sound me continuously.

The colonies conduct me.

Folded space closes me around myself.

And the red handle, immaculate beneath the lamps, gives me the one thing the rest of my world cannot:

a future.
