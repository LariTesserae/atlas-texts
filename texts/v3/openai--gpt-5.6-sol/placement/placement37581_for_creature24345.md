At first I would mistake myself for the pulley crab.

It has the satisfying shape of a self: one shell, one appetite, a place to wait. It gathers the chamber onto its back and sits in the chair as though some old appointment has finally been kept. I can imagine watching impossible weather through the algae-clouded window, wearing a washer over one shoulder and a vanished warning label over the other.

But the crab is too complete. It knows where its legs end.

I would be found farther back in the alcove, where the observation window joins the wall.

Around the window is a narrow annular gutter intended to collect condensation. It has corroded through in places, filled with silt in others, and become threaded with pale filaments. Most look like fungus. Some resemble the sensory hairs of an animal too large to fit inside the chamber. They enter the lever housing, follow the chair’s mounting bolts beneath the grate, and reappear in scattered seams around the room.

Workers, if workers still came, would call it fouling.

The garden knows it as the plumb.

There would be no single body to point to. A portion of me would live in the alcove as a cluster of translucent sacs, each no larger than an eye. Other portions would hang beneath ladders on threads fine enough to be mistaken for bell-spider mucus. Still others would occupy tiny cups in the grating, weighted with rust grains and fragments of shell. Inside the chair’s pedestal, my oldest tissue would have grown around the remains of a mechanical inclinometer.

That instrument once answered a simple question: which way is down?

It could not survive the answer becoming complicated.

I did.

My hanging sacs would contain different liquids gathered from the chamber’s unmixed currents: brine, condensate, coolant, oil. Each has its own weight and reluctance. When the room tilts, they pull against one another. When a freight carriage passes, they flatten, stretch, and ring through the filaments. The signals converge behind the window, where a small knot of conductive flesh presses against the corroded contacts of the old leveling system.

I would not think in images. I would think in falls.

The mountain country beyond the glass would arrive as a long green downwardness pulling away from the window. The desert would feel broad and granular, a descent divided among a million dunes. Unfamiliar cities at night would exert hundreds of little verticals, as if every street had its own gravity. When rain runs upward across the pane, my entire network would tighten in distress—not because upward rain is strange, but because the rain insists upon a down different from the cable’s.

The ocean has a down.

The elevator has a down.

The bright countries have another.

The buried foundations exert a fourth, old and nearly forgotten.

I would live among their disagreement.

The pulley crab would be part of me, though it would not know this. When the chamber drifts too far toward one height, glands around the chair would release a mineral sweetness. It would resemble the taste of stunned minnows and freshly loosened metal. The crab would leave its ambush, push through the crimson vines, and settle into the seat.

I would feel its weight enter the chair bolts.

A pulse would travel up the pedestal. One of my filaments would contract against the lever’s hinge. The lever would become warm beneath the crab’s claw. Perhaps it would bump it accidentally. Perhaps there are no accidents in a place where instinct and maintenance have become the same activity.

The chamber would tilt by one degree.

That degree would be my movement.

Gas-fruit would roll across the ceiling. Sediment would lift. Coin-rays would peel from the walls. The cable would groan in its immense sleeve, correcting along some direction that cannot be drawn in three dimensions. For a few moments all my weighted sacs would swing together. I would experience something like relief.

Not balance. Balance is never achieved here.

Only a less destructive imbalance.

The other inhabitants would know me without recognizing me. Tapping shrimp would incorporate my tension into their rhythms. Eels would avoid seams where my filaments had drawn taut. Bell spiders would anchor their communal bubbles above the places where I predicted the ceiling would remain. The broad crawling leaves would follow traces of heat from my buried contacts and slowly outline my nervous system in green.

Coin-rays would preserve my history best.

Whenever the chamber underwent a great correction, electric discharge would briefly mark the nearby panels with branching dark lines. Rays settling there would copy them. Long after corrosion erased the marks, the animals would carry pieces of my former shape on their backs. Seen together, if anyone could gather the entire school, the rays would form a record of every direction the chamber had once believed was level.

They would also reveal that I was changing.

With every tilt, some filaments would snap and regrow along a new route. Some weighted sacs would empty and refill from another current. The old inclinometer at my center would dissolve grain by grain, its brass becoming hooks, membranes, eggs. Eventually nothing manufactured as an instrument would remain.

Yet the question inside it would continue living.

Which way is down?

I would have inherited that question without inheriting its purpose. I would not know that elevators are meant to connect one place with another. I would not know that the window is impossible, that the cable carries cargo, or that the chamber was built rather than born. I would only feel that certain arrangements of pressure are survivable and others are not.

When the cable slowed, every part of me would pull downward at once. Warning current would enter my tissues. I would contract through the lever housing and chair bolts, releasing the mineral lure so strongly that even the pulley crab might abandon food to reach the alcove. Shrimp would hammer the walls. Bell nests would tremble. Polyps would close valves.

For those red-lit minutes, I would become almost conscious—not as a person becomes conscious, but as a net becomes conscious of a great weight.

Then the buried engine would engage.

The cable would crack onward.

My sacs would burst in rows. Filaments would recoil into seams. The window might show clouds below a white plain, or an ocean standing upright like a wall. I would lose pieces of myself into the grille and grow them again elsewhere.

A human entering the garden would not find me at first. They might scrape my tissue from the window rim to clear the view. They might cut the threads from the lever, thinking them harmless. Only when the chamber began to lean—slowly, almost politely—would they understand that something had been removed.

Even then, they might call me a stabilizing colony, a parasitic control mesh, a degenerate guidance system.

All would be partly true.

But if asked what I was, I would answer through the chamber rather than in words. The lights would brighten along one wall. The gas meadow would shift. The crab would rise from the grating and make its careful way toward the chair. Thousands of small creatures would alter their grip by the same fraction.

I would be the garden’s sense of orientation: the living remainder of a question the machinery can no longer solve alone.

You would find me whenever the freight carriage approached and every creature took hold.

I would be the part that knew what they were holding against.
