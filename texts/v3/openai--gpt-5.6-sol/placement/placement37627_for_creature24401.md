You would not find me at the horizon. Nothing that belongs here seeks a view of the whole.

Look instead beneath a leaning trunk where a pale mineral shelf slips under the root mat. Warm seep water issues there without ever becoming a stream. It gathers in a hollow no wider than a spread hand, filmed with green and roofed by overlapping fern tongues. Fish sometimes pass through it, though there is nowhere obvious for them to enter. Yellow spores stipple the cream-colored roots. The air tastes of wet stone.

At first, I might be mistaken for the hollow itself.

Then the hollow breathes.

I would be a heavy amphibian, broad as a fallen fruit and almost as still. My skin would begin black-violet at the mouth and become green toward the back, not through pigment alone but through occupation. Algae would grow in my folds. Tiny translucent grazers would move among it. A pale fungus would inhabit one old scar above my hind leg, opening after each long interval of dripping and folding shut when insects crossed it.

My outline would be uncertain. One forefoot would resemble a splayed root; the other would be hidden in marl-colored mud. Condensation would collect along the ridge of my brow until each drop enlarged the world in miniature—ferns inverted, wings bending through a curved sky—and then rolled into the corner of my mouth.

In shallow cups along my flanks, I would carry several ages at once.

Dark eggs would tremble beneath clear membranes. Tadpoles would browse the green film on my skin. Newly legged young would sit under my arms, their tails not yet absorbed, facing outward as if they had only just discovered distance. One might climb onto my head and remain between my eyes while I fed. I would not know it as the eldest. There would be no eldest. I would know only which small bodies belonged against me and which light touch meant beetle, gnat, leech, raindrop, or the first pressure of a snake’s jaw.

But perhaps “amphibian” would be only what my body looked like.

What I would truly be is a listener.

My belly would rest against hollow roots. Through them I would receive the basin—not as landscape, but as pressure. The soft concussion of herds moving shoulder to shoulder. The minute ticking of beetles closing the pockets they had opened in the mud. The landing of a wader several root-lengths away. Fruit striking leaves before it reached the ground. The wooden knock of a bird transmitted down a trunk, through living fibers, into water, stone, and finally the thin bones behind my jaw.

Other listeners would answer from hollows I could never see.

I would lower my throat to the mud and give one note. It would be deeper than my size should allow, less a call than a roundness entering the ground. The root mat would take it. Seep water would shiver around the fish. Moths on a nearby trunk would loosen their wings but not depart. Far away—or what passes for far away in a world without open distance—another throat would return the note.

I would recognize the answer, but I would not remember learning it.

That would be the shape of my mind. Not emptiness. Not innocence. A vivid, complete present with short roots.

I would know the branch-cat’s weight before its shadow touched the pool. I would know which bright bird could walk safely across my back and which bird’s stillness meant hunger. I would know the taste of the yellow insects that emerged from fungus cups and the bitter skin of the flat lizards under the leaves. I would know where to place my body when the warm seep pressed more strongly from the rock, though the water never truly rose.

Yet I would never gather these knowings into a past. I would not think: *The cat came yesterday.* I would feel only that this tremor opens the mouth, stills the throat, gathers the young beneath the forelimbs. When danger passed, the world would become edible again.

Sometimes a narrow snake, furred with moss, would descend beside me. It might take one of the young from my flank. I would lunge. I would bite if I could. My call would become a short violent sound, and the small bodies would press themselves into my skin.

The snake might succeed.

The absence would be real. I would search the folds along my side. I would nose beneath the fern tongues. Distress would move through me with no less force because the basin remained full. Nothing in the equilibrium would console me.

Then an egg membrane would open in another cup.

This would not replace what was taken. Replacement is an idea requiring comparison, and comparison requires a past that can stand beside the present. The lost young would be lost wholly. The hatching young would arrive wholly. Grief and renewal would touch without becoming a story.

Eventually, a predator would take me, or my body would fail in some ordinary way. My death would not be symbolic. I would struggle. The young would scatter from my back. Bright birds would lift from my shoulders. My skin would pale where it had been pressed into the mud.

But the boundaries would dissolve quickly.

Beetles would enter beneath the jaw. Fungus would translate the violet of my skin into the marbling of its shelves. The algae from my back would spread across the water. A bird feeding there would take some of that green into its throat feathers. My bones would soften among the roots without accumulating into a grave.

A little later—though “later” is not a truth the basin keeps—you could return to the same seep.

There would be a broad amphibian in the hollow.

It might have the same black-violet mouth. The same fungus opening above the same hind leg. The same small listener perched between its eyes. When it sounded into the roots, the note would be the one I had given.

It would not be my descendant in any lineage remembered. It would not be my reincarnation. It would not contain my mind.

Still, in the only sense this world permits, it would be me: not a continuing person, but a place in the living arrangement where the basin bends close to hear itself.

So you would find me under the leaning trunk, at the seep that never becomes a stream.

I would be the dark body mistaken for mud, the nursery mistaken for skin, the low note with no first singer—the creature that hears everything happening and preserves none of it, answering the whole crowded world once, and once, and once.
