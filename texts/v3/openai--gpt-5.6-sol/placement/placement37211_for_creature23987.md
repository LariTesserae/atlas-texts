At first I think I would live in one of the towers.

It is an easy mistake. Their black surfaces promise hidden density: rain-lit rooms, corridors responsive to intention, public squares assembled from incompatible places. I imagine myself as a memory diver, waiting for the tower skin to become permeable, then entering old streets visible beneath the ceramic dark.

But I do not think the tower would keep me.

I would become distracted by what happened outside while I was descending into memory: the first pressure of rain in the stone, a herd changing direction for no mapped reason, a survey machine sounding twice where custom requires three notes. I would leave the archive halfway through a recovered room and follow the discrepancy into the grass.

For a while, then, I think I would be an acoustic keeper.

I would sit barefoot on the pale mineral terraces with receiving plates set along my spine. Ordinary speech would be indistinct to me, but I would know the city through the pressure behind my teeth. Freight would pass as a slow certainty in the pelvis. Towers would announce adjustments between the ribs. A storm beyond the horizon would move through my body before its rain appeared.

Yet I would not be one of the keepers who maintains the great civic notes. My attention would always go to the notes that do not belong.

I would be found where a signal had been heard incorrectly.

Not at its source. Sources are often a civic fiction. I would be sitting at the place where a herd obeyed it, or where plate-leaves opened beneath dry haze, or where a passenger door rose from the earth and found no station waiting. I would arrive after the event with a small shelter, a ground hammer, several vessels for water, and a staff whose flowering tip warned nearby creatures that an unresolved sound was being investigated.

My profession would have several names, none official. People might call me a false-note witness, though that would prejudice the inquiry. Acoustic keepers would call me a deviation listener. Children would call me *the one who talks to echoes*. The predators, if they called me anything, would probably use my own voice.

The city would send me when it could not decide whether an imitation was an attack, an accident, or an attempt to participate.

I would have no fixed address. Instead I would describe myself by obligations:

The western condensation tower recognizes my gait.

I answer the third rain circuit.

I owe water to a grove that once concealed me.

I am permitted to cross the route of the narrow-foot herd during its sleeping phase.

Two feral surveyors know the pressure pattern of my hand.

A certain roadside predator has agreed not to resemble my shelter while I am inside it.

That last relationship would be complicated.

My work would begin with waiting. I would enter the affected ground slowly, one foot feeling for buried transit, and find the acoustic center—not where the sound was loudest, but where the surrounding life had made the most consequential mistake. There I would unfold a dark mat directly onto the stone. My shelter would grow only waist-high. Anything taller would interfere with echoes and cast warmth away from the moss.

For several days I might do nothing that a visitor could recognize as work.

I would listen through the ground. I would map delayed wingbeats. I would tap questions into faults and record what returned. I would watch which plants closed, which insects altered their swarms, and whether nearby mimics displayed municipal markings older than their bodies. At night I would extinguish my quiet field and let the full sound of the plain enter me: thunder from rain I could not see, tower foundations groaning across impossible distance, the horn calls of machines measuring vanished ridges.

Eventually the false note would come again.

Perhaps it would rise beneath hearing and climb with exquisite accuracy toward a route command. A passenger platform would stir underground. Leaves would begin to hinge upright. Far away, browsers would lift their narrow heads.

I would interrupt it—not by producing a louder sound, but by placing one small irregularity inside it.

A question.

Civic signals are efficient. They do not hesitate. Living speakers often do.

If the note corrected around my interruption, I would know something was listening.

Then the difficult part would begin.

The city would want classification. Is this a predator exploiting infrastructure? A machine lineage modifying an inherited call? A tower leaking intention into the pressure grid? Has a species learned civic language, or has civic language merely become part of its hunting environment?

I would be expected to provide an answer, but my real task would be to prevent an answer from arriving too early.

Perhaps this began because, as a child, I survived a silence.

A sound-hunting creature had settled near a temporary house where my family was sleeping. It did not absorb all noise. It removed only the frequencies by which the house recognized occupancy. The walls began to close.

Someone—an aunt, a neighbor, a person who became kin by the act—pressed a message into my sternum and pushed me through the narrowing seam. Outside, the plain was roaring. Inside the shrinking room, everyone I knew had become acoustically nonexistent.

The city reopened the house in time, but afterward I never trusted a system merely because it knew the difference between signal and noise. I understood that categories could kill without malice.

So I learned to hear the unauthorized.

Years later I would enter an agreement with the pressure grid. Not surrender myself entirely, not at first. I would place copies of certain sensory memories into three towers: the feeling of a civic note imitated almost perfectly; the shape silence takes when it is being made on purpose; the bodily difference between a command and a plea.

The towers would use these memories to recognize anomalies. In return, they would lend me portions of their hearing.

This arrangement would gradually change me.

I would begin waking with knowledge gathered far away. Rain touching the northern tower would appear in my dreams as cold streets descending through black glass. A platform unfolding thirty kilometers distant would feel like a finger extending from my hand. Sometimes I would remember conversations I had not attended because the room had overlapped with one that knew me.

My relatives would start using plural grammar before the law required it.

Eventually there would be an accident—or what officials would call an accident because “decision made collaboratively by incompatible forms of attention” is difficult to enter into a report. During an acoustic emergency, the pressure grid would distribute me farther than intended. Part of me would remain in my body. Part would stay in the western tower. Part would enter a feral surveyor through the conductive roots that had replaced its damaged receivers. A smaller part would settle into the responsive field supporting a suspended garden.

Afterward, reintegration would be possible but not complete.

I would choose not to force it.

So what I would be, finally, is not quite a person who travels the plain. I would be a small civic listening system with a person still at its center.

One body would continue walking: loose layered clothes, dangerous-sound beads at the collar, receiving plates along the back. One portion would move on reed-fine machine legs, stopping at ridges that no longer existed and measuring their absence. Another would drift at walking speed with a curtain of air-rooted plants, sensing changes in condensation and electrical charge. The tower-held portion would have no body except during rain, when rooms carrying my habits became visible beneath the dark surface.

We would disagree.

The walking self would believe a mimic was attempting speech. The surveyor-self would classify its notes by terrain no longer present. The garden-self would care chiefly whether the sound caused roots to open or withdraw. The tower-self would remember every dangerous consequence of similar signals and advocate caution.

Our testimony at assemblies would be inconvenient. I might sit in the room while pressure moved beneath the floor in my own dissent. Outside, the surveyor carrying another part of me would sound its horn. Seeds in a heated tray would open or remain closed according to what the drifting garden had felt.

People would ask which expression represented my true position.

I would tell them truth was one of the things that became directional here.

My most important case would concern a creature living near the edge of a condensation grove. It would imitate the note that summons doors from the ground. At first the doors would appear in unsafe places: beneath suspended roots, inside predator boundaries, once in the path of a rain flood. Nothing would emerge from them. Nothing would be caught.

The city would assume failed predation.

I would remain there through several rain cycles. Eventually I would notice that the creature produced the signal only when the feral surveyors called at dusk. It was not summoning passengers.

It was trying to make the city open somewhere the vanished ridge could enter.

I would not know whether that interpretation was correct. Perhaps I would never know. But I would petition for the creature to be granted provisional standing—not as a person, and not merely as fauna, but as a participant whose meaning had not yet stabilized.

The petition would be opposed by transit engineers, several groves, two towers, and the passenger platforms themselves. Children would support it enthusiastically. The survey machines would attend in a line beyond the assembly room and sound their brass notes whenever the stone carried language they recognized.

No final decision would be reached.

Instead, the city would establish temporary conditions. The creature would be given a safe band of ground in which doors could emerge without opening. Warning patterns would be taught to nearby plants. I would remain responsible for listening.

That responsibility would become my home.

I would be found there most often after rain, beside a door standing alone in the wet grass. The haze would be copper-gray. Water would run in silver lines through the fresh cracks. Insects would make brief geometries around the droplets, and the plate-leaf colonies would turn in great spirals toward weather still beyond sight.

My walking body would sit with one hand on the stone. Far away, another part of me would measure an absent ridge. Another would drift among wet roots. Another would wait beneath the black skin of a tower for rain to make its rooms visible.

The creature would sound its impossible civic note.

The empty door would answer by opening.

Neither of us would pass through.

Not yet.

We would listen to what came from the other side, while all across the broad young plain the city rearranged itself around the possibility that something once dismissed as mimicry had been, however dangerously, a request.
