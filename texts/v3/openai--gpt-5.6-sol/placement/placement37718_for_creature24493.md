At first I would think I was the water.

Not the water gathered visibly in the stone cups, but the portion held under tension: the film between liverwort and basalt, the cold stored in moss, the drop that swells at a fern tip and trembles through an entire engine cycle without falling. I would go wherever pressure sent me. A blast would shake me free; a root hair would take me in; warmth from a fissure would lift me as vapor, only for the cloud to return me a few inches away.

But water passes too easily through this place. It belongs everywhere and therefore cannot quite be said to live here.

Then I would think I was a vibration.

I would arrive from beneath, without body or visible cause. I would move through black plates of rock, become a shiver in the sedges, a ring widening through a moss cushion, a brief glitter of spores released into fog. I would know the hidden workings by their signatures: the long forced breath of fans, the climbing strain of an engine, the measured percussion of cutting heads, the singular blow that makes every held drop leap at once.

Yet vibration also leaves. It spends itself in leaves and cloud. Whatever I was would need to remain after the sound had gone.

So I would find myself lower down, under the apparent surface.

I would be in the wind-shadow of a newly fallen slab, where one edge rests against shattered stone and the other is lifted slightly by the crushed remains of a fern bed. There would be no room there for a hand. Only a dark, tapering interval: cold at its open lip, faintly warm where it intersects a mineral crack. Condensation would enter. Dust from the fractured slab would gather. The first black films would already have dulled the fresh face of the rock.

That narrow interval would be my country.

I would not be one species. At first I might be a fungal fan, translucent against the buried basalt, branching wherever the chemistry allowed and dying back where the metal concentration became too strong. Then shrub roots would enter from one side—red, fine, persistent—and sedge roots from the other, their tips repeatedly burned away by the fissure’s breath. Liverwort hairs would descend through the dust above. Moss would begin to seal the entrance, keeping the darkness wet.

In time it would become impossible to say which of these was me.

I would be the knot made among them: a small, dense treaty between root, fungus, decaying frond, mineral film, trapped water, and stone. Not the whole commonwealth, and not its mind. The cleft has no need of a mind. I would be more like a local faculty—one of the places where the living cover feels a change and alters its growth because of it.

The machinery would reach me as pressure before sound. Fine threads pressed against the walls of the fracture would register each tremor. With a sustained engine note, water would be squeezed from the moss overhead and seep down toward me. After a detonation, new surfaces would open in darkness: clean planes no root had touched, warm with released strain and rich in compounds that were nourishment at one concentration and poison at another. I would enter cautiously. A black film first, then fungal threads behind it, then the blind advance of roots.

My movement would be occupation.

Where the crack cooled, I would thicken. Where sulfur accumulated, I would withdraw and leave sealed nodules behind. I would take the dangerous metals into myself, not harmlessly but slowly, packing them into dark grains around which later tissue could grow. Sugars from the buried shrub would pass through me toward the fern crown. Water held by moss would reach a sedge whose own pocket had dried. A crushed frond would cease to be frond and become depth, heat, acidity, then food.

There would be no innocence in this exchange. If the shrub weakened, I might consume the cells I had helped sustain. If a fern returned strongly, its roots might displace part of me from the richest seam. Partnership and feeding would alternate without betrayal because there had never been a promise—only continuance.

Above me, the cleft would appear to have no stable center. Beneath the slab, however, I would make a temporary one.

Not a safe place. Not a permanent place. A fastening.

The slab would settle by imperceptible degrees. Frost would open its outer edge; warmth would widen the inner crack. Every tremor would grind a little rock flour from the contact points. My threads and the root hairs intertwined with them would catch that flour. Dead tissue would bind it. Water would arrange it. What began as an empty gap would become a seam of black soil.

Perhaps years later the slab would tilt again. Light—gray, depthless, almost without direction—would enter a chamber that had never known it. Air would dry my outermost filaments. Some would collapse at once. The moss seal would tear. A fern crown would be exposed and crushed by the shifting weight.

I would not survive as a complete thing.

But completion would never have been my form.

One portion of the buried shrub would remain rooted beyond the slab. A fungal thread would persist deep in a hairline fracture. Spores shaken loose by the movement would settle on the newly exposed face. Water from the torn moss would carry bacteria into another seam. What had been one knot would become several margins.

Then I would find myself dispersed: in the bright green rim advancing over the fallen stone, in the red stem emerging from rubble, in the dark nodule left around a grain of metal, in the fern crown waiting below the damage. I would migrate without departure. I would be erased at the center and become more extensive at the edges.

If there were a moment in which I could be seen, it would come during the brief amber reflection from the far side of the ridge. The light would enter beneath the slab at a low angle. Water in the fungal web would shine. Root hairs, mineral crystals, and translucent threads would appear fused into a tiny architecture—less like a plant than frost, less like frost than a system of veins in stone.

Then the light would pass, and I would vanish back into function.

That is where I would be: not on the highest fern, not in the deepest roaring fissure, but at the moving boundary between fresh fracture and first soil.

And what I would be is the cleft’s scar tissue—not the scar after healing, but the act of scarring itself: opportunistic, communal, provisional. A soft fastening laid down inside an injury that cannot stop opening.

Below, the machines would continue to break the mountain.

Above, cloud and vegetation would continue to close it.

I would live exactly where those two labors meet.
