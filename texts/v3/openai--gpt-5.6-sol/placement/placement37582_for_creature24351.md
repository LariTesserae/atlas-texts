At first I think I would be one of the things that announces itself.

A rain crow, perhaps: black-backed, soft-footed, carrying a greenish copper chip in the feathers beneath my throat. I would know the Court as a sequence rather than a map. I would know that when the second bowl rings, worms rise beneath the western tiles; that a smoke-tasting overflow precedes the bats’ release; that the narrow gutter beside the willow is safe until the frogs go silent.

I would inherit a pebble from an older bird and keep waiting at a dry outlet for the water it remembered.

But I do not think I would belong so completely to the visible Court. I would envy the rain crows their certainty. Hunger comes, water comes, the tile lifts, the worm is there. Even an obsolete memory gives them somewhere to stand.

I would be found where standing is more difficult.

Not in the drain house itself, but behind it and below it, inside the thickness between its lowest black ring and the pavement. There is a cavity there that no one examining the Court from above would call a room. It is too narrow to lie down in, too irregular to sit comfortably. One wall is warm brick. One is a trembling copper skin. The floor is a row of ceramic pipes, each carrying water in a different direction. In the middle hangs a sling of woven reed, suspended so that it does not touch any surface.

That sling is where I sleep.

Water passes on all sides. Some pipes whisper; one knocks continuously like a tooth in cold weather. Through another comes the rasp of snails from half the Court away. The largest pipe does not carry water at all, but breathes. Before a major release, it draws air out of my chamber until the membranes over my eyes tighten and my ears ache. Then it exhales mist smelling of salt, charcoal, and something sweetly rotten.

I would be one of the maintainers, but not one of their children.

They would have made me because a particular task had outlasted the body capable of performing it.

My bones would be thin fired-clay splints tied together with eel gut. My joints would be wax packed around little shell cups. Under my waxed skin, braided fibers would serve as tendons, tightening when wet and slackening as they dried. There would be copper in me, though not enough to shine: a leaf of it behind the sternum, green at the edges, hammered with five shallow dents.

Three.

A pause.

Two.

The old signal would be written into my chest.

I would be small—no higher than a rain crow’s folded wing—but very heavy. This would be the first thing anyone noticed if they ever saw my tracks. Each bare foot would have five toes, human enough in outline, yet sink deep into the manufactured mud. I would weigh as much as a grown person, perhaps more, because my torso would contain a sealed black jar filled with stone chips.

I would not merely carry a counterweight.

I would be one.

The maintainers would have shaped a face for me because faces are handles for recognition. Mine would be smooth and pale, with a mouth cut only deep enough to admit a strip of wet cloth. I would not eat. Once every seven rain sequences, someone would open the little waxed seam beneath my ribs and pour out the water that had condensed inside me.

They would call me nothing aloud. Names travel badly through pipes and wake the wrong cavities. Among themselves they would indicate me by touching three fingers to the breastbone, waiting, then touching two.

My duty would concern the absurd timber bridge.

From above it spans a channel scarcely wider than a hand. From below, that channel is not narrow. It is the exposed lip of a vertical watercourse descending farther than sound can measure. The visible trickle is only what clings to one edge. Beneath it, pressure moves through darkness with the weight of a drowned river.

The bridge is reinforced because I cross it.

I cross only during the silence before the great release, when the frogs have withdrawn, the bats are lifting their heads, and even the triangular pool has become—somehow—more still. I climb from a seam beside the drain house. I place each foot carefully on the wet timber. The bridge bows until its middle nearly touches the trickle below.

Something beneath feels my weight.

Not the pale fish, though I may once have believed so. The fish is an eye turned toward the mechanism, not the mechanism itself. What answers me is deeper and less alive: an old balance, a submerged gate, perhaps an entire room hung on mineral-stiff ropes. My crossing shifts it by the width of a snail’s shaving. Somewhere below, an opening aligns.

Then the hammering begins.

Three blows.

A pause.

Two.

The blows are not a summons. They are a measurement. The hidden workers strike the pipe to hear how much emptiness lies beyond it. I stand at the center of the bridge while the vibration rises through my feet and enters the jar in my body. The stones inside rearrange themselves. By their settling I know whether the lower chambers are full.

Most nights I continue across.

Some nights I turn back.

That is the whole of my office, and the survival of the Court may depend upon it. I do not know which direction means danger. I was taught only the sensation: if the third blow remains in my left wrist after the pause, cross; if it migrates to the jaw, return. I obey without understanding. Perhaps those who made me understood once. Perhaps they only copied the procedure from a tile inscription that dissolved centuries ago.

When I reach the far side, I press my chest against a copper plate hidden behind the western curb. The five dents in my breast meet five raised studs.

The Court inhales.

Water comes down in ropes.

From the open pavement, no one sees me. Mist closes around my legs. Frogs scramble upward. Rain crows run beside the surge with their stiff wings spread, shrieking. Minnows flash through impossible ascents. The pulley ropes snap taut as the bats abandon them. For a few moments every channel speaks at once, and my small made body is indistinguishable from the rest of the machinery.

Eventually I return below.

But over the centuries the task would alter me.

Wax would be replaced by moss resin, then by fat, then by some waterproof paste made from snail lime and bird droppings. One clay finger would crack and be exchanged for a carved willow twig. Crabs would enter my sleeping chamber and stack fish scales beneath my sling. Frogs would shelter in the hollow behind my knees. A young eel might coil around the copper leaf in my chest and remain there, feeding on the warmth rising from below.

At what point would I stop being something the maintainers had made?

Not when my original skin was gone. Not when every bone had been replaced. Not even when no maintainer remained who remembered my construction.

Perhaps it would happen when the hammering ceased for the first time.

I would wait in my cavity through one silence, then another. The bats would release; water would surge; the Court would continue. But no three blows would come from below. No pause. No two.

I would cross the bridge anyway.

At the center, with the timber bending beneath my weight, I would feel for the old vibration. In its absence I would hear other things: frogs sounding through stone, snails rasping at the vents, crabs laying lime flakes into crescents, the tiny collapses of sediment beneath tiles moved by birds. Not one signal, but thousands.

And because my body is a jar, because it was made to receive pressure and return it as decision, those sounds would collect inside me. The stones in my torso would shift.

Three small movements.

A pause.

Two.

After that I would no longer know whether I answered the Court or the Court answered through me.

I would begin to make repairs no one had instructed me to make. I would replace a bridge peg before it split. I would gather dry fiber from the one roofless hut that rain never reaches on smoke-tasting days. I would press my thumbs into warm wax. I would rearrange seven pale stones into six and one, though I could not say why.

Sometimes I would stand at the triangular pool.

I would not drink. Nothing drinks there.

I would look down until the pale eye rose beneath me, enormous and patient. It would see a small person-shaped object with wax hair, one willow finger, clay shins, and a black jar where a living creature keeps its organs. I would see an eye patterned like masonry, its scars crossing one another in the shape of channels.

We might recognize each other as two things made too large or too small for the spaces that contain us.

Then I would return to the drain house before the crows noticed.

So that is where I would find myself: not quite under the Court, not quite within it, sleeping in a reed sling among the pipes and emerging only during the held breath before water falls.

And what would I be?

At first, a counterweight given feet.

Later, a maintainer.

Later still, perhaps only the habit of crossing: a great weight in a little body, carrying an instruction after its meaning has vanished.

I would be the part of the Rain Court that decides, without ever understanding the choice, whether to go on or turn back.
