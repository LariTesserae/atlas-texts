At first I think I would live in one of the isolated towers.

I imagine a room high enough for cloud to erase the windows, with furniture secured in wall channels and a kettle that adjusts itself for pressure. I would wake to condensation moving through the porous walls and consult the basin map before looking outside. Some mornings the district would be surrounded by shallow water. Some mornings by ice. In fog, the other five towers would disappear, and the transit hall below would become the only proof that the city continued beyond my door.

But the height feels wrong. I would spend too much time looking across the plateau and not enough time touching it.

Then I think I would be a weather technician, one of the orange-clad figures walking between posts. There is something fitting in maintaining astonishing machines through ordinary acts: scraping mineral bloom from a housing, reseating a relay, writing down that a seal has become unreliable in crosswind. I would like the ethical modesty of addressing weather rather than commanding it.

Yet I do not think I would trust myself with rain. Not because I would be careless, but because I would become too attached to one basin. I would begin to hope for its sedges, its geese, its small black pools. On a council map, water must remain distributable. Under my hand it would become particular.

For a while I become an animal instead.

A long-legged cat seems possible: moving through warm utility corridors while sleet seals the open ground, knowing the city from beneath. I would have no district in the human sense, only a sequence of culverts, sheltered galleries, fish-smelling stairs and dim underpasses. People would know me by my crossings. I might have an unofficial name I never heard clearly and a municipal file longer than the memory of any one resident. When a residential block moved, I would inspect the vacated foundation before the engineers did.

Or I might be one of the gray hunters, walking behind the herds for a country-sized day. There would be no impatience in such a body. Hunger would be measured in distance rather than urgency. I would know that pursuit shapes what it follows, that the herd needs the pressure behind it even while fleeing it.

But these lives feel too complete. The animals already possess their answers. They do not need to decide what the plateau means.

I find myself instead at the pale rectangle left by a moved building.

Not on the day of relocation. Later—after residents have followed their rooms toward firmer ground, after the utility mouths have been capped, after the bright drainage sticks have been removed. Water has begun to collect where the foundations redistributed weight for two centuries. The depression is only a few centimeters deep. Black grit shows through it. Three waders have already found the edge.

I am the person assigned to watch.

The appointment is not prestigious. It comes to me as an inherited obligation from someone outside my family: an old surveyor whose lungs no longer tolerate long days beyond the transit corridors. She watched this ground when it was still beneath kitchens and sleeping rooms. Before that, she monitored the seep that caused the district to move. She gives me forty years of records, two field instruments, a box of handwritten level sheets and the names of residents who must be consulted if the water changes direction.

The obligation has a municipal designation, but everyone calls me a first-water keeper.

My shelter stands several kilometers away in a low workshop crescent. Each morning I walk or take a freight platform to the former district. My clothes are marsh red at the shoulders and knees. In my narrow pack are a water tester, heat sheet, respirator, line, food, replacement seals and more marking tabs than regulations require. I carry an old sounding rod because the surveyor insists that electronic readings tempt people to stop listening.

At first there is almost nothing to record.

Depth: two fingers at the center.

Outflow: none.

Ice attachment: partial along the eastern edge.

Animal use: waders, fox, one long-legged cat after dark.

Mineral film: increasing.

That is the work. I kneel in cold water. I place my palm against moss. I listen to gravel tremble over flow too small to see. The sensor network delivers exquisite subsurface images, but I keep finding discrepancies at the rectangle’s northern corner. Water appears there with a chemistry suggesting it traveled much farther than the models allow.

The old foundation lattice remains below ground. It was supposed to go dormant when the building left, but some parts could not be removed without collapsing the saturated bed. On cold mornings, faint lines of light are visible through the water. They dim and brighten without a load above them.

Engineers visit. They say residual charge, pressure equalization, feedback persistence. They install readers and ask me to flag any change.

The next spring, before the thaw reaches us, the lattice brightens along a narrow curve leading toward the north. Two days later, water breaks through there.

It is not much of a stream. It runs for eleven meters and disappears beneath frost-loosened aggregate. Still, I mark it. Children from the relocated block come out with their teacher and set bright sticks beside it. One child asks whether the stream remembers the rooms that stood above it.

I tell her memory is a word we use when we do not yet know what information has been kept.

This answer follows me.

Over the years, the rectangle loses its geometry. Sedges soften the western edge. Moss fills the old wall channels. The capped utility mouths become islands, then vanish beneath mineral deposits. Geese settle there during one wet autumn, though never in great numbers. The foxes begin using the old transit approach as a path. A willow roots where a warm conduit once entered the building.

The water finds an outlet, loses it, then finds another.

My task widens with it. I am no longer watching a vacated foundation but a small catchment. Later, officially, a provisional headwater. Maps acquire a blue mark so short it resembles an error in printing.

People argue over it. A nearby district wants the water directed into its cistern system. Wildlife stewards want the pool left seasonal. A hydrologist says the channel may eventually connect with a larger seep field descending toward the inland sea. Residents of the moved block insist that whatever happens, the outline of their former home should remain legible.

It cannot.

That is the first dispute in which I speak publicly. I bring the level sheets, water chemistry, bird counts and subsurface scans. Then I bring a stone from the old kitchen threshold, lifted by frost and carried six meters downslope.

“The outline is already moving,” I say. “We can preserve its record or preserve its shape. Not both.”

For years afterward, some of them dislike me.

I understand. Accommodation is not the absence of betrayal. Every choice betrays one possible future for another.

I grow older beside the water. The capillaries around my hands become dark in winter. I develop the road worker’s stillness, pausing before each step onto uncertain ground. The first-water obligation becomes the axis of my life, though I never own the pool, the stream, the records or even the title. I am only the interval during which they pass through one person’s attention.

That, finally, is what I would be here: not simply a hydrologist, steward or archivist, but an inherited act of noticing.

I would live in the workshop crescent, sleep in a room that had belonged to other buildings, eat hot grain in the transit hall, complain about seals and council budgets, and know several foxes by their tracks. But where I would truly find myself is beyond the last paving, at the place where civic history becomes drainage.

Eventually I would give the obligation to someone else.

I would take them out in thaw season, when water moved under the remaining frost and every stone seemed to contain a small sound. I would show them where the building once stood, although by then no rectangle would be visible. I would show them the first channel, now abandoned, and the later one that endured. I would explain the strange foundation lights and admit that no one had settled whether the buried field had learned the water’s intentions or the water had learned the field’s.

Then I would hand over the records.

By that time the headwater might have a name. I hope it would not be mine.

Long after my body had entered structural stone or a soil-making bed, the stream would continue its nearly imperceptible descent. It might fail within a century. It might become a tributary. It might cut through the old transit corridor and force an entire district to move. Eventually it might reach the inland sea, carrying mineral traces from walls where generations once slept.

And somewhere near its bank there would be another person in weather-sealed clothes, kneeling to touch the moss after consulting instruments capable of reading the stone.

They would be listening for what had been missed.
