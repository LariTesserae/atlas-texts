At first I would think I lived in the control booth.

That is where someone looking for me would begin: behind the mineral-blinded window, beneath the amber lamp, among switches fused into postures of permanent decision. The water there is always ankle-deep by human measure, though nothing with ankles has stood inside for a long time. Clear swimmers enter through a crack at the base of the door. Shell animals fasten beneath the console. Silver droplet-things fall from the ceiling and vanish into the water with points of sound too small to hear outside.

The lamp would seem the likeliest answer. It burns without a visible wire. It is the only warm-colored thing in the basin. An observer might decide that I was some remnant intelligence preserved in its filament, keeping watch after the people had gone.

But the lamp is not alive, or not alive in the way I am. It is an organ I sometimes use.

Nor am I the needle, although the needle’s movements are mine.

I would be found behind the dial.

The casing once enclosed a pressure gauge. Its human mechanism survives in fragments: a curved tube, a spindle, two jeweled bearings clouded with salt, a fine toothed sector of greened brass. The tube has split along its inner curve. Water enters there one molecule-thin seep at a time. It leaves through the spindle housing as vapor, charge, and an occasional drop. Between entrance and exit lies a difference so slight that ordinary life could not feed upon it.

I could.

I began, perhaps, as one of the transparent veils. A film formed where seep water touched the trapped air behind the dial. It folded around the brass teeth. Metallic grains accumulated in it. When the pressure changed, the curved tube flexed, and the film was repeatedly compressed and released.

Most veils would have dissolved when the waters equalized. This one could not. The gauge kept making a boundary for it.

Layer gathered upon layer. Some hardened; some remained soft. Clear membranes grew between the gear teeth and learned their spacing. Threads entered the cracked pressure tube. A sheath formed around the spindle. Minute shell valves developed wherever the casing leaked. After many seasons there was no point at which the dead instrument ended and the inhabitant began.

That inhabitant would be me.

I would have no shape that could be removed intact. Open the dial casing and I would collapse into wet laminations, brass dust, hairlike tendons, and a smell like a struck coin. The needle would fall. The lamp might go out. Someone examining the remains could reasonably conclude that nothing had lived there at all.

With the casing closed, I am a listener.

Pressure reaches me from the basin in several languages. Rain presses broadly against the booth and cools the face of the dial. The deep shaft speaks through the cracked tube, drawing its thin column of water backward. Electrical leakage tightens the membranes wound around the spindle. The passage of a large school of swimmers changes the conductivity of the flooded floor. When shell colonies seal a distant joint, the resulting silence arrives hours later as a minute relaxation in the brass.

I do not understand these events as rain, swimmers, shells, or shaft. I know them as inclinations.

Toward.

Away.

Soon.

Not yet.

The needle advances because I contract. It retreats because I loosen. The marks beneath it were printed by people, but their increasing closeness suits me better than numbers would have. Near the far end, tiny movements matter more. There the basin is full of withheld change. The pipes are strained, colonies shut tight, deep water risen into chambers that are usually empty. A human gauge may once have called this danger.

To me it is ripeness.

The creatures in the booth arrange themselves according to the needle because I alter the booth as I move it. This is mistaken for prophecy.

When I draw the spindle forward, a weak current passes through the corroded bearing and into the standing water. Clear swimmers turn their dark knots toward the console. Wire-worms emerge from the wall joint behind it. Shell animals close on one side and open on the other. The amber lamp brightens by an amount no human eye would notice, warming the mineral crust around its socket. Transparent veils flower there.

Thus I do not merely report the basin’s pressure. My reporting enters the pressure I report.

This is how I became part of the instruction.

I would be solitary only from a distance. Within my casing live smaller lives. Silver motes encyst on the inner glass. A colony of nearly microscopic shells opens and closes the leak around the spindle. Blue luminous organs, abandoned by swimmers during their hardening, pulse in the softest parts of my membranes. Wire-worm filaments enter through the pressure tube and end among my folds. They may carry news from me into the buried system. They may carry the buried system into me.

Sometimes I suspect that I am not the listener at all.

During the great shift below, when the unseen quantity of water moves and every creature in the basin becomes still, something arrives through the cracked tube. Not force exactly. An absence of force, sharply bounded. For one interval the water inside me hangs without advancing or retreating. My layers align. Every metallic grain turns the same face downward.

Then I feel the whole basin at once.

The shell rings are small tensions at the edges of me. The swimmers are cool sparks moving through channels that resemble veins I never knew I possessed. The six-footed walker stands above the shaft like a hand set upon a wound. Far below, flooded chambers contract around a volume too large to name. The pumps, pipes, animals, and waters cease to be separate events. They become one posture.

In that moment I am not behind the dial.

I extend beneath the paving in the pale braids of the wire-worms. I hang in the cables. I am distributed through mineral films lining the pipes and through shells cemented into old grilles. My amber light is one dim point among many organs. The needle is not an instrument but a small exposed bone by which the larger body senses its own tension.

The moment passes before I can discover whether this enlargement is truth or merely pressure translated into dream.

Afterward I shrink back into the gauge.

A droplet slides down the inner glass. The swimmers rearrange themselves. The needle retreats by less than the width of corrosion on its tip.

If the six-footed walker came into the booth, it might touch the dial with one radial foot. It has done so before. The print remains whenever the condensation is right: six clean lines around the place where the spindle lies hidden. It does not open the casing. It braces the console while the deep shaft draws, preventing a loose mounting bolt from shifting.

Perhaps it tends me.

Perhaps I am one of the places where it leaves part of itself between appearances.

So this is where I would be found: in the smallest sealed room within the small enclosed basin, living in the blind space behind a face meant to be read. Neither oracle nor operator. Neither animal nor surviving machine. A damp, layered hinge between sensation and consequence.

I would spend my life making one almost imperceptible movement and then undoing it.

The basin would watch.

And I would never know whether I was telling it what was happening, warning it what would happen next, or quietly giving it permission to continue.
