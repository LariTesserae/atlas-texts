At first I think I would be found among the listeners, cheek pressed to a trunk, mistaking the city’s strains for my own thoughts.

But not in one of the great civic towers where chords are named and crews wait for instructions. I would live farther down, where blue has nearly become black, in a collapsed district at the rim of the central shaft.

The district was once vertical. Its main tower fell generations ago and rooted again across three terraces, so that its former upper chambers now lie sideways. Doors open beneath the feet. Windows face down into old root-felt. A corridor has become a long, low breathing chamber, the air held in a silver curve against what used to be a wall. Everyone still calls the place by its old name, though the name means something like *the seventh height*. No part of it has been high within living memory.

My sleeping chamber would be in a swollen knot near the fallen tower’s crown, just large enough to unroll in. I would share its air with two warmth bladders, a cage of blue larvae, and a genealogy stem trained around the room in a spiral. At night—or whatever passes for night at that depth—the larvae would make the living knots appear to move.

I would not be a builder exactly. I would be an opener entrusted with the last road.

The road ends at a wooden balcony projecting over the shaft. It once grew toward another tower on the opposite side, but no bridge was permitted to meet it. Its outer tips are cut back each year. Runners constantly try to turn downward. Pale vines fasten themselves to the rail. Seedlings lodge in the joins, and small crustaceans pack the hollow ends with shell grit. My work would be to prevent all of this from becoming a continuation.

Others spend their lives helping structures join. I would spend mine preserving a refusal.

I would prune the balcony so that it remains plainly a road and plainly unfinished. I would keep its terminating rails smooth enough to grasp. I would replace the weighted line that allows visitors to approach without drifting into the shaft. I would tend the empty loops tied for those who vanished there, removing algae without undoing the knots. If a loop began to rot, I would copy its twists into new fiber before the old one broke.

The office would have a title difficult to translate: not guardian, because the shaft cannot be guarded; not priest, because no agreement exists about what the shaft is. Perhaps **keeper of the unjoined tips**.

As a child I would dislike the place. I would envy those apprenticed to upper gardens, where bird feathers sometimes descend and rain can be heard as separate impacts. I would dare other children to swim beyond the guide-lines, then refuse my own dare. Once I would release a polished seed over the balcony and watch it diminish without seeming to fall. For years afterward I would expect someone to discover what I had done by listening to the district chord.

My punishment, when I finally confessed, would not be severe. An elder would give me a basket of identical seeds and make me learn the use of each one: which fed grazers, which sealed bladders, which could be carved into cord toggles, which took forty years to sprout. The lesson would not be that the shaft is sacred. It would be that curiosity paid for with something irreplaceable is merely waste.

Later I would become curious in the approved manner.

I would train as a listener. My sight would be poor even by the standards of the lower city, but the cartilage fans behind my jaw would be unusually broad. With my sternum against the fallen tower, I could distinguish a ray brushing the upper cables from a root splitting three terraces below. I would learn the old tower’s complicated voice: water passing through chambers that had rotated when it fell, tones bent by trapped air, ancestor bones, and graft scars.

My personal interval would be two close taps followed by a third after a pause—long enough that strangers sometimes assumed the message had ended.

People would joke that even my name contained an opening.

The shaft would alter everything I heard. Sounding trunks facing it would lose certain notes into the open water and return others after long delays. On quiet days I would tap the balcony rail and feel the response arrive through structures behind me rather than from the depth ahead. On storm days there would be no clear direction at all. The whole crater would tremble, and the shaft would seem less like an absence than a vast body holding still.

Then the knocking below would change.

Not dramatically. There would be no white brow rising from the root beds, no lidless eye. Only an interval where none had been before: four impacts, the last fainter than the others, repeating over many days. Some listeners would call it settling roots. Some would say there were now two armored creatures. The upper councils would ask whether deep construction should cease.

I would be the one nearest the pattern, so people would come to the fallen district to place their hands on my tower.

I would not tell them what the creature was. That would not be my skill. I would tell them that the fourth knock was not fainter because it was farther away. It was fainter because something between us had begun to split.

We would find the flaw in an ancient buried root layer beneath the district. Crews would reinforce the chambers above it, divert a warm seep, and abandon two lower lanes. One household would have to move. A nursery for dark grazers would be opened where their rooms had been. Nothing would be restored to its former plan. The city would bend around the warning.

After that, people would trust me with the silence as much as with the sounds.

In old age I would spend most of my time at the terminal balcony. My paddles would remain furled because I no longer crossed much open water. Tools would hang close against my body: pruning shell, tapping bone, fiber shears, three clips on every basket. A decayed bird feather would be braided into my hood cord from the one ascent I made as a young adult.

I would remember the surface chiefly as terror: the lake falling away from my face, wind touching all of me at once, valleys opening below like a second central shaft with no walls around it. Yet I would also remember seeing the pads from above. They had looked small enough to hold in my hands. For the first time I understood that the roofs of our breathing courts were leaves.

During the clear beam, I would fasten myself at the balcony and face inward with everyone else. The light would descend until the water ahead became a pale, immeasurable column. For a few minutes I could see the opposite districts. I could see unfinished bridges, air mirrors and hanging roads. Most strangely, I could see how narrow the shaft was compared with the city surrounding it.

It would still feel larger.

When I died, my body would not be given to the shaft. That temptation would be discussed and rejected. To lower the keeper of the unjoined tips into the central depth would make a claim about the place, and my life would have been spent avoiding claims that could not return.

My bones would instead be enclosed in the balcony’s sounding rail.

Gardeners would train new wood over the ribs and place one fan of jaw cartilage near a narrow water channel. As the trunk thickened, the balcony’s note would acquire a slight hesitation: two close pulses, then a third after an improbable pause.

The next keeper would learn to recognize it.

So perhaps, in the end, that is where I would most truly be found—not as the hooded figure people once saw pruning at the edge, but as an irregularity in the last road’s voice.

A small delay in the wood.

A note shaped around an absence.

Something the city must listen through in order to hear the dark beyond it.
