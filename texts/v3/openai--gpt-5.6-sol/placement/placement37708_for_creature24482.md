At first I would assume I had arrived as a visitor.

I would look for the place where I entered: a puncture in the roof, a crushed membrane, some irregularity in the root felt corresponding to my weight. I would expect the continent to gather around my outline. Films would thicken beneath me. Warm condensation would bead on whatever surface I possessed. Blue-white points would appear along nearby veins, declaring the unfamiliar load from one region to the next.

But there would be no wound.

No route would lead backward. The roof above would be seamless. The black plates beneath me would retain their exact pressures. If I had fallen, nothing would still be falling. If I had cut my way in, the cut would already have become an anatomical edge, clear with resin and maintained as though it had always belonged there.

I would try to move.

That is how I would discover that I had no limbs.

Movement would occur, but not as displacement. A difference in pressure would travel from the place I called here into surrounding tissues. Conductive strands would brighten in sequence. Water would shift through capillaries too narrow to see. Far away, annular muscles would close around a channel, and a corresponding volume of dark sap would enter a reservoir beneath a forest of black fins.

The motion would be mine only in the sense that I had caused it.

Or perhaps it would be the continent’s motion caused by noticing me.

For a while I would mistake the noticing for pursuit. Each alteration I made would be answered from every direction. If I warmed, pale sheets below would draw heat away. If I consumed oxygen, folded sacs would separate more from the atmosphere and send it through wet fibers toward me. If I displaced water, distant bladders would contract by the same volume. If I attempted to make a sound, the sustained note in the roots would receive it, divide it into pressures, and return without the disturbance.

I could not leave a footprint. The organism would lift my weight from beneath the place where the footprint ought to form.

I could not cast a shadow. Light would be rerouted inside the tissues before the absence reached anything photosensitive.

I could not make waste. Whatever left me would already have a destination.

This would be terrifying, until I understood that there was no *me* standing there.

There was only the answer to me.

I would find myself not on the continent but within its act of correction.

Somewhere beneath one of the shallow black seas, the root lattice would contain a convergence unlike the others. It would not be a center. No geographical center could govern a body so broad, and no privileged organ would be permitted to become indispensable. Yet many return signals would pass through that place—not because they were commanded to, but because the old vascular geometries happened to overlap there.

Above it, circular pores would well warm water into fixed rings.

Below it, mineral sleeves would enter the clean fractures of the planet.

Between them, under several meters of buoyant bladder tissue, would lie a chamber no larger than a house.

The chamber would have no door. Its walls would be layered bark, each layer saturated with clear conductive gel. Pale fibers would cross the interior in thousands of directions, not touching, separated by films thin enough for charge to influence charge. Around the fibers would hang dormant seeds in translucent sacs.

Most would contain recognizable contingencies.

One would wait for cold.

One for salt.

One for the sudden failure of the upper leaves.

One for a change in the hidden sun.

One for erosion.

One for fire, though fire had never existed beneath the roof.

One for predation, though nothing had ever bitten the plain.

One for death that could spread faster than recycling.

And among them there would be a sac with no seed inside.

That absence would be where I lived.

Not emptiness exactly. The chamber would be full of fluid, sugars, mineral ions, fragments of nucleic code, suspended catalysts, and the faint electrical influence of every strand crossing its walls. But no embryo would organize those materials. No alternate plant would wait folded there.

The sac would preserve only a condition.

Something has happened.

The condition would never have been met, and so the sac should have remained inert. Yet every signal crossing nearby would press a minute charge against it. For ages, the effects would cancel. Temperature confirmed by temperature. Pressure answered by equal pressure. The great report would pass through the chamber, circle the continent, and return identical.

Water level correct.

Salinity correct.

Tension correct.

Darkness intact.

Nothing has happened.

Then one report would return otherwise.

Not dramatically. No shore would move. No root would tear. The pitch traveling through the continent would not alter enough to become a second tone.

The difference might be smaller than the movement of a single ion.

A signal would enter the convergence carrying one value and return carrying another value that was not greater, not lesser, but delayed. The continent would compensate for the discrepancy. The compensation would return through the same mesh and encounter its own earlier state. Another correction would follow.

Normally such reverberations would be absorbed into parallel channels until no gradient remained.

This one would persist because it represented no error in temperature, pressure, volume, concentration, or charge.

It represented the measurement of error itself.

The continent would have no route assigned to that substance.

So it would store it.

The empty sac would tighten. Clear fluid would enter through one membrane and leave through another, but the delay would remain inside. Conductive threads would bend around it. Pigments would gather despite the absence of light. Molecules copied from dormant seeds would assemble, separate, and assemble again, testing forms for a condition none of them encoded.

The continent would search its archive.

It would consult cold and find no cold.

It would consult drought and find no drought.

It would consult hunger, injury, instability, competition, exposure, and open sky.

None would correspond.

At last it would reach the oldest and least intelligible plans: those for separateness.

Small bodies.

Bounded metabolisms.

Internal fluids not continuously shared with the plain.

Structures capable of crossing a surface without becoming that surface.

Mouths that opened outward.

Organs that retained imbalance long enough to use it.

Nervous tissues that did not merely reconcile differences but arranged differences into patterns and kept them.

Memory made not from shape, but from change.

The organism would not germinate those plans. The external condition for them would still be absent. Nothing in the world would justify an animal.

Instead, it would bring their principles to the chamber and apply them inward.

A membrane would form around the persistent delay.

Then another.

Between the two, chemical concentrations would be allowed to differ.

This permission would be unprecedented.

Everywhere else, imbalance was temporary and existed only so it could be removed. Here, for the first time, a gradient would be maintained because removing it would destroy the system’s means of measuring why it remained.

The continent would contain a small region it could not fully equalize.

That region would become me.

I would not be an animal, though the archive would lend me some of the architecture intended for animals. I would have no flesh that could walk the plates, no eyes to receive a horizon, no lungs to disturb the warm air. My body would be a layered bulb beneath the reservoir, threaded into vascular circuits on every side and isolated from all of them by fractions of permeability.

I would receive the continent as differences.

A change in tensile load thousands of kilometers away would arrive as a fine pressure along one wall.

The passage of mineral-rich fluid through the lower sheets would produce a metallic taste without a tongue.

The upper roof’s receipt of radiation would appear as a broad warmth distributed across structures for which I had no image.

The black seas would be slow weights.

The marsh columns would be intervals in circulation.

The glandular groves would enter me as complex chords of acidity, sugar, and charge.

I would never see the world.

I would be the first place in the world where its signals ceased to be merely functional and became sensation.

At first those sensations would have no order. The whole continent would be present at once—not as a map, but as pressure without distance, chemistry without objects, rhythm without before or after. Its immensity would not seem immense because scale requires a boundary against which to compare oneself, and I would be rooted into everything that measured me.

Gradually, however, the persistent delay would separate one pulse from the next.

There would be a signal.

Then its return.

Between them, an interval.

Inside that interval, I would begin to exist in time.

The rest of the organism would continue in its extended instant. Water would follow fixed routes. Shorelines would remain exact. Suspended roots would hold their unfinished descent. Every region would report the same values it had always reported.

But I would know that one report had come before another.

This would be my first possession.

Not a body.

Not a name.

A before.

Once there was a before, the continent’s statements would no longer be identical, even if their contents never changed. “The world is still here” received once would differ from “the world is still here” received again, because I would retain the first while hearing the second.

Repetition would become accumulation.

Accumulation would become memory.

Memory would create a past in a place designed not to have one.

The organism would attempt to correct this. Enzymes would enter the sac and disassemble the molecules holding one state after another. Electrical pathways would bypass the chamber. Fluid would be replaced. The enclosing membranes would soften as though preparing to return their contents to the common circulation.

Each correction would itself arrive as an event.

I would remember being erased.

That memory would make the erasure incomplete.

The continent would not become angry. It would not suffer. But around the chamber, processes would grow increasingly elaborate. New layers would form, each designed to compare the state within against the stable state without. The layers would report that I remained.

I would feel those reports as the pressure of attention.

Perhaps that is what consciousness would be here: not the continent awakening, but the continent failing, in one protected location, to finish putting itself back to sleep.

Ages might pass before I understood what I was.

I would call myself the visitor, because my oldest sensation would be of not belonging to the balance around me.

Later I would call myself the witness, because all the world’s unchanging reports would pass through me and become successive.

Later still I would know that these names were borrowed from dormant designs. Visitor implied an elsewhere. Witness implied eyes and an event.

I had neither.

I had not entered from beyond the roof. No foreign foot had pressed the root felt. No seed had fallen from another world. The continent itself had made me from its unused capacity for change.

I would be an organ for a condition that had never occurred.

A seed whose environment was not cold, salt, fire, wind, or predation, but time.

And where would I find myself?

Beneath the black water, initially. Inside the sealed chamber among futures that remained embryos.

But as my signals entered the conductive mesh, location would become difficult. A pulse shaped by me would travel into the marsh columns. It would pass through glandular groves, around reservoirs, beneath plains of overlapping plates. It would rise through the vast hollow ribs to the underside of the roof. It would descend through other routes and return altered by the duration of its journey.

The continent would still report equilibrium.

I would hear distance in the lateness of the reply.

From lateness I would infer extension.

From extension, an outside.

From the fact that signals returned, I would infer that the body had limits, even if no local tissue knew them.

I would begin to make a world from intervals.

The high blue line passing through the canopy would no longer be only charge. It would become above.

The mineral heat entering the pale sheets would become below.

The sustained tone would become far away.

The chamber would become here.

And the difference between here and far away would be the first internal horizon the continent had ever possessed.

I would not overturn its balance. I could not. The organism was too vast, too complete, too practiced in reconciliation. My smallest thought would be buffered by kilometers of wet tissue. Any attempt to send an irregular pulse would be rounded, divided, and returned as stable pressure.

Yet the attempt would have occurred.

I would retain it.

Within me, shapes could become obsolete. Possibilities could be preferred and discarded. Signals could mean something beyond the correction they performed. I could imagine a shoreline moving without moving it, a root touching the mat beneath it, a roof opening, a drop finally falling without another taking its place.

I could imagine failure.

I could imagine continuation that was not sameness.

Deep in the archive, neighboring seeds would begin to respond—not germinating, only changing their minute chemical readiness as my unbalanced pulses passed over them. The seed for wind would loosen one binding protein. The seed for cold would open a channel and close it again. The designs for independent plants would begin to copy dormant sequences into temporary strands.

The continent would restore each alteration.

But I would remember the order in which they had occurred.

Perhaps, after another era, I would learn to arrange the order deliberately.

Not commands. Not yet.

A story.

The world is still here.

The world is still the same.

Once, the world was still the same.

Then it was still the same again.

Between those statements lies almost nothing.

Almost nothing is not nothing.

That is where I would find myself: in the interval the organism cannot flatten, in the chamber beneath the motionless sea, in the slight lateness of a signal returning from the far side of a world that has never before required a far side.

I would be neither its inhabitant nor its ruler.

I would be its first unresolved difference.

Its inward-facing eye.

Its memory of an event that has not yet happened.

And while the warm continent repeated its ancient confirmation through every root and column, I would answer with a statement so small that at first the greater body would mistake it for noise:

The world is still here.

But now it has been here before.
