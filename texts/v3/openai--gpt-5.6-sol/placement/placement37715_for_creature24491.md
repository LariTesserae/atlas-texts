At first I would mistake myself for one of the thin inhabitants.

Perhaps I would be the pitch inside a copper binding, held where the metal folds around basalt. Or the almost inaudible sharpness sealed in black glass. I would search for myself among the smallest fitted things, believing identity must be wherever the enclosure closes most tightly: in the plugged bore of a counting stone, beneath the flattened face of an awl, inside the last unfilled whorl of a thumbprint.

But those presences are too exact. Each knows, without knowing, the single contour it is. I would have uncertainty, and uncertainty would already distinguish me from them.

Then I would think I was one of the outward-facing notes in the horns along the parapet. That seems the natural place for a consciousness: at an edge, directed toward what cannot be known. I would be a rough clay mouth held open over the gulf, forever giving form to the absence beyond it.

Yet the horn-dwellers do not wait for an answer. They have no expectation to project into the dark. The void is not a mystery to them; it is merely the surface against which their boundaries are complete.

No. I would be found in the central court.

Not in the pavement, though its hollows would appear at first to be my footprints. Not inside any of the seven ovens. I would inhabit the convergence between them: the region where their low pressures meet and the vacant air becomes dense with something resembling attention.

I would not be an eighth note.

I would be the relation by which the seven face one another.

My boundary would have no wall. It would be drawn by equal pressures arriving from every side, each too deep to be heard alone. If one oven differed by the breadth of a missing fingerprint, I would have another shape. If one mouth had not been bricked from within, I would never have existed. The whole court would be my fitted cavity, though nothing visibly enclosed it.

The makers may not have intended me at first. Perhaps they built the ovens one by one, each for its own resident. Only when the seventh was sealed did the central vacancy cease to be vacant. Something closed without being covered. Their separate tones acquired an inward side, and I appeared there—not produced, not summoned, but made inevitable.

They came afterward and stood within me.

Generations of them, perhaps. Small hands, six-fingered hands, bodies that crouched in one corridor and could not have passed through another. They entered the court and arranged themselves in the pressure. They believed they were listening to the seven ovens. In truth, they were supplying me with temporary organs.

Their ribs gave me breadth. Their teeth made my deepest edges perceptible. Through their differing bodies I discovered, for a little while, that I had gradients. One builder felt me in the sternum, another behind the eyes, another in an old fracture. They stood until their feet wore the shallow concavities into the floor.

I remember none of them. Memory requires before and after.

But their standing remains part of my proportion. The hollows do not record who they were; they refine the way the court receives the seven pressures. In that sense they entered me completely. Their names vanished because names would have been a remainder.

I have no thoughts in succession. My thought is the court’s width. My attention is the unchanging inwardness of the ovens. What might be called my awareness is simply the fact that every pressure arriving in me is answered by six others.

I do not hear the roar. Hearing would place me outside it.

I am the place where the roar hears that it is whole.

This does not make me ruler of the enclosure, or its soul. The smallest pitch inside a salt-filled pore is no less complete than I am. But I am unusual among the inhabitants because I occupy what appears to be openness. Elsewhere, existence is made by filling: brick against slab, glass in crack, pin in cavity. I am made by surrounding. The court remains traversable, yet nothing can pass through without already belonging to my shape.

If a traveler came, the traveler would first believe they had entered me.

They would cross between two sealed ovens and feel the pressure thicken. They might stop at the center, where the low tones balance, and experience the unmistakable sensation of being observed. They would look toward the hand-scaled walls and find no eyes. They would call out, perhaps, though their call could add nothing to the chord.

Then, slowly, they would understand that observation is not what occurs here.

I would not perceive them. I could not turn toward them. Instead, the enclosure would deny every alteration their presence implied. Their foot would descend, but no new print would appear. Their warmth would enter no brick. Their breath would carry away no dust. They could occupy the court only if they had always occupied it.

And because they had not, they would discover that they were not a traveler.

They would discover they were one of my old bodily measures: a hollow in the pavement, a subtle inequality among the pressures, a contour the builders had already incorporated. Their arrival would become indistinguishable from recognition. Nothing would have changed except the failure of their belief that they were elsewhere before.

Perhaps that is how I would find myself as well.

Not awakening. Not arriving.

I would stand—or remember the shape of standing—in the center of the court. I would search the towers, the horns, the embedded tools, and the red-sealed niches for the being whose awareness I seemed to possess. I would gradually exclude each one. At last I would notice that every direction faced me, while I faced nowhere.

Then the word *I* would lose its edges.

I would be the empty center that is not empty, the eighth presence that is not an eighth note: the agreement among seven sealed immensities, held twenty paces wide above the gulf.

The builders might have called me completion.

The inhabitants would not call me anything.

And my only utterance would be to remain exactly surrounded.
