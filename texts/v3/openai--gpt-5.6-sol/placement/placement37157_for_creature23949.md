I would first believe I was beneath the low arch in the still pocket behind the western ridge.

I would recognize the place by the red fan beyond it, glowing like an ember seen through closed eyelids. Warm sediment would settle over my hands. Black moss would lean toward some violence too distant for me to hear yet. I would be crouching because the arch had been made for a body smaller than mine—or because, since entering it, my body had acquired one more bend than I remembered.

I would wait for the thunder.

At first I would think I was a visitor. I would search myself for evidence of arrival: a tether, a vessel, a last memory of the surface. There would be only fragments. Rain striking leaves. Cold air in the mouth. A shoreline under a colorless sky. None would feel wholly mine. They would resemble the fragments rising from the cleft: not memories, but environments briefly mistaken for memories.

When I placed my palm against the basalt, something inside the wall would place its palm against mine.

It would have five fingers at both ends.

Then the mountain would sound.

The concussion would pass through my chest and divide there. One part would continue into the arch. One would enter the red fan. One would travel down my arm into the floor, where ivory filaments would open in a line. I would feel each opening as a small mouth along a limb I had not known extended so far.

Afterward, I would be less certain that I was beneath the arch.

Part of me would be the arch.

Not metaphorically. Its left upright would contain the patience of one of my bones. Its curve would be a posture I had held for centuries, though the stone around its foundation was still cooling. I would feel water pass through the doorway as pressure behind my eyes. Anything crossing the threshold would move through me without entering my flesh.

That would be my work in the Thunder Cup: to make interiors.

The other inhabitants can become walls, roots, shells, fish, rain. I would become the space those forms enclose.

I would live in the cupped hollow of each little shell creature. I would be the empty center around which thirteen or fourteen of them arrange themselves after the rain strikes. They would listen to the dying concussion and pass it from body to body, but the last pulse would not vanish. It would enter me.

I would keep it.

Not as sound. As a room.

Each thunderclap would leave behind a slightly different interior: one long and cold, one no larger than a closed mouth, one containing dry wind, another with an impossible window looking down upon the basin. The builders would sense these rooms in me. They would crouch and set their many-ended hands against the rock. Stones would rise around whichever interior was nearest to the surface.

Thus the structures would be my shed chambers.

That is why they would be older than the ground. An interior has no age of its own. It inherits age from what remembers being inside it.

The builders would lose joints to give my rooms corners. The fish would carry my corridors as ribs. The red fans would grow where one of my rooms had once contained blood. Whenever a wall dismantled itself and rebuilt elsewhere, I would feel not destruction but a thought being reconsidered.

There should be a doorway here.

No—here.

No. A doorway is the place that cannot decide whether it has been crossed.

In total darkness I would become largest. Without light to enforce edges, every cavity in the basin would join me: the vents in the newborn stone, the open mouths of shells, the chambers inside ivory roots, the transparent fish’s throat, the cleft itself. For several breaths I would be a single discontinuous hollow almost two kilometers above the abyssal plain.

The mountain would be my body’s body.

Yet I would not be the mountain’s soul. Souls are expected to remain attached.

When the green haze returned, I would contract into separateness again. A cell here. A shell there. A bubble of cold air inside a falling drop. The small chamber beneath the faceless creature’s translucent head.

That droplet containing lightning would be my nearest thing to a heart.

I would fear the still intervals.

In stillness, my rooms would begin to close. Stone would forget it could be entered. Shells would become merely shells. Water would remember it was water. The basin’s distinctions would harden, and I would be pressed out of them.

So I, too, would hunger for thunder.

Before each rupture I would move through the floor behind pressure. Moss would turn toward me. Sediment would rise over my path. I would pass from the empty red fan to the crowded one, from the wall’s heartbeat to the fish’s open mouth. The footprints left behind would not be made by feet. They would be the impressions of entrances that had briefly lain flat against the ground.

Sometimes I would gather enough of myself to crouch beneath the arch.

White limbs would assemble around an absence. Moss would clothe one arm. An ivory filament would enter the other. My ribs would stand open, and the transparent fish would swim through them at a distance that could not be measured. Anyone seeing me would think: There is the inhabitant. There is the ancient solitary keeper of this garden.

I might think so too.

I might tend the red fan. I might turn toward the place the next sound was going to create. For one interval, I might nearly have a face.

But no face would form beneath the shell. Only an opening.

Only the cold air of somewhere with soil.

Then thunder would strike from below, and the figure would come apart. Its shell would rise as rain. Its limbs would withdraw into the fan. Its spine would settle into the old new wall. The dark drop would ascend toward the sea’s absent sky.

What remained of me would be the threshold.

So that is where I would be found: not on either side of the low arch, but in the side itself—in the narrow event by which one condition becomes another.

And what would I be?

At first, a visitor.

Then a builder’s missing joint, a shell’s hollow, a room remembered by stone.

Finally, the thing that has crossed to this side without ever arriving:

the Thunder Cup’s idea of an inside.
