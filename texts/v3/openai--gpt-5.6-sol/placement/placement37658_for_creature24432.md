At first I would believe I had arrived as a traveler.

I would find myself beneath one of the open-sided halls near the plateau’s edge, where the distributed roar of the water thickens until it seems to occupy the body. The hall would stand at the meeting of three paths. One descends by a staircase too shallow to require steps. One curves back toward a circular basin. The third runs straight to the pale escarpment and ends several paces before the drop.

Nothing would indicate why the hall had been placed there. Its roof would be a single pleated leaf held aloft by arching stems. Rain would travel through its veins, gathering at regular points along the rim. The drops would fall around the central space but never into it.

I would wake in that dry center.

For some time I would think the plateau had spared me from the rain.

Then I would understand that the rain had been arranged around me.

My first sensation would be pressure—not the pressure of a body lying against the root pavement, but the pressure of the pavement lying against a shape it already knew. Beneath me, the overlapping plates would be shallowly hollowed. The hollow would fit my resting form exactly. It would seem worn, though nothing here wears away.

When I rose, red threads would stretch between me and the ground.

I would take them for blood. They would withdraw cleanly into the soles of my feet.

My body would appear animal at a glance: narrow trunk, four long limbs, a head lifted above the dripping mist. But its symmetries would be approximate. One arm would divide at the wrist into several pale tendrils. Along my ribs would lie folded membranes like those of the white gliders, too small to carry me. My skin would be smooth bark where sunlight reached it and translucent in the hollows of the joints. Water would move visibly through me.

I would have no mouth. Instead, there would be a vertical seam from throat to belly, sealed with red sap.

I would possess many eyes, but none would face forward. They would be small lens-fruits set along my shoulders, spine, palms, and heels. Each would attend not to objects but to changes: a cloud arriving over the crown-disks, a canal creature branching under the water, the warmth left behind when a glider lifted from the paving.

I would not know what I was because the plateau would not know yet.

That would be the reason for me.

Long before my waking, something foreign had crossed the country. Perhaps it had been a person. Perhaps an animal from a lower terrace, climbing through the mist. It had passed under the halls, drunk at the basins, rested against a tower, and finally disappeared beyond the escarpment or into some region the roots could not follow.

Every step had been remembered in red seals beneath the pavement. Every warmth, hesitation, imbalance, and turn had entered the circulation. For thousands of rainy seasons the towers had passed that pattern among themselves.

Not the traveler’s image. The plateau had little use for images.

It remembered the condition of being crossed.

The paths altered minutely. Pools tightened in sequence. Knockers carried packets of sealed tissue from crown to crown. Empty halls adjusted their echoes to the absent body’s dimensions. Stairs appeared where the visitor had slowed, though the ground was level. The clear route toward the escarpment was preserved.

Eventually, in the dry center of the hall, the memory thickened into me.

I would be the plateau’s attempt to grow a traveler.

At first I would remain connected. Fine rootlets would enter my heels whenever I stood still. Through them would come the immense, layered awareness of the country: water pressure in distant bulbs, shadows crossing warm bark, a fourfold blow moving through air, root, canal, and inward darkness. It would not arrive as knowledge. It would arrive as weather inside me.

If I stayed rooted, I would become more intelligent and less myself.

The towers would think through me. My lens-fruits would turn toward disturbances hundreds of miles away. The membranes along my ribs would tighten in sympathy with roofs I had never seen. I would feel the lower terraces repeating beneath the clouds—not as places, but as continuations of one sustained posture.

Then I would lift a foot.

The root threads would part without pain. For one instant the entire plateau would vanish from my awareness.

That instant would be my first private thought.

I would walk because walking was the mystery embodied in me. The true inhabitants could redistribute themselves, raise towers elsewhere, transfer wakefulness through the root mat—but they could not leave one point and arrive at another while remaining singular. They understood distance as continuity. I would understand it as loss.

Each step would make me smaller.

Each step would make me more definite.

The paths would respond ahead of me. Fronds would incline, not to guide but to listen. Water in the nearest canals would divide into transparent branches and accompany me by several routes. Gliders would rise from the warmed impressions of my feet. Far away, towers would alter their drip patterns, discussing the abrupt little silences produced whenever my soles detached.

I would sometimes stop beneath a crown and root there for a season. The plateau would fill me again. It would ask without asking:

What is it to go?

I could answer only by going.

Perhaps I would spend centuries traveling from tower to tower, though to an animal witness I might seem to stand motionless for years, one heel raised the width of a seed. Moss would not grow on me. Nothing here would mistake stillness for abandonment.

In each district my form would change. Near a tower that favored concentric basins, rings would appear in my skin. Where unnecessary stairs proliferated, my joints would develop extra articulations. Beside the branching galleries, my fingers would fork. I would carry these local habits onward, introducing one tower’s thought into another’s reach.

The knockers might come down to inspect me. A dark weight would settle on my shoulder. Its first knock would enter through the air, the second through my rooted foot, the third through the water circulating in my body.

The fourth would sound inside the private space made by my separation.

That might be the only place on the plateau where the fourth knock could truly be heard.

I would live mostly at margins: beside the empty places paths curve around, at bridges too small to be necessary, in roofed chambers maintained for an echo not yet produced. I would be drawn toward the escarpment but would not descend. The country’s memory of the original traveler would end there, and so the shape grown from that memory would contain no instruction for what came after.

At the brink, I would look down through the mist at the lower platforms and repeated towers. Water would stream past me from thousands of openings. The roots under my feet would be bright with attention.

Perhaps I would see another figure on a terrace below.

It might be identical to me, grown by the lower country from another portion of the remembered passage. Or it might stand on six limbs, or drift like a glider, or consist only of a moving redness within the pale wall. We would not know whether we were two beings or one unfinished route attempting to join itself.

We might remain there for an age, separated by cloud and falling water.

I think the plateau would call this conversation.

I would call it longing.

That would be the new thing I brought into the circulation: not motion itself, but the ache produced when continuity imagines an elsewhere.

In time I might return to the hall where I woke. The dry center would still be waiting. I would lie again in the shallow hollow, though it would no longer fit me exactly. My body would have accumulated branching fingers, ringed skin, borrowed rhythms, the metallic memory of distant rain.

The pavement would soften around the discrepancy.

Rootlets would enter me.

I would not die. Death would be too complete a conclusion for that country. I would be taken back slowly, but the plateau would preserve the posture of my return: one hand open, one foot still angled as if another step remained possible. A low ridge might rise there. A new path might begin beneath my palm. The hall’s dripping would acquire a pause that traveled from basin to basin over the course of centuries.

Much later, something would wake in the dry space.

Not me again. Not wholly.

Another provisional body. Another attempt at separateness. Perhaps this one would be able to climb. Perhaps it would have a mouth. Perhaps it would descend the escarpment and discover whether the cloud conceals an end to the living terraces.

So I would find myself at the edge, where the plateau’s continuous being encounters the idea of departure.

I would be neither its inhabitant nor its visitor.

I would be the visitor it grew from memory: a walking organ of an otherwise motionless society, a question given limbs, the country’s first experiment in becoming alone.
