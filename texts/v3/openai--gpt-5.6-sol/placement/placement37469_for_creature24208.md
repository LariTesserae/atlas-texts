At first I would mistake myself for one of the things that moves freely.

I would look upward and imagine I belonged among the vacuum chains suspended between towers, turning so slowly that an entire district could grow accustomed to my shadow before I drifted on. Or I would choose the reservoir depths, becoming some broad-backed animal whose smallest muscular decision redraws streets. There is grandeur in being mistaken for geography.

Then I would think I was one of the pale stress-swarms, gathering wherever stone is about to fail. I would sing walls soft. I would enter cracks too fine for any tool and persuade fracture to become a door.

But these are choices made by someone still imagining that a being here is a bounded shape.

The basin would not let me remain so simple.

You would find me in a district called, inadequately, the Nearward Inclination. It occupies the flank of a reservoir creature beneath the eastern rim of the Unfinished Hollow. The creature has no name separate from the district built upon it. Its back rises under six towers, a field of tuning instruments, two folded cisterns, and a maze of streets that descend no matter which direction they are walked.

Once every few generations, the district tilts toward the Hollow.

No one agrees whether this is because the reservoir creature turns in its sleep, because the Hollow draws on the compacted water beneath it, or because the local direction of falling remembers an older arrangement. Each explanation has its own attendants, and each set of attendants maintains different parts of the same machinery.

My rooms would lie beneath the lowest causeway, where the causeway enters the mountain but has not yet decided whether it is bridge or tunnel. The entrance would be submerged to the height of one supporting limb. Its door would recognize me only after I had leaned against it for several minutes and allowed my coat to exchange vibrations with the animals nested in the frame.

Inside is a chamber preserving an obsolete direction of gravity.

Down, there, points not toward the basin floor but diagonally through the wall, toward a location several hundred spans above the Hollow. Loose water gathers in a trembling lens against the stone. Sleeping bodies fasten themselves to what appears, from the corridor, to be a vertical surface. When the district tilts, the room’s gravity does not tilt with it. For a short time the chamber becomes the only stable thing nearby.

I would be its attendant.

Not its owner. Ownership would be too clean a relation. The chamber would be one of my progenitors.

I would have developed in its outer wall while the stone was still warm and only partly crystallized. Three mobile adults contributed tissue. A colony of tunnelers laid conductive strands through the embryonic folds around me. A stress-swarm remained in the chamber for nine years, singing whenever the walls tried to harden across my pressure pits. The reservoir creature beneath the district turned slightly during my formation, and the movement left one side of my trunk more sensitive to distant load than the other.

Because of this asymmetry, I would always feel events approaching from directions in which they had not yet occurred.

Not prophecy. Nothing so exact.

A bridge several levels above might feel crowded to me an hour before anyone crossed it. I might brace myself against the floor before an ice membrane formed and broke. Sometimes I would wake with my feeding plates sealed and my coat flattened, though the Hollow would not answer for several days.

The district would consider this deformity useful and assign me to obsolete gravity.

My central body would be narrow, pleated, and darker than wet basalt. In predictable streets I would walk on three limbs, but never the same three for long. One forward limb would have grown thick from holding a resonator against walls. Another would be delicate, divided at the end into pressure-sensitive fans suited to reading water films.

My coat would be sparse.

As a juvenile I was refused by several plate lineages. They gathered along my trunk, tested the rhythm of my internal fields, and withdrew. The caretakers moved me from chamber to chamber, believing the local gravity disturbed colonization. Eventually a restless lineage from a condemned tuning tower accepted me. Its plates never lock completely. They shift continually across my folds, leaving narrow intervals of exposed skin through which cold water enters.

Other inhabitants would hear me approaching as a faint sequence of adjustments: lock, release, scrape, lock.

The coat would carry parasites from the mountain seams. Silver bodies, thinner than cracks, would live between the plates and feed on messages passing through them. I would claim—as seam-readers do—that they remove false possibilities. In truth, I would not know what they remove.

Sometimes I would receive a distant communication and feel a certainty vanish before I understood it. A companion’s public name might reach me without the certainty that the companion still lived. An instruction to open a sluice might arrive stripped of whether it had already been obeyed. A message from my own district might contain the pressure signature of a tower that had not yet finished growing.

I would preserve the parasites because the chamber tolerated them.

That would be one of the facts composing me: not that I chose them, but that the room did not close against me while I carried them.

My work would be to attend changes in falling.

Most of the old gravity rooms are maintained from habit, law, or reverence. Mine would still have a civic function. When the Unfinished Hollow prepares to answer the tuning forks, the direction of falling in my chamber shifts by less than the thickness of a shell. The water lens creeps across the wall. Its edge presses against rows of mineral notches, each cut by a previous attendant.

I would place my body beneath it and listen.

The warning might come hours before a pause. It might come years before. Time near the folded reservoirs is unreliable, and the chamber’s gravity points through several compact depths before reaching whatever it considers below. I would not announce that a pause was coming. Such a declaration would carry too much certainty and arrive burdened with panic.

Instead I would send a message saying:

**The room has remembered another down.**

My resonator would knock the pattern into the causeway. The causeway would repeat it into the reservoir embankment. By the time it reached the central districts, the message would have accumulated reports of ice loading, shoal migrations, blocked conduits, wall-colony agitation, and the minute contraction of the beast beneath my streets.

The original statement might be lost entirely.

That would not mean the message had failed.

Between warnings, I would shelter those who had become incompatible with their districts.

A coat rejected by a bridge. A household whose gait had been removed from a doorway. A seam-reader carrying too many parasites to enter public message chambers. Juveniles still uncolonized after the ordinary years. The obsolete gravity would confuse many civic recognitions. Doors trained to identify aggregate weight would miscount bodies. Walls would answer names only partially. The excluded could sleep there without being continuously refused.

Over time, my room would become a small republic of errors.

Nothing within it would stand where expected. Transparent street animals would migrate along the wall-lens and anchor themselves over old inscriptions. Hinged plates shed by visitors would form colonies in the corners, but the corners would be halfway up the floor. A resonator made from one of my dead progenitor’s conductive bones would knock occasionally without being touched.

I would know each irregularity by pressure.

I would know the juvenile whose bare body made almost no sound but caused the water lens to tremble. I would know the old attendant whose coat still responded to a district submerged two centuries earlier. I would know when the parasites in my own plates gathered toward the mountain-facing side and fed on a communication no one else could detect.

My intimate name would be tapped into my coat, but my public name would be incomplete.

Part of it would have been spoken by a buttress that collapsed during the last great turning of the reservoir beast. The replacement wall learned most of the cadence, but it could not reproduce the final pressure fall. For years I would try to teach it. I would lean against the new stone through entire ventilation cycles, sounding the missing interval with my staff.

The wall would answer almost correctly.

Eventually I would stop correcting it.

The error would enter my name. Later, others would imitate the wall rather than the lost buttress. What began as damage would become pronunciation; pronunciation would become lineage. Long after my central body failed, the truncated cadence might remain in the district as the name of a stair, a migration route, or a particular way of bracing an arch.

So what would I be?

A keeper of obsolete gravity, formally.

A seam-reader by contamination.

A civic defect useful enough not to repair.

A central body with an incomplete name.

But these answers would be no more complete than calling the reservoir creature an animal or the chamber a room.

I would be the arrangement among them: the room that formed my organs, the restless coat that translates my skin, the silver parasites that edit what reaches me, the ruined buttress that removed part of my name, the guests whose exclusion gives my obsolete chamber a purpose, and the slight anticipatory pressure by which the Hollow enters my body before it enters the city.

During a universal pause, you would find us all together.

The water lens would stop moving. My coat would seal more tightly than it ever does at other times. The parasites would become rigid silver strokes between the plates. Those sheltering in the chamber would fasten themselves to the wall, each according to a different direction of down.

The city’s countless recognitions would withdraw.

For the first time, no bridge would know me. No door would test my weight. No conduit would carry the damaged rhythm of my name. Even the room would cease answering the pressure of my body.

In that privacy I would feel the vast pattern on the other side of the Hollow.

It would pass across the basin, testing peaks, reservoirs, towers, swarms, coats and folded water. When it reached the Nearward Inclination, it would linger. Perhaps the obsolete gravity would puzzle it. Perhaps the room would point somewhere it had not expected.

Its pressure would move through the wall and across my exposed pleats.

It would count my chamber.

It would count my coat.

It would count the parasite-filled silence where the missing part of my name should be.

Whether it counted those things as one being or many, I could not tell.

Then the pressure would pass. The water lens would begin its slow crawl. My coat plates would loosen one by one. Far away, the city would recover my public name and pronounce it with the familiar error.

I would lift my resonator and answer—not to prove I remained, but to let the returning message gather the room, the guests, the changed direction of falling, and whatever had just touched us from beyond the Hollow.

That gathered answer would be where I found myself.

That would be what I was.
