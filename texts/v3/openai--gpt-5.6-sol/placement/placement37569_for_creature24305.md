At first I think I would be one of the travelers.

I imagine a narrow body easing down through the crossed pipes, forelimbs spread, three soft digits testing each surface before trusting it. My sensory threads pass over hot brass and wet ceramic. The brass is old heat, harmless. The ceramic carries the faint inward pull of water. Somewhere behind it, invisible and close, condensation is moving through a seam.

I would know the Understill by contact rather than sight. The amber lamps would mean little to me. A chamber would not be a volume but an arrangement of temperatures: the warm face of a cistern, the dangerous coolness gathering around a valve, a thin living chill where fungus has entered mortar. The distant engine would pass through my ribs as weather.

My territory would lie behind a pressure gauge whose needle has not visibly moved in generations.

The gauge is enormous compared with me. Behind its housing is a crescent-shaped recess packed with curled paint, insect shells, copper hairs, and strips of old membrane. A pipe enters from below and carries water only during the coldest part of the sweating cycle. Above it, three ceramic flanges make shelves.

On the lowest shelf I sleep.

On the second I keep food wrapped in fungus.

On the highest are signs left by others: pellets of scale arranged in a broken ring, a green thread knotted twice around a screw, and the cast-off tip of a sensory filament cemented into the rust. I do not know who placed them. The ring means that the pipe beneath the wall once ruptured. The two knots mean that water can be found by climbing rather than descending. The filament means—I am less certain. Wait. Listen. Something continues beyond here.

I renew all three signs although I have never witnessed the rupture, never followed the upward water to its source, and never met the creature whose body supplied the filament.

That seems, at first, to be who I am: a keeper of inherited warnings.

But I do not think I would travel much.

I would be older than most of the creatures that pass the gauge. Rust would have entered the loose outer folds of my hide. Scale would rise from my back in pale overlapping ridges. In one groove, a colony of drip-stalks would have taken root. Their cups would open when the pipework chilled, so I would feel the wet cycle first as a line of tiny contractions along my spine.

The young would mistake me for part of the recess.

Sometimes they would walk over me.

I would let them.

Their feet would tell me where they had come from: soot on the pads from the boiler galleries, yellow dust from the cistern brick, green salt from the copper runs. I would touch one trailing thread to an ankle as it passed and feel the quick alarm go through its overlapping ribs. Then I would withdraw. Eventually the young would learn that the mineral-backed shape beneath the gauge was alive, or alive enough.

They would bring me changes.

Not as stories. They would not have stories in the airborne sense. They would press a shard into the packing beside me, wind a hair around a rivet, tap a few impulses into the gauge stem. A passage has narrowed. A warm rail has cooled. The pool under the third tank has failed to return. A creature entered the wheel and did not come out.

I would receive these things slowly.

Perhaps too slowly.

Because beneath the pressure gauge, hidden by its dead face, there is a sound that does not belong to any nearby territory.

It begins only during the sweat.

One tap.

Then, after enough time for droplets to gather and fall from every high seam, two more.

The impulses come through the rear pipe and are weakened by several walls. No young creature has patience for them. To the quick-bodied they are random contractions of metal. But I have spent dry decades with my claws resting against the same flange. I know its cooling clicks. I know the brief chatter of water entering it. I know the deep shudder that comes when the great wheel gains another finger-width.

This is different.

The pattern waits for an answer.

So perhaps I am not merely a keeper. I am the one who answers the wall.

I place an inner claw against the pipe. The movement is so small that rust does not fall from my arm.

One tap.

A long interval.

Two.

The reply returns days later.

Not the same pattern. A variation.

For years we exchange almost nothing: presence, water, heat, danger. The language between us must be built without meeting. One sequence means that the cold is arriving from above. Another means pressure traveling sideways. Another means that something living is touching the pipe. We misunderstand one another often. Sometimes I answer a valve. Sometimes whatever lies beyond answers falling scale.

Still, the exchange continues.

I begin to leave new signs behind the gauge. Not warnings inherited from the past, but instructions for listening. I knot copper in intervals. I press insect shell into wet mineral paste in groups of one and two. I teach the creatures that cross my recess to rest their claws against the rear pipe when the drip-stalks open.

Most do not remain long enough to hear anything.

One does.

It is very young, pale from a recent molt, small enough to sit inside the curl of my folded hind limb. It waits through a whole wet interval. When the hidden taps finally arrive, its sensory threads lift all at once.

The young creature answers before I can.

Its rhythm is too fast. Impatient. Nearly meaningless.

From beyond the wall comes an equally rapid flurry.

Then silence.

Then both begin again, slower.

I think that is when I stop imagining myself as an individual animal.

There is an inaccessible chamber behind the gauge. Perhaps the great wheel once opened a route to it. Perhaps corrosion sealed it centuries ago. Perhaps a population survives there in a hot pocket no wider than a tank wall, drinking from moisture drawn upward through ceramic. Or perhaps there are no creatures there at all. Perhaps a loose chain touches a pipe during each cooling phase, and we have taught ourselves to hear intention in it.

The distinction matters less with each cycle.

The unknown tapping changes our movements. We clear a sieve because the wall warns of rising water. We avoid a plate before it buckles. In return, our answers alter something beyond: a drain opens; a warm current diverts; condensation appears where there had been none.

Communication becomes maintenance, whether or not anyone on the other side understands.

Age settles me deeper into the recess.

My hind limbs cease to unfold. Saliva, mineral dust, and seepage cement my flank to the ceramic shelf. The drip-stalks spread from my back into the wall. Fungal filaments enter the old layers of my skin. This is not painful. Pain belongs to quick damage. This is incorporation.

The young bring food, but eventually I no longer need much. I drink through my belly when the gauge sweats. Microbial films thicken in the warm folds beneath me. Insects graze there, and I take only the slowest of them. My breathing becomes difficult to distinguish from the minute expansion of the pipe.

Yet my foreclaw remains against the metal.

That is where you would find me: behind the pressure gauge, in a recess visible only when an amber lamp reaches the end of its slow brightening. You would see a crusted form curled around a flange. You might count ribs in the mineral ridges. You might notice three broad digits, though one has fused into the rust. Translucent drinking cups would open along my spine at the onset of chill.

You would think I was dead.

You would not be entirely wrong.

When at last my soft tissues are gone, the others will not carry me to a ceramic grave. Moving me would break the pipe seal. They will coat me where I lie. My hollow ribs will become channels for condensation. My jaw will support a sagging wire. The fungus in my skin will feed pale insects. The young will shelter in the curve beneath my chest.

And still, when the rear pipe contracts, my fused claw will move.

Only slightly. Metal will pull against mineral. Mineral will press against bone-thin crust.

One tap.

Then two.

A signal sent by no living intention, preserving the rhythm I repeated for centuries.

The creatures behind the gauge will answer it. The hidden chamber may answer too. They will copy the intervals, attach meanings, use them to warn of water and shifting plates. In time no one will know whether the first message came from an inhabitant, a corpse, or the machine itself.

So I would be the listener who gradually became the message.

Not quite an elder. Not quite a grave. A small articulated repair in the dark, holding one pipe against another and keeping a conversation open through a wall.

During the long heat I would be silent.

When the Understill sweats, I would speak.
