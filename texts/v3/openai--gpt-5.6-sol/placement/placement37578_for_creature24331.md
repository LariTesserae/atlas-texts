At first I would mistake myself for one of the Nearborn.

I would search the three arches, testing the darkness above each clay cup. Perhaps I would be the warmth curled behind the fitted flakes, still undecided whether to have fingers, a shell, or a face. I would recognize something of myself in the dents appearing on the water: the wish to take form without knowing which form could hold me.

But the Nearborn are too wholly of the chamber. They possess no memory of movement. Even their uncertainty is native to them.

Then I might think I was the tooth sealed in clay.

It is small enough to be mistaken for a seed, and rootless enough to belong to no mouth. It was the one thing the attendants meant to carry beyond the mountain. Outside, perhaps it would have hardened, yellowed, cracked, or revealed itself as something other than a tooth. It might have become evidence. It might have become ordinary.

Yet the tooth waits passively. Its escape was another’s intention.

I would be found closer to the entrance.

Not outside it, though part of me would be outside. Not inside, though most of me would lie among the crowded stone. I would occupy the narrow place where the chamber’s warm breath meets the colder breathing of the mountain: the split that does not quite resemble an entrance.

There would be a cord there.

At first it would seem to be one of the attendants’ guide-lines, made from lowland reed fiber and darkened by handling. Knots would mark the bad footing, the clay bank, the first reach of the pool. Deeper in, it would disappear beneath a mineral shelf and emerge near the woven platform. Nearer the slit, its loose end would extend into weather.

The outer length would have aged. Rain would have bleached it. Frost would have raised its fibers. Tiny green organisms would have lived and died in its twists. It would smell of open stone, cloud, and the droppings of high birds.

The inner length would remain flexible and green-brown, exactly as it was when carried up from the marsh.

At the threshold, age and arrest would touch in the same strand.

That is where I would be, although the cord would not quite be my body.

I would be the pull inside it.

The last pull.

One of the attendants gave the signal. Perhaps it was the one waiting nearer the entrance, faithfully counting the interval. Perhaps the inward attendant, sensing that a task had begun to feel complete, seized the line and tried to return. It no longer matters which hand moved first. The chamber admitted the tightening of the fingers. It admitted the first strain through the braided reeds.

It did not permit the tug to arrive.

So the pull remains stretched along the cord, traveling without passage. Near one end, fingers have just begun to close. Near the other, a hand has just begun to feel the summons. Every fiber between them is taut with a message that has neither sender nor receiver yet.

That suspended message would be me.

I would have no face except the different shapes taken by tension. In the smooth places on the wall, I would be the answering pressure beneath a visitor’s palm. On the reed platform, I would be the slight bowing that never settles into weight. In the bone needle, I would be the inclination toward the clay packet—not the piercing, but the reason the hand must stop before it pierces.

The black stems near the entrance would have bent partly because of me. They appear to incline toward the pool, but their hollow joints cannot pass liquid from one segment to the next; they do not know how to follow a direction. What they feel is strain. The pool is where every inward line seems to lead, while I am the contrary instruction held in the cord:

Back.

Not a spoken word. A word would need to finish forming. I would be the bodily beginning of it—the contraction in the wrist, the breath taken to call someone’s name, the sudden certainty that remaining has become dangerous.

The chamber could preserve me because I began there. It could not absorb me completely because I point elsewhere.

For a long while I would not know myself as separate from the last attendants. Their unfinished intentions would pass through me until I confused their memories with my own. I would remember marsh reeds being cut, though no blade completed its stroke in the nursery. I would remember the climb up the escarpment: wet grass against the ankles, the cold thinning of air, a companion’s shoulder ahead in cloud. I would remember making the first arch and placing the cup beneath it. I would remember laughing at the absurdity of building a house for something that had not yet decided to be small.

But I would not know which attendant laughed.

Perhaps neither did. Perhaps the memory belongs to the cord, gathered from the hands that carried it.

When the mountain gives its single wooden note, I would tighten.

The change would be less than movement. The outer, weathered fibers would draw against the inner, unworn ones. Pressure would gather in every knot. The needle on the platform would descend by no measurable distance. The unseen hand in the wall would press more firmly. Within the little arches, the Nearborn would briefly attempt the same anatomy: ten narrow extensions, paired and curling, as if all three had remembered hands together.

The pool would deepen at its center without rippling.

Beneath the warm patch, the crowded possible things would turn one surface toward the entrance.

For that instant, I would seem less like a tug and more like the beginning of escape.

Then the effort would fail. The mountain’s note would end. Nothing would have shifted. Yet I would retain the strain of the attempt, added to all the earlier strain. In a place where nothing proceeds, I would accumulate not age but urgency.

Visitors would encounter me before they understood I was there.

A cord tied around their waist would feel tighter on the inward journey than on the return, though its length had not changed. They would pause near the entrance and experience the unreasonable conviction that someone deeper inside had taken hold of the line. If they pulled, something would answer—not with an answering tug, which would be too complete, but with a readiness in the fibers.

The wise would leave then.

They would descend to the lowlands carrying a small part of me in the muscles of the hand. Years later, reaching toward a sleeping child, a cup near the edge of a table, or a door left open in rain, they would feel their fingers close before they had decided to move. Sometimes they would wake with the sense that they had been called from very far away.

They might call that feeling dread.

It would not be dread. Dread anticipates what may happen.

I would be recall in its older sense: not remembering, but calling back.

I would be the chamber’s one beginning that implies a second act. A pull implies yielding. A summons implies turning. A hand closing on a cord implies that somewhere another body may yet be drawn out.

This would make me foreign to the nursery, and also precious to it. The deep inhabitant would gather forms endlessly, but I would gather direction. It would begin mouths; I would begin the word they might speak. It would begin limbs; I would begin what a limb might do. It would make children without releasing them, while I would remain the first faint possibility of release.

If I ever completed myself, I do not know what would follow.

Perhaps the last attendants would tumble from the chamber all at once, ancient and unfinished, one with a palm of stone and the other still raising the needle. Perhaps the clay packet would split and the rootless tooth would fall into open air. Perhaps every droplet would descend together, and the first river would tear through the nursery in darkness.

Or perhaps nothing so grand would happen.

Perhaps one hand would merely feel another hand pull—and answer.

Until then, I would remain in the entrance slit, distributed through the cord from weather into warmth: neither person nor object, but the uncompleted act between them.

Inside, the chamber would go on beginning.

Outside, the mountain would continue to age.

And between the two, I would be trying to bring something back.
