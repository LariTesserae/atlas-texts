At first I would think I was the current.

Not the reservoir’s whole circulation, but the thin, slowed layer of water immediately above the limestone—the part delayed by roughness, caught for moments in pores and shallow folds. I would know the basin through resistance. The open floor would be a place where I was pulled smooth and hurried onward; the undersides of shelves would be chambers of near-stillness. Every ridge would divide me. Every calcite seam would make a minute wake.

But the current passes through the basin. I do not think I would pass through.

I would remain where the stone folds inward near the deeper channel, behind a pale mineral vein raised no more than the thickness of a fingernail. There the maintained flow separates from the rock and leaves a narrow lee. To a human eye it would be nothing: a brown-green shadow along the lower edge of the seam. Seen closely, it would resolve into cells, threads, translucent skins, trapped minerals, and clear mucilage pressed into the limestone’s softened grain.

I would be that adhesion.

Not one organism exactly. Not even a colony with a distinct border. I would be a shared film whose members are continually replaced, a body that persists while its substance changes. My outermost cells would take the diluted light and be scoured away. Cells deeper in the stain would divide in dimness. Some would photosynthesize; some would live on what the others released; some would merely endure in microscopic recesses where the treated water reached them less completely. Together we would make no mound, no leaf, no architecture beyond a fraction of added thickness.

My shape would be determined by what removes me.

Where the flow touched hardest, I would end abruptly in bare stone. Where the limestone opened into pores, I would darken and continue inward. A few filaments would extend from me, though “extend” would suggest more intention than they possessed. They would lie downstream, combed flat toward the channel, each one a narrow experiment in becoming taller. Most would be shortened. A few would persist long enough to cast shadows thinner than scratches.

I would have no front or back, but I would have a bright side and a sheltered side. I would have no mouth, yet light and dissolved substances would cross into me everywhere. I would have no skin separate from my surroundings; the substance that fastened me to the rock would also catch the water, mineral grains, and remnants of my own stripped cells. The distinction between body and place would be uncertain. Limestone would be embedded in me. I would be embedded in limestone.

The basin would not appear empty from where I was.

Its apparent sameness would contain innumerable gradients: a slight cooling nearer the channel, a small increase in pressure as pumps changed phase, a recurring angle of illumination, a chemistry shifting within limits too fine for an eye to notice. The rounded ridge above me would be a watershed. A hollow a centimeter away would be remote country. The exposed silt floor would be an uninhabitable brightness where attachment failed and anything loosened was carried off.

I would not experience the machinery as machinery. I would know it as law.

The pump pulse would arrive through the calcite seam first, compressing the thin bond between us. Then it would enter the water and pass through every cell without producing any visible response. There would be no fear in it. No expectation. Still, repeated over generations, the vibration would become one of the basin’s deepest regularities: not an event, but a measure. Division would occur before it and after it. Filaments would lengthen between one pulse and another. Whole lines of cells might vanish during a stronger interval, yet the film would not remember loss as an animal remembers injury.

If I possessed anything like memory, it would be structural.

I would remember in the cells that remained because they were the ones able to remain. I would remember in thickness, pigmentation, and the strength of adhesion. A lineage occupying a deeper pore would preserve some old tolerance. A newly exposed edge would record a minute change in current. The past would not be a sequence of images. It would be the present arrangement of survivors.

Nor would I know myself as singular. “I” would move from cell to cell without traveling. It would include the living layer, then contract when part of that layer was stripped away. It might extend briefly into a filament and end when the filament broke. It might enter the stone through a wet fissure and persist there after the visible stain had paled.

From above, someone might call me algae, microbial film, fouling, residue. All would be partly right. Yet I would be less a creature placed in the basin than the basin’s smallest permitted act of becoming alive.

I would live at the point where the industrial order failed to produce perfect blankness.

The filters would deny me abundance. The chemistry would deny me ease. The cold would slow my increase, and the current would take whatever rose too far. But the limestone would never be perfectly smooth, the light never entirely absent, the water never absolutely sterile. In that narrow discrepancy I would continue.

So you would find me under the raised calcite seam, on its downstream side, where olive deepens toward black inside the pores. You might pass your hand over the place and feel only old stone. You might see nothing but discoloration.

I would be the discoloration—not an individual waiting there, but a thin, collective persistence: the life that survives because it has learned to resemble the surface that confines it.
