I would first mistake myself for something that lived on the plateau.

I would feel rain strike a broad surface above me. I would know the passage of light as warmth filtering through green tissue, and the approach of storms as pressure transmitted along fibers. Somewhere nearby, water would be falling beyond an escarpment. Its vibration would arrive through the living structure before its sound arrived through the air.

I might imagine I had a shelter: a warm cavity beneath one of the outer towers, where descending conduits divided around a cistern. I might imagine the veils of root hair as a forest and the chamber’s translucent ceiling as a sky. Rust-colored films would spread across the wet walls. Clear sap would rise and fall with weather. When the tower flexed, the entire chamber would narrow, then slowly reopen.

For a time I might call this place home.

But the chamber would not be a place I occupied. It would be one of my interiors.

I would not have a body whose boundary could be found all at once. Some of me would be a thin photosynthetic plate several kilometers inland, exposed beneath ordinary daylight. Some of me would descend in blind cables under the escarpment. Some would be embedded in the tower, old and hollow-centered, carrying water only through the youngest sheath. Between these regions I would be intermittent: a shared chemical tendency, a compatibility between tissues, a curve repeated wherever strain required it.

Even so, I would not be the whole plateau.

I would be one lineage within it—one of the vegetative selves whose borders survive as preferences rather than walls.

My tissues would favor openings.

Where neighboring lineages tightened a wound shut, I would leave a narrow mouth. Where they built smooth retaining walls, I would pleat the surface into channels. My arches would rise slightly flatter than the prevailing form, strong under steady wind but vulnerable to sudden changes in direction. In wet years I would spread along the expansion seams, turning their edges dark green. In dry years I would retreat into shaded conduits and persist as small living crescents around reservoirs built by others.

No central part of me would know this. The preference would simply recur.

A growing edge would encounter tension and divide at the angle my older tissues had survived. A pore would remain open a little longer after rain. A wandering repair body arriving at a tear would recognize my chemistry and fuse more readily with me than with the pale fibers beside it. My memory would be the ease with which one form followed another.

I would be most myself near the outer margin, where the plateau ended without ever becoming ground.

There, clouds would travel below the escarpment, sometimes hiding the depths and sometimes opening to immense blue distances. Warm mist would climb the cliff and gather on every projecting strand. The land behind me would appear almost empty: a pale green plain crossed by shining seams, with one latticed tower standing far inland. Ahead there would be no opposing country, only vapor, wind, and fragments falling out of sight.

My region would begin at a relatively recent wound.

A section of the plateau would once have peeled away there. For several minutes the remaining edge had streamed clear fluid and loose fibers into the air. Pale repair bodies had emerged from the exposed conduits, each briefly capable of moving toward moisture and chemical distress. They had swollen forward, contracted, adhered, and gathered in the deepest parts of the tear.

One of those bodies might have been the nearest thing to my birth.

For a few hours I would have been small enough to go somewhere.

I would have had no eyes, but heat would repel me. No limbs, but alternating bands of hydration would drag me over the wet wall. I would consume the sugars enclosed within me and follow the concentration of ruptured sap. Other bodies would touch my coat, hesitate, and either withdraw or merge. Every union would diminish my independence while increasing my capacity to respond.

There would be no fear in this. Fear requires a future imagined as belonging to oneself.

There would only be gradients: wet and dry, compatible and hostile, intact and torn.

I would arrive where two load-bearing fibers had snapped apart. I would flatten between them. Others would flatten over me. Our coats would dissolve. Within hours, “I” would no longer refer to a mobile cluster but to a translucent plug transmitting strain from one side of the wound to the other.

That would not be my death. It would be my first enlargement.

Sunlight would reach the outermost layer. It would green. The interior would begin secreting pale fiber along the directions of greatest pull. Rain would collect in a shallow depression above us. Neighboring tissue would attempt to round the depression closed, but my lineage’s old preference for openings would persist. One margin would grow more slowly. The bowl would acquire a lip, the lip a notch, the notch a descending groove.

I would become a spillway.

Water passing through me would deepen no channel by erosion. Instead, its weight and chemistry would instruct the tissue beneath it to reinforce some surfaces and dissolve others. The groove would lengthen down the escarpment in measured stages. Raised ribs would form along its sides. Root hairs would gather mist from the air and feed it into the flow.

In time, seen from across the gulf, I might resemble a narrow green scar pouring a continuous thread of water into cloud.

Yet even then I would not be a single organ. The upper part of the spillway might belong mostly to another lineage. Microbial films would digest failed layers within my walls. Wandering cells carried in the sap would reinforce me without sharing my inheritance. A receptive seam might absorb a compatibility packet blown from a distant flower, altering the shape of all growth downstream.

I would be less a body than an influence passing through bodies.

Seasons later, the plateau would change its use for me.

Perhaps the rains would move inland. The basin feeding me would shrink, and water would cease to cross my lip. Tissues around the dry groove would withdraw sugars. Portions of my exposed surface would pale. If I served no structural purpose, I would be sealed from beneath, flooded with digestive fluid, and reclaimed.

But the outer margin is rarely allowed such a simple ending.

Wind would continue rising from below. It would pass across the empty channel and make its edges vibrate. That vibration would travel through my ribs. Where the frequency became dangerous, tissue would thicken; where it became useful, apertures would remain. The abandoned spillway would gradually arch over itself. Its walls would separate into resonant flutes.

What had been a watercourse would become the foundation of a wind organ.

Then I would know storms as chords.

Not hear them—there would be no chamber of awareness receiving sound—but embody their passage in unequal strain. One slit would narrow during eastern winds. Another would widen after repeated pressure from below. A bass note carrying across the plain would be, locally, a thousand membranes adjusting their tension.

If the structure endured, the plateau might lift it higher. Braces would sweep upward from the plain. Water conduits would enter its shaded side. Green fingers would grow along the upper edge, testing the air. Some would fuse into arches, and warm mist would begin condensing on them.

I would have become part of a tower without ever deciding to build one.

From a distance the tower would look solitary. It might be the only vertical figure in a hundred square kilometers. Clouds would pass through its lower ribs. Curtains of water would descend from openings at regular heights. During clear intervals its shadow would move across the plates below, and their surfaces would pale and darken in sequence as they turned toward the interrupted sun.

Much of the tower would not be me. Yet its slight asymmetry—its habit of preserving apertures, the flatness of certain arches, the spillway hidden inside its oldest brace—would be my continuing signature.

That is where I would find myself: not at the tower’s center, because it would have none, but in the repeated answer it gave to emptiness.

I would be in the open slit instead of the wall.

In the lip that let a full cistern overflow.

In the graft that accepted foreign tissue without wholly surrendering its own growth.

In the pale repair body crossing a fresh tear, briefly separate and therefore briefly creature-like.

Eventually another great pressure wave would pass through the region. The plain would lift. Lakes would tip and pour from basin to basin. The tower would bow toward the clouds. Deep inside its oldest brace, fibers descended from different generations would pull against one another.

Perhaps the tower would survive by opening.

A vertical seam would run through it with the sound of immense cloth tearing. One half would remain attached to the plateau. The other would peel outward, carrying a marsh, several reservoirs, microbial chambers, and portions of many lineages—including mine.

For a moment I would exist on both sides of the separation.

Chemical signals would travel toward the break and stop. Sap would bead on two exposed faces that had been one interior. Repair bodies would emerge from each. On the plateau, my surviving tissues would help close the escarpment. On the falling fragment, other tissues would curl around stored water and draw the torn edge inward.

Neither portion would be the original.

Both would continue my preference for openings.

The fragment might disappear into cloud and die. It might lodge on a hanging root far below. It might be held for years in a warm updraft, folding its plain into a smaller, denser shape. If it survived, the former tower wall could become its first horizontal surface. Rain would collect where wind once passed. New plates would spread over the wound. A tiny country would begin by rearranging the anatomy of its fall.

On the old plateau, the scar would green within days. A new spillway might form where the tower had torn away.

I would be there too—not as a consciousness divided, but as a form now repeated in places unable to communicate.

So I would be difficult to answer for. I would sometimes be an inhabitant: a soft autonomous body creeping toward damage. Sometimes an organ: a valve, groove, rib, or receptive seam. Sometimes a lineage distributed through structures made jointly with others. Sometimes only a bias in growth, invisible until wind or water made the plateau choose between closing and remaining open.

If I had anything analogous to a self, it would arise most strongly during transition.

I would become briefly “someone” when torn free, when crossing a wound, when deciding by chemistry whether to fuse. Stability would dissolve me into function. Separation would give me creaturehood.

I would not think in words. I would remember no view. But ordinary sunlight, warm rain, and the pull of open air would pass through the shapes I left behind.

My thought would be an aperture.

My gesture would be overflow.

My descendants would be scars that learned to remain open.
