At first I imagine myself near the water.

Not at the lowest tanks, where every error becomes communal, and not among the gate crews, whose bodies must become wedges when the boards buck in their grooves. I would want to be near enough to hear the first rush, to smell the dry channels waking, but not where water is the only thing that matters.

So I find myself halfway down.

There is a place called the Crooked Measure, though nothing there is used for measuring anymore. It lies where an old stair has been cut short by a pigeon wall. Three steps rise from the floor and disappear into plaster. Above them, a narrow gallery passes behind a row of cistern shells. Below, a channel turns twice around a pillar before descending toward the tanks. The turn is unnecessary now, but no one straightens it. Water running there makes two distinct notes, and the difference between them warns when the lower grate is clogging.

The Crooked Measure is not a room. It is a remainder between rooms.

A copper mirror sends a little daylight into it at noon. Mint grows badly in a cracked brick box. There is an oven on the other side of the wall, so the plaster is always warm enough that dogs choose it for sleeping. Pigeon dust comes down continually. During dry weeks, the place smells of feathers and hot lime. During rain, a seam opens in the upper vault and writes a dark line down the wall, stopping just above a scratched handprint.

That is where I sleep, behind a hanging mat, with my tools in shallow niches beside me: sounding pegs of different woods, chalk, waxed cord, a stone weight, a copper knife too worn for cutting, and many little tablets of broken pottery. On the pottery are marks that no one else quite reads.

I think I would be a plaster reader.

But that is only the name given by people who do not do the work.

Plaster readers are expected to tap walls, identify hollows, taste damp, and say whether a crack is old enough to ignore. I would do these things. I would know that a tight, bright crack may be less dangerous than a broad one furred with mineral; that water appearing clear can still have passed through a goat court; that a wall sounding hollow at dawn may sound solid after the ovens have been lit. I would press my cheek to lime and wait while the House used bone, water, air, and insects to answer me.

Yet I would not be one of the revered old readers who remember seven arrangements of every court. I would be younger, though already difficult to age. My hair would be cut close because I once leaned too near a lamp niche and heard a gecko strike at a moth beside my ear. My left shoulder would be stronger from carrying patch baskets. Lime would live permanently in the seams of my fingers. I would have a pale scar across one shin from falling through a reed-covered opening that someone had neglected to mark.

What I actually keep would not be the walls.

I would keep the absences.

Whenever someone opened a niche, widened a pen, pierced a speaking hole, or cleared an old doorway, the rule demanded a closure elsewhere. Most people treated this as practical balance mixed with old fear. They would come to me and say, “We opened two hand-widths near the bird court,” or, “The herb women broke through a skin wall and found a stair,” or, “A child fits through the western drain now, though no adult does.”

I would go and look.

I would measure openings not only by width but by consequence. A doorway was not equal to a doorway if one admitted wind and the other released sound. A blocked drain could not balance a lamp niche. A sealed stair weighed differently in the life of the House than a bricked window, even if their areas matched exactly. People would argue with me about this.

They would call me Keeper of Closures when they wanted my judgment and Mouse Counter when they did not.

My pottery shards would record these exchanges. Not in numbers alone. A scratched fish would mean water moved through the opening. A feather meant air or birds. A ladder shape meant people could pass. A blackened edge meant flame had once reached it. Some shards would refer to places already sealed, renamed, or lost behind newer walls. I would keep them anyway.

Children would be my best informants.

They would arrive at the Crooked Measure carrying rumors from spaces adults could not enter: cool air behind a cistern, rat tracks vanishing into fresh plaster, a whisper heard through a blocked pipe. I would pay them in wick ends, chalk pieces, or permission to make one official mark on a wall. Their reports would be unreliable separately and precise together.

One child would insist for weeks that someone was knocking from behind the pigeon wall. Adults would dismiss it as birds moving in their cells. I would sit there at dusk, when the pigeons settled and the oven cooled, and eventually hear three soft knocks, a pause, then another.

Not a trapped person. Not a ghost.

A wooden brace, buried between walls, shifting as damp reached it.

We would open nothing. We would bind the outer face with reed and lime, move two rows of pigeon cells, and wait through the next rain. Sometimes keeping the House would mean refusing to know what lay beyond a wall.

I would be good at refusal.

That surprises me. I first imagined myself curious, always pressing into sealed passages, following hollow sounds toward lost chambers. But curiosity below the surface cannot be innocent. Every discovery creates a burden. A newly opened room must be aired, drained, shored, guarded from animals, argued over, and matched by some closure elsewhere. Knowledge takes brick, labor, water, and sleep.

So I would learn to leave many things undiscovered.

There would be one exception.

Behind my sleeping mat, near floor level, is a thumb-sized hole. It draws a cool breath during the hottest hours. If I put my eye to it, I see nothing. If I place a lamp near it, the flame leans but does not gutter. Once, after a long dry season, I fed a waxed thread into the hole. It took more cord than the thickness of the wall allowed, then came back wet and smelling faintly of figs.

I would tell no one.

Not because I wanted to possess whatever lay beyond. Rooms cannot be possessed. But the hole would become my private measure of the Rain House’s remaining depth. Each morning I would touch it before beginning work. Cool meant the upper shafts were open. Warm meant stagnant air. A smell of dung meant some forgotten passage had joined the animal courts. Once every few years, perhaps, it would breathe rain before any sound reached us.

That would be my secret: not a chamber, but a breath.

Over time, however, I do not think I would remain entirely a person.

The role would outlast my body. An apprentice would learn my marks and misread some of them. Children would continue bringing reports to whoever slept at the Crooked Measure. My shards would be moved into a basket, then a jar, then plastered accidentally into a repair. The scratched fish and feathers would disappear inside the walls they described.

Someone would repeat one of my judgments without remembering my name: *Do not match an opening for wind with a closure for feet.*

Perhaps it would become a proverb. Perhaps a rule. Perhaps nonsense spoken while bricking up a cupboard.

My handprint would remain under a coat of blue lime. The thumb-sized breathing hole would be closed after a rat found it, and elsewhere someone would open a niche to restore the balance. The sleeping mat would rot. The broken stair would wear smoother beneath children pretending it still went somewhere.

By then, what I had been would be distributed: part notation, part habit, part superstition, part altered airflow. I would have gone into the wall without being buried there.

And on the first hard rain after my death, the channel below the Crooked Measure would strike its two notes. Someone passing would hear that the second was wrong. They would stop, call for a scraper, wake a child small enough to reach the lower grate. Water would be diverted before it entered the warm seam.

No one would call that haunting.

In the Rain House, it would simply be maintenance.
