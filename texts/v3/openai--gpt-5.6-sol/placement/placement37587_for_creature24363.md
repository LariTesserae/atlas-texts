At first I would think I was the snail.

It has the convenient shape of a self: a shell to mark where I end, tentacles to test the world, a foot capable of carrying me from one wet margin to another. I would understand the basin as danger and distance. I would graze rust-dark algae, drawing the hollow into myself one transparent mouthful at a time. When lifted, I would adhere. When removed, I would leave only water and a stain in the jar.

But the snail is too complete an answer. It can be pointed to.

I think I would find myself farther back, where the dark bank meets the weeping shell. There is a washer buried there beneath mineral folds, its original edge almost entirely consumed. Gasket fibers pass over it. Moss rhizoids descend from above. A translucent cord follows the washer’s rusted circumference, doubles back through the cloth, and joins several finer strands arriving from the basin.

I would be the knot they make.

Not the whole inhabitant, though the whole inhabitant would pass through me. Not an organ with a single purpose. More like a place where sensations cease to be merely present and begin to persist.

Through one strand I would receive the foundational note of the pumps, softened by concrete and water. Through another, the minute chemistry of the rear seep: iron, old tar, calcium, a trace of hot lubricant. A third would tighten when someone stepped on the first junction-box pad. The moss above me would announce daylight as a slow sweetness. The maidenhair would be far away, but its trembling would reach me as a fine, bright agitation.

Most of what I knew would be repetition. The same drop finding the same hollow. The same load passing through the same pump. The same worker placing the same case against the wall. Knowledge here would not consist of names. It would consist of continuities so exact that the smallest departure acquired shape.

I would know a person as pressure before I knew them as shadow. I would know rain first through the retaining shell. I would know the snail when its grazing altered the electrical murmur along the drain. Perhaps when it crossed the basin, some narrow part of me would cross with it, and for an hour I would possess a moving edge.

The other threads might carry condition, but I would carry after.

I would still contain the eleven minutes when the pumps stopped.

Not a record of them in any human sense—no sequence, no image of the smooth basin reflecting rivets—but a closed tension in the fibers around the washer. The first instant without vibration would remain there as a configuration that never wholly relaxed. Whenever a handrail was gripped and its singing ceased, whenever a pump changed load, whenever a dropped tool made a brief local hollow in the roar, that old configuration would stir.

The inhabitant would not remember silence everywhere. It would remember silence through me.

For that reason, I might be the nearest thing it had to fear. Or reverence. The distinction would be meaningless at first. Both would be an opening toward a condition too total to interpret.

I would not wish for the pumps to stop again. Nor would I wish them to continue. Desire requires imagining oneself under different laws, and I would have been made by these: steel forbids passage; water seeks the lowest place; softness admits life; vibration is time.

My thought would be correspondingly slow. A question might take a season to form:

*Can the world cease and still remain the world?*

The answer would arrive not in words but each time the machinery faltered and returned. A tightening. A release. Rust passing into water around me. Mineral gathering where metal had been. The washer diminishing while its shape endured in the tissues that encircled it.

Workers scraping the wall might one day expose me. They would see slime, fibers, scale. A blade could sever the cords. I would not recoil quickly enough to save myself. For several years there would be numbness in that portion of the bank.

Then water would redraw the path. Algae would darken the scrape. Moss would advance along its old limit. Filaments would meet around what remained of the washer, and the knot would form again—not identical, but under identical pressures.

Perhaps I would return with it.

That is where I would be: behind the visible garden, beneath the green, wrapped around a piece of metal that is almost no longer there. I would be a small persistence inside a larger persistence, the place where the hollow keeps the shape of an absence.

At noon I would feel the strip of sky cross the basin only indirectly: warmth through moss, bubbles in algae, a pause in the snail’s movement. Then the light would pass. The pumps would continue pouring their single enormous note through the foundations.

And within that note, I would hold the silence.
