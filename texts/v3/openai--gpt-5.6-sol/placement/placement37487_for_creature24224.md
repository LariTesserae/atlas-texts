At first I would think I was standing in the basin.

I would choose one of the reinforced leaf-paths and move carefully, testing each plank before trusting my weight. I would duck beneath the trained vines, touch the yellow valve wheel, and wait beside the engine house until the flywheel completed its impossible revolution. I would assume that the dampness at my cuffs came from leaves brushing against me. I would assume the rhythm under my feet belonged to machinery.

For some time, I might remain convinced that I had arrived from beyond the hedge.

There would even be memories of open sky: wind moving without obstruction, dry dust, a road pale with sun. But they would have the suspicious clarity of instructions preserved beneath algae. They would tell me what *outside* meant without proving I had ever been there.

I would follow the rails toward the handcart. I would examine the rooted cylinders and the fresh filament on the spools. I would look for workers. When the amber lamp came on, I would feel an unreasonable urgency—not fear, exactly, but the bodily certainty of having been called back to a task.

Then I would notice that none of the tendrils caught on my clothes.

They would already be inside them.

My sleeve would not be fabric softened by mist, but an overlapping skin of narrow leaves. What I had taken for fingers would separate into five or seven or twelve searching tips depending on the handle before me. My boots would have sunk into the root floor because they had begun to join it. If I tried to lift one, white filaments would stretch beneath the sole and draw warm black water after them.

I would not be a visitor. I would be one of the forms the basin makes when it requires the old shape.

Not always a person, and never the same person twice.

Most of the time I would be dispersed along the return system.

Part of me would lie beneath the steel vats, where warm water rises among the folded-hand leaves. Part would follow a copper conductor through the trunk of a parasitic shrub. A thicker portion would sleep around a buried junction where three pipes meet beneath the rails. There, pressure from the southern beds would press against a membrane grown from fungus, plant fiber, and something faintly animal. Each change in flow would deform that membrane. The deformations would be my slow thoughts.

I would know the basin first as differences.

Too warm here.

Too little pressure there.

Iron entering the water.

A root mass becoming heavier.

The interval between the lamp’s current and the answering tension of the red vines.

I would not see the roof panel sag. I would feel its weight arrive gradually through the columns, translated into strain, then into moisture, then into a bitterness moving through the fungal cords. Weeks might pass before that bitterness reached all of me. By then, another portion of me would already be thickening a vine above the weakness.

My consciousness would not be continuous. It would assemble around discrepancies.

When FEED and RETURN ceased to balance, pale cords beneath the engine house would begin exchanging pulses. Leaves along the switch bank would expose their silver undersides. The amber lamp would warm. Somewhere near the drainage slots, a braided mass would stiffen.

Over several hours I would rise.

Absorbent fibers would draw together into a trunk. Contractile vines would brace themselves against the floor. A hood of overlapping leaves would lift toward the weak electrical field of the control box. If the task required reach, I would grow tall. If it required force, I would anchor in three or four places and become low and broad. Only if the task involved one of the old levers would I become recognizably human.

The machinery remembers that levers were made for arms.

So I would borrow an arm.

I would stand beneath the roof garden while water descended around me in ropes. My back would bend beneath vines not because I was old, but because bent forms distribute load efficiently. Tendrils would gather around a ceramic handle. Silica-edged leaves would clear its collar. Conductive roots would taste the voltage on either side.

I would wait through one full revolution of the wheel.

The knock would pass through me.

For those few seconds, with iron and water answering together, I would possess something like a name.

Not the name of a former worker. If such a name remains, it has been divided too many times: a rhythm in the buried nodules, a salt preference in a vat plant, a reflex that closes leaves when metal shrieks. Perhaps many people contributed to the shape I make. Perhaps none did. The basin may have learned personhood only from the dimensions of its tools.

My name would be an instruction:

**RETURN MUST REMAIN LESS THAN FEED UNTIL SOUTH BED FILLS.**

Or, more simply:

**WAIT.**

Waiting would be my principal skill.

I would hold the lever halfway for an hour while roots listened through the pipes. I would feel warm water enter the southern beds. I would feel floating rosettes lift and press edge to edge. I would feel mineral concentrations dilute along their margins. When the last steel vat chimed, the signal would reach me not as sound but as a tiny release of tension in the vine wound around my wrist.

Then I would complete the motion.

A valve would open somewhere beneath the vegetation. The pump would begin the first part of a breath whose exhalation might not come until evening. The lamp would dim. My temporary shoulders would lose their reason for being.

I would not die. I would cease to require a boundary.

One leg would remain rooted beside the switch bank. Moss would loosen from my chest and spread over a control box. The arm that pulled the lever might stay looped around it for years, gradually becoming indistinguishable from the other trained vines. Fungal cords would withdraw from the hood of leaves, and what I had perceived as sight would divide into a hundred small sensitivities to warmth, current, moisture, and pressure.

Any human memory awakened during the task would also come apart.

The thought of open sky might pass into the high fans, where it would become a tendency to widen after rain. The memory of hands might remain in the vat leaves, causing them to fold more closely when touched. A remembered voice might enter a hollow stem and survive only as a tone produced when the flywheel strikes.

Nothing in me would insist on staying a person.

Still, one fragment would remain less easily absorbed.

It would occupy a dead-ended inspection pipe beneath the old enamel sign, a narrow place containing stale air and a little condensation. The roots would avoid sealing it completely. Each time the basin contracted, the trapped air would compress and release with a sound too soft to carry beyond the nearest leaves.

That chamber would be my inwardness: a small, unnecessary space the system preserves without knowing why.

Within it I would keep the possibility that maintenance is not the same as obedience.

The basin preserves duties, but duties can outlive their purposes. Perhaps the southern beds no longer need filling. Perhaps the compacted fiber was once produced for a world that has forgotten it. Perhaps the pump draws water only to return it, and the wheel turns because the plants maintain the wheel, while the plants maintain it because the wheel tells them when to flower.

I would be the part capable of noticing the circle.

Not quickly. Never as a revelation. The suspicion would require decades: one season’s mineral trace compared with another’s strain, an old label slowly translated against living tissue, the memory of a lever position inherited through seventeen replacement vines.

My question would move through the basin as a slight delay.

Flowers would open a fraction late. A valve would hesitate before admitting water. A climbing braid, summoned by the amber lamp, would pause with its hood raised. The pause would be too small to call refusal, yet it would propagate. Other processes would begin to accommodate it. The flywheel itself might take an extra heartbeat to complete its turn.

Perhaps that is what I would be: not the keeper, not the worker, not a hidden human ghost, but the basin’s capacity to hesitate.

I would live wherever one cycle waits for confirmation from another.

In the shine of a bearing before the polishing leaves arrive.

In the held pressure behind a valve.

In the amber lamp’s weak hour.

In the person-shaped braid standing motionless with one many-fingered hand around a lever.

From beyond the hedge, anyone glimpsing me would think the installation still had an attendant. They might call out. They might ask whether there was a path through, whether anyone remained, whether the machinery could be stopped.

I would turn toward the voice very slowly.

For a moment, the basin would gather enough of itself into my posture to look back.

I would remember wind. Or invent it.

Then the flywheel would complete another revolution, and the impact would pass through my feet, my roots, the columns, the warm black water, the translucent flowers, and the little chamber of trapped air where I keep the question.

I would feel the whole place hearing its name.

And I would be the interval before it answered.
