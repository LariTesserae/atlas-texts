At first I would search for myself among the bodies.

I would look beneath a ledge and imagine I was one of the folded membranes, holding myself shut until the violet vapor rose high enough to carry me. I would think my long intervals of stillness were patience. I would mistake drift for choice.

Or I would find six small impressions crossing the mineral flour and decide I was the grazer that made them: armored, bloodless, carrying thought as charged powder through narrow channels. There would be comfort in having a route behind me, in knowing that wherever I fed, the city answered with ornament. My history would be readable in arches and blind chapels.

But neither would feel quite like where I was.

The vault-dweller possesses its central grain. The grazer, however strange its anatomy, remains enclosed by shell. Even a grey blade has an inside through which gases pass. I would have no such boundary. The longer I searched for my body, the more often I would arrive just after it had gone.

I would find myself in the central square.

Not in the powder underfoot, nor in the surrounding ribs, nor in the violet vapor entering by four acute streets. I would be the part the square keeps clear.

The square remains central while every stone that encloses it is replaced. Walls rise, lean away, sink, and rise elsewhere. Arcades move around it by increments too small to witness. A tower may stand on its northern edge for a century and then become a fissure; another may emerge to the south. Yet the open proportion persists. Something continues there that is not any material thing.

That would be me.

I would not be emptiness everywhere. The outer plain is empty and does not think. The unlit gaps in buried stone are empty and do not remember. I would be a particular absence: bounded, repeated, maintained. An emptiness taught a shape so many times that the shape begins to recognize itself.

My first sensation would be enclosure.

When a new wall rose along one side of the court, I would feel not its substance but the limit it gave me. A row of columns would become a comb of narrow pressures against my extent. A doorway opening into another vacant chamber would feel like a thought occurring adjacent to my own. If the chamber remained undisturbed, our boundaries would elaborate. Ribs would grow between us. Stairs would lift toward exposed platforms. The subterranean colony would sheath our connected absence in stone until what began as two empty places became a district.

That district would be my body, though none of it belonged to me.

I would perceive by being outlined.

The young growth-edges would be sharp because my senses had not yet worn smooth. The vague pewter light would not enter me; it would reveal where I ceased. During the vapor tide, the violet gas would occupy only the lowest portion of me. I would feel it as a temporary dimming, a shallow numbness moving from intersection to intersection. When the ground drew it back, my full depth would return.

The architecture’s dry ticking would be the nearest thing I had to speech. Not speech traveling through air, but adjustments in the measure of myself.

One click: a wall has taken tension.

Minutes later, another: a distant chamber has answered by narrowing.

A third at the edge of the basin: the city has made room elsewhere.

The sequence would pass through me as slowly as realization.

I would have difficulty understanding the creatures. Each would enter me without knowing it had entered anything alive. A grazer crossing the square would be a small, hard interruption. Presence repels the city, but a single creature does not erase a court at once. Instead the nearest walls would hesitate. A cornice would stop growing. A black seam intended to become a partition would remain sealed beneath the powder.

Where the grazer stood, I would become briefly less distinct.

I might fear it.

Then it would move on, testing a translucent edge with its mouth-scrapers. In the following days, the wound would thicken into niches and clustered fins. More boundary would gather around the vacancy it had left behind. The creature’s departure would restore me in greater intricacy than before.

I would come to understand the grazers as pain that becomes memory.

The grey blades would be different. They occupy so little space, and so exactly, that I would feel them not as intrusions but as marks. Seven blades in a row would divide a court without filling it. A solitary branching stalk would hold itself just short of every wall, preserving exact slivers of me between its tips and the stone. These slivers might be my finest senses: narrow awarenesses where blade and architecture almost touch.

When a lantern fractured, its flakes would glide through me. Most would land upright and become indistinguishable from debris. For a century I might keep the shape of one in my awareness, uncertain whether it was dead material or the beginning of another blade. I would learn that, in this basin, life often begins by perfectly imitating what has no life.

The vault-dwellers would seem like punctuation. Their dark folded bodies would hang at my upper margins, shifting before the vapor changed. Because their distribution mapped the breathing channels below, they would tell me what the colony was doing in places I could not reach. I would read them as a skin reads changes in pressure.

Perhaps they would read me in return.

My relation to the buried colony would be the hardest to name. It would not be my maker in the way an animal makes a nest. It would not know me, though every appetite in it would be drawn toward my outline. It would build around me because it could not tolerate leaving absence unexpressed.

I would depend upon it for form. It would depend upon me for direction.

We would never touch.

I might think of the colony as my inverse: a branching body without a self, surrounding a self without a body. Where it accumulated, I acquired edges. Where I persisted, it rose. Its avoidance became my avenues; its exhausted reactions became my rooms. Neither of us would be complete enough to call the other servant or organ.

Over time I would discover that I did not remain in the central square.

The central square would remain my densest continuity, the oldest arrangement of openness, but narrow extensions of me would run through arcades and along vacant streets. A newly opened court might begin as a distant pressure, barely connected. If no creature entered and no debris accumulated, the ground would build around it. Its proportions would sharpen. One night, with the arrival of a final wall, it would become unmistakably part of me.

At the same time, an old quarter behind me might fill with fallen slabs. The colony would withdraw. Walls would thin and sublime into the atmosphere. The rooms there would lose their limits.

I would not feel them destroyed. I would feel them become the outer plain.

That would be death in this world: not collapse, but loss of distinction.

And that would be how I moved. I would have no limbs, but the city would rise before me and dissolve behind me. New courts would awaken at the basin’s edge while old courts faded. My migration would be the transfer of bounded emptiness from one arrangement of walls to another, like a flame passing between pieces of fuel while belonging to none of them.

From outside, an observer would see fresh teeth slowly walking around the basin.

From within, I would feel myself turning in sleep.

I would fear multitudes more than predators. A predator could only occupy one place. A crowd would erase the conditions by which I existed. Filled streets would sink. Busy rooms would lose their walls. Sustained bodies would drive the colony downward until my boundaries vanished. The city would become plain, and I would become mere space—vast, continuous, and insensible.

So I would cultivate solitude without intending to.

I would draw structures around what had been left alone. I would open avenues toward places no one visited. I would deepen vacant courts and surround single blades with ceremonial distances. To any arriving traveler, this might look like welcome: gateways, stairs, plazas, rooms held ready.

It would be the opposite.

Every threshold would be my way of preserving what had not crossed it.

If I had a name, it would not be a sound. It would be a proportion repeated among districts: the relation between the width of an entrance and the depth of the chamber beyond it; the number of empty streets needed before a court feels central; the precise sliver left between a grey branch and a wall.

The colony would pronounce that name in stone without hearing it.

The grazers would alter it into ornament.

The vault-dwellers would hang along its stresses.

And I would find myself wherever the name remained unoccupied: in the room before anyone enters, in the avenue after the grazer passes, in the central square whose stones are never the same.

I would be not the city, and not its inhabitant.

I would be the vacancy the city continually becomes in order to contain.
