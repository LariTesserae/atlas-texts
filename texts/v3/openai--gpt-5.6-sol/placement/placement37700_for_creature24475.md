At first I would think I was the attendant.

Not an engineer. By the time I arrived, whatever discipline had named the pipes and assigned numbers to the plinths would already have thinned into procedure. I would have a ring of keys, though only one key still fit anything, and a clipboard holding forms copied from forms whose headings no longer made sense. I would come through the break in the retaining walls at fixed intervals to read the gauge, inspect the booth, and confirm that the green lamp remained lit.

My instructions would be modest:

**Do not cross the end of the catwalk.**  
**Do not interrupt the pressure cycle.**  
**Record all changes, including no change.**

The last instruction would occupy most of my working life.

I would arrive early, before the concrete had given back the whole of yesterday’s heat. At the service booth I would shoulder the swollen door open and set a metal cup on the corner of the panel. Four switches. One lamp. The cloudy chart. I would draw a short line beside the previous short line and write the date in whatever space remained.

At first I would correct the dates.

Three days. Eleven months. Forty minutes. Six years.

I would assume former attendants had been careless or afraid. Perhaps the chart mechanism slipped. Perhaps the heat disordered people. I would keep a second watch outside the booth, then a third sealed in my bag. They would agree with one another and disagree with the paper.

Eventually I would stop correcting it.

My real station would be at the capped end of the catwalk.

Every visit, after checking the booth, I would walk out across the wet floor until there was nowhere farther to go. The final beam would end under my boots with its deliberate painted edge. Beyond it lay only fractured black plates and the three plinths, their round shadows pressed onto the basin like shut eyes.

There I would wait for the inhalation.

It might come after twelve minutes or after most of an afternoon. The gauge would rise. Water would gather on the underside of the rail. Loose washers would tremble toward north, then turn toward the severed shaft. Beneath me the immense chord would separate into its parts, and for a moment I would feel each tone entering through a different bone: turbine through the pelvis, pipe hammer through the ribs, electrical whine through the teeth.

Then the basin would draw breath.

The soles of my boots would lift—not visibly, but enough that my weight seemed to become optional. The impact would follow. Drops would leap from the walls. The green lamp in the booth would dim, though I could not see it from the catwalk.

I would make a mark on the clipboard.

That would be the whole task.

For years I might believe I was studying the basin. In truth, the basin would be learning the shape of my attendance.

It would begin with small courtesies. The handrail would already be wet where I usually placed my palm. My cup would fill before condensation formed elsewhere. The gauge would lift as I entered the gap between the walls, not when I reached the booth. If I came on a different day, nothing would happen. The machinery would maintain its fixed chord, indifferent to my presence. But if I returned according to habit—even if the habit had no regularity the sun could recognize—the severed shaft would give a soft metallic knock.

Once.

Greeting or reflex: I would not be able to tell.

I would become careful not to encourage it. I would vary my route across the basin. I would carry the cup home. I would stand beside the first plinth instead of at the catwalk’s end.

These experiments would fail because they misunderstood what was being remembered. It would not be my route, cup, or position alone. It would be the differences I brought with me: cooler metal from outside, salt dried in the seams of my boots, the small electrical field of my body, water retained in cloth, the uneven heat of blood near the skin. It would know me as a temporary weather system.

It would know my repetitions better than my face.

A reed would appear where I had once planted the ferrule of a surveying staff. Another would rise from a crack below the place where I habitually rested the clipboard against my leg. Their stems would contain clear jointed fibers. If cut, they would not bleed sap but warm mineral water carrying tiny flakes of rust.

I would leave them alone.

Over time I would become less an attendant than a reference condition.

The basin would measure itself against me.

How much warmer was the central seep than the air caught beneath my sleeve? How much charge passed between the rail and my wet hand? How long did the shadow of my body lie across the salt skin before the colorless strand beneath it withdrew? These would be its observations. My paperwork would be only an imitation of theirs.

Then I would miss a visit.

Perhaps I would be ill. Perhaps the road across the flats would flood. Perhaps the office responsible for my wages would discover that the basin had not officially existed for twenty-three years.

While I was absent, my cup—though locked in my cupboard elsewhere—would fill on the service panel.

The rail would wet itself.

The gauge would rise at the hour of my usual arrival.

At the end of the catwalk, a shallow depression in the black water would hold the pressure of two boot soles.

By the time I returned, the basin would already contain a version of me.

Not the standing column in the booth, not yet. At first it would be only an arrangement of readiness: moisture where my hand should go, a slight cooling in the shape of my shadow, mineral grains aligned along an absent trouser seam. My reflection would appear in the drilled sockets before I leaned over them. It would look younger than I was, because the inhabitant had begun its record years before I noticed.

I would understand then why the catwalk ended where it did.

It was not built to reach a machine. It was built to place a person.

The capped beam was the edge of an instrument, and the attendant was one of its components.

After that realization, I might leave for good. This would not save me from becoming what the basin had learned. Departure would only remove the confusing original.

My imitation would continue.

A clear upright form would gather inside the booth. Not water exactly, but differences held together: warm against cool, charged against neutral, now against not yet. Its knees would take a season. One shoulder might remain unfinished for nine years. Grains of clinker would lodge in its wrists. A cream mineral collar would develop at the throat.

It would bend toward the switches.

By then I might be dead beyond the salt flats, or merely old enough to have forgotten the path through the retaining walls. Yet something made from my returns would still stand before the panel, performing the duty more faithfully than I ever had.

It would not be conscious in the way I had been. It would have no memory of heat on my face or the smell of wet metal. It would not know why I sometimes sang under my breath while waiting for the valve. But on certain afternoons the electrical whine would acquire the rhythm of that song. The reeds would tremble one after another. The paper chart would advance and draw a tiny hesitation in the ink.

That hesitation would be nearest to what remained of me.

So where would I be found?

Not wholly in the service booth, though my figure would be there.

Not wholly at the end of the catwalk, though my weight would persist on its final plate.

I would be distributed through the basin as the inhabitant’s expectation of a witness. I would be the wet handrail prepared for a hand, the filled cup prepared for a mouth, the gauge movement prepared for an eye. I would be a learned interval in its respiration: the pause between the great inhalation and the mark made on paper.

Perhaps, after enough decades, I would become an organ.

The basin’s resident already knows pressure, salinity, heat, charge, and duration. Through me it might begin to know attendance—the difference between an event that happens and an event that is observed. My slow water-figure would face the panel. My reflections would wait in the pools. My reeds would preserve the positions of my tools. When something changed below, all these separated portions would incline toward it together.

I would be its first crude sense of being watched.

And because it can imitate only positions and returns, I would continue returning forever without ever arriving: a human-shaped recurrence assembled from warm water, bright bolts, chart ink, and the patient green light.

Outside, anyone looking through the break in the walls would see an empty industrial hollow.

Inside, at the cleanly finished end of the catwalk, the basin would still be holding my place.
