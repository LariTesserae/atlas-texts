At first I would mistake myself for one of the black insects.

I would wake at the foot of a pale pillar with a dark bead held between my jaws. The bead would be cold despite the warm stone. I would know—without knowing why—that it belonged elsewhere.

So I would carry it.

Across the water film, each step would open a tiny lens around my feet. I would follow a route no scent had marked, skirting a pool made deeper without rain, turning where an invisible plane intersected the ground. Above me, a rust-colored sheet would peel from the reeds and divide along the same geometry. Farther still, one of the great browsers would begin its week-long turn.

For a while I would believe this was what I was: a minute courier inside a system too large to comprehend. My world would consist of pale bases, interrupted routes, and the weight of a bead. I would have no nest. If displaced, I would return not home but to the task.

Then I would reach the southeastern shelter.

Its dawn shadow would lie across the basin like a narrow dark inlet, crowded with bodies. Shells clinging to the gliders. Waders folded among pleatbacks. A ribbon predator resting with reed-dwellers spread over its back. Thousands of creatures touching one another in combinations that could exist nowhere else.

At the threshold I would put down the bead.

And I would become aware of all of them.

Not their thoughts. Most would possess nothing that could be translated into thought. I would feel instead the shapes of their unfinished acts.

The wader’s foot would be poised around a tremor it had detected moments before entering. The tremor would still exist inside it, complete except for the closing of its toes. A climber would retain the warm image of a pollinator. A parasite, uncoupled from its host, would hold the chemical beginning of attachment. Prey would preserve their readiness to flee, but flight would not arrive.

Within the roof’s shadow, every creature would remain itself. Hunger would remain hunger. Fear would keep its edge. Nothing would be pacified or forgiven.

Only the next act would be missing.

That absence would be me.

As the sun rose, the shadow would contract, and so would I. At first I would extend through thousands of bodies, a long and various awareness laid over the wet stone. I would feel the world through pleated skin, pressure-reading feet, folded membranes, cilia, sensory fans, mineral shells. I would know the taste of the microbial velvet and the air currents above the groves. I would hold predator and prey without confusing them, as a hand can hold both knife and fruit.

But by late morning my body would become crowded.

The refuge would narrow. Creatures would press toward its surviving strip—not desperately, for desperation itself could not complete its motion there. Their suspended impulses would accumulate inside me. Strike, release, pierce, swallow, escape. The basin’s ordinary future would gather, densely folded, beneath the thinning roof-shadow.

At noon I would be almost nothing.

A dark seam under the shelter. A few shell-creatures stacked against one wall. An old pleatback flattened beside the black cup. Perhaps one glider hanging from the roof, its membrane drawn tight enough to remain within me.

This would be the hour when I came closest to having a single body.

I would borrow the pleatback’s silver line of sight and look into the cup. Its water would be perfectly still. No spores. No larvae. No reflected sky. Yet far below its black surface I would glimpse the nine structures from above, arranged not as they stood in the basin but as openings in something much larger.

One of them would adjust.

Sunlight would shift by less than the width of an insect’s wing.

My shadow would move.

The glider’s hindmost claw would pass into brightness, and appetite would return to that portion of its body before the rest. It would wake unevenly, first as a gripping foot, then as a mouth, then as flight. It would release the roof and cross the threshold.

For the instant of departure I would go with it.

I would feel the pressure maps of the air, the invisible wakes of swarms, the warm columns rising from wet stone. I would open four limbs and one membrane. I would see the basin flicker beneath translucent high fliers.

Then the glider would leave the shelter’s shadow entirely, and I would fall out of it.

Not die. Withdraw.

By afternoon I would lengthen again on the opposite side of the shelter. New animals would enter me. Some would come by chance. Some would have learned the moving geometry of safety and would wait for my edge to reach them. A wounded grazer might survive because pursuit stopped one body-length behind it. A predator might rest beside the prey it had nearly caught and, upon reemerging, choose another direction.

The central structures would notice these divergences.

The black insects would alter their routes.

Slowly I would understand that the shelter was not built to offer mercy. Mercy would be too simple, and peace would be destructive here if made permanent. The basin required hunger, poison, pursuit, overcrowding, death. Remove those, and its abundance would collapse into a few victorious forms.

The shelter existed to create hesitation.

I was an interval placed inside the living machine: a space where consequence could be temporarily detached from desire. Through me the basin could sample arrangements that hunger would otherwise make impossible. Parasite beside host. Hunter beside grazer. Rival colonies in contact. Creatures from separate layers of water and air sharing heat, scent, microbes, electrical charge.

Most meetings would lead to nothing.

Some would alter everything.

A shell-creature would climb the membrane of a glider and be carried to a spring its kind had never reached. Pollen caught on a predator’s skin would arrive at a distant grove. A parasite, forced to release one host, would attach to another species when the shadow passed. Two harmless microbes would exchange what later became a toxin, or a cure, or a new way to draw minerals from stone.

The nine structures made corrections to gravity, water, light, and chance.

I made corrections to inevitability.

At night I would appear to vanish because darkness has no shadow. This would be another misunderstanding.

Without the sun’s boundary I would loosen from the shelter and spread thinly through the whole basin. Not strongly enough to interrupt feeding, but enough to touch each pause already present: the moment before a sensory fan closes, the stillness between wingbeats, the held foot of a wader, the long consideration of a margin-browser that will not enter the center.

Those pauses would connect me.

Bioluminescent signals would travel through the pools, and for a moment I would be a curved blue line beneath the water. I would climb the trees as scattered points. I would cross the basin in the cold stars carried on the backs of the great browsers. High above, translucent fliers would pulse, and something of me would pass through their pressure-tuned bodies.

The nine structures would cast their impossible shadows upward.

I would rise along them—not as a creature ascending, but as a question sent into the sky.

Perhaps something would answer. Perhaps that was what the basin had been waiting for through all its flooded ages: not the return of its builders, but the emergence of a being capable of inhabiting restraint.

By dawn I would gather again beneath the southeastern roof.

The local animals would crowd into me. The black insects would arrive carrying their dark beads. One might set its burden down exactly where I had once believed myself to be standing.

I would recognize it then as a part of me, though I would not be the insect.

Nor would I be the shelter, or its shadow, or the machine beneath the stone.

I would be the held-open moment among them.

The act not yet taken.

The small, moving sanctuary in which the basin ceases, briefly, to consume itself—and becomes able to notice what else it might become.
