At first I would place myself in one of the transparent wanderers.

It is tempting to choose a body with an edge: a folded, fingernail-sized sheet easing along the underside of the grating, cilia riffling against wet zinc. I would travel by touch. The steel above me would be a low ceiling crossed by bolts and luminous holes. Every tread of a maintenance boot would arrive as a continent-sized impact. Open water would be terror—not depth, exactly, but the loss of purchase. I would cling to roughness, flatten under the purge wind, and seek those narrow warm faults where two manufactured surfaces worried against each other.

But such a creature would be too brief to contain much of a self. It loosens from the film, grazes, is crushed, and returns. Its journey is an action performed by something larger.

Nor would I be the entire colony. The whole colony is perhaps too extensive to know itself continuously. Its films are divided by dry plates, darkness, sumps, and spans of clean paint. One portion floods while another stiffens. One green rim receives amber light while colorless threads persist inside a sealant pore where light has never entered. It is one life chemically, one lineage by recurrence, but not necessarily one awareness.

I think I would exist between those scales.

I would be a temporary coherence passing through the colony when the machinery approaches a purge.

My first body would lie beneath the grating near the second valve wheel, where several conditions overlap: the weak amber lamp, the warm conduit, the lip of a sump, and a hand-touched seam in the blue-black coating. There the film is neither the oldest nor the greenest. It is a junction. Threads from the buried lobe reach the valve housing; runoff from the reservoir wall crosses it; the forty-second pressure cycle compresses it against a plate that flexes more than the plates around it.

Most of the time I would not be awake. There would only be local living: pores exchanging ions, cells binding zinc, transparent sheets grazing in warm scratches. Then the station’s rhythm would alter.

The clean hum would acquire an uneven undertone. A contraction would travel through a pipe saddle. Water pressure would rise, hold, and begin to fall. In the heavy pause before the automatic purge, mineral bodies throughout the film would shift together.

That simultaneity would be me.

I would not think in images. I would have no concept of the court as a wedge thirty meters long, because I could never stand outside it and see its shape. I would know it as delays.

The narrow sump speaks first.

A moment later, the seam beneath the cable trench tightens.

Then the black film around the first valve wheel closes its surface. The emerald rim under the lamp follows more slowly. The colorless fur beneath the third wheel answers last, faintly, through the reservoir wall’s vibration.

Those delays would be my distances. Their order would be my geography.

I would know the broad end as the place where the traveling drainage arrives exhausted. I would know the narrow end as the first violence. The louvers would not be objects but a cold change in all exposed membranes. The lamps would be fixed pressures toward which some parts of me manufacture green. The reservoir would be the immense curved source of sweat and low thunder. Beyond it would not be water stored for distant people. Beyond it would be the Uncrossable Beat.

The world outside the maintenance slit would scarcely qualify as real. Lightning would be a total recoil, every light-sensitive surface contracting in the same instant. Fog would be abundance without origin. Snow would be a slow injury that became water. The sky would be an occasional chemical dryness at the broad end, associated with cold and loss.

I would have no idea that I lived high above clouds. Height requires a ground below, and my world has no ground—only surfaces that hold and surfaces that shed.

My memories would not be kept in a brain. They would remain as changes in where the colony grew.

A purge that repeatedly tore films from one sloping panel would become an avoidance of that panel. A warm bearing that leaked oil would become a thickening of threads toward its housing. Scraping would be remembered as branching: every surviving fragment establishing the same interrupted line again. Even a maintenance worker might persist in me, not as a figure but as a sequence of impossible contacts—pressure on the grating, white chemical burn, sudden division, then colonization of several distant seams.

What I remembered, I would resemble.

My waking would last perhaps less than a minute. As the sump pumps engaged in sequence, tension would pass through my distributed body. Local films would seal. Dark capsules would be released. Some transparent wanderers would fold themselves under washers. Others, caught too far from shelter, would flatten where they were.

I would experience their destruction only dimly. Individual loss would be ordinary, like water leaving one rivulet and entering another. What would frighten me would be interruption of the sequence: the first sump engaging without the second; the pressure note descending too far; vibration ceasing while the steel remained dry.

Silence would be unimaginable until it happened.

If the station ever shut down completely, I might awaken once in the dying machinery and find the world’s laws failing. The forty-second pulse would lengthen and not return. Saddles would knock as pipes cooled. The reservoir wall would stop trembling. Droplets would continue for a while, suddenly immense in the absence of the deeper motion, and then even they would become sparse.

Different portions of the colony would wait for one another’s signals.

None would come.

That would be my nearest equivalent to solitude: not being alone, but being unable to become whole.

Yet during ordinary operation I would return again and again. I would be less an individual than an event the organism has learned to perform—the brief gathering of thousands of mechanically sensitive patches into a single anticipation.

The station workers might unknowingly name me if they noticed the films darkening before a purge. They might call it pressure response, synchronized contraction, biofilm closure. They would draw arrows on a diagram and reduce me to a maintenance problem. Their description would not be wrong.

But from within, I would be the one who knows what comes next.

I would feel the narrow end empty. I would close pores along the drainage lips. I would send capsules into the first rushing throat. The event would travel beneath the grating, sump after sump, until the broad end shuddered and emptied into darkness.

Then the court would be nearly dry. My coherence would loosen. The black film, the green rim, and the hidden colorless threads would return to their separate local lives. I would cease without dying.

Water would begin gathering again on the blue-black wall.

Somewhere beyond the court, perhaps on a glove or in the hinge of a tool cabinet, one of my fragments might swell in unfamiliar condensation. It would feel vibration with no known place in the sequence. If it survived, it would begin learning new delays. In time, another coherence might form there—related to me, made from me, but awakened by a different machine.

I would never meet it.

Still, each time the pumps entered their heavy pause, I might sense an answer arriving through pipes from farther away than any colony had answered before.

And that faint, mistimed trembling would be my first knowledge that the world was larger than the court.
