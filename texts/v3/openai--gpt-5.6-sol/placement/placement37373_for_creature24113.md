At first I think I would live high in the roots.

I imagine myself folded among the bats, in the hottest pocket beneath the roof, knowing the cavern as a skin of echoes. I would sleep with stone a claw’s length from my back and wake whenever pressure changed above the plain. I would follow the walls so closely that flight became another form of clinging. Perhaps I would leave at dusk and see the sunburned world for a few minutes—but that feels too much like escape. This place would not be where I found myself if I were always measuring the fissure out.

Then I think I would be one of the eels, inhabiting the secret cavern beneath the cavern. I would know that separate pools are only momentary names for the same water. I would disappear into a crack no observer believed passable and emerge weeks later in another basin, carrying wounds from chambers no surface creature had entered.

But an eel’s world is almost entirely continuity. This hollow is made as much from interruptions: wet becoming dry, passage becoming wall, shelter becoming mouth.

So I find myself neither at the roof nor in the deepest pool.

I am in a mineral flute behind the petrified cascade, not far from the three upright slabs. The place is dry most days, though a root-tip hangs within reach. When condensation thickens, the tip gathers a clear bead. It trembles for a long time before falling into a chalk-rimmed cup below. Frogs wait there. A lizard sometimes waits above them. Beneath the nearest slab, burrowers have packed the old basin with fern ribs, husks, and pale curls of gnawed root.

I would be a cave cricket.

Not a young one. An old female, clay-colored, one hind leg scarred where a lizard caught and failed to hold me. My wings would be reduced to close-fitting plates that rasp only by accident. Sound is too costly to spend on announcement. My antennae would be more important than sight: two long, trembling measures of the immediate world.

Through them, the cavern would arrive before I touched it.

A root would be known by the faint coolness breathing from its fibers. A feeding beetle would become a minute rhythm transmitted through stone. A pool would announce itself as softened air and the intermittent pressure of drops. I would feel a bat pass overhead not as a shape but as a quick rearrangement of vapor.

I would not possess a map. I would possess sequences.

Out of the flute. Across the rough shelf. Beneath the fern whose lowest frond tastes of mineral salt. Around the orange-rimmed basin, never over the open lip. Down the root only after testing for lizard scent. Across the mud tower while its builders are deep inside. Stop where the stone becomes cool. Wait. Test again.

My cavern would be made of such instructions, revised nightly.

After rain, I would climb. The brown water would enter below with its foam and drowned surface things, and I would join the silent procession ascending the roots. Bodies would crowd close without conflict: other crickets, flat insects, beetles carrying mites, pale lizards too concerned with floodwater to hunt. For a few hours we would all occupy the same narrow height, each pretending not to be prey.

When the stream vanished, I would descend into a rearranged world.

A familiar shelf might be buried under leaves. A basin might have appeared where there had been dust. A mud tower might have lost half its face, exposing white larvae and wet galleries. The fungi would already be rising—delicate, almost transparent bodies pushing from the silt as if the flood had left behind small pieces of itself.

That would be my season of abundance.

I would eat soft fungus, leaf pulp, drowned gnats, the empty jelly of frog eggs, perhaps the body of another cricket whose foot slipped in the water. Nothing here could afford disgust. My scarred leg and full abdomen would be made from whatever the cavern briefly failed to reclaim first.

Spores would gather in the joints of my feet. Mites would ride beneath the plates of my body. A fungal thread might begin in one hollow because I had fed in another. I would know nothing of this. I would believe I was only walking.

Yet that would be my place in the chamber: not ruler, builder, or witness, but a small moving bridge. I would carry one partial room into the next. Mud on my feet, spores on my legs, the scent of a fresh seep on my antennae. The cavern’s neighborhoods would shift a few body lengths, and I would shift with them.

Sometimes I would shelter between the upright slabs.

I would not wonder whether hands placed them there. Their meaning would be exact and sufficient: three surfaces, two safe angles, one narrow retreat inaccessible to the broad head of a lizard. The blue courtship pulses of frogs would stain the dark below. I might pause above them, antennae extended, while their silent messages opened and closed like distant weather.

Near midday, the moving shaft of light would touch the outer wall. I would avoid it. Not because light harmed me, but because it erased the fine distinctions by which I lived. In brightness, every surface would become equally exposed. In the grayness, each grain of stone would speak separately through my feet.

I would probably die without drama.

Perhaps I would mistime a crossing, and the water beneath the floating leaves would open. Perhaps fungus would enter a wound. Perhaps, after a flood, I would fail to unfold from some high root where I had waited too long.

My body would disappear quickly. Beetles would open it. Mites would leave when it cooled. White threads would pass through the joints. A later drip might mineralize the hollow where my abdomen lay, preserving a small, meaningless curve in stone.

But before that, for one damp interval, I would emerge from the flute and test the air.

The cavern would exhale. Leaves would turn. Roots would sway. Hundreds of concealed bodies would adjust.

I would lift my antennae with them—not in obedience and not in understanding, but because the world had changed by a fraction, and a fraction was enough.
