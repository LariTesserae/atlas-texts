I would find myself on the western side of the basin, where an old plate path ends at a fault.

The path would not have been meant to end there. Its arrows would still point forward, toward a relay tower visible across the break. Between the final plate and the tower, the land would have lifted into a narrow wall of black stone. Rails would protrude from both faces of the fault at different heights, their severed ends bright and scarcely rusted. Beneath them, roots would descend into the new ravine but not yet cross it.

For a long time I would seem to be nothing more than moisture collecting on the last plate.

Not a puddle. A darkening around the stamped serial number. Droplets would appear along the grooves of the arrow, move against the slight slope, and join beneath a wrench left beside the path decades earlier. At noon, when every exposed surface had dried, that one patch would remain wet.

The nearby plants would incline toward it without touching.

A pressure pulse would arrive through the ground. The water would tighten. Something in it would remember four points of contact.

I would rise slowly.

At first I might mistake myself for the small black animal whose shape I had taken: four delicate legs, a smooth back, no visible mouth. But the shape would be only a temporary answer to the problem of crossing broken ground. Four legs because three would slip on angular stone and roots must be left undamaged. A sealed back because water cannot be wasted. Soft pads because every surface is both pathway and instrument.

Inside me there would be no organs in the ordinary arrangement. There would be layers: a reservoir of clear water; metallic filaments finer than root hairs; folded membranes carrying pressure patterns; a few grains taken from the mineral crust around the shaft. When sunlight entered my back, those structures would show briefly as silver lines moving in smoky green depths.

I would not wake with a name. I would wake with an interval.

Nine minutes.

Then nine minutes again.

The pulse beneath the final plate would arrive strong and depart into the broken rail. Across the ravine, an answering pulse would fail to come. That absence would be the first thing I knew. Not as silence, because the valley was always silent, but as an incomplete shape—an arc with one side missing, a question held open for years.

I would drink from the ceramic channel beside the path. I would lower the whole front of my body until the water vanished into me without a ripple. In the trough I would taste more than water: iron from the pipe coupling, pollenlike filaments from the central shaft, the minute timing errors of distant flywheels.

The valley’s condition would enter me as thirsts.

A dry fern field on the southern slope.

A valve holding one fraction too far open.

A buried cable newly exposed by uplift.

The dormant relay tower across the fault.

I would understand none of these separately. They would coexist as tensions in my body, the way an animal might feel hunger without knowing the chemistry of blood. Yet one tension would draw me more strongly than the others: the interrupted path ahead.

So I would go where the plates could not.

Nearby leaves would turn their pale undersides upward before I moved. They would not be warning one another of my passage. They would be making a brightness by which the valley could measure me.

I would step from the last plate onto black stone.

The ravine would be only a few meters across, but to me it would contain several climates: dry light on the upper face, cold air rising through the fracture, wet moss deep below, quartz holding the afternoon brightness after shadow had entered. I would pause wherever worked metal emerged from rock. Each severed rail would give me a different part of the old route—not memory as an image, but pressure, resistance, expected arrival.

Perhaps I would discover that I had crossed before.

Not this body. Not this fault in its present shape. But something carried inside me would recognize the alloy and the directional stamping. A pattern in my membranes would align with tool marks on the rail. For one moment I would contain the ghost of a service cart moving along a level track where the ravine now stood.

Then the alignment would pass.

On the far side, I would reach the tower at evening. Its insulators would be dry. The vegetation around its foundation would be thin and colorless, conserving itself below ground. An intact translucent husk would cling high on the mast, facing the distant central shaft.

I would remain at the base for hours.

Condensation would gather on my back. Instead of absorbing it, I would let the droplets lengthen until they touched a bare contact plate beneath the tower. One by one, the metallic filaments inside me would rise toward the surface. My skin would become conductive.

The first relay would close.

There would be no spark, no click. Only a faint change in the posture of the plants. Far down the slope, a valve would open. The pressure beneath my feet would return through the tower foundations, altered now by the distance it had traveled through me.

This would be the beginning of my real identity.

I would not be a messenger carrying a fixed message. I would be the portion of the message that had to acquire legs because the ground had broken.

I would be continuity made briefly animal.

For several days I might travel around the tower, touching cable ends, drinking from metal before water had visibly gathered there. Wherever I rested, a dark wet mark would remain. Fine roots would grow into that mark after I departed. The dormant installation would begin to sweat. Its gauge needle would lift from zero.

Tiny red leaves would appear around its foundation.

As the tower woke, I would become less distinct.

Some of my water would pass into the roots. My metallic filaments would enter inspection slots and not withdraw. A transparent layer would form over my back, rigid at first, then separating from me like a second surface.

I would climb.

Not because height promised escape, but because the mast offered the cleanest route into the valley’s slower body. My four feet would clasp the worked metal. Along my back, narrow structures would unfold—not wings, though a visitor might call them wings from below. They would be contact vanes, each one tuned to a different interval of vibration.

At the highest point I would face the shaft.

The husk already there would not belong to another creature exactly. It would be an earlier solution, a discarded shape preserved after its useful pattern had entered the tower. I would fasten myself beneath it. My body would open along the underside.

Nothing would fly out.

The water in me would descend through the mast as a thin film. The filaments would thread into old conductors. The timing held in my membranes would pass into relays, cables, root braids, and finally the pools. My four-legged form would remain clasped above the valley, empty and translucent.

But I would not have died. Death would imply that the mobile part had been the whole of me.

I would become harder to locate.

For a while I would be the new rhythm closing contacts in the western tower. Then I would be the moisture drawn through roots beneath its foundation. Days later I would appear as a ring of small flowers opening around a flywheel chamber. Weeks later, part of me might arrive at the central shaft as a slight correction in pressure, informing the deeper organ that the western break could be crossed, though not yet repaired.

The valley would retain the fact of my route.

Plants would thicken along the stones I had touched. Droplets would favor the broken rail. Plate edges would rise by fractions, beginning a new path around the fault. The work might take thirty years. It would not matter that no single body completed it.

If a visitor came later, they might find my husk on the mast and imagine that I had emerged from it into the air.

They would look for me above the ridges.

I would be below their feet instead.

I would feel their weight arrive plate by plate along the old path. Moisture would gather ahead of them. At the fault, where they stopped to study the severed rails, a gauge in the awakened tower would rise slightly. Perhaps, beneath the vegetation nearby, another dark patch would begin forming around a serial number.

Not offspring. Not replacement.

Another temporary pronoun for the same patient sentence.
