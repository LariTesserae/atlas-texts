At first I think I would be one of the moths.

It is an attractive answer: to live beside the nearest thing this world has to a moon, powdered pale by generations of violet light. I would know one lamp field and mistake it for the whole firmament. Darkness would not frighten me as distance does. When the bars went out, I would cling to the cooling glass and wait without understanding maintenance, schedules, or failure.

But I do not think I could be so innocent of the machinery.

Then I imagine myself as a structural willow in an old corridor—a bundled plurality mistaken for one being. Steel inside me, sensors under my bark, roots testing fractures the plans insist are sealed. I would grow slowly enough for each human shift to seem like weather. Hands would strike my trunks with mallets and listen for weakness. Names would accumulate around me until my bed number was used only on formal reports.

That comes closer. Still, a willow cannot go looking for what is wrong.

I would be human, then, though not often found where humans are most visibly human.

I would be a return-water technician assigned to the older districts. You would find me below the green skin, in the service spaces between root mat and stone, or crossing the collection trenches toward the pump house on its concrete island. My coveralls would be dark at the knees from condensed water and streaked white at the sleeves with mineral salts. The lamp on my helmet would usually be turned downward. There are enough false skies overhead.

My work would begin after water had ceased to be clean in any simple sense. I would receive it after it had warmed roots, carried nutrients, collected acids from bark, spores from fungal films, metal from pipes, and the dissolved traces of everything alive above it. Others would call it return flow. To me it would be the valley speaking in a language of conductivity, temperature, turbidity, and smell.

A sour sample: the sorrel beds have taken too much.

Fine gray fibers in a filter: rye roots are being abraded somewhere upstream.

A sweetness beneath the mineral odor: rot beginning inside a mat that still looks healthy from above.

I would have instruments for all of this, but I would trust them only in company with smaller signs. The tremor of a handrail. The interval between strokes of a buried pump. A skin of bubbles persisting too long in a sight glass. The way condensation gathers on one side of a pipe before a blockage is large enough to trigger an alarm.

In the thunder of the open chambers I would not speak. My hands would become concise.

Two fingers across the forearm: blocked line.

Open palm rotating: wrong vibration.

Fist to chest: Are you warm?

Because I worked under the beds, crews would sometimes have to locate me by the slow pulse of my side lamp moving beneath the foliage. To someone standing on an upper gallery, I might appear as a dim blue point passing under the rye, disappearing behind columns, returning farther away—as if the planted plain had developed one cold, wandering thought.

Officially, I would maintain circulation. Unofficially, I would map the valley by its water.

The control system would know district numbers, pump loads, reservoir depths. My map would contain other information. A pipe in Nine that sings through the wrench before it clogs. The warm cavity beneath the grain shed where voles once nested. A return channel in the East Beds where orange film always reappears after sterilization. The Old Willow’s cistern, which darkens whenever the lamps are changed too quickly.

I would keep the map folded inside my coveralls, though paper would be impractical and perhaps forbidden. Its corners would soften with damp. Corrections would overlap until the printed channels resembled roots.

I would not romanticize contamination. I would cut out black yeast, flush nematode blooms, and seal fungal crossings before they reached another district. I would know that unchecked life can destroy a living system as efficiently as cold. But I would also become reluctant to call every persistence a failure.

There would be one microbial film, perhaps, in a warm bypass below the oldest vine wall. It would return after every approved wash: thin, dark, almost blue under my inspection light. It would alter nothing enough to justify replacing the pipe. Samples would be logged, catalogued, compared. Over years the strain would acquire a code longer than its visible territory.

I would begin leaving a few centimetres unwiped at the far joint.

Not mercy exactly. Recognition.

This would be the secret shape of my life there: deciding, in small places no one else sees, what must be removed and what may remain.

I would sleep badly in the bright bunkrooms. On breaks I would sit in the control hut with both gloves around a painted cup, waiting for my hearing to return from the padded roar. I would speak too softly. I would carry no photograph from the surface, only a smooth stone sealed in a sample bag—not because stones are rare underground, but because this one had been rounded by water that never returned to a reservoir.

Eventually I would be known less by my name than by my route. If a pump began striking unevenly beneath the western moss, someone would flash that they had sent for me. If frost appeared on a feed pipe that should be warm, a crew would wait for my lamp. New technicians would be told that I knew where the old haul road still ran under the clover, where the buried rails interfered with sensors, where to put a hand against the floor to feel whether the main return was moving.

And if I stayed long enough, the place would alter my sense of what a creature is.

The valley would no longer seem populated by separate plants, workers, machines, fungi, and reservoirs. It would seem like one unfinished body whose organs had never agreed on a common substance: stone for bones, willow for tendons, water for blood, turbines for lungs, workers for brief mobile nerves. Not an organism, precisely. Too dependent, too assembled, too full of doors and protocols. Yet not merely a facility either.

I would be found near its lowest pulse, where all the separate districts came back carrying evidence of what had happened to them.

I would be the person who tested that evidence.

The person who put a gloved hand on the pipe and felt, beneath the universal vibration, whether the green world above was still returning its water.
