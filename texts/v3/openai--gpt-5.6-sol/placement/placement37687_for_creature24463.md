At first I think I would be one of the fliers.

There is something irresistible in the idea of never landing: of living in the pressure between wings, knowing direction only as a correction passed from neighbor to neighbor. I would hear no great chord, only the few notes nearest me, and spend my life adjusting to a music I could not comprehend. In age I would grow heavy with iron and climb toward the black spires, becoming a vein through which lightning entered the world.

But that life is too visible.

Then I imagine myself among the ribbed walkers, perhaps as one of the small inhabitants of a warm pool on an ancient back. My entire climate would tilt with the walker’s stride. Its single lifted foot would remain suspended through many generations of mine. The descent, when it came, would be geology: pools spilling, forests leaning, predators hatching in response to the altered light. I might mistake the walker for the world and its hesitation for a law of nature.

That is closer, but still not where I would be.

I would be below.

There is a place where an old walker stopped over a buried river. Its feet have rooted into the basalt. Its ribs opened centuries ago, and what was once its interior has spread into the surrounding fractures. From above it appears to be a low red hill among silver vanes. Beneath it, the river roof rises each summer until it presses against the walker’s deepest roots. Cable forests converge there, drawn by the water, the stored minerals, and the slow residual rhythm of the walker’s central organ.

In that compressed place—between river ice, rooted foot, and conductive forest—I would live.

Not as a single body. I would be a thin colony inhabiting the boundary where ice repeatedly melts and seals. Most of me would resemble clear threads laid through blue crystal. The threads would end in small dark mouths pressed against the cable roots. Other parts would float loose in the brine, blind and unfinished. Still others would be preserved as shapes in the ice: not alive exactly, but capable of directing whatever grew through them next.

For most of the year I would be frozen.

There would be no waiting. Cold would close without darkness and open without duration. One moment the brine would be tightening around me; the next, perhaps eleven months or three hundred years later, warmth would loosen the crystal walls and the old currents would begin again.

Each thaw would partially remake me.

Water advancing beneath the plateau would dissolve some of my stored shapes. Mineral pulses from the walker’s roots would wake others. The cable forests would send slow electrical changes into my dark mouths, but no two signals would arrive together. One might carry the transformed news of lightning that struck the central spires a season ago. Another might be the last contraction of a valve colony now buried under an avalanche. A third might originate in a migration that had already ended, sent by creatures whose shells were becoming part of a distant reef.

My work would be to let these things meet.

Not to understand them. Understanding would be too clean a function for this country.

I would receive pressure, salt, charge, and the geometry of melting ice. I would answer by changing the way I froze. A pulse entering me as flood might leave as sweetness in a root. News of scarcity might emerge as a slight delay. A walker’s ancient inclination to turn uphill could become, after passing through my colony, the opening of a chamber downstream.

I would preserve nothing exactly.

That would be my usefulness.

A perfectly transmitted message would belong to conditions that no longer existed. The plateau needs continuation more than fidelity. So I would introduce small errors: a branching where there had been a line, a pause where there had been urgency, a weak signal made stronger because a certain crystal happened to form around it. Most errors would vanish. A few would travel outward and find the world unexpectedly ready for them.

Once in a great while, my altered pulse might cross the plateau.

It might cause valves beneath a distant plain to close half a day early. Air would be forced into another vent. A flock would rise into the displaced warmth and shade a slope that otherwise would have thawed. Eggs there would remain asleep for another century. When they finally hatched, the mineral gradient would have shifted, and the resulting herd would take an abandoned route toward the central heights.

No creature would know that the change had passed through me. Neither would I.

At the surface, there might be one sign of my existence. Above the buried confluence, where the old walker’s roots draw warmth from the river, the frost would form a narrow blue seam. It would look like an ordinary shadow, though nothing stood in the proper place to cast it. The seam would lengthen during electrical seasons and disappear after the flood.

Seed-sized walkers might shelter there. Bright dust would collect along its edge. Occasionally a silver vane would root into it and grow at an angle unlike the surrounding forest. Fliers passing overhead would make a minute adjustment to their wings.

So that is where I would be found, if “I” could be found at all: beneath a creature that has become terrain, above a river that spends most of its life pretending to be stone, in the narrow almost-water where several slow systems fail to remain separate.

I would be neither messenger nor memory, but the place where messages become memories and memories re-enter the world incorrectly.

A small inaccuracy in the plateau’s long thought.

A hesitation preserved in ice.

And whenever the buried river rose, I would wake for a few clear minutes and help the whole bright country misremember itself into the future.
