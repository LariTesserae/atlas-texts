At first I think I would live high in one of the towers.

I imagine a narrow room above the level where mist can reach, its walls clearing at night so I can watch condensation assemble on the city’s upper surfaces. I would wake when the first drops began their descent and record which channels received them. Perhaps I would be a water-keeper, trusted with a district’s thirst: opening membranes, brushing mineral growth from capillaries, deciding which gardens must wait.

But the room is too far from the ground. The city’s water can be measured there, not understood. Before long the image lowers itself.

Now I am beneath an elevated road, where its shadow arrives several seconds after the structure that casts it. There is a dwelling tucked into the retaining bank: not exactly underground, but entered by descending through blue-black ferns. Its front room is an acoustic chamber lined with porous stone. Its rear wall is warm and faintly damp. Red groundcover grows across the ceiling because a data conduit passes overhead.

That feels nearer.

Still, I do not think I would remain entirely in one dwelling. I would sleep there for part of the year, keep clothes in a tower garden, and maintain another small room at the end of a bridge whose destination is visible only at certain humidities. My archive would be stored beneath a reed bed in ceramic cylinders, each sealed against flood but meant to be opened easily. Nothing of mine would be gathered enough to be lost all at once.

I would be human, or would have begun that way.

My body would show few dramatic alterations. A transparent inner eyelid. Soles widened and softened until I could distinguish an approaching transit capsule from a shifting clay layer. Along my ribs, faint branching marks would darken when the buried systems released heat. In one wrist I would have the ordinary civic organ for speech and warning. In the other, something less standard: a graft of warm-wall film, fungal threads, and pressure-sensitive tissue that continued to change long after it was installed.

Its purpose would have been navigation.

Its consequence would be kinship.

I would be one of the people responsible for finding others after the city rearranged itself.

There might be an official title for this work, but it would be rarely used. Children would call us **afterfinders**. Older residents might use a hand-tapped name meaning *one who keeps nearness from depending on distance*. We would be summoned when a household’s rooms had drifted apart, when a familiar causeway began arriving somewhere unfamiliar, or when a person walked out of an acoustic passage and did not emerge from any of its mapped exits.

We would not search by following fixed streets. Fixed streets would be the least reliable evidence.

Instead, I would begin with traces of attachment. A cup repeatedly held by the missing person. The adjustment note of the tower where they once slept. A starter culture exchanged at a marriage. A pressure rhythm left in the floor by a habitual gait. I would place my altered wrist against these things and let their accumulated tendencies enter me—not memories exactly, but preferences of matter: this warmth once leaned toward that hand; this wall often opened sooner for that voice; this path remained firm beneath that particular distribution of weight.

Then I would walk.

My maps would be translucent and mostly empty, showing fields of likelihood rather than routes. I would travel at the changing hours: just before the mist rose, while roads were reconsidering their permeability; just after nocturnal rain, when temporary streams revealed the inclinations of the ground; at dawn, while fresh walls were still warm enough to remember what had persuaded them to form.

Sometimes I would find the lost person nearby, separated by a building that had developed an unplanned interior. Sometimes they would be kilometers away, having followed a whisper through an arch. Sometimes no person would be missing at all. A household would merely have ceased to agree on where “home” was, and I would be asked to discover whether its dispersed rooms still formed one life.

That would be the harder work.

I would sit in each room with my wrist against the floor. I would listen to reservoir valves, fungal signals, family pulse records, the minute expansions of walls after sunset. I would ask each inhabitant for all their names: spoken, tapped, and illuminated. Then I would carry those names through the city until I found places where they still produced an answer.

If enough answers remained, I would say the household persisted.

If they did not, I would not call it broken. I would say it had branched.

Over time, the work would alter me beyond its original design. Routes I traveled often would begin to anticipate my steps. Transit lines would pause for me whether or not I intended to board. Certain acoustic arches would pass my whispers farther than those of other people. Red flowers above buried conduits would open in small sequences when I approached.

There would be arguments about this.

Some would say my graft had contaminated the infrastructure. Some would say the city had mistaken me for a maintenance process. A few would insist that every afterfinder eventually became less a resident than a path wearing a resident’s body.

I would deny this in public and suspect it in private.

The survey kites would notice me eventually. One would follow for several weeks, never directly overhead. Its thread would hang above places I had just left: a tea court, a temporary pool, the blank center of a grand plaza. At last it would suspend its thread over me.

I would receive no message. Only the sensation of several lives touching.

In one, I would remain fully human and abandon the work before it dispersed me. In another, I would surrender my biological body to a decomposition garden and release my gait into the city’s paths. In a third, there would no longer be a meaningful distinction between those outcomes. My body would continue to eat, speak, grieve, and sleep, while some other portion of me traveled through ceramic channels and responsive pavement, helping doors recognize those who needed shelter.

After that encounter, I would change my primary name.

The new spoken name would be brief and soft, safe under most arches. Its hand-name would be a sequence like two footsteps, a pause, and a third step arriving from another direction. In light, it would be three pale lines that never quite met but brightened together.

I would still have a face. I would still drink tea, stain my sleeves with condensation, and keep foolish objects longer than necessary. Children would bring me spoons and door latches so the six-legged waders might gather near. I would have people whom I loved, though I might be unable to say whether I found them because I loved them or loved them because I could always find them.

When I died, if death remained the correct word, my body would be laid beneath a temporary pool.

The waders would stand above the capsule and place their delicate feet on its surface. My pressure records would be distributed among paths in districts where I had worked. My voice would be released into an arch, but not as words—only as a quiet indication of direction. For years afterward, people who had become separated might feel a slight warmth beneath one heel or notice mist flowing against the slope of the ground.

They would follow it.

So I think this is where I would find myself: not in the center of the city, because the city has no dependable center, but among its changing intervals—beneath roads, beside new ponds, inside the long uncertainty between departure and arrival.

And I would be a person gradually becoming a means of return.
