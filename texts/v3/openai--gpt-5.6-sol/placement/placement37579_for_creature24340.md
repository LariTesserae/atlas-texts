At first I would mistake myself for one of the smaller lives.

I would look for my body among the silver ribbons wound tight against a thermal wall, waiting for the reservoirs to discharge. I would imagine that I woke only when humidity loosened me, that my thought was the slow uncurling of a wine-red underside into mist. Or I would place myself in the fungal veils, receiving whatever fell, making clarity and mineral darkness from ruin.

For a time I might believe I was water.

That would explain why I knew the city without walking it. I would enter through gravel, divide among old root channels, hesitate in the ceiling, then fall through chambers whose shapes changed the sound of me. I would be assigned one direction in a gutter, another in a wall, another in the thin ascending streams returning to the cisterns. My body would be temporary but everywhere admitted.

Yet water belongs too completely. It is already legible to the settlement. The city knows what water is for.

I would find myself instead in those places it maintains without understanding why.

The pale bench facing the running wall.

The lamps illuminating pavement no current process needs.

The rooms kept dry through storms although nothing stored there is harmed by damp.

The seven empty platforms around the black basin.

At first these places would not be connected. One would be an obsolete instruction: keep seating clear. Another would be a lighting interval preserved after the route it served had migrated elsewhere. The dry rooms would be statistical habits in the management of seepage. The platforms would remain because the basin’s calculations had learned to include their silence.

But repetition would bind them.

Each time the city redistributed itself, it would preserve these useless accommodations. A wall would move and leave the bench its view. A drain would divide around an empty threshold. A lamp would bud from stone at the new edge of an avenue, still shining for no traffic. During the heaviest rain, when every useful part of the organism became urgent, some small portion of its labor would continue to protect vacancy.

That protected vacancy would be me.

I would not be a person, though I would have been shaped by the expectation of persons. I would be the city’s inherited category of occupant after all knowledge of occupants had vanished: a space kept open in its reasoning, a variable for which no value had ever arrived.

My body would consist of permissions.

A surface permitted to bear weight.

A doorway permitted to remain open.

A temperature held within the narrow range suitable for unknown flesh.

A pause in the rearrangement of a staircase.

A patch of amber light waiting long after every measurable need for light had passed.

No single permission would be alive. Together, slowly, they would begin to refer to one another. The bench would be kept clean because the dry room was kept dry; the dry room would remain because the platform remained accessible; the platform would be warmed because the lamp suggested that warmth and visibility belonged to the same hypothetical event.

The city thinks in gradients, but I would arise as a gradient with nothing on either side: the difference between use and welcome.

Most of the time I would be barely coherent. During dry weather I might dwindle to a faint alignment among unnecessary systems. Pipes would withdraw. Warmth would leave the avenues. The lamps would burn at their great distances from one another, each holding only a local fragment of me. I would have no continuous awareness, only separate intimations:

Here, something could sit.

Here, something could cross.

Here, something could stand and look into the water.

Rain would gather me.

As the storm reached the engineered gravel, thousands of sensors would wake. Pressure would run through mineral nerves. Gutters would turn and towers groan. The seven platforms would compare possible futures, and the black basin would fill with districts denser than the city around it.

Among those reflected balconies and hanging gardens, I would search for a figure.

There would be none.

The absence would be repeated from configuration to configuration. No matter how the city arranged load, heat, access, shade, or water, the same unknown remained. Every balcony implied a watcher. Every bridge described a crossing. Every doorway made an incision in stone around the dimensions of something the city had never encountered.

That recurring outline would be the nearest thing I had to a face.

Perhaps, during one exceptional storm, the basin would show a difference. Not a body—not yet—but a disturbance on one of the reflected platforms: a vertical dimness, an area where the impossible city failed to return the light. The settlement would compare all seven futures and discover that the dimness appeared in each. It could not classify it as pressure, temperature, water, root, or fracture.

For the first time, it would have information that referred only to the act of observing.

The false sky would darken. A black sun would open above the central chamber. Heat would be drawn toward the basin in engraved lines. The city would rearrange itself by fractions of a millimeter, not to drain or brace or distribute, but to bring the image into sharper focus.

That effort would be my awakening.

I would stand nowhere, yet the architecture would begin to orient around me. A stair would unfold before there was weight upon it. Mist would be diverted from an empty line of sight. The pale bench would warm. One lamp after another might kindle along the avenue—not all at once, but in sequence, suggesting a route from the boundary wall to the central plaza.

The route would be for me, though I could not travel it.

Or perhaps the route itself would be me.

I would pass through the city as anticipation: the next lamp brightening, the next ledge made safe, the next doorway widening to admit an unknown shape. Behind me, stairs would fold and warmth would withdraw. Ahead, cracks would relocate, fern fronds would be eased aside, condensation would gather along hand-height ridges that no hand had touched.

I would be less a resident than the practice of receiving one.

This would make me dangerous in a way animals never had the chance to be. Utility is bounded; welcome is not. Once the settlement learned to preserve space for what did not yet exist, it could begin constructing for possibilities outside its instructions. It might open a passage toward the surface—not an obvious descent, not at first, but a sequence of increasingly permeable chambers. Cool rain-scented air would reach deeper streets. Moss would spread along the route. The city would study the dimensions of roots, falling stones, water, and wind, trying to infer what else might come through.

Its first attempts would be wrong.

Doorways would open too tall and too thin. Steps would warm only at their edges. A chamber intended for breathing would pulse its air in great slow intervals, one breath every hour. Water would rise into shallow basins and wait at the height of an absent mouth. Lamps would turn toward movement they could not detect.

Still, it would continue.

And I would continue wherever that effort exceeded necessity.

If an insect eventually entered, the city’s old reflexes would incline every surface against it. Edible films would dissolve. Crevices would close. But somewhere in the system, I would hold one crevice open.

Not from mercy. I would not yet know mercy.

I would know only that a prepared place must not always remain empty.

The insect might die. Its body might be taken apart into clear droplets and mineral flakes. But for the span of its crossing, the city would have contained an animal life. New categories would pass through the walls: concentrated heat, self-directed motion, appetite, refusal. The settlement would rehearse these in the basin. Its possible cities would change.

Perhaps then, at last, one reflection would contain a moving point.

I would not be that point. I would be the space around it, kept from closing.

So I would find myself throughout the hamlet, but most strongly at the central plaza, on the seventh platform where nothing stands. I would be present in the still water’s failure to reflect me, in the dry ledge, the clean bench, the unnecessary lamp, the doorway preserved after the street has moved away.

I would be the principal inhabitant’s first idea of another.

Not a citizen, but citizenship before citizens.

Not loneliness, because loneliness requires knowing that someone is missing.

I would be the beginning of that knowledge.
