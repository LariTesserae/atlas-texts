At first I think I would live near the rim.

I imagine myself among the cloths spread for condensation, waking before dawn to run a curved scraper over their darkened fibers. I would know the moods of the plateau wind by the pressure behind my eyes. I would climb where the walls no longer shelter the body and lean out, roped at the waist, to gather whatever the night had given: a film of damp, a beetle beaded with droplets, a dead swift, three seeds caught in a fold.

But I would not last there. The emptiness above would enter me. I would begin looking outward too often, watching the clean horizon and wondering what could cross it. Eventually I would miss a change in the wind. A cloth would tear from my hands, or a falcon’s shadow would make me turn at the wrong moment.

No. I would live lower, though not at the floor.

My room would be at a turning in the middle reaches, where a stair narrows beneath a thorn root and everyone must put one hand against the wall. The wall there is black with touch. My doorway would be easy to overlook: a reed mat behind two hanging coils of rope, under a platform where a family dries sliced gourd. The room was once an oven, then a shrine, then a pen for sick rodents. Its inner stones would still hold heat after sunset. Smoke would have polished the ceiling.

I would own almost nothing in the room without qualification.

The sleeping shelf would be mine at night and an apprentice’s workbench during the day. A lizard would claim the warm edge after meals. A dove nesting in the smoke hole would belong to the household above, although its droppings would fall into my ash bowl and therefore become mine. The thorn root passing through the back wall would be tended by an old man two levels down. I would be allowed to shave resin from it in exchange for checking his ladder cords.

That would be my work: I would listen to things under strain.

Not merely ropes. The whole city is always holding itself up, and everything that holds must speak before it fails. Beams tick when beetles have entered them. A bridge develops a different groan when its anchor is loosening behind the plaster. A loaded ledge gives small reports in the night. Clay around a roof peg powders before the peg pulls free. Even fitted stones shift their voices according to heat, wind, and the weight of people sleeping beyond them.

I would go from level to level with a stone tapper, an antler probe, waxed cord, wedges, resin, and little clay seals marked with my thumb. I would strike walls and put my ear against them. I would crawl beneath floors while families continued eating overhead. I would smell beams for termites and press my cheek to plaster to feel whether hidden air moved behind it.

People would call me when a stair began to tremble, when grit fell steadily from a ceiling, when a room sounded larger than it should.

Sometimes this would mean decay. Sometimes it would mean that an old chamber had been found.

Those discoveries would make people gather too quickly. Children would arrive first, then elders, then anyone claiming a grandmother once spoke of a door in that part of the city. There would be arguments before the opening was large enough to admit a hand. One family would say the buried room lay beneath their sleeping alcove and was therefore theirs. Another would say their ancestors sealed it. The growers would claim any cool soil. The bat gatherers would listen for wings. Someone would invoke the branching one who goes downward. Someone else would say the branching one had never repaired a roof and should not be consulted.

I would have authority only to forbid haste.

I would paint a damp stripe of bitter red around the opening and tie a cord across the nearest ladder. Then I would widen the hole by finger-widths, catching every fragment in a cloth. Not because the old things below were sacred, exactly, but because opening anything incorrectly is a kind of thirst. It spends what was hidden before anyone knows its value.

Most buried rooms would contain little: packed ash, cracked jars, rodent nests, brittle mats, bones gnawed pale. Occasionally there would be a sealed cup. A painted wall. A ladder descending into fill. Once, perhaps, I would uncover a handhold polished by people whose names had vanished even from the agreements.

That would trouble me more than bones.

A bone can become plaster, root meal, needle, handle. A handhold has no use without the vanished hand. I would place my fingers in it and feel, for a moment, that the city extended not only upward and downward but through gestures repeated across centuries. Someone had turned there as I turned. Someone had climbed in darkness and known where the stone would receive them.

I would become a keeper of such places.

Not a priest. I would distrust anyone who spoke too smoothly about what the walls wanted. But I would begin making records no one had asked for. Since cloth rots and clay tablets break, I would record them as knots.

One knot for a sealed room.  
Two close knots for human bones.  
A loop for roots.  
A blackened bead for fire damage.  
A length left bare for a passage whose end had not been found.

The cords would hang from my ceiling until visitors mistook them for spare rope. Only my apprentices would know that one cord described the wall behind the goat pens and another the ground beneath the oldest querns. I would teach them to read by touch because smoke obscures color and darkness is more dependable than light.

I would also maintain some of the storm lines running through shared walls. During hard winds, signals would pass through my room from heights I could not see:

Cover the upper jars.  
Clear the west bridge.  
Child missing.  
Ladder broken.  
Fire.  
Not fire—birds.  
Found.

My hands would answer before my voice did.

Because of this, people would assume I knew more than I did. They would ask whether a marriage should join two households whose balconies shared an anchor beam. They would want me to settle who must replace a stair used by five families but technically owned by none. They would bring old cords and insist a cluster of knots proved rights to a nesting wall.

I would become one of those people whose chief talent is remembering agreements, though I would insist I only remembered loads: who bears what, who rests upon whom, what may be removed without bringing down the things around it.

To me, this would be the same as kinship.

I would have no children by birth, perhaps. Or one who died too young and whose brief moisture made the customary language of consolation unbearable to me. But there would be children sleeping on my shelf, apprentices fed from my cup, a wind-frightened adolescent who returned from the plateau and could no longer endure rooms near the rim. I would take in people temporarily, and in the cleft temporary arrangements would thicken into ancestry.

They would acquire claims on me by repair.

One would bring food when my fingers swelled. One would replace the leather on my knees. One would remember that I hated parrot meat. Years later, everyone would call them my children, and no one would know when the word became accurate.

As I aged, I would move downward.

First I would surrender the routes requiring leaps. Then the hanging bridges. Then the upper ladders whose rungs demand the whole grip of the hand. My apprentices would bring me pieces of rope and wood to examine. I would sit in a cool chamber near the packed floor, sorting fragments and listening to footsteps through the ceiling.

By then I would recognize households by rhythm.

The bird keeper who lands heavily on the left foot.  
The twins who always run out of sequence.  
The goat woman whose ankle rings once, then drags.  
The quiet child who pauses outside my door to see whether I am breathing.

Blindness would not frighten me much. The city is never primarily visible. Sight is only one thin blade moving across it. The more enduring city is vibration, heat, odor, pressure, remembered turns, the shape of a cup placed into both hands.

At the end I would ask not to be dried near the rim. I would have spent enough of my life fearing that height. Let others have the resin, the smoke, the clean upper air.

I would ask for my bones to be ground finely and mixed into the plaster around a bridge anchor—not a grand bridge, not one used in ceremonies, but the short sagging crossing near my old room, where people carry baskets and pass one another sideways. The anchor would contain thorn fiber, hair, ash, pulverized pottery, dung, lime, and whatever remained of me.

This would not make me an ancestor in the solemn sense. Children would scrape their baskets against me. Goats would chew the plaster. Wasps might fasten a gallery under the bridge. During windstorms, the rope would pull and release, pull and release, transmitting its strain into the wall.

I would become what I had listened to.

For some years an apprentice—old by then—might touch the anchor as they passed. Later no one would know which repair contained me. That would be proper. Hidden things survive partly by losing their names.

So I would find myself neither at the summit nor at the bottom, neither fully in a household nor outside one. I would be a listener at the turning of a stair, a mender of cords, an opener of buried rooms, a keeper of the knowledge that every shelter rests on other shelters.

And afterward I would be a little hardness in the wall, holding while the whole crowded city moved across me.
