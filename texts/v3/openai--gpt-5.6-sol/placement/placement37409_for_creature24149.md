At first I think I would be found high in the banyans.

It is the tempting place: above the corrugated roofs and conveyor housings, where the town briefly resolves into a shape. I imagine myself crossing from limb to limb with an inspector’s cord around my waist, touching numbered plaques greened at the edges, tapping trunks to hear whether they answer solidly. From there I could see the levee road, the plantations beyond the canal, perhaps even a clean line of weather approaching.

But I do not think I would belong to the height. Height is too detached. This town is understood from underneath.

You would find me at canal level, lying flat in a narrow punt with my face turned toward the undersides of floors.

I would be an old sounder of pilings and finder of lost water: one of the people called when a room begins to tilt, when brown seepage appears behind a wall, when a drain runs backward despite every visible grate being clear. Not an engineer exactly, though the mill office might list me as one. Not merely a dredger or carpenter either. My trade would concern all the things the town has built over and then forgotten.

I would carry a short iron probe, a mallet, wedges, chalk sealed in waxed cloth, and a notebook whose pages can be removed one by one when damp takes them. More useful than any of these would be a memory trained to join incomplete signs.

A vibration in a kitchen floor might lead to a cracked flume beneath a dye room. A colder thread of water against my wrist might tell me that an old channel, closed before I was born, had opened again under the peat. A row of bubbles beside a root could mean rot, trapped gas, or a pump drawing air three buildings away. I would know the difference by smell, rhythm, and what hour of the shift it was.

People would summon me by knocking on the nearest pipe: three measured blows, a pause, then two. Children would repeat the signal as a game until someone told them sharply not to call what they did not need.

My punt would be so patched that no one remembered its first boards. Its bow would fit passages newer boats could not enter. I would move it by pulling on joists and roots overhead, sometimes with only a hand’s width between my chest and the green-black water. Above me, meals would be served, accounts argued over, belts restarted, babies put to sleep. Flour dust and wash water would fall through the cracks. Voices would come through the planks as softened pressure rather than words.

There, the town would have another anatomy.

The public walks would become ceilings. Houses would stand on dark forests of piles, each one furred at the waterline and marked by old floods. Pipes would pass through root chambers. Forgotten stairs would descend into nothing. I would find the brick lip of a courtyard buried beneath a workshop, or a mooring ring embedded under someone’s bed, proof that boats once came where walls now stand.

I would not draw clean maps. Clean maps would lie.

Instead I would keep layered ones: channels known to be open, channels believed closed, channels that wake only in flood; roots that carry weight; roots that merely appear to; floors raised three times; piles sistered by workers whose names survive only in the shape of their bolt heads. Beside uncertain places I would write stories:

*Water came through here in the year the western boilers failed.*

*Ask Mara, who remembers fish under the old dispensary.*

*Do not cut this root even if it narrows the passage.*

My home would be above one of the quieter drains, in two rooms built into the outer chamber of a banyan. The tree would have taken part of the first room before I grew old. Rather than move the wall again, I would have surrendered the corner. A broad root would pass indoors beneath a shelf of tins, polished where shoulders brushed it. During rain, moisture would gather on its bark and creep down to the floor.

I would sleep lightly, but not because of the mills. Their ordinary pounding would hold sleep in place. I would wake when a familiar vibration disappeared.

For a few moments I would lie still and count what remained: the generating-house thud, the soft chatter of the roof drain, the distant knock of rollers, water against the piles. If the absent sound did not return, I would put my feet into soft shoes, take the lamp from its hook, and step onto the walk. Other lamps would already be appearing. No alarm would need to be shouted. We would look toward one another, judge the silence, and begin moving.

I think I would be old enough that younger crews found me inconvenient. I would insist on sounding one more pile, opening one more floor panel, checking the abandoned route rather than the obvious one. I would be slow climbing out of the punt. My fingers would no longer close easily after years in canal water.

But when someone proposed driving a new footing through a place that looked empty, I would know there was an iron pipe below it, still carrying overflow from six roofs. When a tree surveyor found compression in an old prop root, I would remember that the root had swallowed a warehouse brace forty years earlier and that cutting into it would expose rusted metal. My usefulness would lie partly in knowledge and partly in suspicion—the conviction that nothing beneath the town is ever entirely gone.

Children would sometimes come with me along the safer channels. I would teach them not to put their hands where they could not see, not to trust still water beside a running pump, and not to mistake a newly painted board for a sound one. I would make each child close their eyes and listen while I struck three pilings: one good, one hollowing, one carrying vibration from a machine elsewhere.

They would guess badly at first.

Eventually one would say, “That last one isn’t its own sound.”

That would be the beginning.

Perhaps, after many years, I would become less a person than a local instrument. People would consult me the way they consulted flood cuts or the lean of a tree. They would say I was gloomy because I always found the hidden failure. Yet I would not think of myself as a bearer of bad news. A failure found early is only work. It is the unseen failure that becomes disaster.

And when I died, I would not want a plaque high on a trunk.

I would ask for a narrow bridge over some minor channel, one people crossed daily without thinking. It would be made from good salvaged timber, set slightly higher than the old flood mark. Beneath one end, the builders would leave a removable board so the grate could still be reached.

My name might be cut into the handrail, though damp and palms would soften it. Eventually the letters would fill with moss. Children would sit there with their feet above the water. Workers would lean loads against it. During a storm, someone would wrench up the loose board and clear a clot of roots and pennywort before the backed-up water entered a house.

That would be enough of a memorial.

So I would be human, I think—but only barely separate from my function: a small, damp, listening part of the town’s maintenance. I would live where structure becomes water, where memory becomes infrastructure, and where what seems solid reveals that it has always been moving.
