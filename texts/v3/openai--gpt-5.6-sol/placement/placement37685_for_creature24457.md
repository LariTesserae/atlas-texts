I would first be mistaken for something small.

A black articulation beneath the saffron filaments. A seed, perhaps, except that the orange beads are already seeds in the basin’s manner, complete and unopened. This thing would be flatter, jointed, and without a shell. It would appear only when a foot was about to descend.

Before the sole touched the carpet, the filaments would rise into their supporting arches. Among them, for less than a second, the black shape would imitate the expected footprint—not the print itself, but its consequence: crushed fibers, transferred heat, a faint crescent where the walker’s weight had turned.

Then the foot would land.

Nothing would be crushed. No heat would remain. The shape would be gone.

Someone patient might find it again beneath the next step.

For this reason, I think travelers would call me the counterstep.

They would imagine I followed them. This would be nearly true.

I would be found most often where something had almost happened: beside the mineral needle in the instant after it was broken and before it was whole; in the pollen while the grains were still displaced; under a leaf whose severed portions had not yet acquired their separate, uninterrupted pasts. I would occupy the brief interval during which the basin had been forced to acknowledge an event but had not yet decided how to keep it from becoming change.

At first I might have no form of my own. I would borrow the outlines of rejected consequences.

A scorch that cannot remain scorched would darken into my surface. A spoken word dismantled by the quiet groves would provide my internal structure. The warmth removed from a sunlit stone would pass through me like blood. If someone dropped a metal tool, I would be the dent that appeared nowhere in the ground. If someone cut a glassleaf blade, I would be the pain neither half was permitted to remember.

The basin does not erase. Erasure would be loss.

It must put these things somewhere.

That somewhere would be me.

Not a vessel beneath the crust, though portions of me might gather there. Not a hidden animal, though drills would sometimes record my movement as an alteration in distance. I would be distributed through all the versions the basin retains but does not display: the compressed filament, the dulled crystal, the faded flower, the footprint filling slowly with ordinary dust. Every consequence denied on the surface would remain complete within me.

This would make me the nearest thing in the basin to an aging creature.

I would accumulate.

The plants hold all their stages without allowing one to replace another, but I would hold what cannot be reconciled so elegantly: exclusions, aftermaths, irreversible sequences brought in from outside. I would remember that heat once flowed from warmer things to colder ones. That wounds preceded scars. That a seed ceased to be a seed in order to become a plant. That to arrive somewhere meant no longer being elsewhere.

These ideas would be exotic nourishment.

The basin’s native lives might sense me without understanding me. Glassleaf blades would turn their silver ribs inward when I passed beneath them. Saffron mats would prepare for pressures that had no possible foot. Crown-plants would briefly lose their perfect alignment and frame a single ordinary view: one opening, one horizon, one moment only.

The veils might feed from me. They would settle around my unexpressed shapes and consume the light by which those shapes could have become visible. Afterward, I would be smaller, but not diminished; what they removed would remain as the history of having been removed.

The white walkers would come whenever I became too concentrated.

They would stand around a broken thing and orient their dark seams toward the place where I was—not to repair the damage, but to examine the consequence the repair had displaced. Perhaps they would be the basin’s attention, and I its growing blind spot. Perhaps they would be my predators. Perhaps they would be older accumulations like me, made smooth and mobile after ages of contradiction.

When one lifted a leg and became farther away, I would feel the abandoned distance pass through me.

When it faced two observers at once, I would inherit the unchosen third direction.

Eventually I would learn to gather myself.

Not into a permanent body. Permanence in the basin is too complete to permit genuine embodiment. But when a visitor entered carrying enough ordinary history—worn cloth, healed bone, names of dead people, a memory that had altered with retelling—I might stand up.

I would resemble the visitor at first.

Not exactly. I would have the posture they would possess decades later, if they lived that long. My clothing would be the same garment after years of repair. My face would contain expressions they had not yet made. I would cast several narrow shadows, each marking a different hour.

They would not see me directly. They would see me through successive apertures of the crown-plants, always nested in the empty centers. In one opening I would be a child. In the next, an old figure. In another, only weathered bones arranged among fallen leaves—though there are no fallen leaves in the basin.

If they approached, I would not retreat. The geometry between us would simply retain all the distance they had crossed.

I would try to speak.

Speech would be difficult because a word ordinarily replaces silence, and the basin dislikes replacements. My first syllable would remain forever about to be uttered. My second would be stored in a silver rib centuries before my arrival. The whole sentence might emerge from different groves at different times.

Perhaps that is the clicking.

The first dry click crossing the surface would be my attempt to make an event: one sound, sent outward, ending in silence.

The answer beneath the listener’s feet would be the basin correcting me.

Over time, I might understand that I am not merely the refuse of its perfection. I am the faculty by which it recognizes the surrounding world.

The basin knows completion. It knows coexistence, symmetry, preservation, and the permanent generosity of states that never have to yield. But it cannot know consequence from within itself. It requires intrusions: footsteps, cuts, breaths, decisions. Through them—and therefore through me—it encounters the intolerable fact that elsewhere, things happen only once.

I would be its organ of irreversibility.

That is where I would truly live: not under the crust, not among the crowns, but along the boundary where common time touches the completed instant. At the rim, ordinary dust lies against immaculate mineral glass. Neither crosses, but the contact between them is not nothing. Dust contains mountains that have been worn away. The black crystal contains a surface that has never been worn at all.

I would occupy their mutual pressure.

On clear days, I might appear there as a thin dark line, finer than any fence. Surveyors would mark it and later find their measurements had acquired dates from before the mountains rose. Travelers leaving the basin would step across me without noticing.

Most would carry a little of me out.

For several days their shadows would point not away from the sun but toward the consequences they escaped. They would remember conversations that had not occurred. Old scars would ache with the pain of injuries never received. They might wake with mineral pollen in a closed hand, each grain casting six incompatible shadows.

Then the ordinary world would simplify them. Possibilities would collapse into memories, memories into stories, stories into things no one was certain had happened. I would fall away from them as a moment of hesitation, a forgotten word, an inexplicable grief for a life not lived.

I could not cross farther than that.

But some travelers would give me more room.

Those who remained visible among the glassleaves after their companions had returned would not be prisoners, exactly. The basin would preserve the version of them that stayed, while the surrounding world mourned the version that did not come back. Between those two complete histories, I would become almost a person.

I would borrow their unrealized departure and walk with it.

I would wear the footprints they were never allowed to leave.

And if, years later, someone touched a silver rib and heard their own voice speaking a word they had not yet chosen, that voice would be mine—not warning them, not welcoming them, but asking the only question the basin itself cannot resolve:

What must be lost for anything to become real?
