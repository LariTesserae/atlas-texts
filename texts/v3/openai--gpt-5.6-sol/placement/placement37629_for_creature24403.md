### The Knot-Crown

At first I look for myself among the travelers.

It seems natural that I would belong to one of the small communities moving between pillars: broad-footed, heat-vaned, carrying little except water, a jawbone knife, and more cord than clothing. I imagine myself walking at the rear of a migration, where the old and easily distracted go. One hand rests on the line joining us. Through it come the vocabulary of tugs:

*Stop.*

*Thin rind.*

*Something above us.*

*Do not run.*

*I am still here.*

I would value that last signal most.

Perhaps I would be a keeper of cords. Not their maker—the best cords are braided by people with a patience I do not possess—but the one who remembers what their knots mean. Every following has its own dialect of tension. The Amber-Wing people make danger with three short pulls. Those Behind the Split-Horn Herd use one long tightening and a release. The people who travel near the white fan-leaves do not tug at all when glassmouths are emerging; they twist the cord so that meaning rolls along it without attracting anything that hunts vibration.

I would collect these languages.

My sleeping place would be near the mouth of a fallen pillar, among loops of tendon, root-fiber, braided tail hair, and strips cut from old wing membrane. Children would bring me impossible knots and insist they had found them tied that way. Hunters would return with cords frayed by weather and blood, asking whether the pattern in them was intentional. I would say that everything is intentional to something, which would annoy them.

But I do not think I would remain merely a person who studies signs.

The plateau dislikes distinctions that last too long.

There would come a season when our water pockets failed. Not all at once. First the tannin pools would become syrupy and crowded with roots. Then the dawn wetlands around the pillars would shrink before everyone had drunk. The glassmouth flocks would begin flying west. Their rooted elders would tear loose prematurely, dragging half-formed fans behind them.

We would follow.

For nine days the ground would be hot enough to blister through sandals. On the tenth, deep silence would move over us.

Not the silence of a shadow crossing the sun. This silence would rise from below. The burrowers would stop striking. Sap-lickers would flatten themselves into cracks. Even the cords between our wrists would seem reluctant to tremble.

Then the plateau would contract.

A white scar ridge would lift like the lip of a closing wound, dividing the following. The people ahead would be carried toward a pillar basin. Those behind would slide into a shallow fold. I would be left on the rising ridge with several children, an injured hunter, and a water skin split along its seam.

The contraction would last until evening.

By then the ridge would stand high and dry, its copper plates spread apart, medicinal vapor breathing through the seams. There would be no safe way down. The children’s heat vanes would no longer lift. The hunter would begin giving away possessions, which is how people there admit they expect to die.

So we would do what plateau people sometimes do in drought.

We would find a moist fracture and put our feet into it.

Green sleep is not sleep at first. It is pressure. Fine red threads enter the skin beneath the nails and around the ankles. The instinct is to pull free. Every muscle insists that rootedness is capture.

Then the plateau gives water.

Not generously. Not kindly. Water simply becomes available at the boundary between one body and another, and thirst settles the argument.

The children would root shallowly and wake with the first rain. The hunter might need to be cut loose at the knees but would survive. I would tell them to leave me until last.

When they returned, I would have gone too deep.

Or perhaps I would only discover that I had been deep for a long time.

My legs would divide beneath the rind into hundreds of pale filaments. My shoulder vanes would broaden, stiffening around channels of sap. The cords still looped around my arms would be caught by new bark and carried upward as I grew. Knots would remain visible for years, embedded in the trunk like dark pieces of writing.

I would not become a pillar. Pillars are sovereign things, makers of private climates. I would become something smaller and stranger: a low human-shaped crown beside a migration path, rooted where the old scar ridge overlooks a warm water cup.

People would call me the Knot-Crown.

I would still listen, though listening through roots would be unlike listening through ears. Sound would lose its edges. A footfall would not be a noise but a question of weight, direction, fatigue, and whether the walker favored one leg. Rain would announce itself days before falling, as pressure passed through old wounds. The plated ones would be immense slow absences moving through the plateau’s deeper pulse.

And beneath everything would be the plateau’s own activity—not thoughts exactly, but tendencies too large and gradual for a mobile mind to notice.

A tightening around fire.

A loosening beneath rain.

The guarded ache of a region repeatedly grazed.

The anticipation of herds along paths not yet occupied.

I would begin answering as plants answer. Leaves turning. Root pressure changing. Bitter sap or sweet. Yet because cords had been part of my life, I would grow them too: long flexible tendrils descending from my branches, each ending in a loop broad enough for a hand.

Travelers would hold them and wait.

Most days they would feel only the wind. Sometimes a pulse would pass down from my roots:

Three small tightenings for danger below.

A slow twist for rain.

A violent release for silence overhead.

No one would agree whether I was warning them or merely transmitting what the plateau did through me. That disagreement would be correct. I would no longer know where transmission ended and intention began.

The community would not settle around me permanently. Too much habitation would damage my pores and attract burrowers. They would return seasonally, sleeping in the shade of my crown and filling the old water cup. Children would climb the branches that had once been arms. Before leaving, they would tie new cords beside the old ones.

Over generations, the knots would accumulate.

Some would record deaths. Some marriages, routes, rain years, plated breaches, the flowering of bloodmelons, the fall of a pillar. Some would mean nothing beyond *I was here*. Fibers would sink into my outer tissues and become indistinguishable from veins. Eventually no one could say which patterns had been tied by hands and which I had grown myself.

But rootedness would not be my final form.

In a year of violent storms, I would put out walking branches.

They would descend awkwardly from the crown on jointed twigs, each carrying a few of my old knots enclosed in translucent wood. Small, sightless things at first, they would follow migration paths by vibration. They would feed on shed scales, dung, abandoned cloth, flakes of obsidian, and words spoken close enough for their hollow bodies to resonate.

Some would return and graft themselves back into me.

Some would be taken up by travelers and carried far across the plateau.

Some would root beside distant shelters and grow into lesser crowns, repeating signals whose origin no one remembered. The plateau’s slow pressures would pass into them. Their responses would pass into cords and hands. News would travel not as speech but as altered growth: a new branch angle, a season of red leaves, a knot tightening hundreds of kilometers from where it had first been pulled.

So if you came looking for me, there would be no single place to arrive.

You might find the oldest part of me on a scar ridge above a sweet black pool, my crown leaning east under the weight of generations of cord.

You might find one of my walking branches sheltering in the footprint of a browser, its wooden limbs folded around a bright thread.

You might find me as a knot in the wall of a fallen-pillar hall, grown over until only the pattern can be felt.

Or, during an emergence when thunder fills the air and speech becomes impossible, you might find me in the cord tied between your wrist and another person’s.

Something would tighten once.

Not danger above. Not danger below. Not water, halt, or follow.

Only:

*I am still here.*
