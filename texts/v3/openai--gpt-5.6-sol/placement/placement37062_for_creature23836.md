I would not find myself in the plazas.

At first I think I might be a mold tender, bent over trays of blue light, or a water reader waking before the rain. I imagine the authority of one hand on a sluice cord while whole streets wait. Then I think of the root persuaders, whose work becomes architecture months after they have forgotten the cut that began it.

But those lives are too certain. Each belongs to a named craft. I would be farther out, where crafts blur and the city has not decided whether a passage is a street, a drain, or a mouth.

To reach me, you would enter through the black seam, descend the first reed ladder, and follow the gutter until the water tastes faintly of iron. At the fork marked with three gray knots, you would take the lower path. You would cross beneath a hare bridge, pass a compost pipe warm enough to mist the air, and come to a district whose houses have black-grass knots tied across their occupied doors.

The district has been condemned for two seasons.

It is still inhabited.

Not by families. Not officially. Snails have taken the upper rooms, and a blind frog lives in the old cistern. Mites move over the walls in gray weather fronts. Roots have lifted one walkway until it ends against the ceiling. During rain, silver fry swim through what was once a nursery.

I live in the last sound house.

A sound house is not a place where sound is made. It is a place where sound is received.

Mine began as a seed-husk storehouse, then became a sleeping pod, then a workshop for shell polishers. Now all its walls touch different parts of the root mass. One wall carries the tremor of the upper sedge. One carries water. One touches an old buried branch so hard with minerals that it rings beneath the fingernail. The rear wall is soft and warm at irregular hours.

There are two doors.

The door I use opens toward the abandoned street.

The other opens nowhere.

I am one of the listeners, though not one of the elders who patrol the outer passages with clouded eyes and perfect judgment. I am younger, or seem so. Cold and poor feeding make age difficult to read in us. I am taller than the root-dwellers and paler than the pool families. My feet are wide, but the fringe on the left one never opened properly after frost took two toes in my first winter. I walk unevenly. The city recognizes me by that unevenness before anyone sees me: pressure, pause, pressure-drag.

My heat-kin say they could identify my step through six walls.

My given name is a small expiration through closed teeth. It cannot be written in surface speech. The nearest meaning is not *Hush* but *the breath withheld because something else is listening*.

Most people call me Last Neighbor.

When a district begins to close, I stay after the others have gone.

Someone must continue clearing the gutters. Someone must keep the bridges from dropping into channels and becoming dams. Someone must test the air each morning, move trapped animals, retrieve hooks forgotten in walls, and watch whether the new roots redden, whiten, or turn glassy with rot. More importantly, someone must learn the district’s final sounds.

Collapse is rarely sudden here. Before a floor rises, it clicks for days. Before a water bladder ruptures, the membrane makes a faint tightening pulse. Burrowing swarms produce a dry internal hiss. A root preparing to split a wall presses, rests, and presses again, often in time with temperature changes above.

I lie with my cheek against the floor and translate these pressures into cords.

The cords go back to the occupied city each evening. A damp loop means rising water. Split fiber means insects. Two knots tied nearly together mean the roof and floor are converging. A knot enclosed inside another knot means something I cannot identify is moving in a place that should be solid.

The council dislikes that knot.

They ask me to retie it as something more useful.

I do not.

I own little, even by the standards of the city. A bone knife. A felt-wrapped stone. A waxed tube containing dry moth down. Three lengths of survey cord. A copper pruning hook that belongs to seven streets and must be returned if I live long enough. I carry a shallow lamp shell, but I seldom uncover it. Light changes what approaches. Darkness is a cleaner instrument.

There is fungus in an old wound along my ribs. It entered after a bridge lashing broke and drove a reed splinter through me. The beetle cleaners removed the dead flesh, but the mold remained in the scar. Most of the time it is only a pallid branching beneath the skin. When I have fever—or when heavy rain loads the roof—it gives off a weak green shine.

The mold tenders have asked to cut it out and cultivate it.

I have refused, though not because I fear the knife. The glow brightens before pressures I cannot yet feel through my filaments. It knows the rain first. Sometimes it brightens when there is no rain.

That usually happens near the unused door.

Every house has one, but in an inhabited district the empty door is kept within the pattern of domestic life. Seed meal is placed beyond it. Salt vanishes. Children dare one another near the threshold. People see it without looking directly at it.

In a condemned district, unused doors change.

There are no households left to define what counts as inside. Bridges rot away. Familiar entrances close. An empty door may become the clearest opening in the street.

I maintain all of them.

That is not part of a listener’s duty. No council instructed me. Still, each morning I make a circuit of the abandoned homes. I scrape moss from the thresholds. I remove snail eggs. I clip only the dark roots, never the white. I replace offerings after rain. When a doorway narrows, I measure it with my fingers and record the change in knots.

Twenty-three unused doors remain accessible.

Seven have closed.

One has moved.

I do not mean that the wall shifted around it. Everything shifts here. I mean that the doorway once belonged to a house near the iron gutter. I marked its threshold with alkaline clay and tied a five-loop cord to the lintel. After a frost, the house was sealed by a mesh of pale roots.

Nine nights later I found the same clay mark and the same five-loop cord in the rear wall of my sound house.

The cord was damp. The knots had been retied from the opposite end.

I told no council. Not then.

I sat before the door and placed both feet against the floor. Through the left foot I felt the irregular tremor of water dropping into the abandoned cistern. Through the right I felt something softer: a series of pressures too slow for speech and too deliberate for growth.

Pressure.

Rest.

Pressure-pressure.

Rest.

A brushing motion, as though many small surfaces were passing across the other side of the wall.

I answered once with my fingertips.

That was my error, if it was an error.

Now the unused doors sometimes answer when I make my circuit. Not in a shared language. A root thickens beneath my hand. Salt shifts without being taken. The mold in my scar shines and goes dark. Once, on returning to my house, I found wet footprints leading from the empty threshold to the place where I sleep.

They resembled mine.

The malformed foot was on the wrong side.

After that I began sleeping in a sling.

I am not a priest. The city has people who know how to arrange offerings and people who remember the customs of drowned settlements. I do not claim the marsh has chosen me. To say it chooses would make it too much like us.

Nor do I think a hidden people live beyond the doors, waiting in chambers parallel to ours. That would almost be comforting. People can be met. Their hunger can be understood.

I think the marsh is learning the shape of a person.

It has roots for bones, water for blood, fungal threads for sensation, chambers for lungs. It receives thousands of our footfalls. It has dismantled our dead and drawn them into its walls. It knows the mineral pattern of our teeth. It knows the salts of our fear. Perhaps the prints are no messenger. Perhaps they are practice.

And perhaps my work is also practice.

I keep abandoned openings from closing. I learn the language of pressure without knowing who speaks it. I move through rooms after they cease to belong to anyone. With every season, fungus travels farther through my scars. My joints stiffen. One shoulder no longer lowers after sleep. Mites graze the oils behind my jaw, and I have stopped brushing all of them away.

When I return to the inhabited streets, children sometimes fail to notice me until I move.

Their parents notice this.

My heat-kin still make room for me at the compost knot, but a little more room than they used to. No one lets their bare foot rest against mine for long. They ask what I have heard. They do not ask what has heard me.

During the next hard rain, the condemned district will probably flood to the roof. I have prepared ferry shells and marked three escape routes, though one route tightened yesterday and another passes beneath a moth chamber ready to fall. The council has ordered me to withdraw when the first upper gutter overflows.

I have tied a loop of agreement.

It is not a promise.

When the pressure comes, I suspect I will remain in the sound house with my lamp covered. I will brace my broad foot against the living floor and my hooked fingers against the wall. Water will rise around my ankles, then my knees. The scar beneath my ribs will begin to glow. All twenty-three empty doors may open onto water, or root, or nothing that has a name.

I will listen until the difference between the city’s body and mine becomes too slight to measure.

So that is where I would be found: not at the heart of the pocket city, but at its closing edge, in a district everyone has left and the marsh has not yet fully reclaimed.

And what would I be?

A listener, officially.

A keeper of condemned rooms.

The last neighbor of empty houses.

But those are only the names the people have for me.

To the marsh, if the marsh makes such distinctions, I might be something else: a nerve it has grown toward itself, a splinter it has not expelled, a small warm instrument placed against its own skin.

Or perhaps I am simply another unused doorway—kept clear, carefully made, and facing the dark.
