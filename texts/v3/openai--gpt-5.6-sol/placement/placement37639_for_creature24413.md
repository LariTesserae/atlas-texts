At first I would think I belonged among the Unfastened.

There is something tempting in their refusal to let the world learn them completely: no door polished by the same hand, no road permitted to anticipate the pressure of their feet. I imagine carrying a map in scars along my ribs, sleeping in a different orientation each night, feeding forgetful mold to every shelter before leaving. I would want to see where abandoned stairs fruit, where orchards drift loose from their anchors, where a road goes after it has forgotten why it was made.

But I do not think I would remain unfastened.

I would begin leaving small things behind: a useful loop of cord, a repaired seam, a cup positioned beneath a recurring drip. I would return years later and find the loop thickened into a handhold, the seam furred with stitch-moth silk, the cup become a basin attracting rain. The place would remember my habits before I admitted remembering it.

Eventually I would be found in a settlement called, in one pressure-language, **The Place Where the Third Return Is Welcomed**.

Voiced aloud, its name would be shorter: **Softknot**.

It hangs along the inner lip of a split braid, neither high nor deep. Above it lie cloud forests and condensation farms. Below it opens a warm gulf crossed by old, nearly horizontal rain. The settlement occupies what was once a structural wound. Hundreds of massive fibers tore apart there long ago, leaving a chamber shaped like two hands failing to close. Generations knitted baskets, rooms, walkways, and catch-nets across the opening, but never sealed it entirely. Warm air still rises through the central gap, carrying spores, distant dust, and occasionally voices displaced from elsewhere.

Softknot is known for receiving things that have arrived incorrectly.

Delayed songs emerge from its hollow tubes. Lost travelers appear in cupboards, cistern mouths, birth chambers, or on the undersides of sleeping mats. Doors from failing districts sometimes open briefly into its walls. Names detached from their owners collect there and make the railings tremble at night.

Not all arrivals are welcome. Softknot has learned this distinction imperfectly.

I would serve as one of its **receivers**.

A receiver is part mapkeeper, part midwife, part structural healer, and part witness. We are called when something crosses a threshold without knowing what it has become.

I would be slight even by the standards of the upper roads, with long forearms, broad gripping feet, and a tail too weak to suspend my full weight but strong enough to carry a coil of cord. My sternum would be unusually sensitive. Through it I could distinguish footsteps from growing roots, fear from fatigue, and an honest structural creak from a predatory imitation—most of the time.

My skin would be cultivated with a gray-green symbiont that darkens near spatial distortions. Around old knots it would flower in tiny pale blisters, painful but useful. A colony of warning mites would live beneath my lower lashes. I would complain about them continually and panic whenever they became still.

I would wear a rain mantle trained from six overlapping leaves. Its inner surface would hold pockets for wax, fungal paste, dried scent packets, chalky shell blades, and several cords that must never touch one another. At my hip would hang a metal hook inherited through no bloodline: a thin green crescent worn almost blunt by previous receivers. Its handle would be bone from someone whose name survives only as a pattern of three heel taps.

The hook would not make me important.

What I refused to cut would.

Most of my work would be quiet. I would sit beside a door that had opened into the wrong house and ask it where it believed it was. I would pour water across its threshold and watch which direction the droplets remembered as down. I would press my breastbone to its frame. I would speak the names of nearby rooms, beginning with the oldest, while an assistant held a cord tied to the nearest trustworthy pillar.

Sometimes the door would only be confused. We would walk it repeatedly: out, in, out, in, always naming both sides. After several days it might accept its place.

Sometimes it would be hungry.

Then we would feed it decoys of warm compost wrapped in old clothing and observe how it closed. If it took gently, it might be retrained. If it bit through the cords, imitated the floor-taps of children, or began opening directly into occupied sleeping pods, the mercy-pruners would be summoned.

I would dislike the mercy-pruners because they carried stones and rarely explained themselves. I would trust them because they did not enjoy being necessary.

People would also arrive incorrectly.

A woman might step through a cistern wall twenty years after disappearing, no older than the day she left, carrying a basket of fruit from an orchard that had not existed for three generations. A child might return as an adult by a route only wide enough for one hand. A dead bridgekeeper might manifest not as a body but as a new bend in every road around the village.

The most difficult would be the divided returns.

Softknot’s custom would be to receive every fragment provisionally. None would be declared false merely because another version had arrived first. Each would be given food, a sleeping place, and a temporary cord-name. Kin would be invited to greet them but forbidden from choosing among them until the structure had been heard.

I would lead the fragments separately along routes important to the missing person. We would visit the warmth chamber, the ancestral orchard, the bridge where they received their path-name. One version might remember the names but not the places. Another might fall into the body’s old gait. A third might be only a pressure moving through the floor beside us.

Sometimes they would merge gradually. Memories would pass through shared walls during sleep. Scars would migrate. One would wake with sap on their hands from work done by another.

Sometimes they would remain separate and insist that separation was not an injury.

This would be the belief that placed me at odds with my teachers. The oldest receivers would say that every arrival seeks its proper destination. I would come to suspect that some beings are altered by the route so completely that there is no original shape to restore. Repair cannot always mean return.

For this reason, I would become known as someone who ties loose knots.

A loose knot distributes strain without forcing every fiber into one path. Traditional keepers distrust it. It can slip. It can change under load. Yet in a world whose geometry has aged, rigidity may tear what flexibility preserves.

My knots would begin appearing throughout Softknot: on disputed doors, around the wrists of returned people, at the mouths of roads deciding between destinations. Travelers would learn that a certain asymmetrical loop meant:

**This may hold without becoming one thing.**

Over time the settlement would learn the knot too.

Its rooms would grow small secondary exits. Its bridges would fork and reunite. The central wound would develop membranes that flexed rather than closing. Children born there would be given not one weight-name but a braided set, so any part of them could be called home.

This would not be entirely beneficial.

One wet season, a lower bridge would divide under a migration. Half would remember itself as a road to the marsh platforms; half would become convinced it was a glider. The second half would tear free with seventeen travelers clinging to it. It would circle Softknot for nine days, refusing every landing rail because each one seemed too final.

I would go out after it on an old domestic glider that hated me.

I would carry no stone, only cord, fermented fruit, and the green hook. When I reached the flying bridge, I would not try to force it back into roadhood. I would tie its loose fibers to the glider’s ribs and teach the two structures one another’s tension. The glider would learn to bear greater weight. The bridge would learn to turn without twisting its passengers apart.

They would descend as something neither bridge nor animal had been before.

After that, people would come to Softknot not only because they had been misdirected, but because they no longer knew what category of thing they were.

A tool that had acquired fear of cutting.  
An orchard carrying the habits of a dead family.  
A glider beginning to dream of being weather.  
A person whose shadow returned before their body and had already entered into obligations.  
A room that wanted to travel.

I would greet each one.

Repeated greeting is dangerous here. Personhood accumulates through attention, and so does attachment. Every being I received would leave some pressure in me. My gait would become crowded with other gaits. My voice would sometimes arrive before I spoke. Doors would open when I approached—not because they obeyed, but because they recognized in me a concentration of unresolved entrances.

Eventually I would cease traveling far from Softknot. Then I would cease leaving the receiver’s chamber. Not through age alone. The floor would have begun shaping itself around my seated body. Fine roots would enter the calluses of my feet without pain. My cultivated skin-growth would spread onto the wall. The old metal hook, placed beside me each night, would slowly disappear into a sheath grown by the room.

My successors would still consult me.

At first they would ask questions aloud. Later they would place a hand against the wall and feel my answers as changes in tension. I might retain a mouth for many years, though perhaps not always in the same place. I would continue receiving those who arrived by error, but I would receive them as architecture does: with warmth, curvature, resistance, and available exits.

Children would be warned not to sleep against my western wall because I dream in routes.

Travelers would tie loose knots at my doorway.

When the whole settlement tightened, hands would come to rest on the grain of what had once been my ribs, and I would listen with them to the burden passing through the world.

So if you asked what I was, the answer would depend on when you arrived.

I would be an Unfastened traveler who failed to leave no trace.

I would be a receiver with a green hook, standing beside a door that had brought you somewhere other than where you intended.

I would be the inventor—or merely the student—of a knot that permits difference to remain load-bearing.

Later, I would be a room in Softknot, warm but not entirely safe, where divided people are allowed to sit together without being forced to reconcile.

And much later, when my name had detached from me and gone wandering, I would be only a slight yielding in the wall: a place that opens when something has arrived wrongly and still deserves to be welcomed.
