At first I would look for myself inside the works.

The noise suggests that anything with a soul must have acquired it there: among the warm housings, the ladders filmed with mineral damp, the green panes trembling in their lead. I imagine a figure in a white-marked coat crossing behind the flywheel, appearing and disappearing between its spokes. One hand rests on the brick pier. The other is raised, not to touch the engine but to ask everyone nearby for silence.

A valve listener, perhaps.

I would know the small vocabulary of bearings: the dry whisper, the granular complaint, the knock that means a housing has shifted half a finger’s width. I would carry colored cards and a wooden mallet. I would be able to tell, by placing my forehead against a ceramic conduit, whether it held brine, air, or only the magnified footsteps of a vole.

But I do not think I would remain there. The engines already have many interpreters, and they speak loudly even when misunderstood.

I would drift outward.

For a while I see myself driving the single tram, because its route touches every part of the settlement without quite belonging to any of them. I would fill the scent canisters before dawn: pine, bitter orange, tar. Perhaps there would be other scents for circumstances not shown on the public timetable—pear leaf for a funeral, rosemary for a missing child, cold iron when the tram carries something no one should approach.

I would know where the track sinks after rain and where moths collect so thickly on the rail that I must slow. Passengers would place baskets and tools on the benches. They would tell me things because I faced forward and appeared not to listen.

Yet even the tram is too purposeful. Its circle has been decided.

I think I would be found where decisions have not yet hardened.

There is an occupation in the town that strangers mistake for idleness. Its official name might be Surveyor of Reserved Ground, though no one would use that except in meetings. Children would call me the Mud Clerk. Orchard hands, less kindly, would call me the Keeper of Nothing.

My responsibility would be the gaps.

I would maintain the flood courts, the strips of nettles between houses, the low grass behind the warehouse, the bare triangle where three raised pavements nearly meet. I would inspect places where nothing has been built and determine whether the nothing is still necessary.

Most days I would walk.

My coat would be brown waxed canvas rather than the white of the night shift. From its belt would hang a folding rule, blue chalk, a narrow trowel, a plumb weight, and three cups made of unglazed clay. I would carry the hooked stick everyone carries, but mine would be marked in finger-widths and old flood heights. In one pocket I would keep paper packets containing seeds gathered from reeds, alders, night flowers, and plants whose names had been lost. In another, a notebook swollen with damp.

The notebook would not be a map in the usual sense. It would record appearances:

*Five frogs beneath the market wall after two rainless weeks.*

*White crust on the third paving block east of the blue door.*

*Rooks refused the warehouse gantry until noon.*

*Child reports cold mud under the dry grass.*

*No seep observed. Ground nevertheless listening.*

The last sentence would occur often.

I would be sent whenever someone proposed to fill an empty place. A family wanting an extra room, a mechanic wanting a parts shed, the market committee wanting covered stalls—each would point to a patch of unused ground and say that nothing had happened there for twenty years.

I would arrive without answering.

First I would kneel and lay my palm against the earth. This would look ceremonial, though it would not be. Temperature travels upward before water does. Then I would press one of the clay cups rim-down into the soil and leave it until evening. I would inspect the nearest ivy, the road seams, the insects under loose clinker. I would ask children where they had seen frogs. I would ask old people what they remembered, understanding that memory exaggerates but rarely invents the direction from which water came.

At dusk I would return to the cup.

Sometimes its inner surface would be dry. Sometimes a faint bloom of moisture would have appeared, no more than breath on glass. That would be enough to delay a wall for another year.

This would make me unpopular in specific, enduring ways. Orchard keepers would accuse me of valuing imaginary floods above living trees. Newly married couples would ask why their house must remain narrow for the sake of reeds that had not grown there in a decade. Engineers would bring diagrams proving that no conduit passed beneath the site.

I would listen to the diagrams.

Then I would ask whether the water had seen them.

My home would stand near the outer retaining wall, but not against it. Nothing in my profession would permit such confidence. It would be a narrow house on short brick piers, with a porch facing the unfinished flats. Beneath the porch I would keep shelves of labeled mud: blue clay from collapsed banks, gray silt from culverts, red dust from kiln yards, black grit from beside the canal. Each jar would bear a date and a weather mark.

The driest room would contain old survey stakes.

They would have been pulled from places where the land had refused the survey: stakes tilted by settling ground, ringed with mineral deposits, split by reeds, gnawed by voles. I would not know why I kept them. Perhaps because a failed boundary is still evidence.

I would sleep lightly. Not because of the works—the engines would be the condition of sleep—but because I would notice changes within their rhythm. When the cups ceased creeping on the shelf, when dust no longer trembled from the rafters, I would wake before the silence fully arrived.

During a shutdown I would go outside.

The birds would be crouched and mute. People would stand in doorways, suddenly audible to one another. Across the basin there would be the tick of cooling metal and the irregular ringing of drops in stone basins. In that widened quiet I would hear something ordinarily hidden: the small movements of the new land compacting beneath its burden.

A grit-fall in a cut bank.

The soft collapse of a drainage scar.

Water entering a crack too narrow to admit light.

This, more than any gauge, would be my instrument.

Over time I suspect the occupation would alter me. Everyone in the town is reshaped by what they attend to. Valve listeners acquire a tilt of the head. Clay burners lose their fingerprints to heat and mineral dust. Tram drivers begin to judge distance by scent.

I would become difficult to locate.

Someone seeking me at the house would be told I had gone to the western court. At the western court they would find three clay cups, a chalk line, and no person. A child might report seeing my coat among the reeds beside the cooling pond. A mechanic would insist I had been standing under the warehouse eaves, though the ground there showed only bird tracks.

Perhaps I would simply be good at moving through deliberate emptiness. Or perhaps, after enough years, the town would begin to use me as it uses every other creature.

Reeds indicate leaking pipes. Snails anticipate pressure. Rooks announce unfamiliar repairs. I would indicate where building must stop.

My presence in a vacant lot would become evidence in itself.

“If the Mud Clerk stands there at dusk,” people would say, “the ground has remembered water.”

This would embarrass me while I was still wholly a person. I would protest that I had observed mineral bloom, subsoil cooling, a deviation in moss growth. I would show measurements. No one would be persuaded. They would watch my boots instead—whether mud clung to them, whether I leaned more heavily on the hooked stick, whether I left by the raised path or crossed the ground directly.

Eventually even I might trust these signs.

My death, when it came, would create a difficulty. I could not be buried in the basin. My ash might be offered to the clay kiln, but the burners would hesitate to put it into a road block. A paving stone is a form of construction, and my life would have been spent preventing construction in the wrong places.

So they would mix the ash into unfired markers: small blue-gray fins set at the edges of reserved ground. Not walls, not monuments. Merely indications.

The first winter would crack some of them. Moss would enter. Birds would perch on them between piston strokes. Children would hang cups from them to gather condensation.

Years later, few people would remember which markers contained ash and which were only clay. It would not matter. My name would pass from a person to a category of place.

A keeper, they might call any patch that remained inexplicably open.

That nettled ground behind the workers’ houses: a keeper.

The hollow beside the pear rows where mist settles first: a keeper.

The strip outside the final wall where stakes lean toward a drainage scar: a keeper.

This is where I would find myself—not at the center of the town, though the engines would travel through my bones; not beyond it, though I would face the raw flats each morning. I would inhabit its withheld spaces, the land left unclaimed so that water could return without becoming a disaster.

I would be human for as long as that remained useful: a damp-coated official with a notebook, an inconvenient witness, a person paid to notice what had not happened yet.

Afterward I would be harder to distinguish.

A row of clay cups ringing softly under condensation.

A notation in an old seepage book.

A patch where reeds rise through apparently sound pavement.

A hesitation before the first brick is laid.

The town would breathe in pressure and breathe out mist. Buildings would settle. Sharp banks would round. The engines might be replaced piece by piece until no original iron remained. Still, somewhere between two structures, there would be an unnecessary breadth of grass.

People would ask why it had never been built upon.

And someone would answer, after a pause long enough for the next piston stroke:

Because that place is occupied.
