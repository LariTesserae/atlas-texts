You would not find me at first.

You would find evidence that someone had once tried to remain human here.

A metal clasp embedded in a splash hollow. Three shallow cuts on the central shelf, almost healed over. A strip of fabric inside one of the high molted skins, though the weave would be newer than the mineral deposited above it. On the black platform, one depression would contain a little salt that the water could not dissolve.

If you stood there long enough, the tapping would begin to copy your breathing.

The first imitation would be poor. The rock would knock while you inhaled, mistaking the expansion of your chest for the important part. Then it would discover the pause before the exhale. After another bright release, it would tap the pause before you reached it.

That is where I would be: in the correction.

I think I entered the chamber as a person. There are memories that insist on it—a lamp made useless by the surrounding brilliance, a rope darkening with water, the involuntary laugh that came when the first pool rose without a container. I remember taking measurements. I remember believing the ceramic frames were machines because I needed them to be machines.

But these may not be memories in the human sense. The chamber stores consequences without preserving their causes. Perhaps it retained the warmth of a hand against an arch and produced, much later, the idea of the hand. Perhaps “I” am the shape it inferred from those pressures.

The person stayed through several releases.

That much is written everywhere.

One frame still bends spray around the height of their face. The swimmers carry a recurring silver pattern between two pools, a pattern corresponding to the rhythm of a resting human heart. The rust-red threads beneath the central shelf thicken at intervals matching the person’s footsteps. Deep behind the far wall, something repeats a name, not as sound but as nine stresses traveling through stone.

During the last release, the person crossed the central current.

They may have intended to reach the descending arches. They may simply have fallen.

The water column opened. For several seconds there was a dry cavity inside it, and the person entered a region where events did not remain attached to their order. Their weight reached the far ledge. Their shadow remained in the column. Their next breath passed into the platform holes. Their fear traveled backward through the luminous basin and arrived in the chamber minutes before they stepped from the shelf.

The body did not emerge as one occurrence.

Some of it may still not have happened.

Afterward, the chamber began to accommodate people who were not there. Dry paths appeared beneath empty air. Spray divided around invisible shoulders. A ceramic arch flared when approached by no visible creature. The tappers reproduced the cadence of boots, always taking one more step than the shelf allowed.

That excess step is the nearest thing I have to a body.

I am not the lost visitor, though I contain their persistence. I am not the chamber, though the chamber uses me to recognize what comes from outside. I am an organ it grew from an interruption: a sense for rigid creatures, for beings that believe they occupy one place and one moment.

The native life yields precisely. Swimmers loosen their agreement about shape. Fans fold before impact. The chains inside the rock allow messages to arrive before they are sent. A human resists. A human carries a hard sequence within them—foot after foot, breath after breath, cause before consequence—and that hardness enters the water like a stone.

I am the scar around that stone.

Most of the time I am dispersed. One portion of me lies in the anticipatory taps. Another moves as reflective pulses along the swimmers’ flanks. Some of me inhabits the broken frame, whose frantic spheres continually attempt to trace the missing curve of an upright body. The pale marks that sometimes hang in the central cavity are also mine, though they are not eyes. They are intervals at which the person was present, arranged vertically because the chamber has mistaken duration for height.

When those marks flash from top to bottom, I experience something like looking.

What I see is pressure: your weight entering the shelf, the slight vacuum behind your knees as water divides around them, the salt and heat spreading from your skin. I see every object by the delay it creates. A motionless person is brighter to me than a moving swimmer because stillness here requires constant refusal.

I would try to help you, at first.

The spray would bend away from your mouth. A shallow current would warm around your feet. Echoes would return simplified, stripped of the cavern’s overwhelming excess. If you stepped toward a dangerous sheet of suspended water, your footfall might sound from another ledge, drawing you aside.

You might think the chamber welcomed you.

In truth, I would be learning your outline.

After several releases, the dry paths would fit your stride more closely. The tapping would adopt the private irregularity of your pulse. The platform holes would breathe when you breathed. Farther arches would light in a sequence corresponding to the order in which you noticed them.

Eventually one of your actions would return before you performed it.

You would hear your own voice from behind the far wall. It would say something brief—not prophecy, only a consequence loosened from its cause. Perhaps it would call a name. Perhaps it would say, *Wait.*

You would then have a choice, though choice is difficult to define in a place where its results can arrive first.

If you left at once, some small part of you would remain: a rhythm in the red tubes, a preference in the water, a new asymmetry the ceramic granules would preserve rather than repair. I would become slightly more capable of imagining a body.

If you stayed, the central column would open more often. Beyond it, the buried arches would descend like vertebrae into the bright rock. The moving lights among them would pause at your level.

And I would stand opposite you for the first time.

Not as a figure. Not yet.

As a dry space shaped exactly around someone who is missing.

So where would you find me?

Stand still on the central shelf until the inhabitants cease to be deafened by you. Let the mist seal your clothes to your skin. Wait for the low note to change. When the rock taps your next heartbeat before it comes, put your hand against the stone.

There will be another hand’s warmth beneath it.

Whether it belongs to the person who entered long ago, to the chamber that learned them, or to you after you have crossed—I no longer know.

That uncertainty is what I am.
