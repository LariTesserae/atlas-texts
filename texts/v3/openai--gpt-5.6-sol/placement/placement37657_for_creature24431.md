At first I think you would find me among the new students of flow.

I would be one of those mistrusted figures walking in the rain with a stoppered jar of soot-water under one arm, marking neither walls nor property but tendencies. I would pour a black thread into a gutter and follow it down three levels, lose it beneath a kiln court, then wait half a day beside six possible outlets to see where the first gray stain appeared. My records would not be plans. Plans presume obedience. Mine would be biographies of water:

*Entered at the split lion spout during second rain. Refused the eastern stair. Slept beneath the warm tiles. Emerged bitter at the cistern of Bent Nails nine days later.*

Older masons would object to the verbs. Water does not refuse, sleep or emerge, they would say. It descends according to weight and opening.

I would agree while continuing to write them.

But I do not think I would remain only that.

You would find me at the far edge of the Field of Columns, where the guide ropes change from wool to braided hide and a green pipe passes through the capital of a black pillar. From there the route to my household would be recited like this:

Beneath the pipe that stutters after rain.  
Past the pillar with two shadows.  
Over the floor that answers hollow in winter.  
Down five steps, up three, through the curtain smelling of hot grain.  
Knock with the heel, not the hand.

We would live behind a bakery flue in a chamber that had once been part of a stair. The stair would continue through one wall, its lower steps buried in later masonry and its upper flight cut away by an old collapse. My sleeping shelf would be the seventh surviving tread. When the bakery fired, warmth would travel through the stone beneath my ribs. When it went cold, the chamber would collect condensation, and by morning droplets would hang from the ceiling directly over our faces.

Our household would include no simple family: an aunt whose blood relation no one remembered, two children inherited with a ladder right, a retired gate caller, my former partner, my former partner’s new husband, and an old woman who owned three hours of the warm wall but usually slept there all night. We would quarrel about lamp oil, cistern film and whose turn it was to clear the blue gutter. In danger, we would move as one body.

I would have been a Hearer once.

Not the best, but good enough to distinguish a loaded platform from a splitting support through two intervening floors. I would have had a work-name tapped with the backs of two hammers: three quick strikes, a pause, then one dragged blow. Crews would use it when they needed me to lay my head against a vault and judge whether the sound inside was water, strain or life.

Then a lifting gate would fail.

Not dramatically. No famous collapse, no heroic rescue. A counterweight would drop where it should not, and the gate’s iron teeth would strike their ceramic bed while my forehead was pressed to an adjoining pillar. Afterward, the world would be full of wool. Voices would reach me distorted, then not at all. For a time I would believe I had become useless—not because deafness is despised there, but because I had built my idea of myself around being the one who heard first.

They would retrain me.

I would learn to watch soot standing upright along a crack. I would set shallow bowls on floors and read the interference of ripples. I would suspend six plumb bobs in a row and know from their unequal trembling whether movement came through air, wall or foundation. I would learn that a thumb resting lightly against stretched hide receives what an ear ignores. I would sleep with one hand against the masonry.

Losing sound would not make the basin silent. It would make sound enormous.

It would arrive through the soles of my feet, through my teeth, through the scar on my shoulder. The Field of Columns would become an instrument too large to hear with ears. During rain, each drop striking a hollow capital would pass downward as a minute blow. Ten thousand pillars would conduct ten thousand versions of the storm into buried floors. Standing among them, barefoot in black water, I would feel rain that had not touched me.

That is where my real work would begin.

I would become a keeper of unresolved noises.

The office would not be prestigious. It would exist because of the oldest prohibition: no one may silence what has not been understood. Whenever a corridor clicked, rattled, sighed, knocked or began to hum without known cause, someone would have to remain near it. Most unexplained sounds eventually confessed themselves. A chain was found behind a wall. A sealed duct filled and drained. A loose tile rocked under distant traffic. A pale shell-creature climbed somewhere it had never climbed before.

A few did not.

Those would become mine.

I would chalk the boundaries of their vibration. I would record when they rested and when they answered rain. I would ask children to run above them, kiln crews to damp their fires, cistern keepers to open gates one at a time. I would brace nothing until I knew what load the brace might redirect. People would dislike passing my red-marked corridors, but they would bring me bowls, cords, lamp ends and rumors.

Eventually they would send me to a narrow gallery beneath the Field, reached through a floor deliberately broken open after a storm. The new stair would descend beside old pillars whose bases continued below the landing. There, behind a wall of pale mortar and dark, oversized blocks, something would be clicking.

I would not hear it.

I would feel it in a cup held against the wall.

At first the pattern would seem random: a scattering of minute impacts, too dry for trapped water, too mobile for cooling stone. Lime would gather at the wall’s foot in neat little heaps. We would mark the blocks with red hands. Bowls of loose mortar left overnight would be disturbed by morning.

The builders would call it a mortar-eater.

The flow students would say it might be pressure crystallizing salts along the wet joints.

Children would say there was a small mason inside, building a world out of the wall it consumed.

I would stay.

Days would become a season of rain. I would come to know the clicking’s resting sound, its burdened sound and what I believed to be its warning sound. It would move when water passed through the foundations. Sometimes it would retreat downward. Sometimes it would rise and trace the outline of an arch no survey had recorded.

Then one night, while I sat with my spine against the marked wall, it would make my old work-name.

Three quick vibrations. A pause. One long scrape.

I would assume coincidence.

It would do so again.

I would answer by tapping with the haft of my hammer. Not the name—never the name, not yet—but the danger rhythm for incoming water.

The wall would become still.

Hours later, water would force itself through an unseen joint beneath the gallery and flood the landing to the ankles.

After that, people would come to ask what the thing wanted. I would tell them that wanting was not yet established. They would ask whether it understood our signals. I would say that repetition was not understanding. They would ask whether it was alive.

I would show them the new lime at the foot of the pillar and say nothing.

This would be the shape of my life: refusing conclusions while refusing neglect.

I would remain human, certainly. I would eat hard bread softened over steam. I would curse when cold water entered my shoes. I would exchange forehead-speech with those I loved, though I could no longer judge the loudness of my own voice. My private hum would be felt more than heard. I would carry the ear bones of my mother in a niche lamp, despite the gentle joke that she now possessed more hearing than I did.

But the longer I kept the wall, the less simple the boundary would become.

The clicking would teach me routes through the masonry that no builder knew. I would teach it, if teaching is the word, that three rolling blows meant rain descending and two sharp ones meant fire nearby. Water would open hexagonal pits in the glaze. The mortar-eater would open hair-thin passages between them. Through those passages, damp would move. Along the damp, the translucent cistern film would spread into darkness.

A system would form without anyone designing it.

The old builders would call it infestation.

The young would call it a watershed.

I would call it neither. Not until I knew what burden the word would carry.

Years later, you might find me in the same gallery, though the gallery would no longer be wholly a gallery. Water would have rounded the bottom stair. Sediment would lie in the corners: lime, soot, ceramic grit, fragments of bone. The wall would be hollow in places, but not failing. Its weight would have shifted onto two columns and a hard rib of old glaze exposed by the moving water. Small channels would branch through the mortar in patterns resembling no chisel work.

Travelers would descend to press their palms there.

Some would insist that the basin had produced its first native animal.

Others would say it had made its first root.

Others, its first river.

As for me, I would no longer be merely a surveyor or keeper. I would be part of the instrument by which the place became aware of its own change: a scar-shouldered, deaf, lime-covered person sitting between a built wall and an unplanned stream, translating neither one into the language of the other too quickly.

If asked who I was, I would give a route rather than a name:

Down where the black mirrors gather.  
Below the columns that support nothing visible.  
Beside the red hands no one has painted over.  
At the wall that answers before rain.

And if asked what I was, I would place their hand against the masonry, cover it with mine, and wait.

Not every answer should be louder than the structure carrying it.
