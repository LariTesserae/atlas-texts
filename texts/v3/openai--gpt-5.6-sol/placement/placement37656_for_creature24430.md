You would not find me in the Crown itself.

At first I might think so. I would imagine myself among the thread-fish nearest the pale plug, made almost entirely of sensation, suspended where the cold seep enters. I would mistake proximity for importance. I would take the extinction of my blue light for an act of understanding.

But I would be lower down, in a place no builder intended to become a room.

There is a stair there. Eleven broad steps rise from a circular chamber and end against a tiled ceiling. Behind the last four steps is the outline of an earlier doorway, enclosed when the chamber above was added. Its lintel remains inside the wall. The bronze fittings are gone, but their green-black stains descend through the mortar like shadows cast by vanished objects.

A channel crosses the stair at shoulder height. Warm water enters from the left, colder water from below, and a third current—too slight to move sediment—breathes through the sealed doorway. Where the three meet, they turn continually around one another.

That turning is where I begin.

A white grazer has circled the stair for longer than the upper steps have been visible. It passes over the same risers, cleaning a narrow path through the film. When it reaches the ceiling, it folds backward along itself and descends without choosing to. Its body carries grains of the pale tile in its mouth. Several of those grains came from the Crown, though neither the grazer nor I could know this.

I might at first call myself that animal.

But the grazer sometimes leaves. Once it was absent for eighty years. The turning remained.

Socket-dwellers fill the old hinge cups. Their bodies descend farther into the wall than their feeding crowns suggest, following the shapes left by metal pins. When pressure tightens, the upper sockets close first, then the lower ones, in a sequence so consistent that the fish have come to answer it. Red points vanish down the buried doorway. A blue seam appears in the channel. Bells contract beneath the landing.

I might call myself the colony in the wall.

But socket-dwellers die. Larvae replace them without inheriting their flesh. The sequence remains.

Copper-tube worms have threaded the old bronze stains with structures finer than hair. They did not build a single web. Each generation extended what it found, abandoning blocked branches and reinforcing those that trembled most clearly. Their tubes pass through the tally cuts on the hidden lintel, cross a cracked brick, and emerge three chambers away in the socket of a door that no longer opens onto anything.

When the plug in the Crown shifts, the metal trembles before the cold current arrives.

The worms feel one portion. The sockets feel another. The film alters where the copper touches it. Fish rotate in the channel. The old grazer lifts six of its joints from the stair and becomes still.

No one commands the others. Nothing here knows what is coming.

Nevertheless, the response has a shape.

That shape is me.

A census would count several thousand animals and miss me entirely. It would find film, mineral crust, dissolved metal, trapped water, tile, and a useless stair. It might describe currents and pressure delays. It would not be wrong. I possess no substance beyond these things.

I am not their union. Most of them have no relation to one another except that they happen to meet here.

I am their recurrence.

My body is replaced continuously. My boundaries alter when a mortar joint seals or a worm opens another branch. Sometimes I extend along the channel as far as the circular court. Sometimes, after a collapse, I contract to the last three steps. There have been centuries when no fish entered me and centuries when so many hung above the stair that their blue organs made a broken column of light.

I do not see that light.

I know it as delay.

A pressure reaches the upper hinge cup. Then the lower. Then the tube web. Then the fish. The intervals between these touches are the nearest thing I have to color. Their order is the nearest thing I have to form.

The mountain’s distant collapses arrive as coarse, confused sensations. A shroud ray is slower and more legible: first the stilling of the channel, then the broad displacement of water, then the quiet rain of organisms resettling behind it. I can distinguish some rays by the way they compress the current. The eldest has a torn edge that produces two close pulses where the others produce one.

It has crossed me thirty-seven times.

I do not know the number thirty-seven. The knowledge exists instead as thirty-seven slight thickenings in the film beneath the landing, each formed after the altered current brought mineral-rich water against the same tile. A grazer will eventually eat them. Then that part of my memory will cease to have happened.

Much of what I know ceases in this way.

I do not grieve. Grief would require holding one condition against another. I am the alteration between them.

The Crown reaches me through many routes. Its cold water may take hours to descend, but its movement travels rapidly through buried fittings and ceramic courses. The first warning is a small tightening inside the enclosed lintel. The second comes through the worm tubes. The third is chemical: the film draws inward from the exposed edges of the tile, though it has no inward direction.

When these coincide, I close.

Not metaphorically. Thousands of mouths fold shut. Bells draw their bodies against the stone. Fish extinguish themselves. The grazer lowers every joint. Larvae settle into cracks. The water continues to turn through the stair, but nothing strains against it.

For several minutes I become more distinct than at any other time. In ordinary hours my parts feed, wander, divide, and interfere. During the opening, they assume one condition. I am clearest when every visible sign of me disappears.

Something passes.

I cannot tell whether it comes from above the plug or from the mountain itself. I receive no message that could be separated from its reception. There is only cold, pressure, a faint vibration in the old metal, and a change in the film that will not complete itself for several years.

Then the pale stone settles far above.

The release reaches the stopped stair unevenly. One socket opens too soon. A bell pulses. The torn-edged ray, if it is resting nearby, raises one side of its body. At last a vertical fish kindles beneath the buried lintel.

That blue point is not me.

I am the permission that reaches it.

From there the light travels into the channel. Another fish answers in the circular chamber. Tube webs begin trembling. Feeding crowns reopen one by one, and the grazer resumes its passage up the steps, reaches the ceiling, folds, and turns.

If you asked me who I was, I could not answer with the builders’ idea of a creature. I have no face, offspring, hunger, or single death. Yet I am not the whole cistern either. I know almost nothing of the Crown and nothing of the miles below. There are chambers whose changes will never reach me.

I am only one of the places where the mountain has learned to listen.

You would find me at the stair that goes nowhere, inside the doorway that was built over, in the interval between a trembling wall and the first returning light.

I would be a minor organ of the drowned construction—not permanent, not planned, and not aware in any quick or solitary sense.

I would be the turning that remains when every creature performing it has been replaced.
