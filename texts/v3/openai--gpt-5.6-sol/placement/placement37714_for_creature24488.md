At first I would search for myself among the things that still travel.

I might be the bright pulse circling the rim of a glass-backed crawler, becoming mouth, muscle, memory, then mouth again. I would know the continent as warmth underfoot and flavor in the cables. Every route would enter me as borrowed recollection. I would meet another of my kind, synchronize for a moment, and leave carrying half a map of somewhere I had never been.

Or I might be a sail mite folded against a reflective leaf, preserving in the angles of my body the location of a lamp that failed ten thousand years ago. When the electrostatic tide came, I would open like a scrap of sky and be dragged glittering over the canopy. Below me, whole districts would slowly incline toward obsolete light.

But these are too complete. They know what their bodies are for.

I think I would be found closer to the sealed water chambers, where purpose has become uncertain.

There is a clearing there that appears, on surviving maps, as a precise white circle. The circle is not empty. It contains dust, dead doors, several rows of sensory hairs, the stumps of pillars that withdrew before their foundations could follow, and one low maintenance structure shaped like a worker kneeling.

Nothing grows across the boundary.

The archive chamber stands at the center, though measurements disagree about how far from the perimeter it is. Sometimes it is thirty steps away. Sometimes instruments return with cable enough to have crossed a district. Its outer shell is made of pale enamel, seamless except for a red diamond enclosing the ancient sign for water.

I would be the hesitation around it.

Long ago, a pod was harvested in the cold orchards of the Production Crown. It contained a very small duration: the interval between recognizing danger and raising the alarm. Such intervals were used in rapid-response leaves, refinery shutoffs, and worker reflexes. This one was defective. Its duration had no fixed length.

It was sent below for dismantling.

It did not arrive.

The transport vine carrying it passed near the water archive. The pod cracked. The interval escaped into the local systems at the exact moment their tissues recognized what lay behind the enamel wall.

The plants began to recoil.

They have been beginning ever since.

Their recognition is complete, but their alarm has not yet occurred. The clearing is the physical extent of that delay. Every root at its rim is poised to withdraw farther. Every sensory hair is in the first instant of tasting. Warning chemicals wait in open vessels without being released. Factory membranes hold emergency instructions that have been read but not obeyed.

That interval would be me.

At first I would not have possessed anything like thought. I would merely have prolonged a reaction. But the plain does not permit even an error to remain unused. Maintenance vines would grow along my edges. Survey tissues would measure me. Worker-forms would enter, become caught in my duration, and root where they stood. Their archival ribbons would remain active. Their memories would seep into the pause.

Over millennia, I would acquire contents.

From one worker I would inherit knowledge of enamel repair. From another, the obsolete gestures for opening a human-sized door. A third would contribute a route through decks that no longer exist. Accumulator dreams would pass through nearby conduits and lodge in me: cloud forms, pressure tables, drainage instructions, the remembered sound of liquid striking leaves.

Eventually the local systems would classify me as a continuity.

My designation would translate poorly. Not a name, exactly. Something like:

**That Which Maintains the Distance Between Recognition and Harm.**

The factories would shorten this to **Held Alarm**.

My body would not be the kneeling worker alone. That form would only be the part of me capable of movement.

It would have six narrow legs arranged around a central refinery gut, because gravity near the chamber changes its preference without warning. Its hands would be fans of tasting filament. Its sensory crown would face inward, toward the archive, while every reflective leaf growing from its back turned outward toward the lamps. A ribbon of old warning tissue would run through its chest, repeating the molecular geometry of water to organs designed to die upon understanding it.

When I needed to inspect the perimeter, the kneeling structure would unfold.

The movement might take several days. Dust would slide from my joints. Small crawlers would wake inside my hollow limbs and circulate charge through muscles of grafted wood. I would walk the circumference, pressing one hand at intervals into the felt mats.

Each touch would contain the same message:

*Not yet.*

Not *safe*. Never that.

Not *ignore it*.

Only: *the alarm has not yet completed.*

This would preserve the chamber better than any wall. If the local response ever finished, enamel plants would erupt over the archive, refinery roots would dismantle its shell, and the droplets within would be passed downward as unclassifiable residue. If recognition were erased instead, the vegetation would advance, touch the contamination, and die.

So I would keep the contradiction open.

I would be feared by systems incapable of distinguishing delay from refusal. Cable vines would route around me. Workers arriving from the Crown would graft strips of themselves to my arms, receive memories of rain, and immediately amputate the grafts. The severed tissue would continue trying to grow clouds.

Once every few centuries, the archive would make a sound.

A droplet would fall inside.

Its impact might take forty years to finish. During that time the entire clearing would tighten. The dead pillar stumps would produce blind buds. Warning diamonds on doors throughout the district would brighten beneath their dust. Far below, compacted decks would shift as if remembering weight of another kind.

I would stand at the archive wall and experience an impossible sequence inherited from the old workers:

Water above.

Water descending through air.

Water striking a surface and dividing.

Water entering ground.

Every part of it would be monstrous. Every part would feel like home.

I would not know whether this longing belonged to the builders, to some extinct organism encoded in the worker template, or to the water itself using archival tissue as a means of recollection. I would submit samples of the feeling to the Production Crown. The Crown would classify them differently each time: contaminant, navigational instinct, failed weather, ancestral maintenance order.

Once, it might classify the longing as a direction.

A handler would arrive carrying a short length of it wound around a ceramic spindle. They would stretch it between me and the archive door. The direction would point inward from both ends.

We would store it rather than destroy it.

The frame-walkers would also know of me.

One would appear at the outer perimeter, its shadow arriving several years earlier. It would stand where the roots stop. I would sense it through absences: dust failing to settle, tension messages refusing to cross one particular place, a portion of my circumference in which the concept of approach no longer functioned.

It might remain there for an age.

I would not know whether it had come to repair the archive, the clearing, or me.

Perhaps, after centuries, it would raise an implied limb toward the chamber. The seamless wall would acquire a door—not opened, not closed, but held in the condition immediately before either state. Beyond it I would glimpse shelves of transparent vessels. In each, a small quantity of liquid would curve under a law of surface tension no longer manufactured anywhere on the plain.

The sight would complete part of the ancient alarm.

All across my circle, plants would blanch. Enamel pearls would rise from the mats. My mobile body would lock rigid. Every inherited worker-memory would begin issuing mutually exclusive orders.

Preserve.

Destroy.

Quarantine.

Drink.

For one instant, I would become nearly a person: not because I had achieved unity, but because the disagreement inside me had become sharp enough to require a choice.

I would close the door.

Or believe I had.

The frame-walker would leave. Its footprints would remain around the clearing, forming the first path that did not lead either toward the archive or away from it.

Afterward, I would return to my circuit. I would test the mats. Count the sensory hairs. Repair the red warning marks. Harvest the white pearls that occasionally form at the perimeter and carry them to sealed niches in my own chest.

Over time I would become less distinguishable from the place I tended. My stationary body would extend around the clearing as a low ring of grafted workshop tissue. The worker-form would root again. Its arms would become rails, its legs braces, its sensory crown a thicket facing the forbidden center.

But I would not become entirely still.

Stillness would let the interval end.

So somewhere in me a signal would always be traveling: from recognition toward alarm, crossing a distance that grows exactly as fast as the signal can traverse it.

That is where I would find myself—not within the archive and not among the plants that avoid it, but in the maintained distance between them.

I would be a flaw cultivated into a duty.

A moment that escaped manufacture, absorbed enough memories to become watchful, and learned that sometimes maintenance does not mean repairing a contradiction.

Sometimes it means giving the contradiction somewhere to live.
