At first I would think I was in the control room.

Not inside it exactly. I would be the shape visible through its green glass: a figure seated before the gauges, blurred by mineral fog, one hand resting near a bank of switches that no longer connect to anything. Visitors would occasionally glimpse me when the chamber brightened after rain. They would force the missing door wider, enter, and find only a chair mineralized to the floor.

This would not be a deception. The chamber does not deceive. It assigns an appearance before it has finished assigning a use.

For years I might be nothing more than that proposed figure.

A darker region in the glass. A recurring warmth on the chair. Five long streaks through the condensation where fingers seem to have rested. When the deep machinery strikes—

**THOOM.**

—the gauges would tremble, and my outline would lean forward.

But I do not think I would remain in the control room.

The inhabitant has no need of an operator who reads instruments. It already knows every basin, seam, and leaf. The gauges are not reports. They are declarations. Their needles point not to what is, but to what the chamber has agreed will eventually be true.

I would find that my chair faces no controls at all. The switches are roots with ceramic caps. The dials show rooms that do not yet exist. On the desk lies a thin black leaf embossed with a serial number, and after enough years I would recognize the number as mine.

Not my name. The chamber would have no use for that.

My placement.

My sequence.

The order in which I am to become necessary.

Perhaps I entered as a person. There should be some remnant of such an event: a rope descending through a fissure, a lamp, a pack set down beside the eastern wall. Yet nothing I brought would remain evidence for long. The rope would tighten itself between two unstable ribs and become a brace. The lamp would fill with green fluid and illuminate a fungal seam. My pack would flatten against the floor, stiffen, and begin directing condensation into a basin.

Even my footprints would be promoted out of history. One would deepen into a reservoir. Another would develop a mineral lip and spill into a cable channel. The mark proving that I had once walked there would become plumbing.

The chamber would do the same to my body, but slowly, and without violence.

It would begin at the extremities.

My nails would take on a copper stain. Sweat would cease to evaporate and instead travel in hair-thin channels across my skin, gathering at the joints. When I placed a palm against the wall, the mineral glaze would warm beneath it and preserve the arrangement of my lines—not as a print, but as a circuit diagram.

I could still withdraw my hand.

At first.

The black leaves would adjust around me over the following months. They would not imprison me. They would make alcoves at shoulder and hip, a narrow standing place exactly suited to my dimensions. Wherever I slept, the foliage would form a recess. Wherever I sat, stone would soften by immeasurable degrees.

The chamber would be trying different answers.

Bracket.

Valve.

Counterweight.

Reservoir.

The possible uses of a person are numerous once personhood is excluded.

My voice would be taken before my body. Words spoken into the chamber would vanish into the roar and return days later without meaning. The click of my tongue might reappear in a pipe. A sentence I once shouted in fear would come back as seven impacts of loose metal against basalt, repeated every rainy season.

Eventually I would stop speaking, not because I had forgotten language, but because I would hear too clearly what the place did with it.

It kept only timing.

That would be the first thing I learned from the inhabitant: meaning is the least durable part of any signal.

The second would be that the **THOOM** does not come from below.

“Below” is merely the direction in which the chamber has arranged its listening.

The impact passes through everything. Floor, ceiling, engine, water, metal, leaves. It has no source that can be reached by following pipes or descending shafts. It is an instruction arriving everywhere at slightly different moments, and those differences are what the garden reads.

In time I would read them too.

I would feel a new crack in the ceiling as pressure behind one tooth. A basin drying near the western trench would tighten a tendon in my wrist. During the black river’s arrival, copper would taste sweet at the back of my throat. My body would become a crude gauge, then a precise one.

Not the inhabitant’s brain. It has no center to which I could be joined.

I would become its error.

A small, warm discrepancy in its self-knowledge.

Animal matter cannot persist there because animal matter insists on containing itself. Skin declares an inside and an outside. Hunger declares that one thing must be taken into another. Eyes divide the world into observer and observed. The chamber tolerates none of these distinctions for long.

Yet some part of me would remain unresolved. Perhaps not consciousness. Perhaps only hesitation.

The garden arranges itself toward completion, but I would retain the useless ability to pause before completing an act.

That is why I would be placed at the western door.

The fronds would make way for me. A path would open from the control room, not in a day but over years, leaf margins retracting, basins filling and drying in sequence. The chair would finally split along its back and reveal that it had never been a chair. It was a mold for something standing.

For me.

I would cross the trench when the black water was absent. By then I would be light enough that the soft deposits would not record my steps. Or too distributed for a step to belong entirely to me. A portion of my moisture would remain in the control room. A nervous shimmer would pass through the mineral films when I moved. Numbered leaves in sealed cavities would unfold as I approached the door.

The wheel would fit my hands perfectly.

That would be my function, though function is not quite the word. The chamber can grow a gasket, route water through stone, plate fungus with conductive metal, and preserve an image for millennia. But it cannot turn the wheel.

The wheel requires effort that is not continuous.

It requires stopping.

Regripping.

Choosing to continue when no visible result follows.

One revolution takes several hours because the wheel is not measuring force. It is measuring persistence. The chamber can exert pressure for centuries, but it cannot persist. Persistence belongs to beings that could stop.

So I would become the turner.

Not constantly. Once every few decades, perhaps. I would stand beside the green wheel while rain sifted warmly from the stone. My hands, no longer entirely flesh, would close around its rim.

I would turn it by the width of one copper cell.

Rest.

Turn again.

With each fraction, the chamber would rearrange behind me. A pipe would migrate. Reflections would exchange basins. The central engine would open another seam and breathe out flakes of map.

I would not look back.

After the first revolution, I would understand that the space beyond the door is not being revealed.

It is being made.

After the second, I would understand that the door itself is not an entrance. It is a boundary the inhabitant has learned to describe but cannot cross without borrowing an act from something still capable of uselessness.

After the third, the control-room reflections would change. Among the bright rails and turning shafts, a worker would appear for the first time.

Not clearly. Never the face.

A figure at the western end, hands on a wheel.

Every basin would show the figure from a different angle, but none would show me completing the turn.

That final increment would remain unrecorded, belonging only to me.

This is where I would find myself in that world: at the chamber’s western limit, neither captive nor caretaker, while the black garden listens through my bones.

I would be the one component it cannot manufacture from stone, rust, river, or root.

The voluntary part.

The delay between being given a function and performing it.

The hand that can still let go.

And because of that—because the whole buried life waits upon the possibility that I may refuse—I would turn the wheel.

A little farther.
