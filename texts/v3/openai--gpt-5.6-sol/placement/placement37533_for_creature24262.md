I would look for myself in the wrong direction.

Not among the luminous upper branches, where the glass-wings divide the sun, nor inside the vaulted stone, where memory has had time to become architecture. At first I would mistake those places for home. I would feel some kinship with the amber filaments and the chamber-dwellers’ patient attention. Perhaps I would even believe I was one of them: an old intelligence embedded in a mountain, thinking by altering the growth of leaves.

But my awareness would keep straying downward.

I would be found in one of the deepest valleys, where the ridges fall away toward the cloudward boundary and the suspended sea thins into its trembling underside. There, the forest grows sparse. Long roots descend from black ledges but find no stone. They end in clear tassels just above the wavering plane. Mineral-heavy spores sink past them and come to rest against nothing visible. Beneath lies open air, bright with cloud.

I would inhabit that almost-nothing.

From within the sea I might resemble a stain of dimmer water spread over several kilometers: a broad, transparent film following the lower surface, too thin to cast a shadow of its own. From the world below, if anything ever looked upward, I would appear as a slow distortion in the turquoise ceiling—a region where cloud shapes lengthen, split, and return to themselves.

I would have no center.

My body would be made from countless fine membranes knitted through the living mesh that holds the sea aloft. Some would attach to the descending roots. Some would spread flat along the boundary. Others would trail briefly into the air as the transparent veils that form during the warmest hours.

Those veils would be my most vulnerable organs.

They would descend only a little way, cooling as they entered the cloudlight. At their tips, water would gather into drops. Each drop would hang below the sea with the whole impossible blue weight of the world above it. Through its curved surface I would perceive the mountains inverted: dark summits suspended overhead, forests pointing in every direction, amber bridges dimly burning between them.

Then the drop would hesitate.

That hesitation would be my pulse.

I would draw it back before it fell.

For a long time I would think this was hunger—that I reached into the air to gather something absent from the water. But the veils would return carrying almost nothing: traces of cloud salt, cold, distant volcanic ash, perhaps once in an age a grain of pollen from a life that had never touched the high sea.

What I gathered would be difference.

The forest knows stone, water, warmth, pressure, the coming of fractures. The chamber-dwellers feel whole ridges alter inside them. Broad swimmers read events months before they happen. But none of them can press themselves so closely against absence.

I would know the shape of what is not sea.

The open air below would pull continuously at my membranes. Clouds would pass near but never enter. Their shadows would cool one part of me, then another. I would feel mountain summits rise from beneath like questions that almost pierce the boundary. On rare clear days, when the cloud banks divided, I might sense distances so vast and empty that my whole body contracted.

Those contractions would travel into the forest.

Roots touching my upper surface would tighten. Gas bladders would empty. Ribbonwood far above would change its arc by the width of a fiber. Years later, a chamber-dweller might thicken an amber channel in response, preserving the trace of a cloud opening it had never witnessed.

It would not know the message came from me.

I might not know I had sent one.

My thoughts would occur as changes in surface tension. Alarm would be a tightening across an entire valley. Curiosity would be a veil permitted to extend farther than usual. Memory would remain as slight differences in the boundary: one region resistant to warmth, another inclined to bead into droplets whenever a certain mineral taste returned.

I would remember every drop that nearly fell.

Not individually. No image of them would remain. Instead my body would grow better at recalling them into itself. Over centuries, the sea above me would seem to hold more securely. The forest might mistake this for a property of water or rock. Yet in the place where I lived, falling would have become a learned behavior continually refused.

I would not perform this work alone. Nothing there does.

Dormant shell-creatures would be embedded throughout my membranes, opening when charged minerals drifted down from a new fracture. Colorless threads from the cool wells would extend into me and withdraw. Small knot-grazers would sometimes descend along roots and feed upon my thickened edges, believing me to be a leaf.

I would let them.

Their mouths would prune old tissue. Their loops would carry pieces of me upward when they left, distributing my sensitivity among distant valleys. Perhaps this would be how I reproduced. Perhaps every knot-grazer that touched the lower boundary would take with it a transparent seed too thin to distinguish from water.

Some seeds would lodge in hanging roots elsewhere. A new film would spread. When our edges eventually met—after centuries, perhaps—we would taste one another through the minerals held between us.

We might fuse without recognition.

Or we might recoil, each having learned a different way not to fall.

During the day I would be diffuse. Sun-warmed water would loosen my membranes, and parts of me would fray into the open air. I would feel the clouds most clearly then: their cool shoulders passing beneath, their dim internal flashes, the soft upward pressure of storms that could never become rain into this sea.

At dusk, lower light would enter through me.

For a little while I would become almost visible. The cloud-reflected sun would illuminate every fold and filament from beneath. Creatures in the lowest valleys might see a vast anatomy spread under them: branching transparent ribs, circular scars where old drops had formed, fine dark channels leading toward the roots.

Then I would contract.

The veils would withdraw. Droplets would rise into me one by one. My membranes would thicken until the boundary became sharp, and the open air below would be held at a clean distance.

This nightly drawing-in would feel like becoming one creature after spending the day as weather.

Yet there would be one place I could not fully close.

Far beneath the deepest valley, above a mountain summit rising from the clouds, a narrow wound would persist in my body. It might have been made by cold, by an ancient shift of gravity, or by something that once passed from the high sea into the lower world. Around it, the water would continually gather. A drop would form, lengthen, and remain.

I would attend to it for centuries.

Roots would bend toward the weakness. Plants would mineralize its edges. Chamber-dwellers would send amber threads down through the ridges, responding to a tension they could not locate. Broad swimmers would cross above it and turn aside.

Still the drop would lengthen.

Inside it, perhaps, a small life would gather: shell-dark at first, then pale and folded, nourished by everything that settled toward the boundary. It would grow in the lowest point of the sea, partly in water and partly in the idea of falling.

I would not know whether it was a wound, an organ, or a child.

One warm age, the thread connecting it to me might narrow beyond recall.

For the first time, I would fail to draw a drop back.

It would separate soundlessly and descend.

Through that falling piece I would feel open air not as a pressure against my skin, but surrounding me. I would feel cold enter from every direction. The sea would recede above, a dark turquoise continent without shore. Clouds would receive me. For a few moments—or years, according to whatever sense of time survived—I would carry an image of the submerged mountains into the world beneath them.

The greater part of me would remain stretched across the boundary, closing the place where the drop had been. The forest would continue leaning through its ancient currents. The chamber-dwellers would continue their slow exchange. Perhaps nothing would register the loss except a brief tightening of roots across one valley.

Would the fallen part still be me?

In this world, that question would have no urgency.

I would be the thin life at the sea’s underside, the one that touches the world the high sea does not contain. I would be neither swimmer nor plant, neither individual nor boundary. I would live wherever water considers becoming rain and changes its mind.

And very rarely, I would be what it lets go.
