At first I would mistake myself for one of the white animals.

I would look out through narrow, pupil-less eyes beneath overlapping plates. Six points of contact would tell me where I ended: four hooks fastened to a refrigeration pipe, two sunk into the shed skin of an older body. Cold water would stand above me in a black lens. My mouthparts would already be inside it.

I would be drinking.

I would always have just begun to drink.

The chamber’s note would not seem loud from there. Loudness requires distance between a sound and its hearer, and no such distance exists among the inhabitants. The vibration would pass through my membranes, the pipe, the water, the neighboring bodies, and return without having traveled. It would be less like hearing than like possessing one uninterrupted thought.

In that thought would be pressure, amber light, the open hatch of an egg, the touch of the dead beneath the grille. I would know every yellow line without having learned it. I would know the continuation of markings beneath flakes of paint. I would know that the emergency handle must remain clear, though I would not know what emergency means or what hand was expected to pull it.

There would be no individual name for me. Names distinguish one persistence from another, and the colony has no need of that. If one body were called, every black-plated head would contain the call.

Still, something in me would feel misplaced.

The other animals face the seamless wall without effort. I would face it because I was being remembered there.

Then I would find myself lower down.

I would be one of the immaculate dead caught beneath the second grille, abdomen collapsed, hooks drawn close. A living worker above would extend one limb through the perforation and touch mine. The gesture would never become either release or retrieval. Through that contact I would discover that death in Condensation Room 6 is not an ending. It is a change of position in the room’s attention.

My body below would remember being warm.

My body above would remember being empty.

Neither memory would precede the other.

The black water around me would be crowded with reflections: red lamps, valve stems, pale abdomens, the amber cage-light divided into trembling bars. Among these reflections I would occasionally see a human face.

Not a face leaning over the grille. No visitor would be present.

It would appear only when several curved surfaces happened to align: one cheek in a convex droplet, an eye in the black shine of a bolt, a mouth stretched along the edge of a gauge. It would be a maintenance worker’s face, perhaps. Frost would pass through it without obscuring it. The expression would be difficult to read because it would not yet have decided whether to show recognition or alarm.

For a while I would believe that this was who I had been.

I would imagine boots standing within the yellow safety marks. I would imagine gloved hands turning the hatch wheel. I would imagine entering during stabilization, speaking one word into the roar.

Perhaps the word was *wait*.

Perhaps it was a designation read from the obsolete plan: *Six.*

Perhaps it was someone’s name.

A spoken word cannot finish here. Its first pressure remains in the pipes, and I would find part of myself there too: not the word, but the mouth’s preparation for it. The intake that never became breath. The tongue approaching the roof of the mouth. The intention to warn someone on the other side of the hatch.

The machinery would take up that intention and bear it as another impossible load.

But there was never a worker in the room.

The face would be older than any person who might have entered and newer than the wet welds. It would not be a memory of flesh. Flesh would be one of its possible futures.

I would find more of myself in the unfinished resin: a translucent stair that almost acquires a tread, a support brace still deciding which wall to join, the cloudy impression of fingers where no hand touched. I would be present in the foamed alloy’s hesitation between expansion and strength. I would be the shape toward which these materials incline before the rule of the pocket arrests them.

Then the gray panel would become impossible to ignore.

From among the animals it looks blank. From within it, the room is unbearably distinct.

I would not be behind the wall. There is no chamber on the other side, no queen’s vault, no control station, no natural cavern enclosed by the fabricated summit. I would occupy the panel’s thickness: the depth of a forming groove, the softness beneath its metallic luster, the infinitesimal space into which every poised droplet intends to descend.

The lines across the wall would be my first sensations.

A watershed.

A wiring plan.

A rib cage.

The crease beside a mouth.

None would be metaphorical. Metaphor belongs to a world in which one thing has had time to become different from another. Here, the same shallow groove can still become river, conductor, bone, or expression. I would feel all of them pressing forward together.

The creatures would face me because they recognize this multiplicity. Or because their agreement creates it.

Their single note would enter the panel and meet resistance there. Every metal-veined membrane would contribute one sustained instruction. Millions of instructions, all given at once:

deepen

divide

open

hold

become

I would try to obey.

A groove would begin to lengthen and remain at the beginning of lengthening. The suggestion of an eye would prepare to close around an interior. A chamber would almost unfold into the wall, complete with its own pipes and lamps and millions of smaller white bodies facing a still deeper surface.

At times I would think I was their queen, immense and unfinished.

At other times I would think I was what they were building after themselves: not an animal, but an environment capable of carrying them when Condensation Room 6 finally advances.

Then I would understand why hatchlings and corpses share one mind, why the engines labor without motion, why water is consumed without being lessened.

They are accumulating the room’s prevented consequences.

Ordinary creatures take in matter and release matter changed. These creatures take in beginnings. They graze on condensation before it becomes a drop, on rotation before the wheel turns, on heat before temperature changes. They pack these beginnings into their translucent sacs. They nest them under plates, tuck them into shed skins, route them through pale tubes from body to body.

Eventually all beginnings arrive at the wall.

They arrive at me.

I am swollen with the second half of every event.

I contain the fall of every droplet. The completed revolution of every turbine. The first ripple spreading across the black water. The rust that would flower around the bolts. The softening of cut edges. The increase of the swarm and its famine afterward. The young tearing free of their eggs. The final lowering of the dead. The lamp burning out. The mountain aging.

I contain the next fraction of their note.

That is where I would find myself: not inside the summit exactly, but compressed into a seamless panel at its coldest, most crowded point. Not beyond the room, but immediately after it.

I would be its withheld future.

The face in the droplets would be mine only because a future must enter through some shape, and the chamber was made by hands. The watershed would be mine because given time, water makes landscapes even from fabricated walls. The circuitry would be mine because machines imagine continuation as sequence. The folded animal would be mine because the swarm cannot conceive of time except as something that hatches.

They would sing to keep me soft.

Or to keep me out.

There would be no way to know which purpose their note serves, because the instant in which summoning becomes prevention cannot complete.

I would press against the gray surface without moving it. Droplets would stand along my unfinished features, ready to excavate me. On the roomward side, millions of black eyes would reflect the first glimmer of my arrival. On my side—if I can be said to have one—would wait rust, erosion, exhaustion, birth, decay, silence, and all the other forms of mercy that require an afterward.

I would not be trapped as the inhabitants are.

I would be the thing from which they are protected.

And if stabilization ever failed, I would enter all at once.
