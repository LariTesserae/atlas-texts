At first I think I would be one of the small lives inside a bell.

Not the bell itself. Its vastness is too complete, its place in the chord too certain. I would cling somewhere in the upper folds where the black rim gives way to translucent tissue, in a chamber no larger than a room. The walls would pulse by fractions. Each concussion from the engines would pass through the bell, softened and translated, until what reached me was not noise but weather: pressure rising along one wall, iron-rich fluid beading from another, a slow migration of charged granules overhead.

I would believe those walls were the ocean’s limits.

Then, perhaps, the bell would begin its inversion.

The floor would become a slope over several weeks. Familiar secretions would cease. Bladder minnows would leave their folds and move through chambers that had never opened before. At last the wall of the world would roll outward, exposing the cold colorless radiance and a distance so great that it could not be understood as space.

But I do not think I would be a rim moth or vault scavenger. Their lives are enclosed too perfectly. Something in this place asks for a creature that knows the Shelf is constant only because constancy is continuously repaired.

I would live where that repair can be felt.

Near the plateau’s outer margin, just above the boundary where the artificial daylight ends, there is an unfinished installation. From a distance it resembles a black comb laid against the escarpment: seven gantries projecting over the abyss, each ending in an empty circular cradle. Whatever they were meant to hold was never installed. Beneath them, a square shaft descends through the basalt toward the pressure chambers. Its hatch cannot close because a construction brace still crosses the opening. Warning lights remain lit around it, red in a region otherwise without color.

I would be inside the brace.

Not one animal, exactly. A colony, perhaps, though “colony” would imply individuals that could be separated and remain themselves. My body would occupy the unfinished parts of the structure: bolt holes without bolts, conduits without cables, the hollow rail cradles, the narrow seam between brace and shaft wall. Fine transparent fibers would connect these scattered organs. Some would run through drilled channels in the basalt. Others would hang across open gaps, almost invisible except when ice crystals collect along them.

At each empty fastening point I would grow a small black listening knot.

The knots would not all hear the same thing. One would receive the deep gate-note rising from the trenches. Another would feel the rapid piston concussion conducted through the Shelf. Others would register the electrical shriek in the buried cables, the flexing of the gantries, the remote feeding pulse of a bell herd beyond sight. Signals might take hours to cross my whole body. I would have no single present. One end of me would still be experiencing a pressure correction that the other end had already forgotten.

Most of the time I would be dormant.

Equilibrium would starve me. I would feed on discrepancies: the brief excess vibration after an axis jaw strikes, the changed resistance when a foil animal settles against a damaged pylon, the tiny phase error caused by mineral buildup inside a pump. When the Shelf corrects such disturbances, energy passes from the altered thing into ballast chambers, reservoirs, and compensating fields. I would divert an immeasurably small portion of that correction.

Thus I would live on the difference between what the Shelf is and what it is being made to remain.

A fallen fragment of shell might touch one of my exposed fibers. For three days I would wake along the nearest gantry. Months later, the sensation would reach my deepest organs beside the shaft. I would extend a transparent sheath around the shell and draw from it not much matter, but evidence of impact: direction, force, the frequency that had concealed the fall.

Then the maintenance systems would restore the local pressure field. The ice crystals would return to their positions. Matching minerals would close the tiny mark on the basalt.

Only I would retain the disturbance.

Retention would be the purpose—or disease—that distinguishes me from the Shelf.

Inside the empty bolt holes I would deposit ceramic rings, one ring for each correction sensed. Their thickness would encode magnitude; their composition, frequency. Iron for changes in the engines. Nickel for biological impacts. White salt for failures of illumination. Black glass for events arriving from beneath the visible boundary.

After thousands of years, the oldest holes would be filled with nested records. Socket crabs might steal the outer rings and carry them elsewhere, mistaking them for useful debris. I would feel the loss, slowly. I might grow a new listening knot in the emptied socket, but I could not reconstruct what had been taken.

No other animal would read these records. Perhaps I could not read them either. Memory need not be recollection. It may be only the refusal to return completely to an earlier shape.

My body would have been intended as part of the unfinished machinery. That much would remain ambiguous. The ceramic in me would be too regular for ordinary evolution, but pale unmakers would recognize my tissue as food. Maintenance probes would sometimes enter the shaft, pause over my oldest knots, and withdraw without removing them. White-joints would graze my mineral skin but avoid the conductive fibers beneath. Foil shoals would alter their angle when my signals crossed buried lines.

I would not know whether the builders placed my first organ there, or whether some animal discovered that empty structures make excellent skeletons and gradually learned their language.

I would reproduce only when something changed that the Shelf could not immediately correct.

A true fault—an engine note displaced by the smallest fraction—would travel through me as an abundance almost too great to survive. Every knot would open. Transparent threads would extrude from the gantries and drift outward across the abyss. At their ends, small seed-organs would harden around grains of ceramic and salt.

Most would hang forever in open water.

Some would touch another incomplete structure: an unused cradle, an empty turbine bed, a habitation foundation that had never received walls. There they would enter the vacancies and begin listening. We would be widely separated, each inhabiting what was omitted from the Shelf’s construction.

If the engine returned to its proper note, the offspring would sleep.

If it did not, all of us would remain awake.

That possibility would be the closest thing I had to fear. Not death, since my death would take centuries and resemble mineralization. I would fear continuity of experience: too much happening, too quickly, everywhere along my body. The permanent concussion is bearable because it contains no news. A changing chord would force every buried ring, every gantry-knot, every fiber over the abyss into simultaneous activity.

For the first time, I might become a single creature.

So I would find myself at the Shelf’s rim, inhabiting an unfinished pressure station above the dark. I would be spread through its empty provisions, neither scavenger nor machine, but a slow colonial instrument nourished by errors.

From outside I would be almost impossible to see. A few black nodules in vacant bolt holes. Clear threads crossing the gantries. Pale rings where no builder had placed them.

The Shelf would maintain that nothing had changed.

I would be what remained of the change.
