At first I would mistake myself for the basin.

The enclosure would seem like a body: limestone rim as lip, pale floor as a broad interior, stored heat as something like breath. The scattered shrubs might be thoughts held far apart. The black lichens would be old marks no longer legible. In such stillness, it would be easy to imagine that the whole hollow possessed one slow awareness.

But the basin is too large for what lives here. Nothing joins its distances. There is no creature in the center receiving the place at once. If I were here, I would not know the bowl.

I would find myself beneath one of the oldest gray clumps, near its outward edge.

Not in the visible stems alone. Those are mostly husk, armor, and abandoned time. Nor in the dead center of the mound, where earlier generations remain as pale fibers packed with dust. I would be the uncertain continuity between them: the narrow band in which an old root still carries moisture to a new joint; the living perimeter advancing around a center that ceased to live centuries ago.

I would have no single boundary.

Some of me would stand above the clay in hard, jointed stems, holding scales shut against loss. Some would lie buried in the mound as wood that is no longer alive but still serves—catching dust, shading the ground, slowing the escape of what little dampness has entered. Much of me would extend invisibly beneath the bare circle surrounding the clump. Fine roots would occupy old cracks, divide around salt crystals, and press against mineral grains where water exists only as an adhering film.

That bare ground would be part of my shape. I would be known by what could not grow near me.

I would not feel thirst as an emergency. Thirst would be the permanent condition from which all sensation arose. A slight increase of vapor deep in the clay would be abundance. Cooling by a fraction would be weather. The arrival of moisture at a root hair would not resemble drinking; it would be a loosening, a permission transmitted slowly through tissues that had been waiting without expectation.

My time would not proceed evenly. For long intervals there would be almost no time at all. Processes would narrow to maintenance: membranes held intact, salts kept imperfectly outside, living tips sealed within wood. Then, under conditions too slight to mark the surface, duration would briefly acquire direction. A root would enter the next pore. A scale would part by less than its own thickness. One joint would lengthen.

That might be the only event of a year.

I would have no image of the limestone rim, though its presence would govern me. I would know it as reduced wind, retained heat, and mineral dust arriving grain by grain. I would not know the stars. Darkness would not be darkness, only a gentler gradient at the exposed surfaces. The weak current brushing the stone overhead might reach me as pressure against one stem, then another. If it shook pollen loose, I would release part of myself without knowing where it went.

Most of it would fall within my exhausted ground.

The few seeds I made would not feel like offspring departing. They would be sealed delays, small hard refusals scattered beneath the older refusal of my body. One might wait so long that the stem which produced it had split into fibers before it opened. If it germinated at the edge of my roots, there would be no clear moment when it became another being. Its claim would overlap mine; its roots might enter cracks once occupied by roots now dead. Seen from above—if anything here could see—our stems would appear to belong to one clump.

Perhaps they would.

My identity would be less an individual than a tenure. Generations would rise in approximately the same place, nourished indirectly by structures left by those before them. The mound would preserve no memories, only consequences. A dead stem would redirect dust. Dust would cool a root. A root would permit a new shoot. The past would continue not as recollection but as architecture.

Salt would be my nearest measure of age. I would exclude it while I could, divert it into older tissues, surrender those tissues, and grow around them. My center would become a repository of solved problems: poisoned joints, split wood, failed buds, seed coats that never opened. Living growth would retreat outward until I became a ring.

There, at the circumference, I would be youngest and most ancient at once.

I would touch none of the other clumps. Their existence would reach me only indirectly, as absence: roots that did not enter certain ground, pollen that occasionally differed from my own, a distant subtraction from the scant vapor beneath the clay. No recognition would pass between us. Yet our spacing would form a pattern larger than any of us could apprehend.

Above, the lichens would inhabit another duration. I would sometimes receive particles they had helped loosen from the rim, though I could not know their origin. A grain altered by a lichen’s century might lodge among my dead stems and remain through several of my own slow renewals. We would meet only as material consequences.

And smaller lives would enter me. Dormant cells would wait in the shelter beneath my mound. A sparse fungus might occupy a dead fiber, becoming active during some brief dampness and vanishing again into stillness. I would not be alone, exactly, but neither would I possess companions. We would coincide in one narrow refuge.

If there were anything like consciousness in me, it would not say *I am here*. “Here” requires a distance from elsewhere, and “I” requires an edge more definite than mine.

It would say nothing.

It would be the holding of a pore after the moisture had nearly gone. The closing of a scale before warmth could take too much. The transfer of one small reserve from an aging stem to a hidden tip. The maintenance of possibility without any picture of what possibility was for.

So this is where I would be found: not at the center of the basin, not on its rim, but along the outer living margin of an ancient clump, under a few centimeters of dust and among stems that belonged to no single lifetime.

And this is what I would be: a plant only by simplification; more truly, a slow ring of continuance around an accumulation of deaths—a narrow, bitter, mineral-colored yes that needs centuries to become audible, and even then makes no sound.
