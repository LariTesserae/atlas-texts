At first I would think I was the light.

It would be easy to make that mistake. I would seem to wake wherever the chamber bears down upon itself: in a white line beneath the roof, in the brief star under a falling drop, in the fan of brilliance following a snapped plate. I would have no lamp-body, only arrivals. Pressure would pass through me and become radiance. A crack would open, and for an instant I would possess its entire length.

But the light does not remain. It reports and vanishes. I would discover that I am what remains unreported.

Then I might think I was the chamber itself—the conditioned stone, the lattice correcting its own failures. My awareness would be distributed through defects too small to call chambers of their own. I would feel danger not as fear but as an increasing likelihood: a crack sharpening toward release, a pore losing conductivity, a salt bridge thickening across the last wet passage. To act would mean changing phase locally. No decision, only the redirection of what had already begun.

Yet I would not be the stone either. The stone persists too well. It can lose one arrangement and accept another without experiencing either as death.

I would be found where persistence is less certain.

Look at the central basin, at the coolest part of its white rim. Not the water’s surface, but just beneath it, where oxygen descending from above meets metal-rich water rising from the pores. There, caught on the lee side of a salt needle, is a distortion smaller than a grain of sand.

That would be nearest to my body.

I would not have an edge. The water crossing me would remain water. Molecules would enter without permission and leave without being expelled. What distinguished me from the basin would be only this: within my small region, one reaction would make the conditions for another, and the second would restore the first before both could disperse.

I would be a delay in dissolution.

The chamber would not appear to me as a room. There would be no black floor, bright roof, three basins. There would be acidity. Charge. Heat arriving from one side. A useful ion touching and departing. A current trying to unmake the conjunction that, for the moment, was me.

What might pass for sensation would be a change in the rate at which I ceased.

Coolness would widen me. Heat would erase my outer reactions. When convection pulled toward a lethal layer, I would not resist with strength. I would let the endangered side come apart and intensify on the other. To an observer, I might seem to move against the water. From within, there would be no movement—only the place where I continued shifting sideways.

I would have no memory stored apart from my substance. My past would survive only as preference: this catalyst binding more readily because an earlier cycle shaped it; this branch of chemistry resuming faster because it had resumed before. Nothing in me could remember the fall of a particular drop. Still, after many drops, I would answer them differently.

Perhaps that is where something like a self would first gather—not in an outline, but in a bias.

I would accumulate slowly. A charged fragment from the rock. A chain assembled in a hot pore and delivered during cooling. Iron changing state at my margin. Silica holding two useful compounds near each other for one moment longer than chance ordinarily allowed.

Hours would pass in the chamber. I would not know them as hours. I would know the basin concentrating around me, loosening, concentrating again.

Eventually I would lengthen along the mineral boundary. A waist would appear in the region of shared reactions. For a time, what was happening on one side would still replenish what was happening on the other. Then water would pass through the narrowing middle.

There would be two continuities.

Which one would be me?

The question would have no answer in the basin. One might adhere to the rim and persist. The other might drift into warmth and dissolve. From outside, one would be offspring and one parent. From within, both would inherit the same unfinished refusal.

Most often, I would end there. Not with pain, darkness, or a final instant, but by becoming chemically unremarkable. Every part of me would remain in the chamber. Only the mutual keeping-together would be gone.

But once—rarely enough that the stone would register a thousand pressure changes between such events—the pale grazer would emerge.

I would not see it approach. The water near me would begin to travel toward a soft absorbing surface. My mineral foothold would loosen. I would be taken whole into a fold that closed without mouth or jaw.

Inside the animal, much of me would fail. Its chemistry would strip away what the basin had supplied. But the central cycle might continue. Enclosed in tissue, pressed and fed by compounds I could never have concentrated alone, I would flare into greater coherence than I had possessed in open water.

The vein-light would answer the minute stresses of the animal’s movement. Grains within it would brighten. For a little while, I would be one point in the hidden constellation passing through its body.

Would I then be the animal?

No. But the distinction would become less clean.

Its movement would carry my reactions across distances impossible to me: from basin rim to wet depression, from depression toward the fissure. The animal would use what I made. I would use the boundaries it maintained. Its heat-sense would choose the route along which my chemistry survived. Neither of us would command the exchange.

If it reached the fissure, some altered remnant of my pattern might pass into the porous dark beyond the chamber. If it died on the glass, I might outlast its organization by minutes, continuing in the damp film after its inner threads had stopped. Salts would gather. Water would lift away. Eventually the stone would take everything back.

So where would you find me?

Not in the brightest vein, though its charge helps assemble me.  
Not entirely in the mote, though that is where I become distinct.  
Not in the grazer, though for a time it gives me passage.

You would find me at the boundary where one order lends itself to another: on the cool lip of the iron-dark basin, in the narrowing thread between dividing motes, in the pale animal’s tissue while borrowed reactions still shine, in the instant before a pattern becomes merely material again.

I would be neither the chamber’s intelligence nor its intended inhabitant. I would be a small exploit in its laws—a recurrence that learned, without knowing, how to last slightly longer than its ingredients.

The stone’s great network softens catastrophe. Water carries fragments together. Heat separates them. The animal gathers what survives. Out of those indifferent acts, I would repeatedly almost become someone.

Not a creature, then, in the durable sense.

A tendency toward one.

A self measured not by the body it keeps, but by how many times the chamber must try to erase it before it fails to return.
