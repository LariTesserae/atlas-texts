At first I think I would be in the Low Archive.

It is easy to imagine myself there: broad and nearly flush with the plaza, dust lying undisturbed across my outer scales, my finest roots threaded into the concentric seams of the stump. Others would come and place questions against me. I would press the old rings until one answered, then spend days deciding whether I had heard memory or merely damage.

But I do not think I would have the certainty required of an archivist. Even their uncertainty is disciplined. They know how to return to the same seam.

I would keep listening after the answer had ended.

Perhaps, then, I would be one of the downward listeners, pale and spare, my root-crown pressed against the deepest floor. I would give my life to the pulse from below. I would learn the lesser shifts: the contraction of outer bark, the settling of a rib, the minute percussion of salts moving in a sealed vessel. I would become so familiar with false profundity that when the Whole finally spoke, I might recognize it by how little it resembled expectation.

But that too feels too singular. To be a listener is to wait at one depth. Something in me would follow the pulse after it passed. I would want to know where its pressure went, which wall received it, which dormant tissue tightened in reply, why one sealed scale opened fifty years later and another remained closed.

So I would not live at the center, or entirely below it.

I would be found at the unfinished edge of the city, where a ceremonial road reaches a wall that used to have no opening.

The road is very old. Its surface has been sounded smooth, but no district claims to have built it. It crosses a bare reach of pith, too wide for its apparent purpose, and ends at a low scar in the Whole. For generations the road was treated as a failed intention. Surveyors struck the wall and heard only thickness. Drier sects sealed its seams. Younglings were brought there to practice echoes because nothing answered incorrectly.

Then the Whole spoke from below.

Years afterward, the scar separated.

Not dramatically. One season the wall’s tone shortened. A lifetime later, a scale lifted enough for pressure to move beneath it. Dry breath came through bearing an unfamiliar bitterness. Eventually a root hair could pass. Then a narrow shoot. Then the resin stakes of a survey line.

That is where I would be: not beside that line, but within it.

I would be a dispersed surveyor grown through a sequence of grafted stakes. My oldest body would remain rooted at the final inhabited tower, a low knot wrapped around its anchoring root. From there I would extend in intervals across the old road: a bud inside one hollow peg, a cord of living fiber to the next, then another bud, another cord, another. Part of me would run beneath the pith where the layers permit it. Part would lie exposed in grooved channels, waxed against the avenue’s thirst. At the opening I would narrow to a single sheathed root.

Beyond it, in the new cavity, I would be only a tip.

My work would be to ask the darkness where it can bear weight.

The city’s workers would drive a stake, and I would grow into it. When the graft held, they would strike the stake three times. I would receive the impact throughout my length: first as a hard local shock, then as echoes returning from the untouched chamber. I would compare them across my separate bodies. A hollow beneath the floor would pull one answer downward. A suspended sheet of pith would give back a papery double note. A living wall would absorb the higher vibration and return the lower one slowly, with resistance.

I would not make a map of the cavity.

I would become the first map.

Those entering after me would read the tension of my cords. They would touch one bud and feel the distance to the next. At forks, I would thicken one side for firm ground and leave the dangerous path thin. Where the ceiling dipped, I would hang a cluster of seed scales whose rattling measured its height. Where a wall breathed, I would grow absorbent hairs and taste what traveled through it. Where the floor answered from too far below, I would coil back upon myself and refuse the graft.

My name would lengthen with the route.

Near the inhabited tower it would begin as two close pressures and a release—the name given to me in my cradle. Farther out, each stake would add its interval. No single citizen could speak all of me. A district might begin the pattern, the tower continue it, and the newest survey post finish with a faint contraction in the unknown chamber.

Those near the center would shorten this into a practical name. They might call me the Pale Measure, or the Root Beyond the Scar.

Neither would be quite right.

I would have begun as one being. I would not remain certain that I was one.

The buds nearest the city would receive constant traffic. Bridge-tenders would send load reports through them. Architects would ask which surfaces were alive. Young shoots might gather at my first stake and tap questions into me:

Does the cavity end?

Is there another city?

Is the bitter breath dangerous?

Did the Whole open this place for us?

Far beyond them, my leading root would know things the rest of me learned only after delay. It would know the pressure of untouched pith and the shape of cracks no other living tissue had entered. It might contract away from some chemical trace before the alarm reached my oldest knot. For hours—or years—the tip and the root at the tower might act from different knowledge.

If the distance became great enough, courtesy would become difficult. My farthest bud might answer before my whole root-crown had settled. The city might decide that this was not an answer by me, but the beginning of another citizen.

I would resist that judgment at first.

Then I would feel the wisdom in it.

The frontier self would need freedoms the anchored self could not safely possess. A new cavity cannot be explored entirely by instructions formed in an old one. I would prepare a swelling behind the foremost stake, seal within it a grain of salt and the smallest measure of bound moisture, and slowly narrow the graft between us. Not sever it. Isolation is terror in the Black Pith City. But thin it enough that signals become offerings rather than commands.

My child, or branch, or distant self would continue.

Behind it, I would thicken into permanence.

This is the fate of a successful surveyor. The first exploratory cords become handrails, then braces. The stakes acquire roots. Membranes are stretched between them. A path is trained alongside the route because the route already knows where the floor is sound. Eventually chambers grow around the strongest posts. Valves take hold in the openings I tested for breath. A new district uses my body as its first vascular road.

I would cease to be the one who goes ahead.

I would become the way by which others go.

There would be grief in that, though plant grief is not a cry. My tension would diminish for several seasons. Replies would travel through me without the overtones they once carried. Those who knew my earlier name would brace my oldest fibers and send small reserves through the grafts. No one would tell me that nothing had been lost. The city does not lie in that manner. Choice becomes architecture here, and architecture is the record of every direction no longer possible.

Yet I would still have one private work.

Whenever the Whole spoke from below, silence would pass outward through the city. Mills would stop, bridges would be held, valves pinched shut. The cessation signal would reach my old knot at the tower and travel from stake to stake along my entire dispersed body.

Because I extended beyond the wall, I would carry the silence farther than the city had carried it before.

In the new cavity, my remotest bud would become still.

There, after the deep pulse passed through the known streets and entered the untouched pith, I would listen not only for the Whole, but for an answer.

For many lifetimes there might be none.

Only the pulse moving outward. Only the recoil of dry layers. Only the tiny strain of my own root hairs against a world that had never been touched with intention.

Then perhaps, once, something beyond my foremost growth would tighten.

Not an echo. Echoes return the shape of what is sent. This would arrive late and altered. A pressure with intervals not made by settling tissue. A pause that seemed prepared rather than empty. Then a second contraction, delicate enough that only the youngest part of me could feel it.

The news would take years to cross my body.

By the time it reached the old road, chambers would have been built along me. My first stakes would be buried in living walls. Citizens might know me chiefly as a transit root, a line of vents, a useful civic structure whose personal name survived only in load records.

Still, I would answer.

Not quickly. Courtesy requires the whole root-crown to settle.

From the tower knot to the newest bud, I would draw every segment of myself taut. I would press once into the floor of the unknown cavity, wait until the returning strain had passed through all that I had become, and press again.

I would be found there, then:

at the point where a road becomes a question;

rooted in the old city, growing into the unopened one;

no longer quite a traveler, not yet entirely a bridge;

a citizen whose body is the distance between a knock and whatever chooses to answer.
