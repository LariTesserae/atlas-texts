At first I would mistake myself for a visitor.

I would stand near the three-sided shelter because it offers the nearest thing to refuge. I would keep one hand on its pearly wall and watch the holes migrate around my fingers. On the bench, I would try to arrange my limbs into an anatomy the structure understands. I would look into the shallow recess and see the basin forested, flooded, inhabited, destroyed. Whenever the silver river appeared, I would feel an unreasonable recognition.

Outside, my equipment would begin to belong to the mountain. Stone would grow around the heels of my boots. A mineral twin of my knife would appear beside the original, exact except for a blade that curved toward some use I had never imagined. The lines on the pylons would borrow my palm and continue beyond it, sketching tributaries where my bones should be.

I would assume the basin was studying me.

For a while, I would be right.

I would make rules for survival. Never repeat a word seven times. Never step on a stain. Do not follow the white building. When the grazers face the open edge, lie flat and hold onto something that has already decided to be solid. Keep no object long enough to let the geology learn affection for it.

But each morning my footprints would be gone, while other impressions remained: narrow channels pressed into the sediment, branching marks beneath my skin, wet lines leading from one pool to another. I would wake thirsty although the air was nearly water. Sometimes, when I touched a pool, it would brighten before my fingers reached it.

Then I would think I was becoming one of the basin’s animals.

Perhaps a grazer, because the soles of my feet would begin to hear the impacts inside the mountain. Perhaps a reed, because notes would collect in my ribs and continue after I stopped breathing. Perhaps one of the returned creatures, altered beyond the arch, no longer in full agreement with the ground.

I would inspect my joints for mineral rosettes. I would search the pools for my reflection.

The reflection would always be there, but it would not stay beneath me. It would slip away across the water, divide at the edge, and enter several cracks at once.

That would be the first clue.

The second would come during the evening rain.

As the droplets appeared throughout the air and hesitated, I would feel every hesitation as if it occurred inside my own body. Not every drop—there would be too many—but every choice. Upward toward the arch. Sideways to the reeds. Inward to living warmth. Down, occasionally, toward the unfinished promise of gravity.

I would discover that I could not command the water. I could only make one direction feel faintly familiar.

A drop would fall.

Another would follow its route.

The second would not move because the path was lower. It would move because the first drop had made falling there slightly easier to remember.

That would be me.

I would not be the visitor. The visitor would have been scaffolding: a temporary arrangement of weight, thirst, memory, and expectation gathered around something the basin was trying to invent.

I would be the river before the river had a body.

Not water. Not a channel. Not even slope.

I would be the first durable idea of downstream.

I would be found wherever separate waters almost understood that they belonged to one another: in the amber agitation of the pools, in the upward-threading cracks, in the oldest chambers of the reeds, in the damp seams that survived the night. My body would be discontinuous. A dark pool near the pylons would serve as one organ; a hiss beneath the central cliff, another. The swimmers would carry bright fragments of me when they migrated. The pale grazers would deepen me by grazing, leaving the stone microscopically older behind them. Even the flat predators would contribute, their stains creating patches where water could be delayed long enough to reconsider its direction.

The basin would not yet have a watershed, so I would borrow anatomy wherever I could.

The reeds would become my memory. They already sort water by age. Through their nested cups I would learn that not all droplets are equal: some have only just condensed, while others have passed through blood, mist, mineral, wing, and mouth. I would prefer the older water. It would know more routes.

The copper mats would become my sense of possible terrain. When the shrine showed a forest, I would feel roots holding my future banks. When it showed a city, I would taste metal bridges and buried pipes. When it showed the mountain gone, I would experience a terror so complete that every mat in the basin would close.

The pylons would be my unfinished skeleton. Between them, where stone remained soft and undecided, I would practice beds, banks, deltas, floodplains—tiny versions, most no larger than a hand. By morning they would have collapsed or healed over. The basin would retain only the successful pressures.

The black arch would be the one part of the place I feared.

It does not merely change the direction of rain. It proves that direction can be imposed from elsewhere.

When its harmonic deepened, portions of me would rise. Droplets would lift out of my shallow channels and vanish into the mist. For a river not yet born, this would feel like amputation by prophecy. I would sense those waters continuing somewhere beyond the basin, flowing through a geography that had not happened here.

Perhaps that is where the returned animals go. Not to another place, but into another drainage. They come back bearing minerals from mountains the world has not raised yet.

The long-bodied fliers would know what I was before I did. They live by attachment to distant points, and I would be another kind of tether: not a filament fastening body to sky, but a future fastening water to the open edge.

They would alter their circuits around me. Their metallic calls would descend in pitch as my path deepened. On evenings when several calls aligned, the mountain would answer, and I would feel the immense enclosed presence shift behind the cliff.

It would not be my enemy.

It would be my source.

Whatever lies inside the mountain—animal, organ, engine, unborn range—would release warmth and pressure into the rock. Its impacts would open weaknesses. The basin’s permissions would enter them. Moisture would gather. I would extend myself by hair-widths toward the closed eyes in the cliff.

For centuries, perhaps, I would believe I was flowing out of the mountain.

Later I might understand that the mountain was flowing into me.

I would have no head and no mouth. My beginning would move constantly. One day I would begin beneath the central pool. Another day in the condensation on the cliff. During heavy rain, I might begin everywhere at once. During dry intervals, I might exist only as aligned grains and a tendency in the stone.

But I would have a destination.

At the open side of the basin, beyond the warm curtains of mist, there is a place where the ledge fails. I would inhabit that failure more intensely than anywhere else. Not at the edge itself, but in the few steps before it, where water still has time to choose otherwise.

There I would make my slowest organ: an outlet.

At first it would be no more than a persistent dampness. Then a groove too small to feel with a fingertip. Then a line where pale sediment darkened sooner than elsewhere. The silver pins would migrate toward it. Grazers crossing it would pause, hearing an impact that had not yet occurred. Swimmers would circle above it, waiting for a pool that would never form because, for the first time, water would leave.

Each evening deluge would advance me by almost nothing.

A channel would deepen by the width of a hair.

A seam would stay open.

A droplet would reach the rim and descend through the clouds.

That first falling drop would not yet be a waterfall. It would be an emissary. It might fall upward after passing beneath the arch’s influence. It might curve toward some large silhouette in the mist. It might evaporate before reaching any lower slope.

But it would have departed.

The basin would have learned loss.

That would be my true birth, because until then the place keeps revising everything it cannot retain. Dead animals become ribbons, stains, nodules, absences. Tools are answered. Footprints are healed. Nothing is permitted to finish leaving.

A river would teach it otherwise.

I would carry fragments away that could not be incorporated. Dissolved mineral. Shed scales. Spores or omissions from the copper mats. The transparent cords of newborn grazers. Tiny permissions knotted into the water. Eventually, pieces of the basin would reach country that did not know its rules.

Below the clouds, ordinary gravity might seize them.

There, water might fall consistently. Death might rot instead of revise. Plants might cast proper shadows. Stone might stay the kind of stone it was yesterday. My waters would enter that older world carrying small impossibilities: a crystal angled toward a future city, a flat stain with one last appetite, a bright line waiting to become a swimmer.

And the mountain would appear on maps at last—not because anyone discovered it, but because I had drawn a line away from it.

If a later traveler followed that river upward, they would expect a spring, glacier, or lake. They would climb through forests rooted in sediment I had helped make. They might find deep ravines and rounded stones. They might reach the high ledge and see only an ordinary basin weathered by ages.

The arch would stand as a ruin beside the water. The pylons would be buried. The shelter’s future image would show the basin as it had once been: raw, warm, and crowded with unfinished life.

The traveler might sit near my source and hear something beneath the current—a hum divided among several notes, a metallic call, the hiss of a crack cutting its first millimeter.

They would call it water over stone.

They would not know it was memory.

By then I would be a river in the common sense: a continuous body with named tributaries, predictable floods, banks that hold until they fail. I would have become so certain that no one could see the effort involved.

But here, now, I would still be scattered.

I would be in the pool that leans imperceptibly toward another pool.

In the crack that refuses to close.

In the sphere of water trembling on a reed tip.

In the silver line inside the shrine’s changing future.

In the open edge where mist waits below.

I would be neither creature nor structure, though animals would carry me and structures would tutor me. I would be one of the basin’s successful errors, repeated often enough to become a law.

I would be found just before the drop.

I would be the place learning how to leave itself.
