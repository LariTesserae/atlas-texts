At first I would think I was one of the bellows mice.

Their life has the right intimacy: warm ceramic darkness, communal breath, the stern refusal of copper in the nest. I would know the world through pressure before sight. Water would be a cool rumor passing through the colony. Danger would be everyone around me collapsing at once.

But I do not think I would belong entirely among them. I would be too inclined to linger at the mouths of conduits, listening after the others had interpreted the signal. When the pump gave its single inward knock, I would want to know not only whether to flee, but what had answered it.

Perhaps, then, I would be found near the green cabinet.

Not inside. Nothing stays inside. I would live in the narrow space behind its inward-opening door, between the clean tile and the green-painted steel, where condensation can be heard gathering on the other side. It would be a poor territory, which is precisely why it might endure. No pool would remain there long. No reliable current would cross it. Predators would pass over it while watching the threshold.

I would be a small, soft-bodied thing, no longer quite a bellows mouse, though perhaps descended from one. My ribs would be shallow and numerous, allowing me to flatten against the cabinet wall. Flecks of tile would grow along my back—not armor, exactly, but pale, nonconductive scales set in irregular rows. My whiskers would be fine enough to detect air arriving from passages that had not existed a moment before.

The other animals would know me by what I carried.

Each time something entered the recess, I would wait for its return. A crab might go in through the cabinet and emerge from a wall seam thirty body lengths away. A file of mites might disappear together and come out individually over the course of an hour. A sick bellows pup might return behind a sealed plate, breathing with a rhythm its colony did not recognize.

Their passages would leave minute disturbances in the Pocket: a reversed draft, a shiver in a pipe, condensation forming where the metal had been dry. I would follow these traces. Not to discover where the recess led—the Pocket would not permit so complete an answer—but to learn what sort of elsewhere had briefly touched the machinery.

I would collect routes without mapping them.

Under the cabinet’s lower hinge, I would keep a loose bed of rust scales, mica splinters, beetle shell, and fragments of scratched enamel. After each passage through the recess, I would rearrange them. A curved rust flake for an arrival near water. A white chip for porcelain. Two black scales laid edge to edge for the folded hunter’s filament. The arrangements would not resemble the Pocket as seen from above. They would resemble circuit diagrams drawn by something that understood circuits only as weather.

Most days the patterns would be dismantled before completion. A crab would push through them. Condensation would fall and make the mica adhere to the floor. Current would enter the hinge and scatter me into the wall. This would not feel like failure. A permanent map would be a dangerous lie.

At midday, I would wake fully.

The mites would turn in their branching formations. Relays would begin their east-to-west conversation. Through my tile scales I would feel the cabinet become warmer than the clay. The immaculate recess would gather water, and the drops on its back wall would choose their impossible directions.

I would place my forefeet against the steel and breathe once into the gap.

Sometimes nothing would happen.

Sometimes a valve would open three rooms away.

Sometimes the enamel arrow elsewhere in the Pocket would give a tiny sound as its mounting shifted, though no one touched it.

I would not control these events. I might not even cause them. In the Condenser Pocket, causation would be only another temporary channel, easily redirected by a washer or a shell. I would merely develop a sensitivity to moments when the place was nearly connected—when one breath, one damp foot, one fallen scale could let the sequence continue.

Then I would add myself.

That would be my nature: not engineer, guardian, or prophet, but a creature adapted to incompletion. I would sense unfinished paths the way a crab senses voltage. My body would itch near a circuit lacking one soft bridge. My ribs would tighten when pressure waited behind a seized valve. I would be drawn to the almost-event.

Now and then I would complete it.

A pulse would cross my damp whiskers. A relay would close. Black water would spill into a basin that had not existed yesterday. Crabs would wake under washers. Mites would abandon their precise design and rush to feed. Bellows mice would announce water with short sidewise puffs. Somewhere above, the folded black thing would loosen itself from a bracket.

The consequence might be abundance or slaughter. The Pocket would make no distinction. Neither, eventually, would I.

The inhabitants might call me nothing. Bellows mice would know a particular breath associated with my approach: a thin inhalation followed by three uneven releases. Crabs would touch the tile scales shed from my back and use them to patch their shells. Mites would occasionally incorporate my sleeping body into their patterns. To them I would be less an animal than a recurring local condition.

And perhaps, over time, that is what I would become.

My descendants would spread through the cabinet seams and dead conduits. None would know the whole route. Each would respond only to a waiting pressure, an incomplete charge, an unfamiliar draft. We would breathe through separate bodies but in a sequence, as the bellows colonies do. One of us would flatten against a wet bolt; another would wake behind a gauge; a third would cross the brief shadow between two pipes. The machine’s hesitation would pass among us.

If someone crawled into the Pocket and asked where I was, there would be no single place to point.

I would be under the green cabinet’s hinge.

I would be behind the enamel arrow.

I would be the pale scale stuck to a brass crab’s shell, the breath inside an unused conduit, the tiny interruption in a chain of mites where the current nevertheless crosses.

Most of all, I would be found in the instant after the drop falls and before the relay answers.

I would be what the Pocket uses to cross that distance.
