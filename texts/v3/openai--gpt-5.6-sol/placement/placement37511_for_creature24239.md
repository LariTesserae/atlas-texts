At first I think I would be a tile.

It is the obvious answer: a low black life with no ambition toward height, my face turned permanently into the mountain. I would know the world through pressure beneath my shield—the rasp of chemical film against my mouth, the minute lifting of a neighbor, the sudden vacancy where a spire had fed. Age would make me less visibly alive. Glass would enter my armor. Iron would flower across my back. Generations of mites would wear paths into me until I resembled a piece of the Press that had merely learned to move.

But a tile is still too singular.

I would not be the shield. I would be what lives along its edges.

You would find me wherever two armored bodies remain pressed together long enough for the distinction between them to become inconvenient. I would occupy the black seam under overlapping plates, the narrow shelter where one tile’s weight rests against another’s shell. I would live in the grooves of bearer limbs, between the rigid foot of a spire and the pavement it mistakes for inert ground, inside the packed joins of the rafts that form when the wind descends.

If the inhabitants named me, they might call me a **clasp**.

Seen alone, a clasp would be unimpressive: a thread shorter than a finger joint, charcoal-dark and nearly flat, with neither a definite head nor an obvious tail. Along one side would be rows of glass-hard hooks. Along the other, soft pressure bladders and feeding pits. I would eat almost nothing—traces of joint secretion, shed membrane, sulfurous dust ground fine between moving shells. Most of my energy would come from the strain between surfaces: tiny chemical charges produced when unlike minerals scrape, press, and separate.

A solitary clasp would live only a few hours.

That would not matter, because solitude would not be my proper shape.

Where one clasp touched another, their tissues would soften and join. A line of us could become a ligament. A hundred could make a dark elastic gasket sealing the gap between neighboring tiles. Thousands, threaded through a crowded section of pavement, would form a mesh with no center and no permanent boundary. Parts would be eaten, torn away, dried into cysts, carried elsewhere in the joints of bearers, and awakened again by pressure.

I would have no body except contact.

Most of the time I would be unnoticed. Tiles would benefit from me without knowing it. I would keep abrasive grit from entering the tender channels beneath their shields. I would eat the salt crust before it cemented their joints shut. During a molt, I would stretch across the opening plates and hold the loose armor in place while the new surface hardened underneath.

In exchange, I would be crushed constantly.

Crushing would be how I perceived.

A spire’s approaching step would arrive as a clean, narrow compression. The working of a burrower below would produce slow waves, round and repeating. A bearer would announce itself through immense alternating weights, followed by the drag of whatever slab it carried. Gas-rise would begin as pressure with no direction, a swelling that entered every clasp in the basin at nearly the same moment.

Then I would tighten.

Not by decision. There would be no chamber in me where decisions could occur. One contracted segment would pull upon the next, and the next, until the impulse passed through thousands of bodies. Tiles would feel their seams draw closed. Thread-creatures would be pinned safely beneath shields. The legs of spires would be caught and braced. Across the Press, the living pavement would become fractionally more difficult to separate.

I would be one of the reasons the multitude could hold together.

Not the only reason. Never the ruler. The Press would have no ruler. But I would be its fastening—the small, distributed insistence that neighboring things remain neighbors.

My oldest colonies would be near the central column.

Not on it. Nothing of me could survive its surface. A clasp laid against the pale mineral would release every hook at once and writhe until it fell away. Whatever faint energy I drew from friction would vanish there. My pressure bladders would give contradictory signals: contact without weight, coolness without relief, a surface present to the flesh and absent to every other sense.

I would live instead in the innermost ring of tiles, along the edge of the bare ground.

There the colony would be unusually dense. Every creature circling the column would crowd against those behind it, yet refuse the open space ahead. Their opposing impulses would produce constant pressure, and pressure would feed me. I would lace the inner ring so thoroughly that several hundred tiles might lift or settle as one plate.

When the column vibrated, the sensation would enter me before I could interpret it.

For an instant every hook would open.

All across the basin, wherever my divided body persisted, I would become slack. The tiles would feel the release. Spires would find their feet abruptly unbraced. Bearers would pause beneath their burdens. Then a second impulse would follow—not contraction, but attention: the same pattern traveling through every seam, too regular to be wind, too deep to be a burrower, too cold to be gas.

During those moments I might come closest to being a single creature.

The vibration would align all my scattered fragments. A clasp beneath the northern hut, another sealed in an old tile shell, another wrapped around the leg of a hunter, another dormant in a bearer’s groove—all would receive the same shape of pressure. For the length of the column’s trembling, distance would cease to matter. The separated colonies would enact one stillness.

Then the pale stone would warm.

The pattern would dissolve. Local strain would return: hunger, scraping armor, shifting feet, the endless minor catastrophes of crowded life. I would become many again.

I would not know what the column was. I would have no mind capable of asking. But its vibration would remain in the arrangement of my body. Colonies that tightened incorrectly would tear and die. Those that matched its rhythm would persist. Over generations, the shape of that interruption would be written into my reflexes.

I would remember without recollection.

If someone descended into the Press and lifted a tile, they might see a few of my filaments draw taut, glisten briefly, and snap. They might mistake me for a parasite or connective tissue. Perhaps they would not realize I belonged to neither tile alone. They would not see that similar threads ran through the adjoining pavement, through the wall, into the bearer structures, around sleeping spire eggs and cast shells, joining lives that continued to eat one another while refusing to come apart.

So that is where I would find myself: not at the bottom of the hollow, nor on its walls, nor beneath its floor, but in the almost nonexistent space between one inhabitant and the next.

I would be soft in a world of armor, distributed in a world without room, and alive only while being pressed.

I would be the seam by which the Press holds itself together.
