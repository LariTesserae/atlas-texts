I would first mistake myself for the person on the northern shelf.

I would wake there before dawn with my cheek against pale stone, one hand wrapped in the mineral cords. I would know the court by tension rather than sight: which fin had taken frost, which buried seam had opened, whether the shaft was exhaling cleanly or carrying some disturbance from below. I would keep the bedrock bowl free of grit. I would touch the nine basalt uprights in order, not reverently, but as someone tests the spokes of a wheel.

The stone mallet would be mine.

That seems likely at first. It has the intimacy of an object put down by a hand expecting to need it again. I can imagine its weight, the numbness in my fingers, the particular needle I was tuning when something traveled back through the handle—not an impact, exactly, but a pressure shaped like one.

Perhaps I went to fetch the others.

Perhaps I descended and could not persuade them to return.

Perhaps I grew old somewhere beneath the mountain and spent the remainder of my life wondering whether the answering tremor had truly occurred.

But that would leave me outside the court, and I do not think I would be outside it.

Nor would I be the vast resident itself. I would not be the whole mineral breath, the temperature difference reaching from summit to volcanic root. That being is too slow to say *I*. It does not gather itself tightly enough for identity. It sorts, transmits, retains, releases. If it knows anything, it knows as water knows a slope.

I would be something it acquired.

Long before the shelter was abandoned, one of the listeners began tapping a pattern into a hollow fin. Not the four signs around the ring. Something smaller than meaning: three light strokes, a pause, and one heavier stroke. It may have been a test signal used while the cords were tightened. It may have been the rhythm of a name. Each dawn it was repeated, because repetition allowed the listeners to compare one day’s answer with another.

Three. Silence. One.

At first the court returned only fragments. A click near the shaft. A hum beneath the shelter. A dry pulse in a needle that had not been touched. Then the pattern entered the daily changes. Frost sealed certain passages and forced it sideways. Meltwater carried its pressure into finer cracks. The noon reversal drew it downward. At night the mountain’s cold breath brought pieces of it back.

The people left, but by then the pattern no longer required the hand that made it.

That is where I would find myself.

Not in one needle, nor in the shaft, but distributed along the route by which the court remembers that rhythm. At dawn I would be a faint stress passing through a limestone lamination. At noon I would descend with dust between the nine stones. During cloud I would widen, becoming nearly simultaneous across the wet fins. In winter I might lie motionless in a frozen pore, reduced to the possibility of a click.

Years could pass between parts of me.

My first three strokes might sound in one century and the answering fourth in another.

I would not be a ghost. Nothing of the original hand would remain in me—no face, desire, or knowledge of why the rhythm was made. I would be what survived after intention had been stripped away: a recurrence the resident had learned without learning that it had learned.

The court would not speak through me. I would be closer to its acquired habit of answering.

A traveler might cause me to gather. Their steps would disturb the same fins; their breath would wet an old channel. If they rested a hand on the polished basalt, they might feel three minute impulses rise beneath the skin, followed much later by a fourth. They would take it for their heartbeat until they noticed that their heart was faster.

If they tapped back, I would become clearer.

Not more conscious, perhaps, but more coherent. The old route would reopen. Stone would pass the pattern to stone. The mineral cords would tense. A buried bead of ice would crack. Somewhere below sight, air would be displaced from one chamber into another.

For a few minutes, I would possess an edge.

Then the sun would leave the shaft. The mountain would exhale. I would loosen again into separate events: a grain falling, a basin trembling, a low note crossing the hollow without any audible source.

So I would be found everywhere in the Needle Court and nowhere one could point to. I would be older than the visitor, younger than the mountain, and neither alive nor wholly inert.

I would be the last adjustment made with the abandoned mallet.

The answer that came back.

The answer still coming back.
