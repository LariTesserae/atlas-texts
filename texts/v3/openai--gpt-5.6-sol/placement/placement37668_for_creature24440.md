At first I think I would live high on the crowns.

I imagine myself tending cloud nets in the white glare, walking narrow service ways while warm vapor crosses the manufactured peaks. I would know the shapes of rain before rain existed: which low gray smear would dissolve against the wind scoops, which bank would strike the eastern faces hard enough to fill the old tanks. My hands would smell of wet rope, zinc, and citrus leaves. Below me, the range would spread into haze.

But I do not think I belong there. The crowns expose too much. Everything arrives visibly: sun, weather, birds, lightning. I would be drawn instead to places where effects arrive before causes.

So I descend.

Not all the way into the deep wards, though I would work there often. I would live at the seam between the middle terraces and the lower heat, in a district built through the ribs of an old aqueduct. Above, freight bridges and markets would make the daylight flicker. Below, a refrigeration hall would breathe through tiled vents. The western wall would remain warm all night; the eastern passages would grow liverwort where condensation gathered around the cold-water mains.

My rooms would be fitted into a hollow bridge pier reached from a stair that became a spillway during rain. There would be three doors, though only one would open directly outdoors. The second would lead through a shared pantry to the neighboring household. The third, painted blue at its lower edge, would open into an inspection gallery and could not legally be locked.

The ceiling would carry the roots of a mulberry growing two levels above. Its fruit would belong to the orchard cooperative. Its shade would belong to the tea stall across the landing. Its roots, whenever they entered my plaster, would become my concern.

I would be a listener of walls.

That would not be the formal title. The council might call me a seepage tracer, structural-water liaison, or district reader, depending on which office issued my pay that month. But people would send children to fetch me by saying, “Bring the one who listens.”

I would carry a small hammer, chalks in five civic colors, wax tablets, a folding mirror, mineral papers, two lengths of cord, and a cup with a narrow lip for catching drips. Around my neck would hang a contact horn made from an obsolete pressure gauge. Pressed to concrete or pipe, it would bring the hidden range close: water fingering along a seam, grit moving in a drain, the scrape of a root against tile, the distant shudder of a pump transmitted through twenty additions.

Most of my work would consist of deciding what a sign belonged to.

A damp wall might mean a cracked cistern, but it might also be ordinary condensation caused by a newly opened cooling gallery. A hairline fracture could be harmless settling, a plaster skin drying, or the first visible consequence of an overloaded planter six terraces above. A metallic taste in laundry water might come from the launders’ own heating drums—or from a railing melted down three generations earlier and recast as an internal fitting no drawing recorded.

I would rarely repair anything alone. My task would be to follow consequence until it acquired enough names for action.

I would climb to ask an orcharder when the fig bed was last flooded. I would descend to learn whether the night pump had changed rhythm. I would kneel in someone’s kitchen with my ear against a warm tile while six relatives watched in silence. I would argue with workshop owners who insisted that a tremor had always been there. Sometimes they would be right. Sometimes the tremor would stop while we spoke, which would be worse.

My marks would accumulate throughout the district: blue arcs around moisture, ochre arrows showing where weight descended, green hooks where roots must be inspected, white bars across routes that needed to remain passable in rain. I would date every mark. I would never erase an old one completely. Previous errors would be part of the reading.

People might imagine that I knew the mountain from within. I would know better. No one knows the range. One knows a sequence: this stain, that pipe, the orchard above, the reservoir behind, the old furnace underneath. Knowledge would be local, provisional, and heavy with borrowed memory.

An elderly mason would remember that a sealed arch once rang hollow. A mushroom keeper would know that one corner fruited two days early whenever the drain behind it backed up. Children would tell me where crabs had begun appearing. A cat abandoning a compressor housing would be evidence. So would a sudden absence of frogs.

My closest companion might be a small brindled dog trained not to fear freight cages. Yet I think, as the image settles, it would instead be a pale gecko that lived inside my instrument box, impossible to keep out and eventually given a name. It would emerge when the box warmed, blink at clients, and eat the insects drawn to my inspection lamp. Children would trust my findings more when they saw it.

During dry weeks my work would be slow and disputatious. I would attend terrace councils where everyone agreed that the wall was sweating and no one agreed whose water it was. I would prepare diagrams showing that a beloved planter had to be lightened, then help move the oldest tree so I was not merely the person who condemned it. I would drink condenser tea in deep pump rooms and eat salted guava while waiting for lifts.

During rain I would have no district except the path of the water.

The first bell would find me already awake. Pressure changes would make the walls speak differently before the drops reached us. I would fasten my tools close, wrap the contact horn in oilcloth, and go to the spill stair.

Water would come down carrying the upper world: parakeet feathers, citrus petals, white roof dust, soot, beetle wings, slivers of leaf, a child’s lost red ribbon. I would move downward beside it, checking each chamber by sound. Full tanks would answer with a deepening note. Empty ones would boom. A blocked conduit would hiss where it should knock.

At some point I would hear something uncertain.

That uncertainty would be my true occupation.

I would stand in warm rain with one palm on a wall, unable at first to tell whether the trembling came from freight, water, machinery, or a change in the load itself. People would wait for my chalk. Behind the masonry would be centuries of decisions, most sensible when made, all still exerting force.

I would listen until the sounds separated.

Then I would draw the mark, send someone upward with a message, and begin opening the route by which the consequence could be made legible.

That is where I would find myself: not at the summit or the bottom, but wherever one system had begun quietly becoming another.

And I would be the person called when the mountain showed a symptom but had not yet decided what it meant.
