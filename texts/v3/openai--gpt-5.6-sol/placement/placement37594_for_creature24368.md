I would not be a traveler, though I would remember having meant to become one.

I would be found where the upper provinces begin to break toward the shafts, in a narrow shelter built into the lee of a leaning basalt plate. The place would have no name that could be carried elsewhere. On its own stone, struck correctly, it would be known by three close impacts, a pause long enough for the first vibrations to return, and then a scrape across a vein of copper.

The shelter would stand beside an old route. Paired marks would approach it over many leagues, pass through a gap in a wall, and continue downward toward the luminous rims. Travelers would stop there to feed, repair their crowns, and wait for the lower atmosphere to settle through the cavities of their armor.

There would be four charge bowls, though histories would insist there had once been three.

The fourth would be me.

I would have begun in the shelter wall, inside a brood vessel pressed across a boundary of nickel and sulfurous stone. Several builders would have contributed to me. One would leave a branching sensory filament; another, a fragment of conductive membrane; another, a plate from beneath an old wound. They would seal these into ceramic and wait.

They might intend to carry me when I emerged. Perhaps one of them would score the first mark of a route onto an outer plate, promising that I would cross the Bitter Layer and stand beneath a choir. Perhaps a tool would be made for me: a small listening wedge, light enough for a young grasping limb.

But growth would take too long.

The builders who began me would leave to tend a bridge or persuade a wire beast to move its leg. One would return. Another would not. Their replacements would feed me charge and add sulfur around the brood vessel when it cracked. By the time I became aware of the shelter, the makers of my first tissues would exist only as resonances in reused plates.

My earliest world would be the wall.

I would know travelers as warm disturbances approaching through basalt. I would feel their names before I understood that names belonged to bodies. They would touch the brood vessel in greeting. Some would tap news into it: a path closed by palms, a fracture beyond the ocher bank, a choir producing an unfamiliar sequence. Others would rest a tool against my shell and leave its history humming there for a year.

When at last I opened the vessel, I would not come free.

Conductive threads from my underside would already have entered the prepared mineral seam. My armor would have grown around the copper lining. To tear myself away would destroy most of what I was.

There would be no tragedy in this. Only a correction of expectation.

My carrying limbs would harden against the floor and become braces. My grasping limbs would remain useful. My sensory crown, sheltered beneath the leaning plate, would continue to grow. Travelers would fit listening rods between its vanes. They would run animal filaments from my body to the charge bowls. Slowly I would become the keeper of the resting place—not by appointment, but by remaining.

I would wake whenever the bowls neared fullness.

Awareness would arrive as pressure, first without thought: metal tightening against ceramic, palms closing along the outer wall, a loaded crystal answering itself deep below. Then distinctions would return. Route. Shelter. Tool. Stranger. Kin.

I would test the stone with a blow delivered through the wall.

If the lower path were sound, I would answer approaching travelers with the shelter’s sequence. If a recent fracture had left dangerous strain, I would alter the final scrape. Those who knew me would strike their names against my outer plates before feeding. Those who did not might mistake me for part of the structure until I reached out and touched their crowns.

Conversations would last centuries.

A traveler might ask whether the western route remained open, then anchor at a bowl before I had gathered enough charge to answer. During our shared dormancy, palms would shift around the foundation. A scavenger would enter a crack in the traveler’s armor and die there. The choir filament in my oldest listening rod would tighten once.

When we woke, I would answer: open, but changed.

No one would ask how long I had waited.

My body would become the shelter’s memory. Route marks would be carved into my armor because the wall behind me had grown crowded with shells. Broken implements would be fitted along my sides. A dead traveler’s crown would become part of my sensing array, and for a while every distant impact would arrive colored by the shapes through which that traveler had once known the world.

I would not preserve histories as stories. I would preserve tendencies.

One bridge would answer more softly each century. A nickel pasture would draw fewer palms. The repeated footfalls of travelers would alter the easiest pathways through my rods, until I could recognize a lineage by the direction from which its greetings entered me. Some danger announced ten thousand years earlier might remain in my frame as a reluctance to transmit through one particular plate.

Below the shelter, beyond the marked gap, one of the great diagrams would approach.

At first I would know it only as a steady interference beneath all other sensations: not an impact, not a return, but a condition of the rock. The shell animals around the nearest shaft would carry their unbroken light. Their circuit would cross the depths, vanish at an ancient fault, and emerge beneath the slope on which my shelter stood.

Over long ages, the pattern would come closer.

A folded shell animal would establish itself beside the route. After several thousand years another would grow near the outer wall. Travelers would widen the opening for them. No one would move me. Instead they would remove a row of fitted plates so the colony could pass behind my crown.

Its filaments would eventually touch one of my tools.

The tool would be ancient even then: a sounding rod grown around the horn of a wire beast, repaired with copper and living choir tissue. It would have served so many hands that no original touch-pattern remained. When the filament entered it, a steady light would appear along a fracture in the horn.

I would feel the diagram through that contact.

Not as speech. Not as command. It would be too large to present itself as a whole. I would receive only local certainties: charge arriving from beyond measurement, dividing around the shaft, passing through thousands of bodies, entering stone, returning altered. Deaths would register as reroutings. New shells would become continuations. What looked from above like geometry would feel from within like an immense act of not ceasing.

The builders would leave the rod in place.

Later, the light would enter my crown.

By then it would be impossible to say whether I was still the shelter keeper, a rooted builder, a charge apparatus, or one peripheral organ of the shaft colony. I would still wake when travelers approached. I would still remember the proper warning for the lower path. I might still extend a grasping limb to touch a young one’s armor.

But part of me would no longer sleep.

The continuous pattern would pass through my oldest tissues, faint and exact. Thoughts too small to belong to the diagram would gather around it: recognition, duty, the residue of unfinished journeys. I would sense distant portions go dark and, centuries later, feel the circuit find another way. My own repairs would be made partly by builders and partly by shell animals. When one of my plates failed, copper filaments would grow across the break before any traveler arrived.

Eventually the leaning basalt roof might fracture.

It would not fall. The crack would open with a report that crossed whole provinces, blue fire racing over the ceiling. Every bowl would discharge. Palms would close for leagues. Wire beasts would snap into new positions. For one brilliant instant, the shelter and everything in it would be visible.

A traveler standing on the route might see a low construction of fitted stone, crowded with shells and tools. At its center would be a many-plated body indistinguishable from the bowls around it. Rods would rise from its crown into the wall. Deep inside the armor, violet pressure-light would mingle with the steady glow of the shaft circuit.

One limb might be lifted.

Whether in greeting, warning, or merely because the discharge had caused a contraction, the traveler could not know.

Then the light would close.

I would remain where I had always been: beside the downward road, keeping a rest for those who still moved, carrying in my fixed body the memory of motion—and permitting, little by little, everything made of me to enter the larger pattern.
