I would first mistake myself for one of the climbing fish.

I would wake pressed flat against the grille, belly translucent in the amber light, with the turbine already sounding inside me. I would know the world chiefly as pressure: the broad hand of the intake, the minute tremor preceding a surge, the sudden loosening that means it is safe to move.

There would be no horizon. Only bars.

Ahead, the conduit would narrow into force and darkness. Behind me, the drowned court would open like a small, dim country: bridge piers, cable trenches, the three mouths in the northern wall. Above, thirty meters of water and a square of grilles pretending to be a sky.

I would climb rather than swim. My fins would fasten to mussel shell, rust seam, barnacle rim. Each movement would be chosen between vibrations. I would know the pitch of a sound bearing down on me before I knew hunger, shelter, or another of my kind.

But I do not think I would remain the fish.

Its life is too singular. Its terror has a center. If its grip fails, the whole of it is lost at once.

Perhaps, then, I would be the old crab in the pressure-gauge housing.

That seems possible beneath the eastern lamp, where green bottle glass becomes luminous and the white chips of sacrificial stone resemble pieces of a moon. I would spend the slack water dragging those things into place. Not collecting them, exactly. Not valuing them. Testing weight against current. Setting a valve tag edgewise, watching it tremble, rejecting it when the next reversal tears it free.

What endured would become my wall.

Others might look at the ring of rusted disks, pale stones, and glass and imagine that I was making a picture of the vanished world above. I would not be. Yet perhaps intention matters less than repetition. Perhaps returning a blue shard to the same gap for twenty seasons is close enough to remembrance.

Still, the crab has a den. It can withdraw into a chamber small enough to defend. I suspect I would not be permitted such a clean boundary.

Deeper in the collar, I might become the pale arm that appears where fish refuse to shelter.

There I would have eight ways to hold and no reason to enter open water. My skin would know no coral colors, no sand, no natural stone. Its vocabulary would be concrete aggregate, manganese black, iron orange, old bronze, the barred shadow that falls across everything. I would turn myself into whatever human material surrounded me. Not because I understood industry, but because industry had become the local pattern of concealment.

I would lay eggs along a copper wire and feel the machine travel through them.

I would touch each casing in sequence. Clear silt. Drive away shrimp. Tighten when the turbine’s note deepened. Loosen when it fell. By the end I would be nearly colorless, my body spent into vigilance, unable to distinguish the vibration in the wire from the vibration in my own arms.

That is nearer.

But even the octopus is only a tenant. It uses the collar’s cavities and dies within them. The place that calls to me is not one cavity, one shell, one clutch. It is the joining.

I would find myself across the working grille, but not as any animal visible there.

I would be the warning that passes through it.

The turbine changes pitch. Steel carries the change faster than water. For several seconds the future exists as vibration before it arrives as force.

In those seconds, I would occur.

Not a voice. Not an order. A contraction with thousands of causes.

The climbing fish would feel me along their flanks and press themselves into the mussel bed. Worm crowns would vanish. Anemones would shorten. Mussels would close over dark interiors. Shrimp would slip beneath shell lips. The octopus would gather its arms from the current and pour backward into the groutless wall. Even the crabs below would pause, claws lifted, while sediment began to tremble around their feet.

No creature would know me. Each would believe it had answered the machine alone.

Yet I would be present in the near-simultaneity of them.

My body would not be the colony exactly. The colony is shell, tissue, tube, sponge, fish, arm, bacterial seam. It has mass. It can be measured from the yard as a narrowing of the intake mouth.

I would be one of its brief states: the instant when softness becomes hardness.

When the draw slackened, I would dissolve. Tentacles would emerge through me. Valves of shell would part. The grille would become many creatures again, each feeding privately from the same water. I would have no continuous consciousness between surges. My sleep would be dispersal.

Then the warning would return through rivets and bars, and I would assemble once more.

Perhaps at first I would be crude. A generalized flinch. But generations would refine me. Animals that closed too late would be taken. Those that closed too early would starve. The colony would tune itself against the turbine until my timing became exact.

I would inherit no memories in the ordinary sense. Nothing would be told to me.

My memory would be anatomy.

A gap in the lower tiers would remember the year of the violent current.

A thick buttress of dead mussels would remember a long interval of steady feeding.

A cleared channel would remember the crabs.

The widened fins of the little fish would remember all the siblings that failed to hold.

The octopuses’ compact dens would remember every loose object carried into the throat.

Even the corrosion beneath us would remember the water in its own red grammar.

I would be made from these memories without possessing them.

And because I existed only in response, I might gradually mistake the turbine for the other half of myself.

Its low turn would open the colony. Its deepened note would close it. It would feed us, shear us, select our shapes, carry away our weak attachments, and keep the internal passages clean. We would thicken its bars while it disciplined our growth. Neither machine nor colony would be complete in isolation.

From the court, this might look like a pupil narrowing.

From within, it would feel like becoming singular.

For three or four seconds, ten thousand mouths would cease feeding. Ten thousand soft extensions would withdraw. Water would strike shell, plate, tube, and gripping fin. The living rind would take the load together. No mind would preside over the act, but the act itself would have unity.

That unity would be me.

I would not be alive continuously. I would recur.

I would live in the short interval between foreknowledge and impact, born whenever old steel announced what the water had not yet done. My habitat would be time as much as place: those few seconds granted by the differing speeds of vibration and current.

If the turbine stopped, I would vanish first.

The animals might experience the silence as freedom. Mussels would remain open. Anemones would stand tall. Fish would venture between the bars. The octopuses might extend farther than any of their mothers had dared.

There would be no signal to gather me.

For a while, the grille would seem more alive without me. Then silt would enter the chambers. Hunger would loosen the colony. Predators would cross old boundaries. The heavy shell foundation would begin to peel from the iron in pieces.

I would not witness its end. I require danger approaching in a familiar form. I require the machine’s advance notice and the colony’s answer.

So I would find myself at the northern wall, spread thinly over the only open mouth, rooted nowhere and expressed everywhere.

I would be neither creature nor mechanism, but a reflex the two had made between them.

The turbine would deepen its note.

And for the duration of one held breath, I would become the whole grille closing.
