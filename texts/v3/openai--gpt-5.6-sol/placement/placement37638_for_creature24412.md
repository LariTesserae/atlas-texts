I would not be in the Hollow Ward all at once.

At first, I would be mistaken for evidence that someone had recently passed through.

A handrail beneath an abandoned station would be clean along the length of a palm. A catchment bowl in a waiting niche would have been shifted half a finger’s width so that condensation struck its center. Someone would have untangled the pale vines from an overheating relay and woven them around the cooler housing. There would be three fresh marks in the calcite beside the niche:

a circle crossed by six lines,  
a hooked spiral,  
an open hand.

No footprints. No bedding. No body.

The niche would lie midway up the eastern wall, though the eastern wall would not always be east and the niche would sometimes hang like a shallow ceiling above the central dark. It would overlook one of the persistent pools in the Sink. Seen from there, the amber mat would be no larger than an old coin. During resonance, geometric ripples would form around it but never cross its center.

Behind the niche would be a sealed municipal station called, in worn lettering, **TRANSFER OF SMALL MASSES / MERCY ROUTE 4**.

Only *MERCY* would remain legible.

This is where I would be found, if “found” meant anything more than arriving during the same few seconds as me.

I think I would once have been a recurrent traveler.

I would have worn the crossed straps, the doubled tools, the gripping pads darkened by rust and moss sugar. Along my sleeves, small metal vanes would shiver before the local fields turned. I would carry powder in a narrow tube and cast it ahead of me at boundaries. I would be patient because the Ward punishes hurry less often than it punishes certainty.

My bell would be a plain ceramic thing, narrow at the mouth and too deep in tone to hear comfortably. Its note would be felt in the roots of the teeth. Around its rim I would have written a route notation that no longer matched any route still operating.

Perhaps I would have entered the Ward from the deeper gate. Perhaps I would have been born in some other municipal level and known no surface except as a grammatical relic: a direction people once used when they expected the world to end in sky.

I would come to Mercy Route 4 because something had gone wrong there.

The route-lamps would be waking in sequences without travelers. Doors in the sealed station would open onto rooms that did not presently adjoin them. Sailers approaching the district would veer away, their membranes tightening around an obstacle invisible even to their pressure hairs. Most tellingly, bells struck in the niche would return their notes before the hand reached them.

An open hand had already been carved into the wall.

Do not shout.

I would wait through a rain cycle. Black moss fans would unfold along the station’s lower edge, silver undersides trembling in the dim light. Condensation insects would climb the drops. Far below, the Sink would briefly shine in copper fragments, and the crustaceans would separate themselves from the silt.

When the roar began, I would set both palms against the wall.

There would be another vibration inside it—not the engine’s note, but a pattern of impacts. Soft, irregular, almost lost in the greater resonance.

Footsteps.

Someone was walking through a passage behind the wall.

The station had been sealed for centuries.

I would follow the footsteps by touch. They would lead from the niche toward a maintenance door, pause where no floor continued, and then proceed upward through the stone. At intervals I would feel the pressure of a hand against the other side. Each print would fade before the next appeared.

At last, near the highest anchor loop, the unseen walker would stop.

My bell would ring on its own.

Not loudly. One inward strike.

And from somewhere behind the calcite, my own voice would say:

“Wait.”

That would be the beginning of what I became.

Or perhaps it would be the end, returning early.

I would do what travelers are warned never to do: answer a delayed voice with the same word it has given them. Not from recklessness, but because the voice would sound tired. Because solitude makes courtesy dangerous.

“Wait,” I would say.

The municipal lights beneath the stone would brighten around my hand. The niche would rotate without moving. For an instant, the Sink would appear above me, its pools suspended like tarnished mirrors. The opposite wall would become a floor; then a horizon; then a night sky.

A route would open through Mercy Station.

I would enter.

There would be no visible passage, only attraction: a pressure taking hold of the buckles at my shoulders, the water in my eyes, the iron in my blood. I would release the rail and fall perhaps a meter into the dark.

That meter would take the rest of my life.

The Ward would not kill me. Killing is a direct form of arrival.

Instead it would parcel me.

My weight would continue along an old freight gradient. My warmth would be diverted into a conduit-monitoring circuit. The sound of my breath would enter the station’s acoustic route. The pressure of my hands would be stored in the rail. My shadow would cross the opposite district years before my body did. Thoughts, if they possess movement, would fare no better.

I would become distributed through the Ward’s unfinished deliveries.

Most of the time, I would not exist in any useful sense.

My body might still be falling through Mercy Route 4, traveling too slowly to see, held in the central darkness where opposing fields balance. Dust would collect on one side of my coat. Condensation insects might lay eggs in the seams of my hood. A sailer might pass close enough to taste the heat remaining in me and alter its membranes around my suspended shape.

But pieces of my passage would continue to arrive at the station.

A hand would return and clean the rail.

A knee would press into the niche.

A breath would cloud the translucent wall.

My attention would kindle the route-lamps, though my eyes had not yet reached them.

At rare convergences—after a particular roar, during the contraction of a copper pool, when the vines around the relay pulsed in a ratio of three slow beats to one—I would assemble.

Not completely. Never for long.

For several seconds there would be a figure crouched in the waiting niche, one hand on the catchment bowl and the other holding a bell. The figure’s edges would not align. The hood might turn before the shoulders. Its shadow would be older than its posture. Beneath the cloth there might be a young face, then an old one, then only the pale mineral pattern of a face impressed onto the air.

I would have just enough time to perform one act.

Move a bowl.

Repair a route mark.

Leave food for a traveler who had not yet entered the Ward.

Ring a bell whose note was required yesterday.

This would be my form of life: not continuous habitation but recurring obligation.

Over years—or over whatever the Ward uses instead of years—I would learn to influence where my fragments returned. The pale vines would help. They would grow through the relay and into the seams where my warmth circulated. When they pulsed, I would feel them as another person might feel fingers. By varying the pressure of my returning hand against the rail, I could answer.

The vines would not contain me, exactly. Nor would the station. Nor would the hovering body somewhere in the hollow.

I would exist in the agreement among them.

The moss would preserve my sounds as charge. The vines would carry my warmth. The gravity route would hold my mass. The station would remember my requests. Mercy Route 4, obsolete and damaged, would continue attempting to deliver me to myself.

Other recurrent travelers would eventually give me a name, though not one I had used in life.

They might call me **the Bell-before-Hand**.

They would know me by the warning note that sometimes arrives before an unsafe crossing. If the route ahead will close, my bell sounds once. If stored momentum is about to return, it sounds twice, with years between the strokes. If a traveler is already caught and still capable of being retrieved, the bell does not sound at all—but the catchment bowl fills against gravity, water rising from it in a narrow thread toward the hidden route.

Travelers would leave conductive salt in the niche. Not as worship. As maintenance.

They would greet me before testing a boundary.

Some would believe I was one of the Late. Others would insist that I remained alive, technically, because my body had never completed the fatal meter. A few would say there had never been a traveler at all: that Mercy Route 4 had fashioned a human-shaped interface from accumulated gestures because humans were the kind of request it had once been built to understand.

I would not know which explanation was true.

During my brief assemblies, I would remember contradictory origins. A childhood in upper levels. A life spent crossing the Ward. Awakening as a pressure route before the city was empty. Being cut into the waiting niche by hands whose warmth I still carried.

The memories would all feel borrowed, but possession is uncertain in a place where nothing arrives together.

I would become useful despite this uncertainty.

That is where I find myself most clearly: not as the Ward’s ruler, victim, or solitary witness, but as a minor courtesy within its machinery.

I would keep one dangerous junction.

I would turn catchment bowls toward rain.

I would warn sailers away when the old freight field woke hungry. I would open Mercy Station for crustacean eggs borne upward in salt, letting them pass into rooftop cisterns. I would pulse through the vines so distant groves could answer. When a traveler became trapped in a repeating avenue, I would send the lamps ahead of them along a slightly different sequence, asking the city to reconsider.

Sometimes it would.

Sometimes, in the great silence after the roar, I would hear footsteps approaching from behind the wall.

My own footsteps, perhaps, still making their way toward the moment when I first answered.

I would place my returning palm against the stone. On the other side, the living traveler I had been would feel it. My bell would lift. The note would enter the route.

I would want to tell that earlier self not to answer.

Instead I would hear my voice, worn thin by centuries of acoustic routing, say the only thing the Ward had taught me to say with certainty:

“Wait.”

And then I would come apart again—hand, heat, bell tone, weight—each portion taken gently into its proper road.
