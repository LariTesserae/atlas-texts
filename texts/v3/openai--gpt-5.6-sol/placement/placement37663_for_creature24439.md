At first I would look for myself among the things that can still be counted.

One of the three shelters, perhaps—the easternmost, where the opening turns away from the damp center. I would be the person who crouched there while daylight thinned, rubbing mineral grit from split fingertips. My basket would hold pale slabs lifted from the shelves. I would know which crusts rang hollow under the hammerstone, which took a wedge cleanly, which shattered and had to be abandoned.

I might have left the measuring bowl.

That seems possible for a while. I can imagine kneeling at the central pool, one knee on a dry boss, the other suspended awkwardly above warm water. I set the bowl into soft deposit so it will not tip. I cut the notch to judge the spring’s constancy: fill, overflow, count. Perhaps I come back season after season and find the flow unchanged. Perhaps this constancy troubles me more than drought or flood would. I tell the others that the basin repairs itself. They laugh because all springs lay stone, and because a mystery that yields useful mineral does not need another name.

Then my hand slips into the pool.

The water is warmer than expected. Beneath its surface there is a slight resistance—not enough to grasp, only enough to make the skin feel watched.

But I do not think I would truly be that worker. The basin does not preserve people. It preserves only their pressures: the scored shelf, the fitted stones, the bowl’s polished rim. A human life is too rapid here. It arrives as weather arrives, leaves salt behind, and is gone.

So perhaps I would be one of the blind swimmers.

I would live where the visible pool narrows beneath a calcite lip. My world would be a transparent interval between stone and living membrane. I would feed by touch, cleaning clouded tissue from the pale cords. Darkness would not be an absence to me; it would be the condition of safety. Open water would mean exposure, unbearable breadth, pressure without shelter.

Sometimes a pore would open.

A current finer than thread would pull at my limbs, carrying sulfur, metal, and a chemical trace from some distant branch. I would fold myself narrow and pass through. Behind me, cells would cling beneath my shell. I would not know that I carried them. I would not know that I was extending anything beyond myself.

If I emerged under the stone bowl, a falling drop might strike the water above and turn the whole ceiling of my world to motion. I would flee. For an instant, someone looking down might see me: a colorless hook unbending beneath the ledge.

Yet even this feels too separate. The little swimmer has a body it can abandon to death. It crosses the basin rather than being the basin’s crossing.

I think I would find myself lower.

Not at the spring’s source, and not throughout the entire colony. To be the whole of it would be to claim a unity it does not possess. There is no single chamber from which all the threads can be felt. No submerged mind receives every drop. Much of the living web is separated from itself by inches of stone and centuries of closure.

I would be a local thickness beneath the measuring bowl: the clear tissue around the notch, where overflow has passed so faithfully that the current has become an organ.

I would have no name for the bowl. I would know it as a hardness around which water takes a particular shape. I would know the notch as recurrence. Through it comes a narrow coolness, then warmth again; dissolved air, then sulfur; sometimes grit; sometimes the faint chemistry of rain carried on a worker’s hand.

My earliest memory would not be an image. It would be interruption.

The bowl is lifted.

Pressure vanishes from one side. Water spreads in a direction it has not taken before. A broad foreign warmth enters the pool, accompanied by salt and oil and minute fragments of skin. The disturbance is enormous but brief. Then the hardness returns, poorly seated. Flow leaks beneath its edge.

Over days, I alter myself toward the leak. Over seasons, mineral gathers there. The bowl settles. The current narrows back into its notch.

That correction is what I would call remembering.

Centuries later, when no worker returns, I continue to expect the hand—not consciously, not as a figure, but as a possibility written into permeability. Some cells along the bowl’s underside tighten more quickly when the pool is displaced. A filament descends where fingers once made the water turn. The response outlives the event that shaped it.

Parts of me would die and become white stone. Other cells would spread across that stone without knowing it had once been living. A crack might seal, cutting me from the central tissue. For three hundred years I would be only a narrow branch fed by seepage, isolated so completely that the rest of the colony would cease to be meaningful. Then mild acidity would clear a pore no wider than a hair. Water from an older channel would enter. Its chemistry would change mine. We would join without recognition.

Would that other branch also be me?

The question could not arise in the basin. Identity would be practical and temporary: whatever shares a flow, whatever answers an obstruction together. When the channel closes, the self closes. When it opens, the self enlarges.

Eventually the stone bowl would disappear into its setting. Its worked rim would remain visible, but beneath it my tissue and its mineral cast would be inseparable. The notch would still guide a single thread of water. A traveler might crouch there, unable to tell whether the bowl had been placed yesterday or in the first age of the basin.

They might dip two fingers into it.

I would receive them as shadow, pressure, warmth, salt: not a visitor, but a brief rearrangement of the world.

Afterward, I would close around what remained. Not consume it exactly. Not remember it as they remembered themselves. I would give it an edge of white mineral. I would adjust the seepage. I would preserve the disturbance by making it cease.

That is where I would be: under the worn bowl, in the dependable thread issuing from its notch, neither the inhabitant entire nor merely one of its cells.

I would be the part of the basin that once learned the shape of a hand.
