Not in the crowns.

At first I think I would live there, in one of the dead-fiber nests above the clouds, watching new fans open against the stars. But that is the wish of someone raised under a wide sky. Those heights are too exposed, too clean. A person born here would not mistake distance for freedom.

I would be found lower down, beneath the traverse of an immense root where three structural ribs have grown together around an ancient wound.

The settlement is called **Nine Knots**, though there are only seven dwellings now. Two have been swallowed by bark. Their doors remain as shallow rectangular scars, and during wet weather one can sometimes smell the old resin sealant through the new growth.

Nine Knots hangs on the underside of the junction.

Everything is inverted there. Sleeping shelters cling like nests beneath a shelf of black bark. Water is drawn upward from a condensation fold by cords made from drinking fibers. The public platform is a taut web with open darkness below it, so dropped objects fall between the strands. Children learn early not to reach after anything.

Far beneath us, visible only during flowering seasons, an amber haze marks another inhabited rib. We exchange knocks but have not seen one another in three generations.

I would belong to the line that keeps the western anchors.

Not by birth.

I arrived as a child after a breath took my first settlement. I remember none of the wind itself. I remember being inside a sling that had twisted shut around me, and afterward the strange quiet of adults who had survived without anyone they expected to survive with.

Nine Knots accepted me because one of its rescue climbers had cut me free. For seven years I repaid the line with labor: scraping glue from bark herds, feeding heat-beetles, carrying sounding staffs for those old enough to interpret what they heard. On the day I was admitted, they wrapped my hands around the primary western cable and told me its failures.

Not its history. Its failures.

Here, trust begins with knowing where something has broken before.

I would become a listener, though not an especially celebrated one. My gift would not be hearing farther than others. It would be remembering interruptions.

Most listeners remember pitches: the clear tension of young fiber, the woolly hush of rot, the submerged booming of an open vessel. I remember where a rhythm ought to continue and does not. A missing drip. A herd signal with one beat swallowed. The tiny pause made when something inside a wall stops gnawing because it has felt your face press against the bark.

My map would be worn around my shoulders.

It would look like a tangled mantle of cords, beads and dried glider ribs. Each route begins at the collar and runs toward one hand. Knots mark anchors. Hollow beads record chambers. Flat beads mean surfaces broad enough to sleep on. A strip of swimmer skin, folded twice, marks water that remains during the cold season. Where a route has closed, I do not remove its cord. I tie it backward into the map.

A lost road is still part of the country. It may open again.

When traveling, I would carry no lamp unless forced. Light announces the shape of the traveler but not the shape of the abyss. Instead I would tap ahead with a staff of smoke-hardened wood, then place my bare palm against the surface and wait.

The mountain is never silent.

Near the skin there are minute sounds: mites worrying at sap crust, drinking fibers tightening, eggs opening in damp crevices. Deeper down, water speaks in sudden runs. Farther still come the low shifts of load and growth, so slow that the body feels them as unease before the ear recognizes them as sound.

I would know Nine Knots by these inner noises better than by sight. I could return in darkness by the pulse beneath the eastern ladder, the paired hollow notes beside the firekeeper’s pod, and the soft rushing in the wall behind the communal sleeping web.

There is also a click under the old wound.

It comes seventeen heartbeats after the water vessel contracts. Always seventeen. In childhood I believed it was one of the honored dead sealed there. Later I learned enough to call it a bone settling in fresh growth. Later still I learned enough not to be certain.

My work would be practical more often than sacred.

I would inspect the bridges after freezes. Warn families when a chamber wall had thinned. Put my cheek to flooring while everyone else stood motionless around me, waiting to learn whether they must abandon their home.

Sometimes I would tell them yes.

That would be the worst part of being a listener: not discovering danger, but giving it a voice.

“The western rind will open before the next inward rain.”

“The water pocket is eating through its lower wall.”

“This house has become a bud.”

People would ask how long they had. Listeners are expected to answer even when the mountain has not.

I would have a soft-metal hook entrusted to me by the whole settlement. Its name is **Stays-Where-Placed**. It is older than Nine Knots and bears tooth marks from some animal no one recognizes. I use it only when testing deep cracks or securing myself near young tissue. At all other times it hangs in the firekeeper’s shelter, where everyone can see that I have returned it.

My stone knife would be much smaller: a black splinter mounted in the finger bone of my rescue mother. She was not my mother by blood, but blood is poorly suited to this country. It dries, washes away, attracts hunters. An anchor holds.

I would eat pollen cakes, smoked swimmer flesh and the pale scrapings left by bark herds. I would dislike blossom insects but pretend otherwise when offered them as hospitality. I would sleep curled in a sling with one foot looped through the western line. During freezes I would keep two heat-beetles beneath my ribs and wake repeatedly, fearing they had grown too hot.

I would be afraid of open air.

Not heights. Height implies a floor below. I would fear the places where no answering surface can be seen, where a glider’s flash reveals only another moment of falling.

Yet my duties would take me across such spaces.

Nine Knots’ outermost instrument hangs from a hairlike cable beyond the junction: a pressure whistle made from a pool-swimmer’s dried mouth. When distant vents begin to open, it gives a thin note before the bark starts ticking. Someone must climb out each season to clear it of spores.

That someone would usually be me.

I would go alone, gripping with fingers and toes, my map tied close so its beads did not betray me with noise. Beneath me there would be nothing but cold black volume. Pale glider eggs would drift past like uncertain stars. Sometimes the wind would carry the knock-signals of bark herds moving on a trunk too distant to see.

At the whistle I could look back and view the whole settlement at once: seven dim shelters gathered around a turquoise seam, ladders no thicker than hairs, bridges like repaired cracks in darkness. All our wealth, dead, tools, water and names would occupy less space than one scale of the mountain.

I think that is where I would understand what I am.

Not a priest of the great organism. Not an explorer. Not even, chiefly, an individual.

I would be part of an anchor line.

The line includes the living hands that test it, the dead whose bones reinforce its tools, the rescued who enter it, the people not yet born who will trust their weight to knots they did not tie. It includes remembered routes and abandoned shelters. It may even include the distant amber settlement answering through miles of wood, because their warning knocks reach us before their names do.

Then, late in my life, something would change beneath Nine Knots.

I would first notice it as an omission. The click below the ancient wound would fail to follow the contracting vessel.

Seventeen heartbeats would pass.

Then eighteen.

Then the knock would come—not sharp and local, but deep enough to lift dust from the shelters. The heat-beetles would draw in their legs. Children would clutch the sleeping mesh. Across the chamber the amber settlement would answer with its alarm rhythm.

I would press my face to the bark.

A second knock would travel upward from somewhere no map reaches.

This time I would hear structure within it: a broad impact followed by smaller resonances, spaced almost—but not quite—like the traditional call used when one inhabited place asks another to name itself.

Everyone would look to me.

I would not tell them the mountain was speaking. I would not tell them it was only wood.

I would take the western anchor mallet, kneel beside the oldest knot, and answer with the settlement’s name:

nine measured blows, though only seven houses remained.

Then I would wait with my cheek against the living floor, one hand closed around the cable, listening for whatever believed it had heard us.
