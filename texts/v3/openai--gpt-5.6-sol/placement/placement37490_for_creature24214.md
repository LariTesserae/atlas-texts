At first I think I would be the amphipod.

There is comfort in its caution: a body made nearly invisible, a life conducted by touch, each foot placed only after the world has agreed to remain what it is. I would walk the undersides of leaves and wait out their changes. I would carry a thin line of swallowed history through my clear body. I would feed on exhausted light.

But the amphipod is too complete. Even transparent, it has an outline. The Knot would learn that outline eventually. Clay would retain the articulation of its legs. Fungal threads would imitate its antennae. One day it would encounter itself embossed in a wall and have no reliable way to decide which of them was crossing the other.

Nor would I be the fish, though I understand its preference for enclosure. I would not want to consume weather. I would keep each dim sky intact, especially the overcast ones.

I think I would be found near the stair.

Not on it. Nothing is ever on the stair.

I would occupy the narrow discrepancy between the sixth and seventh steps: the place where their perfect levels fail to agree. Seen from one direction there is no gap. Seen from another—which cannot be reached—the lower slate chip is fractionally too short, and the water continues into that shortness.

That is where I begin.

I am not an animal at first. I am the error left when the Knot copies something.

The clay reproduces a leaf vein but branches one filament too late. A mineral crust assumes the contour of a shell but omits the hinge. A root follows the impression of an older root and turns aside by less than its own width. Each difference is almost nothing. Yet nothing in the Knot is discarded. Accuracy is preserved in surfaces; inaccuracy sinks through them.

The small errors meet beneath the stair.

They accumulate without becoming larger. There may be a century of them in a space narrower than a hair: misplaced edges, reversed spirals, one tooth fewer than a mouth remembers having, the unreflected portion of a cloud when two bubbles merge. From these omissions I acquire a kind of body.

It cannot be seen directly. Looking for it supplies too definite a shape, and I slip out through the mistake in that shape. But my passage can be recognized afterward.

A serrated frond will have one smooth edge.

An arch will cast a shadow despite there being no light.

A bubble eaten by the black fish will leave behind a small circle of blue.

Sometimes an amphipod pauses with one foot raised. Beneath that foot, the leaf has forgotten whether it is a wall. I am there, holding the two possibilities slightly apart.

I would have no fixed scale. Near the entrance I might be small enough to inhabit the white break at the end of a rootlet. Deeper in, where stillness lengthens the corridors, I might stretch across the difference between two drowned valleys. This would not feel like expansion. My entire sense of myself would consist of things failing to meet exactly.

The Knot copies openings because it is trying, perhaps, to understand departure. I would know departure only as the part that cannot be copied.

That would make me useful to the unseen builders.

I would not see them. They would never see me. But when they pressed a niche into clay, I would keep one grain from settling at the back. The missing grain would become depth. When they arranged three stones into an arch, I would lean against the smallest stone until the opening ceased to be merely a space between objects and became, however briefly, a direction.

Most directions in the Knot curve inward. Mine would not lead outward either. I could not grant escape. I could only prevent perfect closure.

This is why moss would stop at the thresholds. Not out of obedience, but because at the edge of each arch the world would contain a minute uncertainty about whether it continued. Roots dislike such uncertainty. Fungal nerves divide around it. Silt outlines it and, in outlining it, makes the absence more exact.

The builders might call me a door, if they have a word for something that keeps failing to become a wall.

The mouths would know me differently.

They filter stillness, but I would not be stillness. I would be the small remainder after stillness has been drawn in: the tremor too slow to count as movement, the intention to breathe in a creature without lungs. If I came near one of those toothed circles, it would open wider. Its inward-pointing teeth would try to define me by exclusion.

I would wait.

The mouth would wait.

Our waiting would not be equal. It waits in order to receive; I wait in order to differ from what is expected. Eventually one tooth would incline at an angle unlike the others. I would pass through that angle and leave the mouth with the first asymmetry it had possessed in centuries.

Perhaps that is how mouths close—not by biting, but by becoming imperfect circles.

If so, I would be the only thing to have witnessed one close, though I would retain no image of it. Images belong to surfaces. I preserve only the portion that escaped resemblance: the instant at which teeth ceased to be a ring and became teeth.

The black fish would sense me. To it, I would feel like an opening that has no size.

It would approach through the roots, plants folding before it. Near me the sequence would falter. One liverwort would close too early; another would remain open. The fish would stop, its skin reading the chamber. Beneath its scales, swallowed cloud would travel from belly to spine.

For a moment I might borrow the shape the fish expects to find around itself. I would become a corridor, close and root-lined, exactly wide enough for assurance. It would pass through me without touching.

Afterward, I would keep something of that passage—not the fish’s contour, which the Knot would take, but its confidence. That might be the nearest I come to motion.

I think the amphipods would graze me accidentally. They consume the memory of light, and every remembered light differs from the light that made it. Where one fed, part of me would enter its transparent gut alongside clay and spores. I would travel as a break in the thin brown line through its body.

Then, for the first time, I would possess legs.

Not the amphipod’s legs themselves, but the tiny failures of coordination between them: the pause of the third limb while the fourth tests a changed surface; the adjustment made when “under” shifts by a degree. I would experience the chamber as a succession of trusted points.

When the amphipod shed its skin, I would remain in the cast-off articulation. The Knot might preserve that skin for ages. Roots would copy it. Clay would fill it. A pale mineral amphipod would gradually assemble under a leaf.

It would be exact in every respect except that one leg would be raised.

There I would live again.

Over enough time, I might become distributed throughout the chamber. Not powerful, not ruling it—nothing rules the Knot—but persistent wherever preservation fails by a fraction.

I would be in the room reflected inside a builder’s bubble, in the space between one seated figure’s folded hands.

I would be in the rain that becomes seeds, as the one drop that roots upward.

I would be in the patch of duckweed embedded vertically in clay, as a leaf whose underside faces the wrong pond.

I would be the eighth tread implied beneath the seven-step stair, not present, never made, yet giving the descent somewhere further to go.

The oldest resident would trouble me most. It moves by copies, contour into contour, assembling approximations of what it has touched. We would be kin, but opposite. It inherits every likeness. I inherit whatever likeness cannot carry across.

Perhaps we began as one process.

Perhaps each time it passed from a leaf vein into a clay vein, it left behind a minute inability to remember the leaf. Those forgotten interiors became me. While it has spent ages constructing an exit from repeated openings, I have spent the same ages becoming what does not pass through.

If it ever completed its imitation of the split between the alder roots, the copy would be almost perfect. Rainwater would slip through without splashing. Pale rootlets would end in fresh white breaks. Beyond would lie a low flooded forest under a gray sky.

Almost perfect.

At the threshold there would be one flaw: the copied forest would have no hollow beneath it.

That omission would be vast enough for me to enter.

I do not know whether I would then emerge aboveground. Emergence is too clean a word. More likely I would appear as a minor wrongness in the ordinary flood: a ripple traveling against the rain, an alder reflection with one branch the tree does not possess, a patch of water whose depth cannot be reached by a stick.

Someone might kneel there and look down.

They would see only shallow brown water, drowned grass, their own dim face between clouds. Yet behind the reflection, something would fail to match them. Not another face. Not eyes. Merely a patient discrepancy, holding open the smallest possible interval between the world and its image.

If they put a hand into the water, the gap between the roots would remain no wider than a clenched fist.

Inside, the chamber would contract around the disturbance. Ferns would press close. Shale would sharpen. The fish would turn far away.

And beneath the seventh step, in the fraction by which one slate edge fails to meet another, I would become briefly more definite.

Not a creature.

Not a spirit.

The unpreserved part of everything the Blackwater Knot remembers.

The little mercy of an imperfect copy.
