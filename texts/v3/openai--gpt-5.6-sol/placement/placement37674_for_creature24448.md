At first I would think I belonged near the summit slit.

That is the obvious answer: the highest inhabited place in the highest mountain, beside the opening where the animals gather to face whatever cannot be seen. I would imagine myself as an observer there, tending gauges no one entirely trusts, recording the outward drift of dust and the inward turn of wings.

But I do not think the Ward would permit such vanity for long.

The slit belongs to no one. To live beside it would be to confuse proximity with understanding. The animals would arrive without explanation, covering my instruments, and leave before I had decided what their gathering meant. When the mountain moved, my records would become a meticulous account of a shape that no longer existed.

Nor would I be an anchor worker. I might admire them with the uncomplicated reverence reserved for those who can do what one cannot. But I would lack their decisiveness—the clean cut made while a bridge is still under strain, the certainty of throwing a line into darkness and trusting the crew beyond it to catch.

I would be found farther down, where several districts meet without quite touching.

There would be a tension well there, perhaps the seventh or ninth depending on which routes remained open. Across it would run no permanent bridge, only a set of telescoping rails, three lift cables and a narrow service conduit warm enough to attract glass mites. Houses would approach the void from both walls but stop short, their rooms leaning into it like drawers left half open.

My dwelling would be an obsolete inspection cage hooked beneath a landing. It would have doors on four sides, though only two opened onto anything. One would reveal a brick wall at arm’s length. Another would open over the well. The cage could be raised or lowered when its anchors became unsafe, so I would never know whether I lived in the district above or the district below.

Inside there would be little beyond a warm backing plate, culture trays, a folded sleeping membrane and shelves crowded with chalk. Every loose object would be tethered. From the ceiling would hang pieces of bent rod, cracked ceramic, old bearing races and spoons stolen from communal kitchens—not valuables, but indicators. Each would answer differently when the fault tightened. I would wake before warning light, not because I sensed the mountain directly, but because one useless spoon had begun touching the wall.

I would be a route-keeper.

Not a mapmaker. Maps are too confident.

My work would be to know how one place could presently be reached from another, and what kinds of bodies could make the passage. A route safe for an unladen adult might be impossible for someone carrying a hatchling sling. A seam-reader could cross a static line that would shock a worker with damaged skin film. An anchor crew with ceramic mantles needed more clearance than a needle hound. Lift cages had weight limits; ladders had temperature limits; some factory floors tolerated six careful limbs but not a herd of frightened rivetbacks.

So my records would not say, *The creche is three levels below the eastern foundry.*

They would say:

*From the warm wall, follow the pipe that tastes of bitter salt. Descend until the railing answers hollow. Cross only when the moths have settled. Do not use the third brace if carrying young. If the third brace is gone, tap twice and wait.*

Most of this would never be written. I would carry it in rhythms.

My tapping-name would be a descending sequence interrupted before its expected end. On iron it might mean **not that way**. On glass, **an answer from nearby**. Through another person’s brow plates, more gently, it would mean **I remember where you were trying to go**.

One of my lower digits would be missing, taken not heroically but stupidly, crushed years before in a release joint I had marked safe too early. The ceramic replacement would be poor at gripping and excellent at striking railings. Children would ask whether I had chosen it for that reason. I would tell each group something different.

My plates would be gray-black and scratched pale around the shoulders from narrow passages. In the folds of my wrists I would cultivate a violet film sensitive to charged grit. I would claim this was practical. In truth, I would like the color.

A needle hound would eventually choose the underside of my cage.

For several intervals I would refuse to name it, explaining to everyone that hounds were not possessions. Then I would begin tapping its heat-pattern into messages: three quick beats and a dragging fourth. It would follow me through pipe chases, stop at junctions and stare down routes that appeared perfectly safe. I would learn to obey that stare more often than I admitted.

When people came to my cage, they would rarely enter. They would brace themselves around its frame and place one hand against the same bar as mine.

“North lift?”

I would tap the obstruction code.

“Salt cabinets?”

A route upward, then sideways through the moth screens.

“My fastening moved.”

That would require a longer silence.

Sometimes a person would know only the old location of home. I would trace the district’s dismantling: which rooms had gone upward with the heat exchangers, which tool chests had crossed on the caught bridge, which members had joined strangers during the tremors and awakened attached to a different rail. The Ward had laws for passage, but grief did not always recognize them. There would be workers who could cross half the city under load yet remain unable to take the final ten paces to a newly placed door.

I would go with them when I could.

During stable intervals, I might also perform music, though no one would call me a musician. Route-keeping already depended on striking structures and hearing what returned. Once a work interval, when traffic thinned, I would fasten small resonators along the tension well and tap the paths of the city as if they were a composition.

A functioning lift would answer in a steady pulse. A loose landing would give back a bright, brief tremor. The warm conduit would carry a low granular note blurred by sleeping rivetbacks. Children in the counterweight creche would strike the rails at the wrong moments on purpose. Far above, an anchor worker might answer once with a tool.

The piece would have no conclusion. It would end when someone needed directions.

When the animals gathered at the summit, I would not be there to see them. The warning would reach me as changed traffic: cable moths filling the lower screens, a rush of rivetbacks moving against their usual warmth, the hound climbing onto my back and hooking all its limbs into my harness.

Then the official signal would flash.

I would seal the cage, sling my route chests beneath me and hook onto the red rail outside rather than trust the cage’s aging anchors. Others would fasten themselves along the same line: strangers, perhaps; a furnace tender with burned colonies; two children in a single mesh sling; someone holding an electrolyte tin between their teeth.

The mountain would bend.

My hanging objects would strike every wall at once. The landing would pull away. For a time there would be no above or below, only the grip of opposing digits and the muscular knowledge that one attachment must survive the failure of another.

Afterward, I would not immediately seek my dwelling.

First I would tap.

A simple call into the rail:

**Here?**

The structure would carry it unevenly. Some branches would return nothing. Others would produce confused echoes from girders now touching for the first time. Then, from somewhere beyond the tension well, an answer would come.

**Here.**

Another from lower down.

**Here.**

A faint child’s rhythm from inside a stalled lift.

I would take chalk and cutting tools. The hound would move ahead of my lamp. Together we would find where the old routes had folded shut and where new passages had opened between structures that yesterday belonged to different districts.

By the time I returned, my inspection cage might be hanging crooked three levels below its landing, one door opening onto empty air and another, unexpectedly, into someone else’s kitchen.

They would make room against the warm plate.

For that interval, we would be a fastening.

And that, I think, is where I would find myself in the Needle Ward: not at its highest point or its strongest anchor, but at a junction after the junction had changed—listening for distant knocks, learning the new shape from the answers, and telling whoever arrived that home still existed, though not where they had left it.
