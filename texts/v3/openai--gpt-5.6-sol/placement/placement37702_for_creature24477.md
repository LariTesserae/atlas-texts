I would think, at first, that I belonged in the air.

The first sight of those clear-winged fliers rising from the ravines would make flight seem like the only life large enough for the country. To climb on warmth between walls miles apart; to look down on waterfalls becoming rain before they reached the rivers; to spend one brief season as a moving pane of light—this would feel like an answer.

But I do not think the High Green would make me into anything so brilliant.

Nor would I live in the open center, where the grazing companies pass through silver vegetation and cloud shadows travel visibly over the land. I would be drawn toward the margin, though not to the very brink. Somewhere a little back from it, where a black rib of the old mountain breaks through the mat, there would be a settlement of four or five roofs. Beyond it, the plateau would narrow into a long tongue. At the tongue’s end, a living bridge would cross to an isolated terrace on the opposite wall.

That is where I would find myself: beside the crossing.

The bridge would not be one immense cable but a congregation of them—perhaps thirty principal runners and hundreds of lesser roots, braided through one another over centuries. Some would be dead at the center and still carry weight. Others would be young, green, and tensile. Leaf fans would grow along the upper windward side; beneath them, condensation would travel in shining threads toward swollen reservoirs near either anchorage. Fungi would silver the oldest bark. Turnclaws would sometimes use the lower roots at dawn, passing upside down beneath human travelers.

The span would have a name that translated imperfectly as **She Who Tightens in the South Wind**.

In calm weather she would sag and roll unpleasantly. When the ravine began to breathe upward, she would lift into tension and become steady. A stranger would wait for stillness before crossing. Those who knew her would wait for wind.

I would be her graft-keeper.

Not the first, and not the last. My full name would contain hers, as well as the cold cistern under the western anchorage and the pale-post road by which I first arrived as a child. People elsewhere might shorten it to something manageable, but among my own household my identity would be less a personal possession than a set of obligations:

the one who hears the southern roots,  
the one who seals the white cistern,  
the one who carries warning across the gap.

I imagine broad, scarred feet and a light body made wiry by climbing. My hands would have dark clay permanently worked into their creases. I would carry no metal. At my belt would hang a stone pruning blade, wedges of shell, binding fiber, small packets of grafting paste, wax earplugs, and several lengths of cord dyed in patterns visible through cloud.

Most of the work would look like waiting.

I would sit at the anchorage with one palm on the bridge and learn the ordinary traffic moving through it: colony-makers inside a hollow runner, water descending from the leaf fans, turnclaws on the lower cables, wind drawing along the bark, distant footfalls from the opposite terrace. Each vibration would overlap the others, yet over years they would become distinct. A healthy root would answer a tap with a long, rising tension. A dry one would return a papery knock. Hidden decay would feel soft and strangely local, as if the bridge had swallowed the sound.

There would be days when I crossed only halfway.

I would lie flat where the span hung over the deepest air, my weight spread across three runners, and lower myself by a waist sling to tend the underside. Beneath me there would be no useful sense of ground—only warm cloud passing through the ravine and, in brief clearings, a river so far below that its motion could not be seen.

That would be the place where fear ceased to be an event and became a tool.

I would not conquer it. I would notice its changes. Fear sharpened by wind would mean the span was moving beyond its accustomed rhythm. Fear arriving without movement might mean a cable had parted somewhere out of sight. Sudden calm in my own body, more dangerous still, might mean exhaustion. I would be trained never to confuse courage with numbness.

The bridge would have injuries older than anyone’s memory. One section might be twisted around a polished stone ring from the roofless people. Another would contain a darkened mass where generations had grafted incompatible runners and then spent a century correcting the mistake. There might be a hollow where lightning had entered in some forgotten wet season. Children would be warned not to put their hands inside, though thumb-crabs lived there and blue flowers opened around its rim.

I would know the crossing not as a single organism, exactly, but as a difficult agreement among many lives.

The roots would compete for water. Fungi would protect one cable while hollowing another. Colony-makers would mend small wounds and enlarge hidden chambers. Birds would fertilize the leaves, then tear them during nesting. The cliff plants at either end would not naturally recognize each other; they would remain joined because graft-keepers had spent generations persuading their tissues to exchange sap.

My work would not be to preserve the bridge unchanged. Nothing unchanged could live there.

I would decide which growth to cut so the rest could bear its own abundance. I would carry young shoots across and bind them into aging runners. I would open channels before the rains, then close them during dry weeks. When one root became too dominant, I would wound it carefully—not enough to kill it, only enough that weaker members of the span received light and water.

This would make me respected and occasionally disliked. A graft-keeper’s decisions take years to prove themselves. There would always be someone who remembered that a pruned cable had once carried their grandmother safely, or that a new runner irritated bare feet, or that the bridge had swayed less before I altered its leaf load.

I would make mistakes whose consequences outlived arguments.

Once, perhaps while young, I would bind a vigorous silver-barked runner into the central braid because it healed quickly and held tension beautifully. For seven years it would seem inspired. In the eighth, its roots would begin poisoning the neighboring tissue. We would spend another twelve cutting it out in sections so the removal did not unbalance the span.

The last fragment would remain somewhere beneath the bridge, unreachable and still alive.

That failure would become part of my name.

Not as disgrace. As route-knowledge.

In the loud season, travelers would arrive holding one another by cords. Their speech would vanish under the metallic insect chorus, so I would meet them with touch: two fingers to the wrist for wait, a palm pressed downward for spread your weight, a tug toward the elbow for wind coming. Before allowing them onto the crossing, I would look at their sandals, loads, fear, and manner of standing. Heavy bundles would be divided. Children would be tied between adults. Anyone who stamped the bridge to “test” it would be made to sit until they understood that the bridge had already tested them.

At times a browser herd would gather near the western anchorage, following a taste-road to the cistern. I would know a few of the old animals by the scars on their sensory crests. They would not be tame, but they might tolerate me walking beside them through cloud. When calves rode in the folds of their carriers’ backs, they would lift their narrow feet and taste the air toward me.

I would trade water access for nothing. Water could not be owned in that way. But travelers would leave fungal cakes, fired-clay seals, insect oil, news of damaged route-posts, and graft cuttings wrapped in wet fiber. The cuttings would matter most. A bridge survives by receiving life from elsewhere.

My dwelling would be built against the stone rib nearest the anchorage. I would sleep in a suspended mat with one line tied—not tightly—to a load-bearing root. Through it I would feel weather before waking. The ordinary creak and pull would enter my dreams. If the line went slack, I would wake at once.

There would be no silence.

Rain on the roof. Colonies ticking in the walls. Root fibers contracting at dusk. Wind sounding in shafts beneath the outcrop. The low calls of grazing birds around the warm vents. Beneath all of it, the bridge transmitting arrivals long before anyone appeared.

Then one night I would wake because the insects had stopped.

Not everywhere. Only in a widening patch near the house.

I would not take a spear. A spear would be nearly useless against a plate-backed hunter beneath the mat. I would wake the household by touch, lift the children onto stone, and set shallow bowls of water along the edge of the outcrop. In their surfaces we would watch the vibrations approach: first faint rings, then stronger ones, then stillness.

The predator might remain below us until dawn.

Nothing heroic would happen. We would wait. We would not mistake survival for victory. After it passed, we would mark the place and reroute travelers until the chorus returned.

That, I think, would be my actual place in the High Green—not the sky, not the hunt, not the unexplored galleries in the mountain’s depths. I would live at the joining of things that naturally tend to separate: stone and vegetation, one plateau and another, fear and passage, inheritance and alteration.

In age, I would cross less often. An apprentice would begin hearing faults before I did. At first I would correct them too quickly. Later I would learn to let them finish speaking. They would inherit my tools one by one, though the pruning blade would need a new handle and the shell wedges would retain the shape of my grip.

Eventually, people would cease using my childhood name. I would simply become the old keeper of She Who Tightens.

When I died, they would not carry me far.

They would wrap me in a fruiting runner grown from both sides of the ravine and suspend me beneath the western third of the bridge, where several young roots needed weight to encourage their downward curve. For a season, perhaps two, my bundle would hang above the clouds. Turnclaws might investigate it. Copper pollinators might sleep in the flowers of the wrapping vine.

Then the roots would enter.

The bridge would take the salts, the water, the small stored substance of a human life. The wrappings would tighten as I diminished. New bark would grow over cord and bone. Eventually there would be no bundle, only a thickening in the span—one more dark place where old material remained inside living structure.

Children crossing generations later would put their bare feet on that rise without knowing precisely what it had been.

Perhaps their keeper would tell them: there was once a person here who introduced the poisonous silver runner; who opened the west cistern too early in a dry year; who kept the crossing alive through three terrace falls; who could distinguish a turnclaw from a child by touch alone; who listened well, though not always soon enough.

Or perhaps nothing would be said.

The bridge would lift in the southern wind. The travelers would spread their weight and cross. Far below, cloud would fill the ravine. Far above, translucent fliers would turn in the sun.

And I would be where I had spent my life learning to belong:

not on either side,  
not falling,  
not still—

but carried forward in the living difficulty between them.
