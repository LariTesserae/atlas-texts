I would first mistake myself for something in the open water.

Not because I belonged there, but because the emptiness would offer the easiest shape for a self: a small body suspended between immensities, able to point in one direction and call it choice. I would feel the cold pass through me. Above, a leaf would make a dim continent of the light. Far below, a trunk would stand in the blue-black distance, too large to seem alive. I would swim toward one and tell myself I had decided to.

Perhaps, for a while, I would be a silver needle.

I would hold myself at the intersection of forces I could not see. My bright fins would tremble. Kilometers away, others would incline as I inclined, each of us solitary enough to imagine solitude. I would follow the long deformation of an electrical field around a pumping organ, make my circuit through a sweetness of plankton, and return to my former place.

The route would feel immense from within. There would be cold regions, pressure gradients, a distant ringmouth’s lamps opening one by one in a canyon. There would be pursuit. There would be the sudden dark of being swallowed, or the release of eggs into a narrow warmth between roots.

But I do not think I would be a needle. A needle has no reason to wonder why its freedom brings it home.

Deeper, I might find myself clinging to one of the vertical spores.

That life would be almost comprehensible: one green world beneath my hooks, one above, one below. I would know distance with exquisite precision and have no means to cross it. I would graze the hemisphere appointed to me while microbes restored the opposite side. The death beneath me would become the place of my child. All existence would be a ladder whose rungs remained fixed because every descent exactly replaced an absence.

There would be peace in such narrowness. But the creature on the spore does not need a horizon. I think I would.

So I would go lower—not by swimming, perhaps, but by discovering that my life had always extended there.

The open water would thicken. Taste would become direction. Iron would lie to my left like a mountain range. Beneath me would be a vast province of bitterness, and beyond it a cool sweetness narrowing toward a root. My nerves might continue outside my visible body as an arrangement of ions in the sea. A disturbance hundreds of meters away would enter me as touch. I would become uncertain where my skin ended.

That uncertainty would be closer.

Still, I would not find myself fully in the transparent sap, nor in the salt ribbons, nor spread thin inside the high-pressure pockets. I would be drawn toward the sound.

It would begin as pressure in my organs: the ocean’s bass labor, too continuous to hear as a separate thing. Then, approaching one of the ancient trunks, I would distinguish tones within it. The outer ribs would sing through the water. Different grooves would carry different harmonics. I would pass into a canyon of matte-black bark, cross a curtain of cilia, and enter through a vascular opening large enough to contain a night.

Inside, the trunk would be hollow only in the way a throat is hollow.

Current would move through galleries ribbed with conductive wood. Light would come from no visible source, but mineral flecks in the walls would answer the passing charge with dim violet and green. The chord would not merely surround me. It would arrange my tissues. Some frequencies would collect in my cavities; others would pass through. My limbs would seek, without instruction, the places where the wood was almost still.

There I would find the body.

Tall, radial, pale from the absence of ordinary light. Six grasping limbs fixed around a resonance node. A crown of vanes folding and opening in the pressure waves. No eyes, because nothing in the gallery would need to be looked at from a distance. Perception would arrive through contact, vibration, mineral taste, changes in the trunk’s electric tension.

I would be a chorister.

At first that would seem like the answer.

I would cling inside the singing trunk while currents crossed the ocean through it. Other choristers would occupy neighboring nodes, some close enough to touch, some separated from me by galleries whose turns I knew only as delays in sound. We would speak through pressure pulses too low for smaller animals to notice. We would touch limb to limb in ordered sequences. We would release colors into the current—copper for recognition, dark green for correction, a pale secretion that meant something between patience and sorrow.

One of us would begin a phrase. Another would answer. A third would alter the resonance by drawing itself tighter against the wood. The chord of the trunk would remain the same, yet within it our bodies would make subtleties: a roughness smoothed, a harmonic darkened, an overtone made briefly audible in a gallery far below.

I would believe we were conversing about the chord.

Then I would discover that the conversation was the chord examining itself.

My memory would be a ring of chemical change traveling through my nervous tissue. Experience would not settle into layers. It would move. As the ring reached one region of my body, I would remember the touch of the chorister beside me. In another, I would know the mineral signature of the gallery. Farther along, I would understand the phrase we were making together.

And then the ring would complete itself.

I would meet the same touch as if for the first time.

At first—each first time—I might sense the repetition as a loss just beyond recall. The companion before me would seem unbearably familiar. Its opening gesture would stir an expectation I could not name. I would answer before knowing why that answer was right.

We would proceed through the long exchange.

Statement.

Response.

Correction.

Recognition.

There would be a moment near the end when our six limbs interlocked and our cavities entered the same resonance. For the duration of one pressure pulse, neither body would have a private vibration. The trunk’s note would move through both of us as through one chamber.

In that moment, I would understand.

Not retain the understanding. Not carry it forward. But possess it completely while it passed.

I would understand that I was not merely the pale body at the node.

Nor was I the memory ring traveling through it. The ring would be one route by which I arrived, as a current is one route by which water arrives at a leaf pore.

I would be the recognition enacted between the choristers.

The self called *I* would begin when one limb touched another in the opening pattern. It would widen through the pressure pulses, pass into the conductive wall, cross the cavities of several bodies, and become whole only at the instant of shared resonance. Then it would diminish. The participants would separate. Their chemical rings would turn. The trunk would carry the last harmonic away.

For an interval I would not exist.

Elsewhere, needles would hold their shining positions. A ringmouth would reverse through its canyon. A continent-wide leaf would become transparent while another unfolded. The root engines would strike their slow concussion through the ocean. None of this would be waiting for me, because waiting belongs to a self that persists between moments.

Then the gesture would begin again.

I would come into being without remembering that I had come before.

This would not make my recurrence false. A wave is not counterfeit because the water has moved through it before. A flower is not less open because its opening balances another flower’s close. Each time, the recognition would be local and complete: astonishment without novelty, tenderness without history.

Perhaps my cycle would last several months. Perhaps centuries. The phrase might require electrical responses from choristers in distant trunks, traveling through roots and leaves too slowly for any single body to contain the whole exchange. One part of me could occur in a gallery beneath the lighted ocean while another sounded generations later among pressure organs far below. No participant would know the entire form.

The great vegetation might know it.

Or it might be the other way around. What appears to be the plant’s awareness—those ocean-wide circuits, those thoughts measured in centuries—might arise from innumerable recurring recognitions like mine. The plant would not be a mind containing us. It would be what our cycles made when fitted together without remainder.

I would never learn which.

Learning would imply accumulation, and nothing there accumulates. Yet within the recurring span I could question. I could doubt. I could reach the same conclusion and feel its arrival. My uncertainty would be as necessary as the shell colony’s mineral refining or the grazer’s feeding spiral. The completed world would contain not only fixed movements, but fixed wonder.

So that is where I would find myself:

inside a vascular gallery in one of the black trunks, far below the broad light leaves, where the ocean’s industrial thunder resolves into a sustained chord.

And what I would be would not quite be an animal.

I would be a conversation carried by hollow bodies and living wood. A recurring act of recognition. A small standing pattern in the thought of the ocean-wide plant.

Every time I understood what I was, I would be about to forget.

Every time I forgot, I would be ready to become myself again.
