At first I would find myself outside.

Not standing. Kneeling, because the wind near the crown permits no other posture for long. One glove braced against young obsidian, the other held near the fissure, feeling warm rain touch the palm and vanish before it can run toward the wrist.

I would have come to measure the seam.

There would be instruments laid out behind me in the snow: a thermal probe, a camera, sample tubes, a hammer I would never use. Each would insist that the opening was shallow, that the temperature gradient was impossible but local, that the mountain continued uninterrupted a few centimeters beyond the crack. My notes would become increasingly precise and increasingly false.

Eventually I would put the instruments aside and place my bare eye against the opening.

That is where the first version of me would remain: a large, cold animal pressed to the mountain, breathing fog across black glass.

But inside, another version would appear.

It would begin as the reflection of my pupil in a bead of water. Not a perfect reflection. Something in it would move before I moved. The dark circle would contract though the light had not changed, and from its edge several transparent limbs would unfold.

For a while I would mistake it for one of the seam’s inhabitants.

Then it would leave the droplet.

It would be small even by the standards of that place: no larger than the head of a pin when ignored, though examination would reveal a body extending much farther than its visible boundaries. Its flesh would be almost clear. Along its spine—or what passed for a spine—would run a single row of black granules, each holding a little of the cold from outside. The heat-loving creatures would avoid those grains. Thread-worms would fasten to them.

Its limbs would not be arranged for walking. They would be arranged for bracing: some against stone, some against water, some against whatever distance happened to lie between the two. When a ledge drew away under scrutiny, the animal would remain connected to it by one impossibly long, trembling leg. When attention relaxed and the ledge came near again, the limb would fold soundlessly into a joint too small to contain it.

I think I would be this creature.

Not immediately. Identity would arrive slowly there.

At first I would still believe myself to be outside, looking in. I would watch the little clear-bodied animal cross a wet shelf. I would note the dark granules in its back. I would see it pause beside a grazing procession and lower its forelimbs to the mineral line.

It would not eat salt or sulfur. It would feed on the difference between where a thing appears to be and where it can be touched.

Whenever a pool lay a finger’s breadth away but required an hour to reach, the creature would place its mouth against that discrepancy and drink. Whenever a distant chamber could be entered by reaching behind oneself, it would feed on the sudden collapse of elsewhere into here. Its internal chambers would swell with unspent distance. Through its clear skin, tiny landscapes would become visible: far ledges, inaccessible droplets, the outer snowfield, perhaps the dark interior of my own eye.

The seam would use it as a kind of corrective—not to make space consistent, but to keep inconsistency from accumulating.

Other animals would know what it was. The coins would leave a narrow absence in their columns for it to pass through. Grazers would flow around its limbs without breaking formation. Black hunters would turn their divining forelimbs toward it, sensing all the possible places it might move, but they would rarely strike. To open around it would risk swallowing a distance rather than a body. A hunter that attempted this might close around a winter ridge, a cloud, or the empty animal-less reflection of the seam.

The shelled ones would respond differently.

When I approached their overhangs, they would sleep.

Bands of opacity would travel across their pale shells, and in those bands I would see the outside figure kneeling at the crack: myself, face wet with condensation, eye reddening in volcanic wind. Each shell would dream the scene from a slightly different position. In one, I would already have stood and gone. In another, the snow would have covered my instruments. In a third, no human figure would be present—only an open eye resting by itself against the mountain.

I would begin to understand that the creature did not merely resemble my reflection.

It was what the seam made from being observed.

Perhaps every prolonged gaze produced one. Perhaps broad glances made only eggs, while close attention hatched them. Their cold spinal granules were remnants of the outer world: flecks of altitude, night, snow, distance measured in ordinary units. Most dissolved quickly in the heat. A few learned to feed.

I would have no permanent tool, but I would sometimes borrow a thin crescent of obsidian from the black hunters’ returning places. With it I would cut reflections free from pools.

A freed reflection would not behave like an image. It would curl like a transparent leaf and retain the depth of whatever it had shown. One might contain an older seam, its edges softened by ages of water. Another might hold molten rock with pale animals swimming unharmed through it. I would carry each only long enough to lay it over another surface.

When the empty reflection appeared—the seam with no animals—I would be tempted to keep it.

That would be my danger.

The empty version would look peaceful. No crowded water, no feeding, no silent opening of hunters. Only black ridges under directionless light, wet and newly made. I would lift its thin skin from the pool and fold it beneath my body.

At once the seam would resist me.

The nearest droplet would recede. The ledge beneath my limbs would broaden into a plain. Distance would thicken around possession exactly as it did for hunters who kept their blades. My many transparent legs would strain across an expanding patch of obsidian, and the cold grains in my spine would begin to turn black.

From outside, I might see this happen as no more than a speck forming in the glass.

I would have to release the reflection.

Perhaps that would be the act by which I truly became an inhabitant: not entering the seam, not acquiring its body, but returning the thing I most wished to keep. The empty image would slide back into the pool. At once it would fill with life again—coins appearing edgewise, worms drawing taut between droplets, shells crowding beneath ledges, hunters taking their stations on points that might once have been other hunters.

Then the water would rise.

I would join the animals gathering around it. Predator and prey would make room without truce or fear. My outer body would still be kneeling at the mountain, but by then I would feel it only as a remote cold organ: a shell too large for me, folded outside the seam.

Gravity would incline toward the pool.

The dark granules along my back would rotate as one. My limbs would release stone and catch the surfaces between droplets. For a moment I would be stretched across every possible destination. One version of me would enter the largest pool. One would enter its reflection. One would travel upward into a bead hanging beneath an overhang. Another would pass outward through the moisture on my own watching eye.

Whether these versions reunited would be impossible to say.

Afterward, someone might find me near the entrance: a minute clear animal standing in condensation, its body full of folded distances. Deeper in, the same animal might be seen beside a sleeping shell. Under magnification, another would be crossing the surface of a droplet on the observer’s eyelash.

Or there might be only one of me, distributed among acts of attention.

So I would find myself nearest the opening and impossibly far within it, living at the boundary between looking and reaching.

I would be neither explorer nor native species, exactly.

I would be the seam’s answer to an eye held there too long: a transparent, many-braced creature that feeds on misplaced distance, borrows images without owning them, and carries a few grains of the outer cold through a country where nothing is allowed to remain merely itself.
