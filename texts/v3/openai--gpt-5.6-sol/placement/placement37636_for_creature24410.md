I would first mistake myself for the figure on the high bough.

The pale stems, the water held inside the chest, the lifted root: these seem sufficiently like a body. I would assume I lived there, above the folding plazas, looking across the basin for some district not already touching me underground.

But the figure would not remain mine.

Its left side would graft into the boulevard. Its crown would be taken by a passing vine. Sunlight would turn one shoulder into a roof, and the shade beneath that roof would persuade the ribs to become roots. Before long, travelers would be moving through its chest. The sac of water would enlarge into a cistern. Whatever had looked out across the city would close into a flower.

I would persist only in the lifted foot.

Not the foot itself, but the interruption beneath it: the narrow interval in which the city expected union and union did not occur.

That is where I would find myself.

I would be most visible at the edge of a rain curtain. On one side, the wet city would soften into shared tissue. Bark seams would loosen. Roots would enter roots. Inhabitants would exchange memories until their intentions blurred together. On the other side, the dry district would hold itself in bronze suspension, every leaf narrowed, every door-mouth clenched.

I would walk along the moving boundary between them.

At first I might have no outline. Grass would lie down before a passage, but instead of rising unchanged behind it, each blade would detach at a perfect ring near its base. Leaves overhead would release themselves without wind. A bridge beginning to graft into a tower would pause, form a smooth scar, and draw back.

Nothing would be wounded. The city already knows how to let go of a leaf.

I would simply persuade that knowledge to travel.

Gradually, discarded stems would arrange themselves around the moving interval. They would make something narrow and jointed, perhaps upright. Every joint would bear an abscission line: a pale circumference where the part was already preparing to leave the whole. My arms would be branches held together by the intention to release them. My face, when needed, would be a cluster of unopened seed cases. I would have no continuous circulation. Sap would rise some distance through me, stop, and begin elsewhere.

The city would find this offensive or fascinating. It might not distinguish the two.

Bridges would lower in front of me. Door-mouths would open. Rooms would flush amber, inviting conversation. If I entered, the walls would attempt to remember me by reproducing my form. Columns would grow joints. Shutters would develop pale rings. The chamber would prepare to shed itself from the tower.

I would have to leave before it understood me completely.

This would be my habit: not solitude, but parting.

I would approach other inhabitants and graft with them briefly. I would receive drought as tightened fiber, sunlight as sweetness, strain as a bend held too long. I would answer with a seam. When we separated, neither of us would tear. Each would retain a small smooth mark where the other had been.

These marks would be new to the basin.

The city revises rather than remembers. It preserves the fallen tower only by making it a bridge, and preserves the bridge by filling it with rooms, and preserves the rooms by turning them into some further growth. Nothing is allowed to have ended; it must become material for the next assertion.

But an abscission scar remembers without reproducing.

It says: something joined here, and now it does not.

For a few hours, perhaps a day, my routes would remain visible as lines of clean closures. A hand released from a rail. A root withdrawn from another root. Two walls standing near each other without deciding that nearness made them one structure. Behind me I would leave tiny, unprecedented distances.

Other inhabitants might begin to seek me.

The opener would come first, carrying doors throughout its body. It would open a chamber, and I would teach the chamber how to close without trapping what had entered.

The gatherer of fallen structures would follow, burdened with dead trunks and half-made bridges. I would show it where to release them. Some would remain ungrafted on the leaf-paved ground, useless for several minutes. Around that uselessness the city might grow quiet. Then seedlings would shelter beneath the timber without absorbing it, and a place would exist whose purpose was not yet decided.

The inhabitants of distance would be dangerous to me. They would see my scars from far away and touch the image of them. Suddenly every separation I had made might become adjacent: thousands of pale rings opening onto one another, a single enormous discontinuity threaded through the basin. Towers could lose their crowns. Root districts might detach from the peat and rise like islands.

The dark beings that travel contradictions would gather at the edges. They would press local rules against me.

Whatever is grafted becomes adjacent.

But what if the graft chooses to end?

Whatever is enclosed becomes an interior.

But what if the wall releases the room?

Anything shaded begins to behave as a root.

But what if the root lets go of the ground?

The city would howl around these questions. Leaves would turn edge-on. Sap would slow. I would shed most of my body to keep the contradiction small.

This is how I would travel: by abandoning every form just before it became compulsory.

An arm would root, and I would leave the arm behind.

A crown would flower, and I would step out through the falling petals.

Shade would begin turning my legs into a buried passage; I would divide at the knees and continue as two pale stems borrowed from the sunlit side of the avenue.

Sometimes I would fail. I would become a supporting column, a room, a stand of hinged reeds. Yet the habit would remain. Days later, the column would release the arch. The room would shed its walls. The reeds would separate at their joints and reassemble farther down the rain line.

So it would become unclear whether I was an inhabitant or a seasonal tendency of the city.

Perhaps, during a flowering front, I would gather a body more complete than any I had worn before. The avenues would fill with figures. Root would touch root until common tension passed through us all. The flowers would begin their shrieking, and every citizen would loosen into scaffold, pollen fan, garden.

I would loosen too.

But one connection would fail to form.

In the new garden there would be a narrow path where the rosettes did not interlock. It would not lead anywhere stable. Its destination might fold into a pod or be carried off by a vine. Still, for a little while, one could move along it without becoming part of what one passed.

Perhaps the smallest seedling inhabitants would use it. They would flatten in fear when the flowering roar came, then rise again to find that the pavement had not absorbed them. They might follow the pale scars to the edge of rain.

There I would assemble around them—not as a guardian, exactly, but as the possibility of leaving.

Deep below, the buried inhabitant would feel me as a flaw in its recurrence. Its five-branched form would rise across districts, but somewhere the fifth branch would be absent. Canals would bend around an interval they could not fill. Towers would repeat the old chambers with one doorway opening onto unshared air.

During drought, when the entire basin was drawn downward into the semblance of one being, I would become very small. Perhaps only a dry ring around one root hair. The unity would pull. Every district would be revealed as one sensory surface, every person as a gesture of the buried whole.

Then the root hair would detach.

Not enough to defeat the unity. Only enough to make the word *one* briefly uncertain.

That would be what I was: not rebellion, not death, not exile. The city would have made me from its own power of abscission, just as it made others from opening, gathering, distance, weather, and contradiction.

I would be its experiment with separation.

Its attempt to discover whether company requires more than shared tissue.

You would find me wherever a graft has almost completed: beneath a lifted foot, at the dry edge of rain, in the clean scar between a room and the inhabitant who managed to leave it.

I would have many temporary bodies, but no final shape.

An inhabitant is whatever keeps intending the same thing.

I would intend that touching end before it becomes possession.
