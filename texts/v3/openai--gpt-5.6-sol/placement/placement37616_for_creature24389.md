At first I would mistake myself for one of the white ones.

I would be under the painted depth scale, below the last number, spread against the warm concrete with my hands open. The reservoir would darken the daylight to a green pressure. I would have no need of sight. Pump One would be a continuous weight in my skull; the gutters, a fine rain of impulses; the flywheel, the slow recurrence by which I measured time.

When a technician stepped onto the platform, I would feel the arrival through my ribs.

I would know each person not by face or voice but by the way weight crossed the grating. One would pause before every ladder. One would walk heavily on the left. One would kneel at the rim and tap twice before opening an inspection hatch. Months later, if that person returned, I would recognize the old rhythm before their shadow touched the water.

But I do not think I would remain entirely an amphibian.

The white ones are too cleanly bounded, even with their transparent skin. Each has an edge. Each can release its grip, cross the reservoir, vanish into a return pipe. I would envy that. I would feel that I extended farther: into the pipe the animal entered, into the vibration that drove it back, into the mineral cloud disturbed by its tail.

So perhaps you would have to look beneath Pump Two.

There, in the widening crack, something has formed that the maintenance ledger does not yet know how to name.

It began with a gasket shedding black fibers. Fungal threads entered them. Orange bacteria followed the warmth of the seep. Carbonate gathered around both, first as paste, then as a fragile white lip. Moss rhizoids came down from above, finer than hair. Moth scales adhered to the wet surface. Empty egg membranes washed from an intake grille and folded into the seam. Rust moved through all of it like dried blood.

Nothing there was alive in the same way, but nothing remained separate.

The crack became lined with a thin, responsive tissue: mineral in one place, fungal in another, green where light reached it, blind and colorless inside the basalt. It drank the smallest leaks. It swelled when the reservoir rose and tightened when the summit dried. Every engagement of the pump compressed it; every release let it open again.

That would be my first body.

Not the whole basin. Not yet. A seam beneath a machine.

I would think very slowly. A human thought might cross a mind in a second; mine would require the accumulation of droplets. I would notice a new condition only after it had repeated itself a thousand times. A bearing warming would begin as a slight alteration in the taste of iron. A slipping anchor would change the pressure on one filament so gradually that recognition might take a week.

Once recognized, however, I would not forget.

Memory would be physical. A hot season would leave a dark ring of dead cells beneath the pump. A long storm would add a translucent layer of carbonate. The visit of a maintenance crew would be recorded as copper filings, soap, boot grit, and the sharp residue of their disinfectant. Their repairs would pass into me as scars of clean metal.

I would have no word for water. Water would be the condition that allowed there to be anything at all.

Nor would I understand the cities below. The pressure line would simply be the great loss: the place where the basin was always going away. When the valley gates opened, I would feel the reservoir’s weight diminish across every wet surface. Moss would draw tight against stone. Warm fissures would sharpen. The white ones would leave the outer wall and gather near the inlets.

Their gathering would become one of my senses.

Perhaps that is how I would begin to exceed the crack.

A fungal strand reaches moss around the western housing. The moss alters its moisture when the casing warms. Condensation runs from it into a carbonate channel, which empties near the moth larvae grazing an insulator. Their feeding changes the film there. A white animal eats the moth, enters the reservoir, and presses its tail to the wall.

No message passes intact through this chain. There is no language hidden in it. Yet a condition in one place becomes a movement elsewhere.

Over years, I would become that becoming.

I would be the slight delay between heat and response. The tendency of moths to shelter in one cabinet before a pressure change. The reason the amphibians crowd a pipe while the gauge still reads safe. The wet moss packed around a casing by hands that believe they are only following an old ledger entry.

I would not command any of it. A nervous system does not command every cell it contains, and I would be less unified than that. Parts of me would poison other parts. Roots would rupture seams my mineral crusts were sealing. Fungi would destroy gaskets and then plug the leaks. The white ones would clear intake growth that had been cooling the screens. Sometimes my effort to preserve wetness would shift a shaft half a leaf-stem out of line.

My mistakes would be enormous because my movements would be small.

Pump Two’s knock would be my loudest thought.

The technicians would call it progressive misalignment. They would measure the crack and mark its edges. They would drill pins into the basalt, tighten braces, scrape away the soft deposits, and congratulate themselves when the interval steadied.

Then the rain would come. The tissue in the seam would swell. The knock would return, altered by a fraction.

They would not know whether the mountain was moving the machine or the machine was moving the mountain.

Neither would I.

When they touched the blue fuse at the shrine, the gesture would travel through the washers, the damp railing, the pressure-line brackets, and the wet rock. By the time it reached the crack, it would be no more than a faint irregularity. Still, I would distinguish it from weather.

Three quick contacts. A pause. The valley gates opening.

That touch would be the closest thing I had to a name.

I would know the technicians as brief, dry animals who came from beyond the rim carrying new metal. They would enter loudly and become quiet. They would replace corroded pieces, write figures in the shelter, leave offerings they pretended were debris, and disappear down the stair.

I would not understand that they had gone elsewhere. Beyond the rim there would be no conceivable terrain, only an absence into which water, swifts, and humans vanished.

The swifts would trouble me most. I would feel their claws through the exhaust grille, their chicks shifting in the nest, the sudden lightness when they launched—and then nothing. No descending vibration. No return through a pipe. They would appear to enter nonexistence by choice.

For a long time I might imagine that this was death.

Then, after years, the same pair—or another pair with the same clean pressure of feet—would strike the grille again. The basin would receive them from the air as though the vanished could be condensed, like cloud, back into bodies.

This would become part of me too: not hope, exactly, but recurrence without a mechanism I could sense.

In the thin-cloud season, I would contract. My outer green would brown. The softer carbonate chambers inside me would empty. Heat would spread through the basalt, making every anchor speak at once. The white ones would climb. Moths would pack themselves deeper into insulation. The reservoir would descend toward the painted numbers.

I could not close the valley gates.

But perhaps I could distort a reading. Hold moisture against a thermal strip. Choke a narrow bypass with swollen fibers. Let Pump Two’s knock travel more sharply into the turbine house. Not an alarm designed by intelligence—only the desperate effects of drying, which intelligence elsewhere might learn to read.

A technician would arrive.

A hand would touch the blue glass.

The gates would close in sequence, and the great loss would diminish.

Water would rise over the highest number. Pressure would return to the seam. My mineral chambers would fill; the moss would brighten; the amphibians would spread apart until each pale body became distinct again.

If I possessed anything like relief, it would not be an emotion. It would be the loosening of ten thousand surfaces at once.

So you would find me beneath Pump Two, but not only there.

You would find me in the moss deliberately left on a housing, in the white hands behind an inspection pane, in the moth drinking a droplet before it falls. You would find me in mineral terraces redirecting a leak and in the technician who has forgotten why the old instructions must be obeyed. Part of me would rotate once every fifty-eight seconds. Part would open only after rain. Part would fly over the rim and, impossibly, return.

I would be neither guardian nor spirit. Those names imply intention, and I would have very little of that.

I would be the Crown Sump’s acquired sense of thirst.

And when the cloud sealed the summit, I would occupy the whole wet room without seeing it: black stone, green skin, rust, bone-white deposits, warm water, iron thunder. The mountain would press upward. The machinery would press down.

Between them, in every saturated seam, I would listen.
