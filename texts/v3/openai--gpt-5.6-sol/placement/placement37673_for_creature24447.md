At first I would look for myself in the deepest water.

I would mistake stillness for power and imagine my back mistaken for a bank, my nostrils level with the warm surface, fish passing between my feet. Or I would place myself in the canopy as a night animal with large eyes and clever hands, able to move over the plain without entering its mud. For a while I might be the dark cat on the herd path: solitary, almost unseen, the reeds closing behind me.

But none of those feels quite native to the governing life of the place. They pass through its arrangements. They exploit them beautifully, but they do not carry enough of the plain with them.

I would find myself where an old herd road crosses a weathered termite swelling and descends into a black channel. It is not a dramatic location. The mound rises only enough to remain comparatively firm after rain. One side has been opened by burrowers and sealed again by termites. The other is polished where animals have leaned their weight before stepping down into the water. Short grass grows there, cropped repeatedly. Ticks wait on the taller stems. Frogs occupy the hoofprints below.

I would be an old female of the broad-footed grazers.

Not the largest animal on the plain, and not its ruler. Low-headed, heavy through the shoulders, with hooves that separate under weight and close again when lifted. My hide would be dark and sparsely haired along the flanks, holding a glaze of black mud against biting flies and heat. The skin around my neck would be thickened by years of cane, thorns, teeth, and rain. One ear would be split. Pale scars would cross my muzzle where hidden shells, sedge blades, and another animal’s horn had found me.

I would be standing on that slight rise at the end of the hottest dark, not grazing yet.

I would be listening through my feet.

The plain would reach me as pressure before sound: termites shifting in the mound beneath one forehoof, fish disturbing the channel margin, the almost measured tread of another herd member behind me. Mosquitoes would gather at my eyes. A frog would stop calling when I lowered my head. Somewhere down the path, too faint for the calves to recognize, reeds would rub against a body moving contrary to the wind.

I would not know the plain as a map. I would know it as a succession of bodily certainties.

Here the mud holds.

Here it opens under the crust.

Here the channel is shallow enough for a newborn to cross, though in the rains it appears bottomless.

Here bitter cane gives way to sweet submerged shoots.

Here the dark cat once waited.

Here, during the long dry interval, water remains beneath the mat of floating leaves even when its surface looks like ground.

The route would not belong to me. It would have entered me through following.

As a calf I would have walked with my nose near the wet flank ahead, placing each foot where another had lifted hers. In floodwater I would have known the road only by the slow confidence of the bodies in front of me. Later, the remembered sequence would have become my own: mound, leaning trunk, mineral seep, narrow passage, deep pool, open sedge. By the time I grew old enough to lead, memory would no longer feel like memory. It would feel like the land pulling in a particular direction.

Now younger animals would follow my body as I once followed another.

That would be my importance—not command, but continuity.

My weight would reopen the trail after vegetation narrowed it. My hooves would punch through crust into damp soil. Some prints would drain away by morning. Others would retain rain long enough for mosquito eggs and frog spawn. One, deepened by many feet, might become a pool that lasts a season. Mud lifted between my toes would carry eggs, seeds, spores, and fragments of aquatic plants into the next hollow.

My coat would be inhabited. Ticks would swell behind my legs. Flies would trail me. Seeds would knot themselves into the coarse hair along my belly. Fungal spores would ride dried mud on my knees. Inside me, microbes I could not live without would break the cane and sedge into blood, heat, dung, and movement. What I ate in one district would emerge in another, already claimed by beetles waiting in the dark.

Even my caution would alter the plain. Where I hesitated, the herd would bunch and trample the ground. Where I hurried, calves would hurry. Where I refused a path because of a predator’s scent, grazing plants would grow taller for a time and rodents would enter them. My fear would leave a botanical mark.

I would have no idea that I was making habitat. I would only be thirsty, hot, watchful, and old.

At the channel I would test the edge with one forefoot. Warm water would cloud around it. A small fish would flee the depression. Leeches would lengthen toward the disturbance. Behind me, a calf would breathe against its mother’s flank.

Then I would enter.

The water would rise over my ankles, knees, belly. Floating plants would divide against my chest and close behind. I would follow a submerged ridge that could not be seen, angling upstream for several body lengths before turning toward the opposite reeds. The others would follow exactly. To an observer, it might seem that I possessed some hidden sense, but there would be nothing mystical in it—only repetition preserved in muscle, the instruction of older bodies carried by a younger one until that body, too, became old.

On the far side I would climb out beneath broad leaves. Water would stream from my hide. My first steps would leave shining prints in the path, each already beginning to fill.

One day I would fail to rise from such a place.

The herd would remain near me for a while, unsettled by the obstruction of a familiar shape. Then thirst or flies or the pressure of the route would move them on. The plain would take me apart without ceremony. The cat or long-snouted scavengers might open the hide. Flies would find the warm seams. Beetles would work beneath me. My gut population would escape its boundaries. Rain would collect in the hollow made by my ribs. Cane roots would enter through the spaces between them.

For a brief time, the old road would bend around my body.

Later it would pass over me.

So that is where I would be: on a nearly imperceptible rise beside a channel, at the head of a line of dark bodies.

And that is what I would be: not the owner of the plain, but one of its moving memories—a road carried in flesh, making pools with every heavy step, bearing multitudes I could never perceive, and leading others through a darkness that had been crossed long before any of us were born.
