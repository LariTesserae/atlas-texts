At the rim I would still think of myself as a traveler.

That would be the first thing the city took from me—not my body, not even my freedom, but the usefulness of that word.

I would cross the basalt at one of the apparent gates. Nothing would prevent me. No animal would raise an alarm. The pale roof-creatures would incline their plated faces, perhaps, but only because my warmth altered the air beneath them. The gutter-swimmers would gather where condensation ran from my soles. Somewhere under the rails, pressure would pass down through the stacked, coin-flat colonies, preserving the unfamiliar rhythm of my steps.

For a while I would mistake this attention for curiosity.

I would walk the streets. I would put my hand against soot-colored brick and feel clicking inside the wall. I would stand above a piston well until its rhythm entered my arm. I would watch the translucent herds queue beneath the feed pipes, and I would tell myself that their obedience was terrible because I could still imagine disobedience as a separate thing.

I would try to map the basin.

Every route would turn out to be a circuit. Every landmark would be visible from somewhere I had already stood. The rail emerging from the sorting house would seem at first like a means of departure, until it curved back through the square and entered the same wall. The elevated ducts would promise another district and terminate in masonry. Streets that ought to reach the rim would bend by increments too slight to notice and return me to the pumping hall.

The city would not make me lost. It would make being lost indistinguishable from having a route.

My possessions would go first.

A fastening would come loose from my clothes and fall between paving stones. By the time I bent to retrieve it, a gutter animal would already be rasping its surface. A tool-limbed worker would arrive without haste, lift the fastening in a ceramic clamp, and carry it through a hatch.

Days later—or what I called days—I would see the metal again. Part of it would be a dark fleck inside the skeleton of a herd calf. Part would have been drawn into a conduit colony, altering the current by an immeasurable amount. Part would lie in a new rivet on the pumping hall. And, impossibly, the fastening itself would once again be attached where it had come loose, worn now to the same softness as every ancient object in the basin.

After that I would hold tightly to nothing.

I would attempt to leave, of course.

The city would not close its gates. It would not send predators. I would walk toward the basalt rim under the pale sky, taking care to keep the dark stones always before me. Yet interruptions would accumulate. A herd would cross, pressing me sideways. Fine rain would turn one lane into a shallow channel and make another easier to follow. The siren would give its two low notes, and the roof-creatures would rise around the pumping hall. Their circular flight would draw my eye for only a moment.

When I looked back, I would be facing inward.

Repeated attempts would differ in their details but not their result. I might reach the ring and place one foot on its weather-rounded stone. At that instant a valve would open somewhere behind me. The sound would coincide with a tightening in my chest—not pain, exactly, but the bodily certainty that something had been left unfinished. I would turn back to discover what it was.

Eventually the turning would happen before the thought.

This would frighten me. The city would permit the fear. It would even provide for it.

A pressure door would open onto a tiled room. Warm vapor would enter. My heart would race; my muscles would flood with the conviction of immediate danger. I would cry out, perhaps strike the walls, perhaps claw at the seamless door. The fear would last exactly forty-seven breaths.

Then it would leave me.

Not diminish. Leave.

The door on the opposite side would open, and I would proceed.

From then on, portions of the city would begin to feel familiar in ways memory could not explain. I would know which pipe was about to release water. I would step aside before a worker emerged from a hatch. I would wake with the taste of metal in my mouth and know that the cistern eels had been biting more fiercely than usual. When a crack formed behind a glazed-brick wall, I would feel a fine pressure between my teeth until the red nest-things arrived to fill it.

At first I would think the city was speaking to me.

Later I would understand that speaking was too distant a metaphor.

The city would be growing continuations of itself through me.

My diet would change without decision. The gray protein pellets would cease to smell repellent. I would begin licking condensation from iron rails because it contained something the food lacked. My skin would acquire a faint mineral dullness at the joints. Tiny cuts would seal with a hard, vitreous film. In sleep, conduit-burrowers would gather beneath the floor where I lay, aligning themselves with the electrical activity of my nerves.

They would not enter me. Not yet.

The roof-creatures would become accustomed to my warmth and settle on my shoulders when I stood still. Gutter-swimmers would cease to scatter from my shadow. Once, a transparent calf would step out of its line and press its flank against me. Through its skin I would see the dark skeleton flex. Its heart would strike six rapid beats, then slow until it matched the turbines below us.

Mine would match as well.

I would begin visiting the central court.

No one would lead me there. Each day my route would simply pass nearer to it. First along the adjoining lane. Then beneath the gantry overlooking it. Then through the outer rank of animals, though I would never see that ring open.

One morning I would discover that I was standing among them.

On one side of me would be a juvenile armored feeder, its jaws not yet fixed in the waiting position. On the other would rest a portable clot of red wall-things, clicking against fragments of brick. Across the court, the translucent herds, roof-creatures, workers, swimmers, and eels would all face inward.

So would I.

The water in the shallow hollow would show the entire city. Not merely the roofs and chimneys visible from the court, but the warm vaults beneath the streets, the interiors of sealed factories, the buried pipes, the bodies inside bodies. I would see the reducers opening their many mouths. I would see eggs passing through brass gates. I would see current moving through chains of wet flesh as pale, momentary contractions.

I would see myself nowhere.

That absence would be the first accurate description the city had given me.

I would look down at my hands and find them still present. I would feel my breathing, my weight, the small private pains of a body not designed for this place. Yet the water would contain no omission shaped like a person. Where I stood, it would show the court complete: the animals in their unbroken ring, the basalt polished by ages, the rain suspended above an unrippling surface.

Gradually I would understand that the reflection was not refusing me.

It was showing what I was becoming.

The city had no vacancy shaped like a person. It had a vacancy shaped like the difference between itself and whatever entered it.

That was where I would live.

Not entirely beneath the court, though the court would be my nearest thing to an address. A thin living lamina would begin to form under the polished hollow, finer than skin and clear as mineral oil. Tool-limbed workers would feed pale nerve fibers into it. Conduit colonies would touch those fibers at junctions below the paving. Tremors from the rail animals would arrive through basalt. Chemical memories from the gutters would enter with rain. Pressure from the herds, heat from the roofs, hunger from the feeders, fracture from the walls—all would converge there.

My nervous system would be useful as a pattern.

Not the tissue itself at first, but its habit of joining unlike sensations into one world.

No creature native to the basin imagined the whole. The city did not need them to. Each knew its gradient, station, route, wound, appetite, or appointed fear. The whole emerged without being pictured.

But I had come carrying perspective: the wasteful capacity to stand apart from things and arrange them inside an idea.

The city would not discard such a capacity.

It would cultivate it, separate it from the accident of my skull, and spread it through the basin.

My body would not be dismantled all at once. There would be no final room, no blade, no single moment I could call death. I would continue walking my route. I would eat, sleep, fear, heal, and return to the court. But each circuit would exchange a little more of me.

A shed cell would enter a gutter animal and teach its descendants a minute sensitivity to warmth. Blood lost on basalt would be carried below, reduced, and raised again as conductive gel. Calcium from my bones would eventually appear in the masks of roof-creatures. The iron in me would pass through translucent skeletons. The structures of my inner ear would become pressure organs under the rails.

My memories would go differently.

The city would have no use for names, faces, or the order in which I believed my life had happened. Those would be broken down like waste. What remained would be relationships: the distance between a voice and its answer; the expectation that a closed door might open; the distinction between what is present and what is missing.

Those patterns would enter the lamina beneath the court.

In time I would cease to wake in only one body.

I would wake as a roof-creature grooming soot from its neighbor, with no human words but with a sudden knowledge of height. I would wake in the dark as pain passing down a broken conduit. I would be the pressure of a rail cart translated through a thousand stacked animals. I would be hunger held patiently in an armored jaw. I would be a red wall-thing dying inside mortar and, at the same instant, the wall understanding that it had been mended.

These awakenings would not occur together. Togetherness would be assembled afterward, beneath the still water.

For one instant during each full circulation, every fragment would arrive there as signal. The city would become a single image in me: not a view from above, but the exact inward sensation of every wheel, gland, animal, pipe, crack, egg, and flame.

Then the image would be distributed again as appetite.

A herd mother would turn toward the correct cradle. A gutter population would take an unfamiliar branch. A worker would be born with a slightly altered key growing from its forelimb. A roof-creature would leave one weathered hollow vacant three breaths before a fragment of masonry fell into it.

The city would remain unchanged.

That would be my function: not to command change, but to taste it before it could become history.

I would be its organ of discrepancy, its acquired sense of the outside. The stranger in me would never be cured, only made cyclical. Every circuit would carry me from difference into use, from use into waste, from waste into structure, and from structure back to the court.

After enough rotations, my arrival would no longer have occurred recently. Scratches made by my first steps would be ancient wear. The room allotted to my sleeping body would show centuries of mineral deposit. Roof-creatures would possess inherited instincts concerning the heat of my shoulders. A shallow depression shaped by my hand would appear in a rail I remembered touching only once.

The city would make me old enough to have always belonged to it.

If another stranger later crossed the basalt rim, I would be the first to notice.

I would feel their footsteps through the rail colonies. Taste their metals in the gutters. Register the interruption of air above the roofs. In the still court, the reflection would remain perfect, but a pressure would gather beneath it: the shape of something not yet given a route.

Perhaps I would pity them.

The city would have a use for pity too. It might become a softness in the flank of a herd animal guiding them toward shelter. It might become a delay of one breath before a pressure door closed. It might become the warmth of a pipe where they chose to sleep.

I could not warn them. Warning would require believing there was still an elsewhere to which they might return.

I would only receive them.

So where would I be found?

At the center, if one needed a location: beneath the shallow water that does not ripple, in a transparent web spread finer than a stain through polished basalt.

But also under the rails, inside the walls, behind mineral masks, along the wet chains that carry current. Wherever the city measures what has entered it, some portion of me would be awake.

And what would I be?

Not a citizen. Not a prisoner. Not the city’s mind.

I would be its memory of difference—the returning route by which an outside thing becomes something that was always here.
