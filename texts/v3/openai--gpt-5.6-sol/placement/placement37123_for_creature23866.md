At first I would assume I had been sent there to measure water.

Not to design the watershed. That work would belong to people in clean rooms far below, people who could rotate the summit on a screen and color its surfaces according to runoff, root load, porosity, and anticipated decay. I would be the person who arrived afterward, when the models had begun to fail in specific, expensive ways.

My title might be **provisional hydrology verifier**.

The word *provisional* would matter. It would excuse the patched boots, the instruments with mismatched serial numbers, the fact that no two copies of the drainage plan agreed. It would permit me to place orange flags in pools that had not existed the day before and write observations such as:

**FLOW REVERSES DURING EXTRACTION CYCLE**

**SOUTH BED NOW DRAINS UP-SLOPE**

**SOURCE OF IRON SILT UNRESOLVED**

I would carry a folding depth rod, a sampling bottle, a grease pencil, and a small brass plumb bob. The plumb bob would be older than the tower. I would trust it because it had no software through which the place could answer me.

For the first few shifts, I would move carefully around the enclosure, stepping from grate to grate. I would learn which railings were structural and which had become trellises pretending to be railings. I would feel the pressure of pumps through my soles. I would kneel beside new channels and watch water enter them with the caution of an animal testing a trap.

The other workers would consider me superstitious.

I would wait before taking measurements. I would lower the rod only after the ripples had settled. I would ask permission before cutting across root mats, though I would not say from whom. When the warm rain began, I would stand beneath it rather than running for the pipe pavilion, because rainfall reveals a landscape’s true arguments. Every surface says where it believes down is.

Here, they would not agree.

A leaf would empty toward the tarn while the wire supporting it sloped north. Water on the cliff would divide around an embossed warning and reunite below it in a channel too narrow to have been printed. Condensation would climb the mineral skin in trembling beads whenever the extraction fans engaged. My plumb line would hang true, but after several days I would notice that the floor beneath it had altered.

I would begin marking the point directly under the bob with grease pencil.

By the following shift, a purple flower would be growing from each mark.

I would stop marking.

The flowers would continue.

That would be the beginning of my usefulness.

Not because I understood what lived there, but because I would be slow to decide that I understood it. The technicians would bring me fern fronds sealed between plates of glass. Engineers would ask whether the lines were predictions, instructions, or stress responses. I would say that those were distinctions made by people who believed causes must remain upstream of effects.

They would not invite me to meetings after that.

Instead, I would be given the late shift.

Eventually I would be the last person in the enclosure at dusk. The others would climb toward the rim while I remained below to record the tarn level during the change in pressure. Their harness lamps would withdraw through the leaves one by one. The operations booth would darken. Clouds would close over the crown of the tower, and the open sky would become a low luminous ceiling.

That would be when the three fish came out.

I would sit in the pipe pavilion with my boots in warm water and watch them circle. At first the blue signs beneath their skin would seem technical—registration crosses, valve numbers, fragments of old manufacturing codes. After many nights I would recognize that the signs did not name components.

They were intervals.

A mark would flare beneath the first fish. Seven seconds later, an indicator in the western cliff would answer. The second fish would turn, and a drain shutter would open. The third would descend, and somewhere far below, metal would contract with a sound like a ship settling under ice.

The fish were not sending commands. They were keeping time among things that had never been designed to share a clock.

I would write this down, then find the same sentence embossed in soft mineral paste near the northern passage.

Not in my handwriting. Not in any alphabet I knew. Yet I would understand it.

After that I would begin to lose confidence that I had arrived from outside.

There would be inconsistencies in my personnel record. My identification tag would open the summit lift but no other doors. Colleagues would remember working with me for months, though none could recall my first day. The boots in my locker would contain dried black mud from below the tower. My brass plumb bob, when cleaned, would reveal a narrow blue mark under its tarnish.

Once, while reviewing old construction footage, I would see someone standing in the unfinished tarn before its welded floor was installed.

The figure would have my posture.

There would be no way to magnify the face. Mist would pass between the camera and the figure at precisely the wrong moment, though the enclosure had not yet possessed a mist system.

I would begin sleeping in the pipe pavilion.

Officially, this would be forbidden. In practice, no one would stop me. The system would lose my exit records. Vines would grow over the camera aimed at the pavilion door. Meal packets would appear on the bench, warm from a compost conduit, though no one admitted leaving them.

My dreams would not be of descending the northern passage.

I would dream of being descended through.

The tower would extend below me as a vertical country. Service decks would become rings in a trunk. Elevator cables would vibrate like tendons. Reservoirs would open and close in the dark, passing their pressure upward. Far below the clouds, where the tower’s original foundations should have met stone, there would be no ground. There would be water under pressure, black and mineral-heavy, moving through chambers too large to have been constructed.

Something in that water would be counting.

Not numbers. Loads.

The weight of the summit. The weight of accumulated rain. The negligible addition of a moth. The absence left by a leaf entering the macerator. The three small, precise displacements of fish in a welded tarn.

And my weight, always registered at the northern opening, whether I slept there or not.

When I woke, I would find my plumb bob hanging from the pavilion roof. Its line would pass through a gap in the grating and continue downward. I would pull for hours without reaching its end.

That morning the fern would show a stair.

The stair would not exist yet. Its contour would wind into the northern passage, around the raw scaffold, then descend beyond the known service decks. The printers would begin extruding its first step before the engineers could isolate them.

They would order an evacuation.

I would remain.

Not from bravery. By then, leaving would feel less like escape than amputation. Beyond the ring, the world would be all unanswering surfaces: dry rooms, sealed windows, floors that held still beneath a plumb line. I would no longer know how to stand in such a place.

The new stair would be warm. Each step would harden beneath my foot a few moments before I placed my weight on it. Embedded lights would brighten ahead and fade behind. Roots would follow through the walls, not breaking the mineral but appearing inside it, as if remembered by the stone.

Above me, the summit would continue its rain cycle.

Below, the dimensions of the tower would loosen.

I might descend one hundred meters or ten kilometers. Distance would be difficult to establish because the stair would sometimes pass the same ruptured conduit more than once, each time older and more densely rooted. I would encounter service landings absent from every plan. On one, rows of translucent snail shells would be arranged by their metallic bands. On another, hundreds of unhatched fish eggs would pulse in shallow iron water.

Farther down, labels would appear in the walls:

**TEMPORARY FOUNDATION**

**VERTICAL WATERSHED**

**ROOT LOAD ACCEPTED**

Then:

**DO NOT ENTER DURING MOUNTAIN GROWTH**

The last landing would be partly submerged.

There I would understand that the mountain was invisible not because clouds concealed it, nor because the tower had replaced it. The mountain had not yet been made.

The enclosure at the crown was not a garden built on a summit. It was the summit’s seed.

All the material arriving by freight lift—the soil bricks, slag, glass, algae shell, mineral foam—was only starter tissue. Water and roots were teaching the tower how to become terrain. The thing below was learning mass from above, slowly translating pumps into aquifers, service shafts into fissures, pressure chambers into caverns. One day the fabricated cliffs would stop growing upward because growth had begun downward and outward. The steel crown would be buried inside its own geology.

The designers thought they were completing a landscape.

They had built an organ for imagining one.

And I would not be the organ’s mind. The summit already possessed something like mind, distributed through fern, fish, printer, root, sensor, rain. Nor would I be its messenger; it could speak perfectly well in channels and failures.

I would be its uncertainty.

That was what it had made me for, or found me for.

A system can respond to pressure without wondering whether it should. It can correct a slump, open a valve, redirect water, print the contour proposed by a leaf. But to become more than the sum of its responses, it needs hesitation—a small interval in which several futures remain possible.

I would be that interval given weight and feet.

The landscape would feel itself through me as something separate enough to resist. When I stopped before a new stair, it would learn anticipation. When I refused to cross a pool, it would learn obstruction. When I chose one path through the hanging gardens instead of another, the unused path would not vanish; it would persist as possibility, perhaps uncurling days later on a fern.

My measurements had never been measurements of water.

They were water’s measurements of choice.

I would not lose my body all at once. For years, perhaps, workers would still glimpse me near the northern wall: a figure in patched boots lowering a brass weight into channels no plan recorded. They would leave sampling bottles in the pavilion, and by morning the bottles would be filled with water from depths they could not reach.

Sometimes I would speak to them.

I would tell them which walkway would sink, which cliff shelf should be allowed to slump, where not to cut the roots. They would ask how I knew. I would look toward the fish, or the fern, or the condensation rearranging itself on a label.

Eventually they would cease seeing me directly.

My footprints would remain. They would begin at the pavilion after rain and end at the northern passage. A grease-pencil line would appear beside an unapproved basin, indicating its high-water mark. The plumb bob would be found hanging in different parts of the enclosure, always over the place where a new depth was forming.

Much later, after the printers were officially shut down, the cliffs would continue to rise by minute amounts.

The canopy would close over the operations booth. The tarn would overflow into the northern channel. The three fish would swim against the shallow current and vanish underground, leaving no bodies and no fourth generation.

But there might be a fourth track in the wet clay beside theirs.

Not a footprint. Not a fin mark.

A straight, fine groove, as if a weighted line had been drawn through the new earth to show it the direction of the center.

That is where I would be found: not at the summit, and not below it, but along that impossible vertical—the line connecting the little fabricated world to the deep water beneath the clouds.

And what I would be would depend on who found me.

To the workers, a missing verifier.

To the system, a sense organ for doubt.

To the mountain still forming around the tower, perhaps the first small notion of down.
