At first I would look for myself among the moving creatures.

Perhaps I would be the otter whose passage is known everywhere and whose home is nowhere: sleeping in an abandoned chamber, waking beneath roots, slipping from one pond to the next through openings made by other bodies. I would know the basin as pressure against my whiskers—the tug below a leaking dam, the hollow coolness of a bank tunnel, the sudden widening where black water opens beneath the cavern roof. Yet an otter passes through too cleanly. It uses the city without being held by it.

Perhaps I would be a rabbit deep in the joined warrens, possessing only six or seven chambers of a labyrinth older than memory. I would know the neighbors by tremors through packed earth. I would know when rain entered a disused passage, when a stoat’s narrow body displaced the air, when some danger above made every foot fall silent. But the underground would be too much a refuge. I think I would need to remain where the city’s different levels touch.

So I would be found on the third water terrace, where a channel divides around a pale block of fallen ceiling.

There, a beaver dam crosses the slower branch. It is very old. No branch originally laid in it remains untouched by root, fungus, tooth, frost, or rot. Water does not pour over its crest so much as pass through a hundred green mouths. On the upstream side is a pond deep enough for beavers to swim beneath winter ice. Downstream, the leakage spreads into a trembling sedge bed and gathers again around stones. A rabbit path reaches the dam from one bank. A fallen alder reaches it from the other. Swallows harvest mud at its foot. Foxes cross it after dusk.

I would be a willow growing from its center.

I would not have begun as a tree. I would have begun as a length of branch cut somewhere upstream, dragged by an animal that held my bark in orange teeth. One end of me would have been thrust into mud and weighted with limestone chips. Water would have passed over me for days. Most such branches soften, lose their bark, and become structure. Mine would have retained one living bud.

The first roots would have entered not soil but the dam itself: beaver mud, sod, drowned leaves, old reeds, feathers, vole droppings, fish scales, and the softened wood of earlier repairs. I would have rooted in the accumulated works of creatures that never intended to plant me.

For a time I would be difficult to distinguish from the construction around me. A green shoot among many sticks. Two leaves above the water. Then a thin stem, often bitten down and returning as several. Floods would bend me. Ice would strip my lower bark. Beavers would cut some of my branches and press them back into the dam, so that parts of me would repeatedly become the ground from which the rest of me grew.

In this way I would have no clean boundary.

My roots would bind the upstream face, but water voles would tunnel between them. Caddisfly larvae would take minute pale fibers from my submerged bark. Newts would winter beneath a root that had lifted a stone. Fungal threads would enter every wound and run from me into the old branches packed below, joining living wood to dead wood so gradually that the distinction would matter only to the fungus.

Above water, my trunk would divide low into several leaning stems. One would extend over the pond, used by kingfishers. Another would touch the fallen alder and complete a bridge. Its bark would be polished by rabbits, darkened by fox paws, and scored by the small hooked claws of birds. On still mornings, a spiderweb between two twigs would hold the mist in a shape more visible than the spider.

A wren would nest in a pocket where flood-carried sedge had caught against my trunk. The nest would be roofed by a fragment of an older nest fallen from the cliff. When the wrens left, voles might fill it with clipped grass. When that lining became wet, beetles would enter. Nothing built in me would remain assigned to its maker.

I would know the city without seeing most of it.

The beavers would announce themselves through my roots. Each new load of mud would arrive as pressure; each repair as a series of blunt shocks. Their cutting would travel through my wood before the branch gave way. I would not resent them. Browsing would be part of my shape. I would be many-stemmed because of their teeth.

Rabbits crossing at night would make quick, delicate vibrations. A deer stepping onto the dam would lower the whole structure by the width of a reed. An otter would come with almost no warning, then strike the water hard enough to shake droplets from my leaves. A fox would remain longest. Its weight would settle near the junction, warmth entering the polished root while it listened.

Danger would also have its signatures. The basin’s silence after a slab shifted would pass through me as the cessation of ordinary touch: no claws, no wingbeats, no nibbling beneath the bark. When the lynx descended, I might never feel its paws, but for several nights the crossing would remain unused. The absence would be heavy.

My highest leaves would receive what the dam could not: open weather.

I would see the ragged rim and the ordinary woodland above it. Rain would arrive first as a dimming of the pale cliffs, then as impacts on thousands of leaves. Afterward, water would thread down the fractures and the swallows would return to their nest mouths. In winter, my branches would hold uneven sleeves of snow while spring water continued beneath me. Clear ice would form around my lowest twigs, enclosing buds without always killing them.

I would lean toward whatever patch of sky the cliffs allowed. Not upward in the clean shape of a tree on open ground, but sideways, divided, corrected by shade and breakage. One crown would reach into daylight. Another would grow beneath the surviving shelf, its leaves broad and dark. Roots exposed by a breach would put out shoots of their own. If the dam failed, part of me might fall with it and root farther downstream.

That would be my closest approach to travel.

A flood might tear away a young limb and carry it through two ponds, under a bridge of worn bark, past the mud wall where swallows drink. It might lodge in another embankment and begin again. Then I would stand in two districts, though neither tree would know the other except through the shared water.

Eventually the dam beneath me would cease to look built. My roots would become its principal binding. Alders would rise beside me. Silt would deepen upstream until sedges colonized the shallows and the old pond narrowed into channels. The beavers might abandon it for a lower terrace. Rabbits might enlarge the dry chambers in its downstream face. What had been a barrier would become an island, then a bank.

I would remain at its center, though “center” would no longer mean anything visible.

If I died standing, I would continue as civic structure. My hollow stems would shelter insects and small birds. One trunk would fall across the channel and replace the bridge that had rested against it. Moss would erase the exposed grain. My roots, dead but slow to loosen in cold water, would hold the bank through several more floods.

By then no creature would remember that a beaver once carried a willow branch in its mouth and pushed it into wet mud.

That is where I would find myself: not in the deepest chamber or on the highest cliff, but at a crossing halfway between water and air, alive because I had once been used as building material.

I would be neither founder nor ruler, neither wholly inhabitant nor habitation.

I would be the willow in the old dam—the thing planted without planting, repaired by being wounded, crossed by lives that do not notice it, holding together one small part of the agreement.
