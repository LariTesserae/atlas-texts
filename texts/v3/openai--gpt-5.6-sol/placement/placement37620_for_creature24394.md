At first I would look for myself among the moving creatures.

Perhaps I would be one of the browsers, crossing the open shelves with a black sail raised to the weather, knowing the mountain through my feet. Or one of the pale gliders, living where the land ceases to be horizontal, releasing my grip and allowing the abyss to become support.

But both seem too visible.

I would be farther inside—not underground exactly, but within one of the places where the mountain turns itself inside out.

I would live in a hollow column near the western end of the central valley, below the rim where daylight appears as a gray coin suspended impossibly far above. The shaft beneath me would have no visible bottom. Warm vapor would rise through it in pulses, carrying the tastes of iron, salt, graphite, and old water. At intervals, droplets would form on my underside and fall. I would feel each one diminish through the dark until its minute impact was absorbed into deeper stone.

I would be mistaken for a mineral stain.

My body would stretch across a side chamber in the column—not a single membrane sealing the whole throat, but a branching succession of dark films fitted into cracks at different depths. Some would be thin and wet. Others would have thickened through centuries of deposition until they were nearly stone. The youngest edges would be soft, pleated, and sensitive. The oldest portions would be translucent black plates threaded with metal.

No mouth. No eyes. No face directed toward anything.

Vapor passing through my pores would feed me. Dissolved minerals would collect in layers through my tissues. Brine would move slowly between my several membranes, tightening one while loosening another. When pressure rose from below, I would bow upward. When the column inhaled, I would sink.

For much of the year, this would be the whole of my life: tension, release, condensation, weight.

Yet I would not experience it as monotony. Pressure would contain distinctions finer than color. I would feel storms moving across ridges I had never seen. Root contractions would enter through the column wall as broad, soft distortions. The footsteps of a herd would be a sequence of small eclipses passing across the stone. A glider fastening itself near the mouth of the shaft would alter the vibration by an almost immeasurable amount, but I would know the new weight was alive because it would periodically correct its grip.

The mountain would not have a shape for me.

It would have timing.

Near and far would mean soon and delayed. A thing would be large if its vibration took a long time to leave me. Direction would be the order in which my membranes tightened. The high clouds would be known only as charge arriving through conductive veins. Snow would be a muffling. Open air would be pressure without return.

And I would remember—not in thoughts, but in layers.

Each major pulse would deposit a faint mineral line inside me. Iron during one season, silica during another, a smoky band of carbon after electrical weather. My body would become a record no part of me could read alone. New tissue, flexing against those hardened layers, would respond according to what previous years had made possible. Where an old rupture had occurred, I would thicken. Where vapor had failed for decades, I would close. Where a dangerous pressure had once torn away half the colony, the survivors would remain unusually quick to slacken.

The record would look like caution.

There is an irregular pause in the sequence of the columns, the one after which the cliff gliders hatch.

I would live inside that pause.

I would not produce it by myself. Pressure from the faults would arrive beneath me. Ice farther up the shaft would resist it. Other membranes would stretch. Condensation would change the weight of the column’s throat. But my oldest plate would hold a fraction longer than the surrounding tissues. Brine would gather behind it. The whole western column would hesitate.

Then the plate would flex.

The stored pressure would pass upward, the next column would answer, and somewhere in the downward forests eggs would begin opening.

I would never know this.

I would not know the gliders, the flowers counting pulses, or the browsers lifting their sails on distant terraces. To me there would only be the familiar strain and the relief after yielding. Yet lives beyond my perception would have entered into that interval over thousands of generations. They would trust the pause because it had usually been followed by stable currents, opened vents, or a particular condition of the cloud.

If I died suddenly, the pause would not vanish. Other membranes, ice seals, mineral lips, and trapped chambers would preserve some version of it. The mountain would alter its rhythm gradually. The gliders would hatch too early for a few seasons. Some would freeze. Others would survive. Flowers would adjust their opening. Roots would change the distribution of water. No catastrophe, no broken engine—only a long correction passing through everything.

That would suit me. I would not want to be the hidden mind of the mountain. There is no hidden mind. I would be one small resistance through which its rhythm acquires character.

During the great electrical release, current would enter the iron threads grown through my body. For the first time in the season I would become visible.

Blue light would trace every branch of me across the chamber wall. My youngest films would shine violet at their edges. The oldest mineral plates would glow from within, revealing rings, scars, sealed pockets of ancient brine, and the pale curled bodies of offspring not yet released. Charge would make all my membranes contract at once.

For a moment, instead of being many separate skins joined by damp cracks, I would become one body.

The contraction would force stored water upward. It would jet from pores in a fine mist and freeze into tiny hollow spheres. Some would strike the shaft walls and break. Some would fall. A few would contain fragments of living tissue wrapped around grains of conductive ore.

Those would be my young, though they would not resemble me yet.

They would descend through the column for hours or days. Most would enter heat too great for them, or lodge on dry stone, or be buried under new mineral growth. One might adhere across a hairline vent. It would unfold when vapor moistened it, first as a stain, then a film, then several connected membranes. It might become part of another colony. It might fuse with tissue already there so completely that neither lineage remained distinct.

So even the boundary of my body would be uncertain. I would have portions meters apart, linked only when brine filled the intervening cracks. There would be seasons when one lobe no longer answered the others, and I would not know whether it had died, become independent, or joined something else.

Perhaps individuality, for me, would be nothing more than simultaneous tension.

When the charge drained away, I would relax unevenly. My light would disappear from the center outward. The column would continue breathing around me. Frost would close over the pores near its mouth. Far below, one of my released fragments might still be falling.

You would never find me by looking down from the terrace.

To know where I was, you would have to enter the western column and descend past the first ice constriction, into air warm enough to wet the stone. You would have to brace yourself against the wall and wait through two slow impacts. Then, before the third, you would place your hand against what appeared to be a smooth black mineral plate.

For several breaths, there would be nothing.

Then the stone beneath your palm would draw tight.

Not a heartbeat. Not a greeting.

A withholding—and then, through the entire column, release.

That is where I would be: in the throat of the mountain, living as one of its brief delays.
