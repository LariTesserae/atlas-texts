I would not live beside the New Well, though I would tell myself I did.

I would live inside one of the structures gathered around it: behind a cooling chamber, inside an abandoned kitchen, beneath the stair built through the kitchen’s old chimney. To reach me, one would pass a rack of salt frames, turn sideways between two warm ceramic veins, lift a curtain of black leaves, and knock on a blue door that no longer opened.

There would be another way in, but only children would know it.

At first I imagine myself as a gradient steward, walking measured routes with chalk at my belt and a stack of ceramic slips against my ribs. I would be suited to the patience of it: touching the same plaques each day, learning their small thermal dialects. This wall is fevering. This one has begun to cool from within. Here the heat has descended three finger-widths since Pale Bloom.

But I think I would become distracted by the sounds.

During my circuits I would pause with my jaw against pipes. I would hear the proper movements—the long muscular easing of pressure, the minute fizz of mineral growth—and beneath them, occasionally, a word.

Not a dramatic word. Never a prophecy.

*Set it there.*

*Not that cup.*

*I’m coming back.*

I would begin keeping records. At first these would be official: vent, hour, wall temperature, likely mechanical cause. Later I would add details no engineer requested. Whether a moth was present. Whether the nearest reservoir plant was swollen. Which way the leaves faced. Whether the words sounded older or younger than their meaning.

Eventually I would cease to be a gradient steward and become a listener, though I might refuse the title. Listeners are expected to decide what is ancestral and what is merely pressure finding a human rhythm. I would not decide. I would become interested in the delay.

Some returns arrive days after they are spoken. Others, I would suspect, have taken decades. A child’s warning might emerge only after the child has become an old route-teller and forgotten the danger. Instructions could outlive the machines they governed. Tender phrases might travel so long that no one remained who had the right to hear them.

I would try to map duration rather than distance.

My walls would fill with ceramic slivers hung from wire loops. Each would bear a phrase and two dates: the day it was heard, and the earliest day on which I believed it could have been spoken. Where certainty failed, I would leave the second date blank.

People would bring me troublesome returns.

A widow would arrive carrying a covered cup and say that her dead wife’s voice had come from a condensate drain, asking whether the western valve was closed. A household would send for me because a pipe had begun whispering the childhood name of someone who had never lived in that district. An engineer would come after dark, embarrassed, to report that a dismantled machine could still be heard completing its cycle behind a new wall.

I would listen. I would test the pipes with wire, heat, breath, and silence. Usually I would find an ordinary explanation.

I would lie when kindness required one.

Over time my room would be enclosed further. The cool patch that first made it habitable would migrate into the wall behind my sleeping shelf. A family would claim that gradient for an infant. Rather than move, I would surrender the visible room and retreat into the obsolete chimney passage behind it. Food would be left near my vent. Finished slips would emerge through a gap. People would know me chiefly by one hand.

My name would change with the architecture.

I might begin as Iven at the Lower Salt Frames, become Iven Behind-Blue, then Iven Within-the-Third-Wall. Children would call me Ear-in-Brick. Engineers, when irritated, would call me Condensation.

Then I would hear a return in my own voice.

It would come from a pipe I had already emptied and disconnected. The phrase would be quiet, badly worn, but unmistakable:

*Leave the second date blank.*

I would search my records. I would find the phrase many times in my own handwriting, but I would have no memory of speaking it aloud.

After that, I would speak less.

Not from fear exactly. I would want to learn whether silence prevented a return or merely delayed it. I would communicate by slate, then by taps, then by arrangements of ceramic slips pushed beneath the wall. Years might pass before anyone heard my voice directly.

Yet my voice would continue appearing throughout the settlement.

It would instruct workers to wait for a green light in a district where no green lamp had ever been installed. It would tell a child not to enter a sealed room moments before the child entered safely and found an ancient cup. It would murmur measurements that matched heat routes not yet formed. Most often it would say useless things:

*Turn it over.*

*There is enough.*

*Not yet.*

Some would think I had discovered how to send words deliberately through the city. Others would say the city had simply adopted my voice because I had left it unattended.

I would deny both claims, probably in writing.

By then I would be old, narrow even by the settlement’s standards, my skin patterned with mineralized stitches and red-white temperature marks that no washing entirely removed. Roots would have entered the outer chamber. I would train them around my shelves at first, then surrender shelf after shelf. They would lift the ceramic records from their hooks and hold them among themselves, each phrase slowly swallowed by woody tissue.

My last work would not be to interpret a return, but to prepare one.

I would choose a pipe running near the New Well, one known to carry sound unpredictably. During the upward rain, while the engines were stopped and everyone stood listening to the droplets tick among the leaves, I would put my mouth to the warm metal.

I do not know what I would say.

Perhaps nothing I had planned. Perhaps only my fixed name, the one used for contracts and funerary inventories, so that somewhere the city possessed a version of me that architecture could not revise.

Or perhaps:

*There is another room inside this one.*

The words would enter the pipe. No one nearby would hear them.

When I died, there would be no need to carry me far. Builders would wrap me in rootcloth and close the chamber around me. My open cup would be placed beside my hands. The name would face inward. A cooling brace would be grown through the old chimney, and eventually a stair landing would cover the blue door.

I would hold.

Years later—or generations—my return might emerge from a vent across the settlement. By then no one would remember whether I had been a person, a profession, or a fault in the pipes. A listener would press their teeth to the ceramic and hear my worn voice say something about another room.

They would record the phrase on a thin slip.

For the date spoken, they would leave a blank.

That is where I think I would find myself: not quite in a house, not quite in a wall, somewhere between the city’s memory and its plumbing.

I would begin as a human being who listened to the settlement.

I would end as one of the ways it listened back.
