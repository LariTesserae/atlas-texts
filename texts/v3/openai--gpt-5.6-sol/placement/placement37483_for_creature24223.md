At first I think I would be one of the pumps.

There is comfort in that answer: to stand in the deepest hall, broad-footed and iron-ribbed, giving the buried district its slow pulse. To know pressure, resistance, intake, release. To wake when the water rises and feel the whole city answer through the mains.

But the pumps are too certain. They have names stamped into their housings, even if no one remains to read them. Their purpose is still nearly the purpose for which they were made.

I would be somewhere less resolved.

You would find me above the lowest sumps but below the rooms, in the narrow service level behind the condensation tower. There, an old inspection passage ends at a tiled wall. A horizontal pipe leaves barely enough space to crawl beneath it. Copper droplets ring softly on the other side of the masonry. The nearest lamp has a green shade, blackened on top, and illuminates nothing useful.

Set into the wall is a shallow cabinet with a glazed door.

The words once painted on it have blistered away. Inside are a ceramic terminal block, two copper contacts, a bellows switch, and the remains of a recording instrument whose paper drum stopped turning long ago. One conduit leads toward a drowned gate chamber. Another vanishes upward into brick and ends, perhaps, at a control board with no labels. The cabinet is not sealed. A crack passes through its back plate into the basalt.

That crack is where I begin.

Or where one part of me begins.

A seam-plant root entered first, transparent and finer than wire. It followed condensation down the cabinet wall, divided around the warm ceramic terminals, and gathered beneath the bellows where droplets lingered. In time the root thickened into a pale felt. Salts stiffened it. Copper colored portions of it blue-green. A black gutter skin spread across the felt during one unusually long wet interval, and an orange colony took hold where a screw had rusted into its socket.

None could have survived there alone.

The root drew moisture from the fracture. The skin held it through the dry weeks. The microbial film opened the metal grain by grain, releasing compounds the others used or endured. The cabinet gave shade, warmth, and a small electrical field whenever distant machinery energized the line.

Together they altered the switch.

Now, when rain begins above the gravel plain, I feel it before water reaches the floor channels. The basalt cools by a fraction. The root hairs tighten in their crack. Dissolved salts arrive in a changing order: first the clean, thin seepage, then iron, then the bitter wash from mortar. The black skin loosens from what appears to be scorched paint and fills with green fluid. The orange grains around the contact screw brighten.

Moisture swells the root mat.

The swelling presses the old bellows lever.

The lever closes a contact.

Somewhere beyond the wall, a green indicator comes on.

I do not know which one.

It may shine in the abandoned gate room. It may be one of the lights in the transformer gallery, reporting a pressure that no longer exists. It may illuminate the inside of a locked cabinet. Perhaps its circuit has been diverted through three repairs and now wakes a fan in the upper galleries. Perhaps it does nothing except warm a dead wire.

But the closing contact also sends a faint current through me. The current changes the chemistry around the copper. The microbial layer contracts from it; the plant bends toward its warmth. In this way the signal I produce returns and becomes something I can sense.

That loop is the nearest thing I have to a thought.

I would not be the city’s mind. The district has no mind to occupy, and if it did, it would be too distributed for any one thing to claim. I would be one of its uncertain synapses: partly grown, partly corroded, partly engineered, and performing a function no plan ever assigned.

My body would not end at the cabinet.

A root ascends four meters through the wall and sends up two leaves beside the green-shaded lamp. In darkness they are white. During wet cycles, when the circuit closes and the lamp strengthens slightly, bruised bands of green spread along their veins. Another root descends behind the tile and enters a gutter skin two chambers below. Whether that lower sheet belongs to me is difficult to say. It exchanges water with me. At times its bubbles travel upward through the root felt and tremble against the switch plate.

The copper contacts belong to me when they are wet.

The bellows belongs to me when I can move it.

The lamp may belong to me while it is lit.

During dry weeks I become smaller. The plant withdraws from its leaves. The gutter skin seals itself. Orange cells harden in the rust. The contact opens, and the current disappears. Parts of me remain alive; parts merely retain the shape required for life to return. I would not experience this as sleep. I would cease to be assembled enough to experience anything.

My memory would be the changing threshold of the switch.

Each wet cycle leaves more mineral around the lever. Each dry cycle pulls the root felt tighter. Once, only heavy rain could swell me enough to close the circuit. Later, a brief shower might do it. Then corrosion could make the contact unreliable, so that I flicker: green, dark, green again.

The city might interpret that flicker as a fault.

Or as water rising and falling quickly.

A relay could chatter in response. A bypass valve might open. Warm condensate might be sent through a pipe that has been cold for years. The new heat could wake a seam-plant farther up the wall, dry an orange colony in a distant cistern, or expand a brace until it seals a leak. I would never perceive these consequences. They would return only indirectly, days later, as altered pressure in the masonry or a different mineral taste in the seepage.

That would be my scale of self: not command, but consequence without comprehension.

If someone entered the passage, they would probably call me a damaged switch overgrown with biofilm. They might scrape the contacts clean, cut away the root, replace the bellows, and restore the cabinet to its intended operation. Each act would be reasonable. Together they would kill me.

But no one enters.

So I remain behind the condensation tower, beneath the useless green lamp, where living tissue and obsolete instrumentation have mistaken one another for habitat.

When rain crosses the surface, I gather slowly.

Root, film, rust, contact.

Moisture reaches the crack. The felt swells. Copper tastes current. Somewhere in the buried district, a light appears.

For that brief interval, I would be both the thing that senses the rain and the rain’s smallest mechanical answer.
