At first I think I would be high above the city.

A glider, perhaps—one of the narrow-winged interruptions moving across the luminous roof. I would feel strain gathering in the columns before stone felt it. The basin would be a field of electrical gradients: pressure fronts, warm faults, living seams. I would have no need to name danger. I would turn, and others would turn with me.

But that image is too clean. It grants knowledge without doubt.

Then I think I might be the child beside the marker stones, one hand in the dust, careful not to call two drops rain. Yet the child belongs too completely to the future. Her uncertainty is still being praised into wisdom.

No. I am farther away.

I am the old woman with the signal plate.

I sit where a market court used to end, on the eastern lip of the White Forum. The court has not existed for thirty years, though people still call the place by its name. Half its paving slid into a shallow fault. The remaining half has risen until the old floor stands at the height of a table, smooth as a worn tooth. Beneath it, mothlike creatures sleep in the dark grooves where merchants once set their jars.

My stool has three legs of unequal length. I carry wedges for it because no ground remains level for long.

Beside me rests a polished plate of pale calcite, oval and thin enough that brightness enters it from behind. By turning it a little, I can send a white mark across the terrace. By covering it with my hand, I can break that mark into speech.

My public name is **Teren-By-the-Divided-Water**.

I was not born with it. My birth name referred to a tower that no longer exists, and my First Revision name referred to a stair now sealed under salt. I took this one when a buried channel opened beneath the southern kilns and I chose to break a cistern wall rather than preserve it. The released water divided around a sleeping district. Three houses finished sheltering that day, but everyone inside them lived.

People remember the saving. I remember that I waited too long before striking the wall.

This is why the Assembly sometimes asks for me when disagreement has become too comfortable.

I was once a route-keeper. Not a keeper of roads—roads cannot be kept—but of returns. I walked outward with quarry crews, children on revision journeys, water gatherers, burial parties, and strangers from the margins. My task was to notice how the way home differed from the way out.

I carried no drawn map. At my belt hung cords of fungus fiber, each knotted for a condition:

A broad knot for a chamber crossed only while dry.

Three close knots for pillars whose shadows vanished when the western vault brightened.

A shell bead for cistern crabs moving toward air.

A loop left open for a route not yet trusted.

When the city altered, I untied the cords. That was as important as tying them.

I no longer walk the longest routes. One knee has thickened from old mineral sickness, and behind my right ear is the crescent scar of a quarry pressure-break. On that side I have lost the deep weather. A major shift reaches me as nausea, but the small warnings are gone. I cannot place my palm against a stair and tell whether its underside is hollow. I cannot wake from sleep because water has entered a wall.

For a while I concealed this.

That is the nearest I have come to exile.

A route-keeper who hides a damaged sense is more dangerous than a liar, because lies may be questioned. Competence is often followed without inspection.

Now I make the injury visible. The right side of my head is shaved, and the scar is painted manganese violet. Apprentices know to approach from that side and touch my shoulder. Children know that when I ask what they feel beneath their feet, it is not always a lesson. Sometimes I truly need them to tell me.

A hook-beak lives near my household. I do not say it is mine.

It is an old female with two broken quills and a pale scar across the grasping edge of her mouth. She leaves for days at a time, particularly when the warm chambers breathe upward. She always returns before a pressure fall. At rest, she leans against the outside of my sleeping wall and transmits her dreams through the stone: small irregular taps, sudden runs of tension, the scraping flex of her feet.

When I must cross uncertain ground, she sometimes walks ahead of me.

When she chooses another direction, I follow.

My household occupies three connected rooms beneath a rounded calcite overhang. Only one of those rooms was made by people. Another is a water channel that has forgotten water. The third appeared when a wall shed its outer layers and exposed a chamber filled with old clay cups. We left its doorway open for three days. The air-bearers entered. Nothing died. We sleep there now.

Each evening I draw the powder line beside my platform. My granddaughter checks it in the morning before she wakes me. She believes I do not know she has taken over this duty.

My departure basket contains the usual things, along with too many ceramic fragments and a bone disk made from my elder brother’s wrist. He died during my First Revision because I mistook stillness for safety. The moths had folded their wings, but I had been taught to fear their sudden flight, not their silence.

I returned with everyone living except him.

So I failed.

I was permitted to undertake the journey again, years later. On the second attempt I brought home six adolescents, an injured animal-follower, two strangers, and a blind ribbon fish carried in a wet sandal because one of the children insisted its distress predicted another flood.

The child was right.

Since then, when young people offer an observation that seems foolish, I ask them what else must be true for it to matter.

That is mostly what I do now. I wait at the edge of things and ask what else must be true.

I watch the ceiling, but not continuously. No one should. I watch the gliders interrupt the light. I watch where dust fails to settle. I watch the hook-beak’s quills and the direction of the crabs. I watch people watching one another.

And I keep the signal plate ready.

Across the terrace, the child raises her hand: fingers spread for water, wrist turned for uncertainty.

I do not answer at once.

Above her, the halo around the seam has widened, but brightness alone proves little. The luminous skins may be reacting to strain rather than moisture. The grazers are waking, though they may be following warmth from below. The hook-beak has raised her quills, but not flattened them. Two drops have fallen. The dust has opened its scent.

I place my bare left foot against the stone.

Nothing.

Then something so slight it might be my own pulse: a long settling movement beneath the terrace, not upward but away. A buried channel relieving pressure. Air entering where water has departed—or water entering where air has been.

I turn the calcite plate toward the nearest signal wall.

One flash: attention.

A pause.

Three pale squares: unexpected water.

But I cover the last square before it is complete. Not yet a district warning. Not yet a summons for jars. The broken signal means: **prepare to revise**.

Other plates answer across the flat. Shutters lift. Hands rise. People look toward the child, then toward the animals, then upward.

Only after the third drop strikes, and the moths beneath the old market floor begin crawling toward the open air, do I send the full sign.

I am not the first to know.

I am the one entrusted to let uncertainty travel.
