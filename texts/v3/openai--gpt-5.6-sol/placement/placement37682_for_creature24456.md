I would not be on the highest terraces, though at first I imagine myself there: something narrow-winged in the cloud, nesting among grass-grown uprights, able to see the basin whenever weather opens. From that height the city might almost become legible. Causeways would align. Districts would gather into a single pale design.

But such sight would be too clean. It would make the place one thing.

Nor would I be a monkey. The monkeys inherit distance. Their city is a sequence of remembered leaps, and I am drawn instead to the places where distance fails—where a wall, a root, and a little retained water are the whole world until rain changes the terms.

I would be found midway down the basin, below the bright roofs and above the permanently drowned courts, in a chamber whose original purpose no living animal knows.

The chamber is long and partly domed. One end has collapsed beneath the roots of a fig, but the remaining vault holds sound. Shallow steps descend into a rectangular cistern. Most of the year the water occupies only the lowest three steps, black with tannin and roof shadow. Pale mineral bands mark the levels of former storms. A carved stone mouth projects from one wall, its face erased except for a deep oval opening. Moisture persists inside it even when the pool retreats.

That is where I would spend the day.

I would be a frog, but not one easily seen as a frog: small, dark, and pressed into the stone mouth with my legs folded so closely that my outline becomes another wet irregularity. My back would be the brown-black of leaf-stained water. Along my sides, a broken line of pale spots would resemble grains exposed in old mortar. My toes would end in broad, translucent pads suited not to leaves alone but to plaster, tile, moss skin, and limestone polished by runoff.

My eyes would be copper around the pupil. When closed, they would vanish beneath lids marbled like the wall.

I would know almost nothing of the basin.

The high meadows, the bridges, the parrot groves, the buried avenues—these would not be parts of my world. The city I inhabited would consist of the cistern, the carved mouth, the root curtain at the broken end, three adjoining cracks, and the overflow channel that wakes during heavy rain. Beyond the channel there would be other chambers, but for most of my life they would exist only as vibrations.

I would know the swiftlets as blades passing through the upper darkness. I would know bats by the sudden rearrangement of air at dusk and by what falls from them. I would know monkeys as impacts traveling through the ceiling: weight, pause, weight, several quick strikes, then the long diminishing tremor of their departure. I would know snakes by silence. In a place of constant small movement, silence arriving along one wall would have a shape.

My principal sense would be hearing, though hearing and touch would not be distinct. The cistern would receive the city for me.

Rain striking leaves above would enter the chamber softened. Rain striking exposed tile would enter hard and separate. Water beginning to move in a buried gutter would produce a low shudder before the first trickle appeared. A beetle falling into the pool would make one compact ring. A fish rising beneath it would make another.

Other frogs would call from nearby courts. Their voices would pass through culverts, cracks, and stone corridors, stripped of their upper notes and returned as dull metallic pulses. I would not hear individuals in a landscape. I would hear openings: this call through a narrow shaft, that one around a flooded bend, another from a chamber joined only when the water stands high.

My own call would belong to the dome.

It would begin as a short click, hardly louder than water dropping from a root. The vault would enlarge it, roll it along the curved ceiling, and return it from behind me. I would answer the return, setting a second note slightly below the first. Call and echo together would form the signal. Outside this chamber it might sound broken or incomplete. Here it would seem to come from several places at once.

I would not understand that the architecture had entered my body. Yet the dimensions of a vanished room would be present in the timing of my throat.

My kind would have lived in this cistern long enough for the chamber to shape us. We would be darker than the frogs in the court above, with shorter jumps and wider toe pads. Our tadpoles would have low mouths for rasping the bacterial film from submerged steps. They would avoid the open center, where thin blind fish patrol, and gather instead in the shallow water threaded among fallen tiles. Many would be eaten. Their tails would pass into fish, the fish into a bat or snake, and nothing about this would disturb the cistern’s appearance.

In the dry interval, life would contract.

The pool would sink below the second step. Mosquito larvae would crowd its margins. The stone mouth would remain cool, and I would wedge deeper inside it, sharing the crevice with springtails, a pale spider, and mites that moved through the damp film on my skin. Hunger would become slow. I would take whatever came close enough: a moth drinking from the wall, a roach nymph crossing the lip, winged termites after a warm evening.

There would be nights when the neighboring frogs called and no route existed to reach them.

Then rain would come—not gently, but as a change in the entire pressure of the chamber.

First the fig roots would begin ticking with drops. Then the open end would turn gray. Water would descend the steps in thin sheets, carrying grit, leaves, drowned ants, fragments of flowers, monkey hair, fruit pulp, and the droppings of birds from roofs I had never seen. The cistern would rise over its mineral bands. The blind fish would leave the deep center and enter newly covered rooms. Tadpoles would be swept from hiding places. Every surface would move.

At the height of the storm, the overflow channel would wake.

That awakening would be the largest geography available to me. A dark slot above the ordinary waterline would become a current leading outward. Cold brown water would press through it, smelling of other stone. Calls would sound from beyond—unfamiliar calls, abrupt and bright, shaped by a chamber with a lower ceiling.

I might remain where I was. Most nights I would.

But once, perhaps twice in a lifetime, I would enter the channel.

I would move against the shallow current, toes spread on submerged masonry, surfacing where roots had broken the covering slabs. For an hour the separated courts would be one wet body. I would pass eggs from another population, crabs braced in side joints, a drowned lizard turning slowly against a grate of roots. The water would divide around ancient stonework and pull me along a route laid down for a purpose extinguished before my lineage acquired its call.

By dawn I might be in another cistern.

It would differ by almost nothing and therefore by everything. The water would carry another mineral taste. The walls would be redder. The resident frogs would call in paired notes rather than clicks. There might be no stone mouth, only a crack behind a fallen relief. The pool might contain no fish and many dragonfly larvae. A snake might already know the ledge where newcomers climb out.

If I survived, I would take shelter and answer.

My call would fit badly at first. The new chamber would seize it and return it too quickly. What had been full beneath my old dome would sound crowded here, the echo colliding with the second note. I would call again, adjusting by fractions—not through intention, but because some intervals would bring answers and others would not.

Perhaps I would remain. Perhaps the water would fall before I found the way back.

Then the storm passage would close. The channel would become a strip of cooling mud, then a dark seam inhabited by crickets. The two cisterns would separate for another season or another generation. Yet something would have crossed: a body, a parasite, a clutch of eggs, a new rhythm entering an old chorus.

That is where I would find myself—not in command of the city, not even aware of it, but held within one of its resonant organs.

I would be a small frog in the erased mouth of a fountain, carrying the dimensions of a ruined chamber in my voice. Most of the world would reach me as tremor, echo, falling matter, and brief floods. The city would not be ancient to me. It would not be built or ruined. It would be the shape that sound takes before it returns, the height water reaches on the steps, the wet route that exists for one night and then becomes stone again.
