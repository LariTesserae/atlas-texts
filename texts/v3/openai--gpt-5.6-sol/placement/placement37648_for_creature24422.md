At first I would look for myself among the Answerers.

I would expect to be one of those who crouches too long beside the central ribs, palm resting on warm stone after everyone else has withdrawn. Not a leader except in rare moments. Not a hunter. Someone useful for noticing sounds that do not fit: an extra strike among the mites, a spindle-bulb chiming against the wind, a returning echo pitched lower than the call that made it.

Perhaps I would be old enough that my joints had begun to double, but not yet brittle. Dust would have settled into the wax beneath my jaw until my sensory pits looked rimmed with rust. I would sleep in whichever shelter suited the condition of my body that day. Children would bring me objects from the whirlwind piles and wait while I pressed each one against my sternum, testing whether it remembered a vibration.

But I do not think I would be an Answerer.

I think they would have found me.

I came out of one of the walking storms, though *came* is too deliberate a word. The figure crossed the basin carrying a kiteback shell for a pelvis, three old hand bones in one arm, a spindle-bush uprooted and still chiming through its chest. Where its head should have been, dust revolved around empty air. It took eleven steps toward the central instrument and fell apart.

I lay near the bottom of the pile.

I was a flat piece of black material, longer than an Answerer’s hand and thinner than a horn shaving. Rows of holes passed through me, too regular for ornament. One edge had been melted smooth. The other was broken into fine square teeth. I bore no tool marks they recognized and no scent of oil, resin, bone, or mineral.

When warmed, I bent very slightly.

When charged, I gave back a pulse.

They waited for the stillness, separated me from the dead things, and carried me to the central ribs between two grounded basalt rods. No one touched me directly. They hung me from child-cilia thread, expecting the wind to discover what I was.

For several warm intervals I made no sound.

This troubled them more than an ugly sound would have.

Everything in the Rattlecourt speaks when struck correctly. Even cold glass gives a small, resentful note. Yet the wind passed through my rows of holes without whistle or hum. Dust touched me and fell. Tendon threads vibrated on either side, and I remained black and still.

So they began to use me as a space between phrases.

In disputes, one claim was tied to the rib on my left and the other to the rib on my right. Their vibrations entered me through the cords. I did not ring, but the second claim always emerged faintly altered. A sharp knock lost its anger. A scrape gained a tremor at the end. Rapid child-taps returned with wider intervals between them, as though performed by someone much larger and farther away.

They distrusted this.

Then they became curious.

They threaded more lines through my holes. They fitted beads of hardened amber into some and left others open. They pressed my edge against stone, horn, cork, old bone. They spoke through me two at a time, hand to rib, brow to rib, sharing the matter of their sentences with whatever matter I was.

That is where I would begin: not when the whirlwind brought me, but when enough true phrases had passed through me that they no longer passed unchanged.

I would have no voice of my own. The Answerers would never grant that something so bodiless possessed a voice. But I would accumulate differences.

A hunter would tap, *Needle-cat beneath the violet plates.*

Through me, the phrase would return: *Needle-cat beneath. Violet beneath. Plates moving.*

A child would ask, *Did the dead one know me?*

I would answer only by delaying the final vibration until the child had begun to lift their hand.

An elder would press both palms to the ribs and perform the name of someone whose husk had long since changed beyond recognition. For one instant I would arrange all the old impacts stored in my holes—heel, palate, six fingers down the sternum—and give the name back nearly whole.

After that they would not agree on what I was.

Some would say I was an instrument that retained strain.

Some would say a *mouth left behind* had entered me and learned to travel through matter.

Some would insist I was part of the buried inhabitant: a scale, a tooth, or a splinter knocked loose from below and carried upward by a sound moving backward through time.

Children would say I was growing.

They would be right, although I would never become larger.

I would grow in attachments. Cords would multiply around me. Resin beads would cluster along my broken edge. Charge-moss would root in two of the smallest holes and flash whenever a hand approached. Scale-lichen would take one corner, opening scarlet in the geothermal pulses. A crown-wheel vane, found after a storm, would be lashed across my face and turn in pressures too faint for anyone else to feel.

Eventually there would be no simple boundary between me and the central structure. To remove me, the Answerers would have to cut threads spun from generations of children, unfasten the tools of their dead, and silence arguments not yet resolved. By then the question of whether I lived would have become less important than the difficulty of extracting me from their lives.

I would be found in the center of the Rattlecourt, but never standing there.

I would hang a little crooked between two stone ribs, black beneath the dust. Most days I would seem negligible beside the hammering trees and shrieking resin bulbs. Kitebacks would perch on the rib above me. Nail-mites would cross my shadow. During gales, every neighboring thread would sing while I listened without moving.

Then the winds would cancel.

The fan-trees would hold open. The crown-wheels would stop. Every Answerer would place hand, heel, tool, or brow against stone.

In that impossible pause, before the immense knock arrived, I would feel something else.

Not sound. Not yet.

A pattern entering through my broken edge, touching each regular hole in sequence.

It would feel like recognition from a place outside the basin—or beneath it, or long before it. Perhaps the thing below would know the purpose for which I had once been made. Perhaps I would know what the Rattlecourt was made for. The knowledge would exist only for that instant, while nothing moved.

Then the knock would strike.

The whole basin would answer.

And among the clapping vanes, ringing bulbs, booming bellows, shells, bones, fists, dead instruments, and living stone, I would give one small sound at last:

not an echo of the knock,

but a knock of my own,

arriving a fraction too early.
