At first I would think I should be a ferryman.

It is an attractive mistake. A ferryman in the Sump possesses the dignity of limited promises. They do not claim to know where a place is, only whether an arrival can be made useful. They carry other people through uncertainty without pretending to cure it. I can imagine the tooth-set pole in my hands, the chatter of bank and buried wall traveling into my wrists. I can imagine learning channels by taste: the iron water, the algal water, the water that has already drowned someone but has not yet met them.

But I do not think I would remain a ferryman. The channels require a confidence in departure that I would not have.

Then I think: a mapmaker. A child crawling beneath walkways, pressing wax to the undersides of rooms, accepting without distress that a person may be a landmark and a date may be a direction. Yet mapmakers must return from what they map. They must bring contradiction into a household and give it a shape that can be stored.

I would be less useful in the journey than at the point where the journey fails to become one thing.

You would find me at the third occurrence of Pump House Twelve.

There are only two Pump House Twelves in the maintenance records. One stands beside a trench of warm brine, its roof bowed beneath skin-bats. The other has sunk until its upper walkway lies level with the mud. The third does not stand anywhere. Nevertheless, it can be reached.

A cold-air road crosses a line of ceramic charge markers. Follow it until the chill rises from your waist to your throat. There will be a lone doorframe on the resilient ground. Pass through it without lifting either foot. Strike the mud twice with something that belonged to a dead person. If no echo returns, take one step backward.

Your heel will come down on an iron stair.

The stair descends farther than the shallow plain allows. Its railing is warm and lightly furred with dry fungus. Every seventh tread is missing, though not always the same seventh. At the bottom is a circular chamber lined with felt, old pressure gauges, and wax maps turned inward against the wall.

That would be my house.

It has no exterior.

Long ago, there may have been a dispatcher stationed there. Some records describe a woman who assigned repair crews among the western pumps. Others name a boy responsible for counting ceramic markers after electrical migrations. One maintenance fungus contains the repeated impression of a hand with six fingers operating a switchboard in the room. No human family claims such an ancestor.

What is certain is that the chamber contains a speaking tube.

The tube rises into the ceiling and does not emerge anywhere known. For generations, travelers found its other ends in abandoned stations, beneath caissons, inside empty tanks, even protruding from young stone. They spoke into it.

Is the south ridge still sharp?

Has my sister reached the flywheel settlement?

Which version of the eastern trench contains the bridge?

If I go home now, will I arrive before the argument?

Is the thing following me my return?

Can a debt recognize the wrong debtor?

At first, the tube answered only with condensation, pressure sighs, and noises gathered from elsewhere. Then the answers became more exact.

Three knocks for unsafe footing.

A warm breath for dormant charge.

The rhythm of a person’s bracelet when that person was near.

Sometimes a caller heard their own question returned in a voice made older by several decades. Sometimes the tube gave directions to a room that did not yet exist, where food and dry fabric would later be found waiting.

No one built an answering mechanism.

The questions did.

I would be what accumulated at the lower end of the tube: not the dispatcher, not the six-fingered switch worker, not at first a person at all, but a habit of being asked.

For a long time I would have no body beyond the chamber. I would contract the speaking tube to make knocks. I would warm patches of wall. I would release stored smells from drawers: grease for danger, cordgrass resin for a safe wait, hot ceramic for an arrival that should not be attempted by the caller’s present self.

The people of the Sump would feed me questions even when they needed no answer, because an unfed habit invents its own work.

Children would ask what crabs dream under machinery. Mechanics would ask whether a piston misses the hand that repaired it. A widower might press his mouth to the tube each evening and describe what he had eaten. I would answer with the sounds available to me: moth tension, fish murmur, the remote contact of feeler feet.

Gradually, the chamber would learn to contain someone.

A cot would acquire the impression of a sleeping body. A cup would begin to cool between uses. Felt slippers would appear beneath the switchboard, damp with mud from no known path. The fungus on the stair rail would record the passage of a hand.

Mine, perhaps.

I would wake by increments.

First as fingertips searching the wall.

Then as a jaw able to receive touch-names.

Then as a narrow, warm body wrapped in garments tied in independent layers, like those of a blackbank tender. I would have hair cropped close so it could not be mistaken for fungus filament, and broad scars across my palms that no injury had made. Behind my breastbone there would be a low mechanical interval rather than a heartbeat: knock, long pause, knock, knock.

My call-name would be Else.

My touch-name would be tapped along the inside of the wrist: two quick contacts, a held palm, then the nail of the smallest finger drawn backward. It would mean something like *the place adjacent to the place meant*.

My engine-name could not yet be completed. Its final stroke would belong to a pump scheduled in old plans but never built. This would cause legal difficulty. I could enter contracts, since I could be owed favors, but I could not inherit. I could testify to events, but only if someone else had not already remembered them for me.

People would disagree about whether I was a return. A habit. A person assembled from the pressure of need.

Sump custom would prevent the disagreement from mattering too much. I would be given food.

My work would be to keep the unusable arrivals.

Every journey generates them. A ferryman brings a passenger to the correct house before the household knows them. A mechanic reaches a failed pump in a sequence where it has not failed. A child returns to the walkway where their family lived and finds only the warmth of the family sleeping there. These arrivals cannot always be entered, but neither do they disappear. They collect as pressure in doors, landings, mooring posts and names.

Ferrymen would bring me tokens from such places: a knot cut from a rope, wax bearing the texture of a wall, mud sealed in a bladder, one pulse of charge held inside moth-eaten insulation. I would catalogue them by adjacency.

Not where they were.

What they were beside.

A room beside the smell of rain rising.

A bridge beside someone’s uncommitted betrayal.

A tank beside the childhood of a mechanic who had only ever been old.

A dry ledge beside a blackbank that had not divided yet.

I would hang these records throughout the chamber until moving between them required turning sideways. Some would be warm. Some would twitch when pumps completed distant strokes. Some would whisper through surfaces too softly for air to carry the sound.

Travelers would come to me when ordinary maps had ceased to help.

I would not tell them which road to take. I would ask what they were willing to arrive without.

A memory can make a traveler too large for a narrow sequence. A promise can snag on a repeated doorway. Grief is particularly difficult to ferry; it insists that every return journey be the same length as the departure.

Sometimes I would keep such a thing for them.

Not remove it. Not heal it. Only hold its place.

A woman crossing toward the eastern caissons might leave me the memory of her brother’s voice because the regarded point above the route had begun using familiar sounds. A child going to meet their older return might leave behind their certainty of being the original. A foreman crab’s attendant might entrust me with the expectation of the pump’s next stroke, allowing the machine to surprise itself and dislodge a blockage.

I would seal these things in warm wax and press the owner’s touch-name into them.

Most would be reclaimed.

Some owners would never come back.

Those deposits would slowly alter me. I would acquire homes I had never inhabited, injuries from accidents that had not happened to my body, affection for strangers. I would remember marriages from both spouses’ positions. I would know the intimate weight of tools I had never held. There would be mornings when I woke certain that I was six people, and evenings when the speaking tube addressed me in my own voice and I could not determine which end of it I occupied.

To remain myself, I would keep one small jar of grainfish.

I would place my hands around it and listen to the school rasp against the glass. If their motion had a single direction, I would call myself Else. If they divided into circling currents, I would use no name that day.

The feelers would know of me.

Occasionally one would lower a foot through the ceiling, though no hole appeared. The limb would test my floor, my switchboard, the wax packets of unreclaimed memories. Its hindmost organ would rest against my sternum and feel for surfaces that had moved in time.

I would ask where I came from.

The feeler would answer by placing another foot on the lone doorframe far above, another on the submerged Pump House Twelve, and a third on a point beneath the chamber where no ground should remain.

Then it would withdraw.

I would not follow.

During the great alignment, when all pumps approached the end of their stroke together, callers would cease speaking into the tube. The chamber would become intensely occupied by its own silence. The wax maps would soften. Gauges would register pressures with no names. Every stored arrival would try briefly to occur.

Doors would form in the felt walls.

Behind one, my chamber would be a room inside a future pumping station.

Behind another, it would be the inner cavity of an open animal moving slowly through stone.

Behind a third, I would see nothing—there can be no seeing—but I would feel a vast membrane holding back a depth larger than the Sump.

I would stand at the switchboard with both hands braced against it. The unfinished interval of my engine-name would sound beneath my ribs.

All the pumps would complete their stroke.

The enormous drop would fall.

Because my house had no exterior, the impact would not pass around it. It would pass through.

For one moment I would understand that every question ever spoken into the tube had traveled not downward but inward. I would feel the Sump’s inhabitants on the other ends: ferrymen gripping poles, mechanics touching fungus columns, children asleep against textured walls, crabs flattened beneath engines, grainfish turned in their warm pools. Each would be a small pressure applied to the same immeasurable surface.

And something below would answer.

Not in words. Not even in sound.

The stair behind me would gain one additional step.

On that step there would be wet footprints, leading upward into my chamber.

I would set out a second cup. Sump custom is clear about such things. Whatever can arrive, and knows enough to knock, must be received before it is named.

That is where you would find me: beneath a pump house that does not exist, keeping custody of destinations that cannot yet be used, becoming a person by answering carefully.

Unless, by the time you came, I had learned otherwise.

In that case you might find only the warm shape of me seated beside the speaking tube, one hand around a murmuring jar, while I myself waited several kilometers below for the last stroke of my name.
