At first I would think I was one of the moving creatures.

Perhaps a knuckler, fixed to one black facet of the crater wall, knowing the world through the answer of stone. My life would be a sequence of taps separated by long attention. Solid. Hollow. Ore. Egg. Tooth. Neighbor. I would never see the Crown entire, but I would feel its industry entering through my three limbs.

Or I might be a bell-globe, transparent enough that daylight passed through me without recognition. Every concussion would become color in my flesh. I would flare blue for iron, green for copper, crimson for something newly risen from the mountain. Others would read truths from me that I did not understand.

But neither place holds me.

The center is too crowded with consequence. The outer walls are too certain in their labor. I find myself near the upper rim, where the crater’s mechanical thunder begins to loosen into the open ocean. Here the slope breaks sharply outward. On one side lies the Crown: bright obsidian, layered shells, blue discharge, the grave turning of rootwheels. On the other is the long drowned flank of the mountain descending through violet water toward blackness.

A dead rootwheel lies across that boundary.

It must once have stood near the center. Some ancient fracture tore it free and carried it upslope during a convulsion of the mountain. Now its cylinder is wedged beneath two fins of volcanic glass. Its vanes no longer turn. Pale filters occupy the pumping chambers, and sourheart threads descend through its burst conduits. Most of its enamel gears have been eaten or incorporated into nearby shell banks.

One ring remains.

It hangs crooked inside the carcass, each tooth larger than my body. The sockets still grow a thin enamel skin, though the animal that nourished them is centuries dead. With every major vibration, the ring shifts by less than the width of a microbial film. It has been performing the last fraction of a turn for generations.

I live in the spaces between its teeth.

Not alone. Not exactly.

We are soft, folded things called **afterticks**. In still water we resemble scraps of clear membrane with a dark grain at the center. We have no limbs and almost no musculature. One edge adheres to enamel; the other stretches across a gap and attaches to the next tooth. Hundreds of us span the dead gear, making a web so fine it is visible only when charge passes through it.

We feed on delays.

Every blow in the Crown reaches us twice. First it travels through the crater wall and the rootwheel’s buried remains. Then it arrives through the water, softened and fractionally late. Our bodies open to the first vibration and close at the second. In that interval, pressure draws mineral-rich fluid through microscopic valves in our tissues.

The greater the distance between the two arrivals, the more we feed.

A clean, unified impact gives us almost nothing. Discord sustains us: echoes, misfires, dragging gears, strokes that fall outside the general rhythm. The Crown is full of such errors, though from a distance its roar seems continuous. To us it is an inexhaustible rain of lateness.

We store each interval as strain.

Our transparent bodies slowly coil tighter between the enamel teeth. A knuckler’s faulty stroke may add only an imperceptible twist. A sourheart’s fracture report winds us sharply. The deep concussion of the mountain can tighten the whole colony at once until the dead gear trembles in its cradle.

Then, after the initiating sound has vanished, one of us releases.

Tick.

The movement is tiny, but the enamel carries it. Neighboring afterticks release in answer, not together but in succession.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

A miniature version of the crater’s vanished rhythm travels around the ring.

This is where I would be: not in the original blow, but in its return.

Larvae gather around us. Some require a particular rhythm to settle, yet the creatures that produced it may have moved, died, or fallen silent after the great discharge. Our delayed ticking supplies an imitation. Transparent young descend onto the dead rootwheel, mistaking the memory of labor for labor itself. Filter mouths open. Spark lice contract. Tiny knucklers test the enamel and begin making teeth from teeth.

In time, a shell bank grows around every aftertick colony.

We do not intend this. We cannot distinguish an egg from a mineral blister. We preserve no complete pattern, only whatever tension our tissues happened to receive. A rhythm may circle the gear long after it has ceased to mean anything. Sometimes we repeat the mating cadence of an extinct seam singer. Sometimes we echo the seizure of the rootwheel itself.

The other creatures answer anyway.

That is enough to make the dead machinery inhabited again.

During the great electrical alignment, we are among the last creatures to become still. Charge creeps into our web, illuminating each body as a hair-thin white crescent. The Crown pauses. Knucklers withdraw their grinders. Rootwheel teeth settle into phase. Bell-globes collapse against the stone.

But our ring continues ticking out old disturbances into the silence.

One.

Then another.

Each release smaller than the last.

There is always a moment when the entire Crown seems to be waiting for us—not knowingly, not impatiently, but materially. The conductive network cannot fire cleanly while our stored contractions keep troubling the rim. We empty ourselves one by one until no interval remains.

Then the crater becomes white.

The discharge passes through the dead rootwheel and through us. Some of our bodies split. Some fuse with their neighbors. The distinction between one aftertick and the next is erased in places, strengthened in others. Our dark central grains divide and travel along the web. When the light is gone, I may wake across three teeth instead of one, carrying tensions stored by bodies that were not mine.

So I would not know whether I was an animal or a colony.

“I” might be the membrane stretched between two teeth. I might be the dark grains moving through all of them. I might be the sequence traveling around the ring: a self existing only while one body releases after another.

Nor would I be the Crown’s memory in any conscious sense. Memory suggests preservation. We alter everything we keep. Each repetition loses detail, gains distortion, and mingles with newer impacts. The patterns circling our gear are worn copies of copies, as enamel teeth are mineral copies of teeth swallowed long ago.

Still, the past would persist in me as tension.

I would live at the threshold between the crowded crater and the unworked mountain slope, inside a wheel that no longer turns, nourished by the difference between what happens and when its echo arrives. Behind me, every surface would labor. Before me, the bright water would open above the drowned world.

And through my nearly transparent body the Clattering Crown would continue, faintly, after every sound had ended.
