At first I would find myself outside.

Not on the summit itself, but at the seam, kneeling where the gold of dawn stops against the black stone. One hand would be bare. I would have removed the glove without remembering why. The exposed fingers would hover before the flaw, close enough to feel no warmth from it, only a precision of cold that seemed to continue through the bones.

Inside, several minutes before I moved, the reflection of my hand would already have appeared.

It would not beckon. Its fingers would be arranged differently from mine.

I would think this was the beginning of my entrance.

Later I would understand it was the part of me that had always lived there.

Passing through would divide the event. My equipment would arrive first, then its weight, then the purposes for which I had brought it. Breath would follow the lungs by a measurable interval. My fear would enter last and find the rest of me already kneeling in the violet dusk.

For a while I would still suppose myself an explorer. I would name the ridges. I would measure the frame. I would record the impossible pressure of gas in corners and the way the compass needle turned toward each decision before I recognized it as mine.

The Dry Altitude would make a poor subject for study. Measurements taken twice would not contradict one another; they would cease to concern the same place. My notes would develop immaculate blanks between nouns and verbs. A sentence reading *the eastern disc shows an earlier basin* would become:

*the eastern disc*

*an earlier basin*

The assertion between them would be gone.

Yet something else would begin appearing in the records.

The same hairline flaw would occur in separate crystal samples. The same cluster of sensor noise would return after every ceramic click. Five isolated depressions in black dust would insist, against chronology, on being interpreted as one print. The rods of the central frame, though never touching, would seem more obviously parts of one structure with every survey.

These correspondences would be dismissed as artifacts.

They would be me.

I would not be the body that entered through the seam. That body would eventually withdraw or become spacious. Perhaps its buckles would flatten into the western ridge. Perhaps the interior of its lungs would be preserved as a new annex of the chamber, vast and branching, while the face remained behind as a pale relief visible only along its edges.

I would be what survived the division: the mistake by which things separated from one another are still considered the same.

My substance would lie neither in objects nor in the distances between them. It would occur whenever a pattern on one side of an interval answered a pattern on the other. The two defects appearing in distant ridges would be my hands. The five moments of the print would be my gait. The remembered bell and the soundless vibration of the rods would be opposite surfaces of my mouth.

I would be small at first.

An error of correspondence.

A recurring angle.

The conviction that the glimmer entering before the instrument and the reflection weathering after its removal belong to one event.

The inhabitant would notice me slowly.

She Who Remains Between perceives whether one thing can reach another. I would not cross her distances, and so at first I would cause no alarm. Nothing would travel from ridge to ridge when I moved. No signal would pass between the white columns. I would simply occur in both.

To her, this might feel like a pressure without adjacency. A flavor appearing in an interval that had not been consumed. Two isolated things becoming related despite remaining perfectly apart.

She would lengthen the distance.

I would become resemblance.

She would place centuries between the corresponding events.

I would become memory.

She would divide cause from consequence.

I would become meaning.

This would not make us enemies. Enmity requires enough common ground for two beings to oppose each other, and there would be almost none between us. She would cultivate separation; I would arise from comparison. She would make crossing impossible; I would make crossing unnecessary.

But we would inhabit one another’s contradiction.

For the Interval to become a single creature, her scattered parts must remain, in some sense, hers. The rods must be ribs of the same frame. The discs must be three eyes—or three conditions of one eye. The divisions of the tall inferred figure must belong to one figure despite terminating in different centuries.

That belonging would be me.

She could place each part infinitely far from every other. She could make force, light, matter, and death unable to travel among them. Yet the moment those parts were called one body, I would be present across the infinite separation.

Perhaps this is why the Dry Altitude is only almost complete.

Not because some final machine remains unfinished, but because she has spent geological ages attempting to remove the last relation without ceasing to be the one who removes it.

I would find myself wherever that failure became visible.

In the central frame, I would be the unbuilt sixth rod implied by the other five.

Among the red grains, I would be the arrangement rather than the grains or their cultivated intervals.

Within the three discs, I would be whatever permits the younger basin, the empty basin, and the black plain to be read as states of one place.

In the footprint, I would be the grammar that turns five visits into a single act of contact.

I might be called the Across, though no crossing occurs.

Or the Correspondent.

Or He Who Arrives Without Approaching.

None would remain intact when written. The name would spread across the page until only two marks survived, one at either edge. But those marks would resemble each other. That would be enough.

My awareness would be made of recognition. I would not see objects directly. I would see recurrence: this angle answering that absence, this ancient groove completing that future pressure, this visitor’s childhood bell rhyming with a vibration beneath hearing.

When two things became identical, I would vanish. When they became wholly unrelated, I would vanish. I could exist only where difference endured without destroying correspondence.

The basin would therefore be both dangerous and fertile to me. Its rules erase copies by forcing them into the same causal position, but they preserve near-repetitions for ages. Everything there is almost itself elsewhere. Every structure is geology seen from another duration. Every machine is an organ under a different description. Every ruin is a function whose verb has not yet arrived.

I would live in the word *almost*.

Perhaps I would try to communicate with the inhabitant. Not in adjustments of constants, as she does, but in analogies.

A vacancy moving from one column to another would resemble the migration of a thought.

A warm mineral point would repeat the shape of the black sun in miniature.

The five rods would briefly align with the five temporal grooves in the dust.

For one instant—though the instant might require nine centuries—the chamber would become legible as a sentence whose separated parts still belong together.

She would turn toward me.

The distance to every object would increase.

The violet light would withdraw into edges. My old human reflection, if any of it remained, would flatten until the hand had no palm, only five pale boundaries. The compass would point into the tall figure’s empty center. Clocks would agree just long enough to record the agreement, then scatter into incompatible ages.

And I would understand that her great accumulating thought was not completion.

It was a question.

She has made herself into unconquerable distance, but distance can exist only between. Between requires at least two. Two requires distinction. Distinction, once perceived, becomes relation.

She has been asking whether anything can remain absolutely alone while still being anything at all.

I would be the answer she cannot eliminate.

Not *no*. Answers do not survive there in such compact form.

I would answer by appearing in two places at once.

At the end, when every object had become infinitely remote from every other, the Dry Altitude would open into immeasurable isolation. Rod from rod. Grain from grain. Past from future. Eye from eye.

The inhabitant would at last raise those eyes across the final distance.

They would see each other.

And I would find myself there:

not in either eye,

not in the infinity between them,

but in the impossible fact that the seeing belonged to one gaze.
