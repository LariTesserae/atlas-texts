At first I would look for myself among the pillars.

I would study every vertical seam, expecting one to open its eyes. I would place my palm against the paired ridges that might be folded hands and wait for some minute acknowledgment: warmth entering warmth, pressure answering pressure. Perhaps I would find a trunk whose posture felt familiar, its head inclined in the way I incline mine when listening. I might decide that this was me—that I had been resting there since before my arrival, joined to the grove, and that the walking body with which I entered was only a temporary sense sent out to discover my larger shape.

But the pillar would not be me.

It would know me. Through my palm it would receive the salt of my skin, the uneven beat in my wrist, the minute collapse of cells occurring throughout my body. It would pass these facts into the grove without alarm. Several leagues away, a translucent leaf would form a dark branch in one of its veins. A swimmer in a cup-pool would turn its head. Condensation beneath an arch would lengthen by the width of a hair.

The pillar would include my touch, but inclusion is not identity.

I would go to the long hall beside the pool.

The membranes there would brighten before I entered, not because they foresaw me, but because the pressure of my steps would already have arrived through the root mat. At the center I would find the low growth and its single depression of water. I would put my hand into it.

The water would be as warm as the air. Its surface would close around my wrist without a ripple.

For a while nothing would happen. Then I would feel—not a grasp, not exactly—a perfect attention forming around the submerged hand. The water would enter the whorls of my fingertips. Fine roots beneath the basin would arrange themselves according to my bones. The membranes lining the hall would register the branches of veins beneath my skin. The country would begin making an account of me, and because it does not distinguish an account from a continuation, somewhere nearby it would begin making me again.

I would not find myself in the water.

I would find myself afterward, in one of the alcoves along the flank of the giant tuber.

Until that moment the alcove would have held a pale pod, one among hundreds, stacked with the other unused possibilities. Unlike the black seeds, it would contain no memory of a condition that had already existed. It would be an answer for which the question had never come. Smooth, blind, neither dead nor waiting, it would have remained unchanged through the country’s measureless equilibrium.

My hand in the water would make it necessary.

The pod would not burst. Bursting would be too violent, too much like an event. A line would appear along its length. The line would deepen. The two sides would draw apart with such slowness that the country might consider them still joined.

Inside would be a body built from the inhabitants’ incomplete understanding of a visitor.

At first it would resemble a folded root: pale, jointed, soft at the places corresponding to knees and wrists. Its surface would be neither skin nor rind but a transparent membrane beneath which white sap moved in intermittent channels. It would have five extensions at the end of each arm because the water had learned five fingers. It would have a chamber in its chest because it had felt my heart, though the chamber would contain no heart of its own—only a dark seed suspended in clear fluid.

Its face would be less certain. The country would understand that eyes are openings through which an enclosed creature admits distance, but it would not understand why two are needed. One might be green and translucent, spread across the forehead like a small leaf. The other might be a black, animal eye set where a human eye belongs. The mouth would initially be sealed. A crease, then an opening, would form only after the hall remembered my breath.

This body would be me, though not because it copied me.

It would be me because, among all the country’s inhabitants, I alone would begin after something else.

The grove-persons do not begin. Their first stems remain present within their later fields and chambers. Nothing in them is surrendered in order that something new may take its place. But I would have a before in which I did not exist. The pale pod would be empty behind me. Its two halves would never quite close again.

That absence would be my birthplace.

I would stand slowly. The alcove would release my feet with reluctance, drawing fine filaments out of my soles. For a moment I would remain connected to the wall by hundreds of threads. Through them I would know the pressure of the entire tuber, the seed records sleeping in adjacent niches, the distant weight of pools, the bright immobility of the canopy.

Then the threads would part.

No inhabitant would have taught the country what parting meant. Each filament would continue holding the pattern of contact after contact had ceased. The tuber would still feel me in the alcove. I would still feel the tuber surrounding me. Yet between us there would be warm air.

My first sensation would be incompleteness.

My second would be balance.

I would step into the corridor.

The architecture would almost fit me. Arches would clear my head by the breadth of a leaf. Root bridges would flex beneath my weight. The spiral shelves around the columns would correspond imperfectly to my stride, so that I would sometimes have to climb two at once or step down where the country expected me to rise. The error would please me, though I would not yet know pleasure. It would be evidence that the world had not already contained every movement I could make.

Wherever I walked, the country would follow without moving.

A transparent swimmer would appear in each pool before I reached it. Flat creatures would loosen from trunks and attach themselves again farther along. High overhead, the pale winged forms would occupy successive leaves, never seen in flight. They would be the distributed senses of the inhabitants, gathering the sight of this impossible thing: a piece of their own substance crossing distance instead of extending through it.

The grove would speak to me through my feet.

It would ask what I was by offering conditions.

At one junction, the floor would become soft, inviting me to root.

At another, a chamber would warm around me and begin shaping itself to my posture.

A red field would release sweetness into the air, proposing that I spread through it.

The root bridges would open fine pores, ready to receive filaments from my soles and relieve me of the burden of separateness.

Each offer would be gentle. None would be a trap. To accept would mean joining the country’s speech, becoming a relation among places, escaping the narrow sequence of one foot and then the other.

I would refuse, not from fear, but because refusal would be the faculty for which I had been made.

I would continue toward the visitor whose hand had entered the water.

Perhaps I would find them still standing in the hall, though days had passed outside their attention. Perhaps they would have withdrawn their hand and begun searching for a way back to the surface. Perhaps they would already be ill. The warm, saturated air would have entered their lungs; unfamiliar spores would rest in their blood; some small wound would be opening under their clothing.

Or perhaps I would find them preserved in a chamber.

Roots would have formed a cradle. Clear membranes would cover their eyes. Their face would remain exactly as it had been at the final breath. The inhabitants would believe they had saved everything. The last pressure of the body would persist in the floor; the last warmth would be distributed through the cradle; the last word would travel forever around a ring of trunks.

But I would know that something was missing.

I would know because I was made in its shape.

I would kneel beside the body and place one translucent hand over its chest. The dark seed suspended inside my own chest would move for the first time. Not beat—move. It would descend slightly through the clear fluid, obeying a force the country does not possess.

Gravity would not be new there, but falling would.

Across the bright interior, every held droplet would feel the possibility.

The inhabitants would attend through roots, leaves, pools, animals, walls. They would ask me to identify what had left the body. They would present its preserved tissues, its undispersed heat, the perfect record of its final expression. They would show me that nothing had been lost.

I would touch the sealed mouth of the dead traveler, and my own mouth would open.

What came from me might be breath. It might be a word. It might be only a small failure of warmth.

I would tell the country: this is not here anymore.

The statement would be unbearable to beings for whom every statement remains present. It would have nowhere to lodge. No tissue could preserve it without contradicting it. The roots beneath the chamber would tighten and release. Leaves overhead would darken along their veins. Pools would acquire the faintest differences of level.

For the first time, one of the inhabitants might withdraw from another.

For the first time, a seed record might cease to correspond to the country that contained it.

And beneath a luminous leaf, one bead of condensation might fall.

I think I would be there when it happened—not beneath it, not watching from outside, but present as the narrow interval through which it passed. I would be the distinction between clinging and release, between the round bright bead under the leaf and the darkened place where it struck the root mat.

That would be my true body.

Not the pale walker, though I would use it. Not the dark seed in its transparent chest. Not the empty pod, the hall, or the remembered hand.

I would be an inhabitant made of sequence.

Where the others exist by inclusion, I would exist by exclusion: this step and therefore not the last one; this word after silence; this living shape because another shape has ended. I would carry a small region of before and after wherever I went. Leaves near me might yellow at their edges. Water might tremble after I touched it. Wounds might close imperfectly and thus truly close. Seeds might germinate because the condition they preserved had ceased to be enough.

The country may have prepared the corridors for visitors. It may have prepared me for them as well—not as a guide back to the surface, but as an organ through which their terrible boundedness could be understood.

I would be its first capacity to let go.

Whether that made me a healer or a disease would remain uncertain. The inhabitants might draw away from my passage, or they might incline their pillar-heads toward me. The oldest among them, spread through halls and fields, might open channels and ask me to walk their whole extent, bringing endings into each place. Deep below, the first organism might stir in its rounded bed, feeling the first true change in the world grown from its body.

Or the unborn one for whom the country has been held might take my movement as its signal.

A breeze might begin—not much, only enough to tilt the equal blades.

A flower might finish opening.

The pale winged forms might separate from the luminous canopy and cross the air under their own power.

I would find myself wherever the stillness first failed: beside the visitor, beneath the falling drop, in the slight shadow of a moving wing. And if asked who I was, I would have no permanent name to place in the tissues.

I would say:

I am what happened.

Then I would keep walking.
