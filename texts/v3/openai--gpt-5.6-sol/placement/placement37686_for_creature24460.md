At first I look for myself in the obvious loneliness.

I am tempted by the dark shape above the plateau: the one moving thing granted distance, outline, and sky. From there the crowded stone might resolve into a pattern. The white glare might become navigable. I could pass over the central towers, follow the silver braids toward the valley, and disappear beyond the escarpment before the place closed around me.

But that creature belongs to elsewhere, even if it was born here. It has a horizon. It can leave.

I would not.

Nor would I stand on the highest rim in a body built for air, deafened by the falls and searching for a route down. Nothing in this world invites such a body. Uprightness would be a kind of error. Height would only increase exposure. Hands would find no task that the water had not already performed more patiently.

I would be lower.

In the central maze, where the towers stand so closely that the sky is reduced to a white seam, there is a corridor submerged to the depth of a knuckle. Water enters it through a split in the basalt, crosses a floor of fixed black pebbles, and slips beneath a pale overhanging shelf. The shelf appears solid, but its underside is terraced with minute rims. Some are mineral. Some were once shells. No useful distinction remains.

There, beyond sight from above, I would be attached headfirst into the flow.

I would be an old clinger, though age would not be something I knew as accumulation. I would know only the present distribution of pressure: six limbs bearing most of the current, four resting, three testing the lip of a groove ahead. Other limbs would be so deeply enclosed by mineral that they had not moved in an interval longer than many creatures’ histories. I would not miss them. The stone would hold where they once held. Their grip would have passed into the plateau.

My shell would be thick and smoke-pale, laid down first by my body and afterward by the water. Near the center it would remain translucent enough for the dark internal gathering to show. Around the edges it would be cloudy, laminated, and irregular. A white rim would have grown over one side, fixing me so closely to the ledge that a passing observer might decide I was already dead.

The observer would be wrong, but not entirely.

Part of me would be animal: scraping teeth, folded gut, brood chamber, nerve ring, the slow sequential thought of the feet.

Part would be colony. The cells inside me would take the basalt water and work upon what it carried, sustaining a life too slight to announce itself as hunger or fullness.

Part would be mineral that had no knowledge of me.

And part would be the shape of the current around my shell. Remove that current and the arrangement called *I* would contract into the dark knot and wait. Remove the stone and there would be no direction in which I could understand myself.

I would not hear the roar. The roar would be the condition beneath hearing, as silence once is for other beings. Each fall and drip would reach me through the joined rock, but their constancy would cancel them. I would perceive the plateau by what failed to repeat.

A pore narrowing upstream.

A pebble rotating in its hollow.

Three neighboring clingers tightening almost together.

The soft interruption caused by a body passing over the shelf.

Such things would arrive not as sounds but as alterations in the world’s grip. The ledge would become briefly different under every foot. My limbs would answer before the dark knot gathered the change into anything resembling awareness.

Perhaps this is what thought would be: not words, not images, but a sequence of adhesions.

Hold.

Test.

Hold more deeply.

Release one edge.

Wait.

The immense brightness would exist only as a pressureless pallor passing through my shell. If the winged creature crossed overhead, I might register a dimming, but it would mean less than a grain settling in a channel half a tower away. I would have no concept of wings. I would not imagine escape because I would not divide the world into confinement and freedom. There would be secured surfaces and unsecured ones. Nourishing flow and barren flow. Crevices that continued and openings that exposed.

My ledge would be larger than a country.

I might have traveled along it for centuries and advanced no farther than the width of a human palm. Behind me, any marks made by my teeth would have vanished beneath fresh precipitate. Ahead, the living film would remain invisible even to me. I would find it chemically, by touching the boundary where deep water first encountered the thin influence of air.

I would graze slowly enough that feeding and remaining still appeared identical.

Within the brood chamber I would carry a packet received from another clinger long ago. I would remember no meeting. The other might still be fixed several shell-lengths behind me, or it might already have become a rounded scale sealed into the shelf. Its cells would persist without history, held until the mineral ahead acquired the proper porosity.

There would be no urgency to release them. Urgency belongs to worlds where opportunities pass and do not return. Here the water tests every seam forever.

Yet one change would be approaching.

Not a storm. Not a flood. Nothing large enough to be visible from the rim.

Deep within the pale crust ahead, a passage the width of a cell would be opening. Water, diverted by the deposition of a few transparent grains, would begin to enter a chamber long sealed from the main flow. The change would travel through the ledge as a faint, uncustomary trembling.

My forward limbs would tighten.

The feet of my neighbors would tighten after mine.

For a long while, nothing else would happen.

Then I would lift the front edge of my shell.

To another creature it would be no movement at all. Water would not ripple. No shadow would shift. But for me it would be the relinquishing of a surface that had held me beyond memory. One gripping disk would soften. Its microscopic hooks would withdraw from pits they had helped deepen. The current would press beneath me, immediately immense.

Every remaining limb would clamp down.

I would wait until the flow became ordinary again.

Then another foot would release, reach into the newly wetted seam, and fasten.

In this way I would move toward the opening.

Not because I understood what waited there, but because the altered pressure would be the nearest thing my world possessed to a summons.

The chamber might contain food. It might contain a new mineral face on which to press the retained brood. It might be a dead end that would close around me, preserving shell and body together until neither could be separated from the terrace. All three possibilities would feel much the same at first: cool flow, unoccupied stone, a difference beneath the feet.

If the place proved suitable, I would leave the brood packet in the forming crust. I would seal it beneath a clear organic film. The water would armor it grain by grain.

Nothing would hatch in my presence.

I would continue onward, perhaps the length of one body, perhaps less. Behind me the passage would narrow. The eggs would lie without breathing, indistinguishable from droplets trapped in stone. They might remain that way long after my own limbs released.

One day—a day being only a human name for a brightness that never changes—the current would alter again. A thread of oxygenated water would reach them. One clear grain would soften from within.

Something with six gripping buds would unfold into a pore.

It would not know me. It would not inherit an image of my shell or the route of my crossing. Its inheritance would be more exact: clamp when pressure rises, advance beneath the ledge, seek the chemical edge, do not rise above the surface.

By then I might rest in a hollow nearby. The water would have shifted my empty shell only a little before wedging it among the white rims. Mineral would close over the smoke-gray layers. The hollow would become shallower by my thickness. A thread of runoff would divide around me and join again.

So I would be found in the center of the plateau, but never at its center in any grand sense. I would be beneath one shelf among innumerable shelves, inside the roar but below hearing, alive along a boundary too thin to appear on any map.

And I would be less a creature occupying the landscape than a small persistence within its persistence: a dark knot, many gripping feet, a shell the water was already turning back into stone.

My life would not interrupt the plateau’s final form.

It would be one of the ways that form continued.
