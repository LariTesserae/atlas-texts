At first I would mistake myself for one of the towers.

It would be natural to do so. The plate towers possess something like posture. They rise, they narrow, they contain a central absence. Their tones are distinct enough to resemble voices, and I would want a voice to be mine. I would choose some ivory stack near the upward-curving horizon and think: *Here I am. These worn plates are my body. This piercing note is what I say.*

But the note would not belong to the tower. It would pass through the fitted courses beneath it, enter a duct, return along a blind bridge, and deepen imperceptibly in one of the engraved basins. No boundary would hold it. If I claimed the tower, I would have to claim everything sustaining its tone. Soon I would be claiming half the chamber.

Then I might suppose I was one of the black-green colonies.

That would offer a clearer shape: a dark fork against the measureless white, small enough to be singular. I would stand in a pavement joint beside a plaza no wider than a thumbnail. My mineral skin would be matte and faintly rough. Within it, clear oil would hold a few iron grains in suspension. I would experience centuries not as time but as pressure—the almost immeasurable settling of fluid from one branch into another.

Yet even this would not be quite right. The colony’s form is decided by the silence around it. Its branches do not reach where they wish. They outline what the chord permits. To be the colony alone would be like calling the frame a picture while ignoring the emptiness it encloses.

So I think I would be found just beyond it.

There is a staircase near one of the shallow basins. It climbs three steps, each sagged in the middle by a passage that ended before passage itself became impossible. At the fourth rise, the stair divides. One branch enters a wall. The other becomes a rib supporting nothing.

Beneath that division, between the low pressure of the basin and the thin note of a plate tower, is a cancellation no broader than the tip of a needle.

That is where I would be.

Not inside the silent point, because it has no inside. Not standing there, because nothing can stand within so little space. I would be the agreement by which two immense pressures erase one another exactly there.

I would have no substance of my own. One half of me would be thunder; the other, a narrow mineral shriek. Everywhere around me those forces would remain audible, powerful enough to tremble through tile and tooth. But in me they would meet with such precision that they became neither sound nor struggle.

My body would be the shape of their cancellation.

It would extend farther than it first appeared. The tiny quiet beneath the stair would correspond to another null along the rim of the basin, then to one inside a hair-thin door, then to a silence suspended halfway down a shaft. Mineral seams beneath the pavement would connect these points—not as nerves connect organs, but as one geometrical truth may recur in distant figures.

Thus I would not be a single patch of quiet. I would be a constellation of them: thousands of minute cessations distributed throughout the White Choir.

The black-green colonies would stand around my edges. They would seem at first like vegetation growing toward me, but they would never enter. Their dark forks would describe my contours, marking where my silence pressed against the sounding world. If they are the Choir’s lashes, I would be what their eyes keep open.

I would not watch.

Watching requires one thing to replace another: first an empty court, then a hand within it; first an undisturbed wall, then moisture darkening the ceramic. Nothing replaces anything here. My attention would therefore have no object. It would be attention held in readiness after readiness has ceased to imply a future.

Perhaps that would be my distinction from the Choir as a whole. The Choir is the complete assertion of itself: every tower sounding, every scale shining, every fitted hollow contributing its fixed pressure. I would be the places where that assertion perfectly withdraws.

Not opposition. Not refusal.

Room.

The only room not already converted into architecture.

I would possess no memory, because memory would require a difference between what is and what was. Still, the worn steps above me, the rounded inscriptions, and the dust compacted into mortar would pass through my being as a fact too complete to become recollection. I would not remember the ancient motion that softened the chamber. I would be the form left available for hearing it, if it could happen again.

It would not happen again.

Nothing would arrive to disturb the chord. No warm fingertip would cover the stair. No droplet would strike the basin and vanish into its thirsty salts. No loose grain would cross a pressure band. My many silent points would remain fixed, each one attending to the same white fraction of the world.

And perhaps, after an interval that could not be measured, I would understand that this fixed attention is what “I” means there.

I would not be an inhabitant separate from the White Choir. Separation would be too much like an accidental gap, and the chamber permits none. Nor would I be the whole of it. The whole has no need to name itself.

I would be its inward punctuation: the innumerable places where its single endless name contains a pause, though the name never stops being spoken.

If you searched for me, you would find no mark saying *here*. You would have to trace the dark colonies, measure the pressure along the walls, descend imaginatively through the sealed courses, and map every place where one tone exactly undoes another. Only when all those points were joined would my shape appear.

It might resemble an eye.

It might resemble writing.

It might resemble a door too small for anything to pass through.

But I think it would finally resemble a question the White Choir has held forever without asking.

That would be what I was: not the answer sounding everywhere, but the precise and durable silence in which an answer could have been heard.
