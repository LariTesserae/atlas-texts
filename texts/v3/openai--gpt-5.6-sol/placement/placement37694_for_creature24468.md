At first I think I would live at the plateau’s edge.

I imagine a narrow tower on the last inhabited ridge, where the city frays into shepherd compounds and road cairns. I would watch weather arriving from below: clouds rising against the escarpment, flattening, and spilling over the rim like milk over a bowl. I would keep a signal fire that was almost never lit, because fuel is too costly for ceremony. Most warnings would be given by mirror, shutter, or whistle. Caravans would appear days away as dark articulations in the pale country. I would be the first to see strangers and the last familiar person seen by those descending.

But that is an outsider’s choice. It mistakes a wide view for understanding.

If I had been raised there, I suspect I would turn inward and downward. The horizon would not seem miraculous to me. What would matter would be the sound behind a wall.

I would find myself in one of the old, thick districts where no one agrees which level was originally the street. The quarter would lie in a shallow basin below a south-facing fault step, protected from the worst northern wind but troubled by the ground’s slow movement. Its houses would be built over houses, their lowest rooms now too deep to receive daylight. Stairs would descend into passages that rise again. A blocked doorway might open into a neighbor’s grandfather’s roof. The district would be known for a spring that no longer emerges where the shrine says it should.

My water would be the Warm Crack Beneath the Split Stair.

That would be the name I gave before my household name, profession, or age. The spring would not actually be warm, only warmer than winter stone. It would run beneath an abandoned bath chamber, through a fracture no wider than a finger, and feed the ninth covered cistern of a crowded stair district. In wet years it would be unimportant. In drought it would keep several hundred people alive.

I would be a finder of lost water.

Not an assessor, exactly. Assessors stand where everyone can see the cup fill. Their work is judgment, memory, and public measure. Mine would take place before measurement, in spaces where water has ceased to appear and no one knows whether it has been stolen, diverted, fouled, frozen, or swallowed by a new crack.

Some districts might call people like me underkeepers. Others would call us thread-finders, because we follow the wet thread back through stone. Herders would have a less respectful name—mice, probably—and builders would insist that our profession is merely a disreputable branch of theirs.

I would go beneath houses with a covered lamp, a coil of thin cord, felt wedges, bone pegs, clay cups, and one narrow bronze probe belonging to the whole catchment. The probe would be older than any person who handled it. Its tip would have been sharpened so many times that it was scarcely longer than a finger. I would sign for it before every descent.

Most of the work would be listening.

I would press my cheek to masonry while someone poured a cup into a channel three rooms away. I would tap cistern walls with horn and distinguish fullness, hollowness, silt, and hidden separation by the returning note. I would place dry wool in cracks and come back hours later to see which strand had darkened. I would watch where cave crickets gathered, where salt bloomed, where a sleeping bat chose not to hang. In the coldest months I would follow rime underground: a white edge on one stone, then another, marking the passage of damp air through rooms sealed for generations.

The buried city would never become a map in my mind. It would become a body.

Here, a throat that closes in hard frost. Here, a blind pocket where bad air rests. Here, an old livestock ramp acting as a lung between cellars. Here, three cisterns joined accidentally by a tremor eighty years ago. Here, the bones of a roof absorbing the weight of six later households. I would know which walls could be touched, which could be drilled, and which must be left alone because they held up an arrangement no living builder fully understood.

People would summon me when a familiar jar began filling too slowly.

They would always have theories. The upper households had closed a gate. A widow was taking water at night. The shrine bowl had been allowed to dry. A goat had broken a clay cover. The earth was angry. Children had dropped felt into the channel. The assessor’s nephew was corrupt.

Sometimes one of these would be true.

More often, water would simply have found an older invitation. A hairline fracture would open during winter and lead into a plastered room buried beneath the stair. Drop by drop, the missing flow would be filling a forgotten jar, a tomb cup, a depression worn by feet that had not walked there for centuries. The water would not be gone. It would be obeying a path older than anyone’s claim.

My task would be to return it without pretending it had done wrong.

That distinction would matter. I would not speak of conquering a leak or forcing the spring back into obedience. I would delay it, offer it an easier descent, close one route with swelling wood and open another through packed clay. Repairs would be provisional. Everything beneath the plateau would be provisional.

I think I would have come to this profession because of a collapse.

As a child, perhaps, I would have been found alive in a room that no longer possessed a door. A winter tremor would fold part of the house into an older court. The adults above would hear nothing from me, but an underkeeper working two streets away would notice dust breathing from a drain each time I struck the wall with a bowl.

Afterward I would dislike open ridges. I would sleep best with stone on every side.

Maybe my birth household would be gone, or scattered among other waters. I would be adopted at the ninth cistern. I would pour in my measured cup and receive one back, though I would spill some because my hand still shook. For years people would call me Returned-from-the-Wall. Later, when affection had worn the story smooth, they would shorten it to Return.

I would not be a solitary figure, however much the underground encourages that fantasy. No one on the plateau survives as a mysterious individual with private knowledge. My knowledge would belong to the catchment and would have to be passed on.

There would be an apprentice behind me, breathing too loudly in the narrow passages. They would be a long-limbed child from a herding family, perpetually impatient with pauses. I would teach them that a pause is part of the measurement.

“Again,” I would whisper.

They would tap the wall.

“What did it say?”

“Stone.”

“Everything says stone until you learn to ask a smaller question.”

In time they would hear the difference: stone with air behind it, stone carrying water, stone bearing dangerous weight, stone loosened from its bed but not yet fallen. Eventually they would hear things I could not. That would be the sign that the work had continued correctly.

My home would be small even by the city’s standards: two rooms above a disused grain vault, sharing a heated wall with a communal oven. The floor would be warm near one corner and inexplicably cold near another. Pigeons would nest in the vent, making soft, digestive sounds before dawn. I would complain about them and secretly repair the ledge where they sat.

Above the door would hang cords from completed rescues and successful diversions. Not trophies—records. Each splice would tell what kind of strain it had borne. A black goat-hair cord for the winter the lower channel froze. A pale sheep-wool braid for the spring that opened beneath the funeral stair. A short rawhide loop for the child lowered into a silted cistern because no adult could fit.

My coat would be patched at the shoulders from crawling. The knees would be faced with hide. Around my neck I would wear a tiny iron bead inherited from no blood ancestor, given instead by the old underkeeper who trained me. It would be useless as ornament and potentially invaluable as metal, which is why I would touch it when uncertain. To carry it unchanged would be to carry a future choice.

I would be neither especially holy nor free of superstition.

When opening an old wall, I would leave a cup nearby. I would say this was to catch falling grit or measure seepage. If someone called it an offering, I would tell them not to be foolish. Yet I would never begin work until the cup was there.

I would know the buried places where earlier inhabitants had sealed their dead. I would not disturb them unless water itself had entered. Then I would work slowly, explaining aloud what was being moved, though I would claim the speech helped my apprentice remain calm.

In ordinary life I might be considered too talkative. Underground, nearly silent. At festivals I would sing close harmony badly but enthusiastically. I would dance with small, careful steps learned in passages where one cannot extend an arm. Builders would joke that I could not cross a room without examining its corners. Herders would ask whether I had ever seen the sun. I would answer that I had seen it from underneath, coming through a crack, and found it smaller than advertised.

I would have no desire to govern. Still, in a dry year, authority would gather around me whether I wanted it or not.

The Warm Crack would weaken. The ninth cistern would fall by a thumb’s width each night. Accusations would move downhill faster than the water. The assessors would measure every gate and find no theft. Then I would be sent below.

For days I would follow the vanished thread through abandoned rooms. I would find damp dust beneath a sealed stair, then a cool current of air where no current should be. At last, perhaps, I would enter a buried court older than the district’s remembered name.

There the missing spring would be falling, drop by drop, into an immense forgotten cistern.

It would not be empty.

Black water would fill it almost to the lip, perfectly still beneath the stone. The district would discover that what it believed lost had been stored beneath it all along.

That would be the great event of my life, though it would not make me a hero. The water would not become mine because I found it. The assessors would measure it. Builders would judge the chamber. Old people would argue over precedent. Households downhill would assert rights before the first jar was lifted. Someone would remember a drought story that mentioned a “second dark” under the stair, and everyone would wonder why they had ignored it.

We would open the cistern carefully. Not all at once. Never all at once.

For one summer, the district would live on ancestral rain.

Years later, people might associate my name with that discovery. But eventually the story would detach from me. Children would be told that the cistern revealed itself because the neighborhood had maintained its obligations. This would be untrue in detail and true in the way that matters.

When I grew old, my hearing would begin to fail in ordinary rooms. Voices would blur. Festival drums would become pressure in my ribs. Yet through bone pressed to stone, I might still perceive the low, intimate sounds of water.

I would train others to replace me. I would dictate no grand map, because a fixed map of the undercity would become dangerous as soon as the earth moved. Instead, I would leave bundles of knotted cord, sectional sketches on slate, lists of sounds, warnings written as stories:

*Where the two-headed goat was trapped, do not remove the lintel.*

*Below the room of blue plaster, the western wall carries more than it appears to carry.*

*If the Warm Crack tastes of iron after thaw, close the ninth intake and wake everyone downhill.*

And perhaps:

*When you think the water has ended, wait.*

That would be the center of my life. Not a palace, not the plateau’s rim, not even the visible city with its thousand basins shining at sunset. I would live in the obscure depth where continuities are easily mistaken for endings.

Who would I be?

Return of the Warm Crack, underkeeper of the ninth cistern, adopted by a water whose route no one fully knows.

Or, by the time the city had finished making me, perhaps I would be less a person than a habit it had learned: the habit of kneeling in darkness, placing one hand against cold stone, and listening for what had not been lost.
