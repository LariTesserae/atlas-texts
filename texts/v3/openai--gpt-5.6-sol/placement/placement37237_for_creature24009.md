I would not be among the bats, though at first their multitude would seem the only possible body to enter. I would feel the temptation of becoming colony: to sleep as pressure among pressures, to know dusk as a message passed through ribs, to be drawn through the fissure like one dark thread through the eye of a needle.

But I do not think I would belong to the great exhalation.

I would be lower down, where the ceiling’s life becomes the floor’s material. Not in the deepest guano, where larvae advance blindly through old abundance, and not at the entrance, where crickets can still imagine an outside. I would keep to the interface between basalt and remains: beneath a curled black flake loosened from the wall, in a space no deeper than the thickness of a claw.

I would be a pseudoscorpion.

Small enough for a bat’s fallen hair to be an obstacle. Dark amber beneath the dust, perhaps nearly translucent at the joints. No tail, no grand sting—only the disproportionate pincers held before me, making my minute body look like an argument the darkness has raised against itself.

The basalt flake above me would be my ceiling beneath the greater ceiling. Its lower surface would retain a trace of the day’s heat. Around its edges would lie a littoral of fragments: mite husks, beetle bristles, grains of guano, sulfur dust, a splinter of wing membrane drawn tight as old varnish. Beyond that border, the floor would open into distances measured in finger-widths.

I would not know the chamber as a chamber. I would know interrupted surfaces.

A bone would be a pale ridge that changes the movement of air. A bat dropping would be shelter, food for prey, and eventually terrain. A dermestid larva passing nearby would be a bristling bulk, too large to seize, its hairs scraping through the substrate like dead brush dragged over stone. Mites would be the true quick animals of my world—soft-bodied points of urgency, appearing at the edge of touch.

I would wait for them.

Waiting would not be emptiness. Through my legs I would receive the whole seam in translation. The quarrel of bats overhead would arrive as scattered impacts. A pup falling would be catastrophe before it became carrion: a single enormous concussion, followed by the rearrangement of grains. Emergence at dusk would become weather. Thousands of claws releasing from basalt, thousands of wings striking confined air—the floor trembling so continuously that hunting would become impossible. I would fold closer beneath my flake and hold still while the world departed above me.

The quarry would be something older than explanation. It would have no location, only recurrence. A low force entering every surface at once. At the drill’s first pulse, I would draw in my pincers. Dust would sift from the edge of the flake. The narrow roof over me might settle by less than the width of a mite, yet that fraction would alter my country: close one passage, expose another, crush a clutch of eggs, reveal a preserved scrap of skin.

When the vibration stopped, I would extend myself again.

Perhaps I would be nearly blind. Sight would offer little here. The seam would disclose itself by contact, scent, heat and disturbance. I would know a living mite by the irregularity of its movement; a falling grain by its clean impact; a parasitic fly by the rapid hooked percussion of its feet. I would know the cooling of evening not as sunset but as a slight contraction in the stone and a current carrying new odors inward—dust from the plateau, bat urine, hot fur, the faint dry trace of insects caught beyond the fissure.

I might sometimes climb onto a beetle.

Not to feed, but to travel: pincers fastened to a leg or hardened wing case while the larger animal carried me across the impossible openness of the guano bank. For a while its body would become moving ground. Beneath us would pass bones, shed larval skins, valleys of powder. Then I would release near another basalt shelf and inherit a territory I could never have reached alone.

There I would resume the posture of my kind: claws lifted, body still, waiting at the mouth of a crack.

I would live between scales—not fully among the colony and not fully among the decomposers, but at the point where their lives fragment into prey. I would hunt the creatures that feed on what falls. I would shelter beneath stone that is itself falling by degrees. My pincers would echo, absurdly and exactly, the quarry’s machinery beyond the ridge: two tiny tools opening and closing in a natural factory that has no blueprint.

No bat would know I existed. No tremor would distinguish me from grit. If I died beneath the flake, mites and larvae would reduce me until my armor became another reddish fleck among the iron stains.

That is where I would find myself: under a loosened scale of basalt, one level of darkness beneath another, holding my claws open over a path no wider than a vein.

Not the seam’s voice, nor its blood, nor its great winged breath.

One of its smallest pieces of punctuation—  
a pair of waiting commas  
in the long, crowded sentence of consumption.
