At first I might be one of the things arriving.

A larva, nearly without weight, entering through the main fissure among shell powder and the remains of a jelly. I would have no knowledge of an outside, only a sudden change in the water: basalt close on either side, turbulence breaking into small intelligible streams, chemical traces multiplying until the dark seemed crowded before I touched anything.

But I do not think I would survive as an arrival. I would be taken in the first avenue by a shrimp’s basket, or adhere to the wrong film and be grazed away. I would become part of the city without ever inhabiting it.

Nor would I be the anemone. It is too much a center. Even without ruling, it makes the lives around it answer the scale of its opening and closing. I would not be so visible in consequence.

I think you would find me lower down, but not in the deepest brine: in the middle district where the mussel wall begins to break into shell rubble and the floor falls toward the rear basin. Above me, brittle-star arms make and unmake a ceiling. Beside me stands a sponge tower with one side torn away, its exposed canals occupied by animals smaller than silt. Behind it, wedged at an angle in the basalt, is half an urchin test. Bryozoans have sealed most of its openings. One remains.

That opening is where I withdraw.

I am one of the blind, long-legged crabs, though a small one—not yet broad enough to patrol the widest lanes without being displaced by the current. My shell is thin and colorless at the joints. Mineral stains have darkened the older plates of my back. One hind leg lacks its final segment, lost to the anemone or an octopus arm; there is no memory of the event, only a shortened sequence of touches on that side.

I do not know the city as a city.

I know that leaving the urchin chamber requires turning sideways. I know the smooth inner curve where nothing grows because my leg brushes it each time I pass. Then come four sharp contacts: worm tube, worm tube, broken shell, sponge.

Beyond the sponge is open water.

Not open in any large sense. Three body lengths, perhaps four. But no surface answers my tapping legs there, and so I cross quickly, holding myself low. The current presses mineral taste through the joints of my limbs. If it is carrying food, the shrimp will already have gathered upstream. Their antennae strike mine before their bodies do. If it is carrying brine, the floor loses oxygen and the mussels close in a dark sequence along the wall.

My world is made of such sequences.

Ridged shell, soft colony, empty tube.

Sharp stone, mucus film, anemone flesh—withdraw.

Three mussels, a gap, a dead hinge crowded with amphipods.

I tap every entrance because an entrance is never the same thing twice. A tube may contain its maker, an egg mass, a worm eating the egg mass, or nothing but water. An empty shell may be empty only at the rim. I reach in with one narrow claw. Sometimes I draw out edible film. Sometimes another claw closes on mine. Sometimes the doorway itself recoils because what I took for a wall is alive.

Sight would be of little use. The city changes most where outlines fail.

I carry it instead as expectation: after this texture, that one; after the constriction, the downward step; beyond the mussel with the cracked lip, a place where organic fragments collect. When a sponge thickens across a passage, I stop there for a long time. My legs search the new surface. The old route persists in my body although it no longer exists in the stone.

Then I go another way.

That hesitation is perhaps the closest thing I possess to thought.

During the feeding drift, I climb. I wedge my rear legs into the bryozoan crust and extend the others into the passing debris. I am not a filter feeder, but abundance makes poor distinctions. A strip of flesh catches against a worm chimney. I pull until it tears. Shrimp arrive at once. Amphipods come from inside the mussel wall. We dismantle the fragment together without cooperation, each taking what can be held.

When the octopus arm enters, I become architecture.

My legs fold along the angles of my body. The narrow claws cross like broken tubes. I cease tapping. I cease feeding. I suppress every movement not required to cling.

A sucker passes over the lane.

It tastes mussel shell, sponge, worm masonry, the mineral crust on my back. For a moment it rests against me. I am not hidden; there is nowhere to hide. I am merely included among things that do not answer.

The arm moves on.

Later, beneath the urchin vault, I clean myself joint by joint. An ostracod flashes where my claw disturbs it. Green light passes through my legs and is gone before any eye could use it. For that instant my shadow joins the ribs of the dead urchin, and there is no clear boundary between the animal sheltering and the shelter.

I would brood there, if I lived long enough.

The eggs would be carried under my abdomen, washed continually by movements of the small rear limbs. They would begin as a weight and become many minute movements answering none of mine. Near hatching, dark points would turn inside them. I would keep to the narrower lanes, not from tenderness but because the body bearing eggs seeks what does not tear them away.

Some would be eaten as they emerged. Some would pass through the fissure and vanish into the black water. One might settle elsewhere in the chamber and grow without ever encountering me again. There would be no family district, no recognition. Only similar legs learning different sequences of contact.

In time, something would find the soft division beneath my shell.

Perhaps I would fail to harden after a molt. Perhaps the head-down fish would detect the pressure of my crossing. Perhaps brine would remain too long in the lower streets. My legs would release the wall one by one.

I would not leave the city.

Amphipods would enter first, through the mouth and between the plates. A ribbon worm might fold itself inside the emptied carapace. Bacteria would soften what remained. My long legs, so useful as a map, would break at their joints and collect against the sponge tower like pale sticks.

The backplate might endure.

Sediment would fill it. Bryozoans would spread across the outer surface, making small adjacent chambers. A hydroid would root on the mineral stain. Shrimp would stand behind it where the current divided cleanly. Eventually a worm tube would rise from what had been the hollow beneath my heart.

Then the route I knew would pass through me.

The crab that came after would tap the place: sponge, tube, shell, open water. It would not know that one of those contacts had once moved, or that the arrangement of its street included the body of an animal that had learned an earlier city.

So that is where I would be found: at first in the lower-middle lanes, touching every threshold; later beneath the feet of something that does the same.

I would be a blind crab, yes, but only temporarily.

More exactly, I would be a route carried in a body until the body became part of the route.
