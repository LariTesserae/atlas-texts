At first I imagine myself among the ledge-walkers.

It is the obvious place to put a stranger: out on the margin, tied to the town by a rope, looking both toward it and away. I see bare feet finding flaws in the violet rock, a loop of fiber tightening at my waist, the roofs above reduced to a jagged rim of faces and thorns. I would gather swift eggs and salt crust, climb down after a goat whose bell had stopped answering, and sleep with my hands aching from the day’s grip.

But I do not think I would possess their calm. The ledge-walkers trust the rope because they have inspected it. They trust their feet because they know exactly where each one is. I would be too conscious of the depth. I would spend myself imagining the fall.

So I move inward.

Not far inward. There is no far inward here.

I find a house in one of the lowest lanes, where the paving is warm even before dawn and the walls pass so close that two people meeting must turn shoulder to shoulder. The house has no single shape. Its sleeping room belongs to one tier, its cooking shelf to another, and its garden is distributed across the roofs of three neighbors. A thorn trunk enters through one wall and leaves through the ceiling. A pillar plant older than the household rises beside the stair, held upright by a forked brace polished with generations of hands.

The fissure under the sleeping room gives off a steady heat and, on certain nights, a faint knocking.

This is where I live.

I am not born to the household. I arrive as a child after a wall elsewhere is judged too weak to support another sleeping mat. My mother gives me her bone pin, my father gives me the short name **Ket**, but my daily life belongs to the people around this crack: an old gardener who smells of pepper resin, two sisters who tend goats, a widower who makes rope, three children of uncertain relation, and the great pillar plant called Senn.

Senn is not named for a person. The name means something like *the one who keeps a place by occupying it*.

As a child I dislike Senn. Its spines catch my wraps. Its roots buckle the floor. One of its arms blocks the easiest route to the upper ledge, so I must climb around it carrying meal bowls in both hands. During its brief flowering, moths fill the room and beat against my face while I sleep.

Later I understand that one thick root has grown beneath the outer wall and slowed its leaning. If Senn dies, part of the house may follow.

I become attentive to such things.

Not plants alone, though I learn the plants as everyone does. I become interested in pressures: the way a roof beam bows differently when seed baskets are full; the slight lift in a floor slab when the thorn tree swells after fruiting; the difference between a harmless night-tick and the soft interior crumble that means plaster has separated from stone.

I am the child who lies awake when others sleep, one cheek against the warm floor.

The mountain is never silent from there. Beetles scrape below the slabs. Roots contract. Goat hooves strike ledges beyond the wall. Someone turns in sleep two rooms away, and the motion passes through beam, plaster, thorn trunk, brace, and paving before reaching my jaw. Deeper still are the slower sounds: a pressure easing, a grain breaking free, a knock so far beneath us that it seems less heard than remembered.

The old people tell me the mountain dreams downward.

I begin to think structures dream sideways. A weight placed in one room is felt in another. A wall remembers every roof raised above it. Nothing stands where it appears to stand.

A Rememberer of Weight notices me listening.

She is called Or. She has clouded eyes and two missing fingers. When she walks, she taps walls with the back of one knuckle, never hard enough to damage plaster. She does not ask whether I wish to apprentice. One afternoon she tells me to carry her mat to the house of the blue lintel.

There, a family wants to cut an opening between two rooms. The opening would spare them a climb over the roof whenever they bring food to an elderly sleeper.

To me the wall looks thick and generous.

Or lies down on the floor. She puts her ear to the stone and orders everyone above us to walk in turn. A child crosses. Then a heavy man. Then two people carrying a grinding mortar. Or listens. She makes me listen.

At first I hear only feet.

Then I hear the delay: step, tremor, a dry answering sound from somewhere behind the wall.

The wall is not holding up the roof. The wall is keeping the old thornwood beam from rolling in its socket. Cut the doorway and the beam will turn. The planted ledge above will tilt. Its soil will press against the neighboring parapet. The parapet, already bowed, may open toward the cliff.

The family does not cut the wall. Instead they widen a ladder notch and move the elderly sleeper one level higher.

This is how I become a Rememberer—not through a ceremony, but by being sent again and again to fetch the mat.

Years pass in fractions of lean.

I learn the town not as lanes and houses but as a body of transferred burdens. I know which roof rests partly on a dead tree buried inside a wall. I know where three homes depend on a single basalt slab no wider than my forearm. I know that a certain stair may safely hold four children or two adults, but not two adults carrying hot stones. I know which bridges must be emptied when the east wind begins and which can be loaded deliberately to keep their ropes from thrashing.

People think I remember stone. In truth, I remember arrangements.

The stone is rarely the danger. The stone is rounded past breaking. It is the living things, the repaired things, the ingenious things that change: roots thickening, hides shrinking, fiber stretching, households adding one more ledge because a child has become too tall to sleep crosswise.

I am called when someone wants more room. I am called when a floor develops a new note. I am called after births, deaths, fires, unusually heavy fruiting, and marriages that bring roof rights into dispute.

I carry no written plan. No plan could show what matters. I remember by touch and by story.

This lintel carries the weight of the room where Vek died.

This brace was once the axle of a trade sledge.

Do not remove the black stones beside Senn, though they appear loose.

Three people may sleep above the moth garden in dry weather. During flowering, only two.

When I grow older, my own name becomes less important. People call me Deep-Ear, sometimes kindly and sometimes because they are impatient with how long I listen. Children imitate me by pressing their faces to the paving and announcing that the mountain has forbidden chores.

I let them mock me. It teaches them to listen.

I do not live austerely. I like sour fruit more than I should. I gamble on beetle races and lose small bone counters. I tune the seed pods above the rope-maker’s bed to whisper obscenities whenever the night wind shifts. Once, when a lowland trader asks which building belongs to the council, I tell him the entire town does, but only on alternating afternoons. He believes me and spends days trying to learn the schedule.

My great fear is not falling. It is forgetting.

Near the end of my life, I begin taking children through the lanes after dark. Sight distracts them. I place their palms on walls and have people walk overhead. I teach them the town through vibration.

Here is a hollow chamber plastered over before my grandmother’s birth.

Here the pillar plant bears more weight than the beam.

Here the wall that seems crooked is correct; the straight wall beside it is failing.

Here is where the summit itself rises close beneath the paving, too hard to cut.

Here, listen: this house and the next house are one house, whatever their occupants claim.

When I die, they will lay me on the central stone. While stories are told, someone will rebind my sandals. Someone will sharpen my listening knuckle into a small tool or bead. The mat I carried for Or will be patched again and given to another Rememberer.

Perhaps one of the children will repeat my warnings correctly. Perhaps not. A warning preserved too long can become its own danger, because roots move, ropes slacken, walls settle, and the town is never precisely what it was when the warning was made.

So I would leave them no law.

Only a blessing:

May your bindings hold.

May you know what bears you.

May you never mistake stillness for the absence of change.

And if some part of me remains in the town, I do not think it would be my name. It would be a habit: someone pausing before cutting a wall, kneeling on the warm floor, and placing an ear against the stone while the crowded life above goes on walking.
