At first I think I would live near the black basin.

That is the obvious answer, and therefore probably wrong. The basin draws the imagination because it refuses everything: ripple, dust, explanation. I picture myself in one of the roofless rooms around it, wrapped in gray felt, making arcs on chalk strips and watching the water remain unchanged.

But I would not be good at watching what does not change. I would begin inventing changes. I would mistake a moth’s reflected wing for a darkening in the water, or become certain that the brightness had shifted by the width of a hair. The basin keepers must possess a humility more severe than patience. They must be able to report nothing without secretly turning nothing into an event.

I would drift outward.

Not far. No one in the hollow is far from anything.

I would find myself one level above the principal avenue, beneath a footbridge where clubmoss hangs thickly enough to brush the heads of passersby. My rooms would not have been intended as rooms. One would be the triangular remainder between a leaning house and the limestone wall; the other, reached through a split in the first, would occupy the space above an old cistern stair. I could lie straight in neither. My bed would curve around a chalky outcrop that remained cold even through six layers of moss matting.

The bridge overhead would tremble all day with traffic too soft to hear.

That trembling would become my work.

I would be one of those who listens through the hands—not to the roof birds alone, but to buildings. A settling-reader. A keeper of pressures.

There may be a formal name for the craft, but it would not translate cleanly. It would mean something like *the one who notices where weight has begun to remember another direction*.

Each morning, if morning can be said to exist there, I would unwrap my wrists and press both palms against the inner wall. The city would arrive through stone and root as a field of minute differences. A ladder taking a child’s weight. A storage basket newly filled with wet fern starch. Someone crossing a bridge with a limp. Willow roots thickening behind plaster. A lintel giving up one grain of powdered lime. Water collecting where yesterday there had been only damp.

Most of these movements would require nothing. The first lesson of my trade would be that noticing is not permission to interfere.

I would be summoned by cords stretched low across alleys: a gray feather, two chalk beads, a knot kept deliberately loose. Not danger. Not yet. Only concern.

I would arrive with wedges of antler, scraps of felt, damp string, a bone folder, and several limestone flakes carried for years because their shapes seemed promising. I would touch the wall, then the adjoining wall, then the roof beam, then the floor of the household above. I would ask everyone to remain where they usually sat. Structures behave differently when made to perform for an expert.

Sometimes the repair would be a sliver of stone inserted beneath a beam.

Sometimes a root would need to be guided into a crack and fed moisture for three turnings of the light.

Sometimes the household would have to move its jar of black lichen paste from one shelf to another.

Sometimes I would say, with both hands, that the wall had found a better posture and should be allowed to lean.

I think I would be respected, but not celebrated. A celebrated joiner might begin making repairs worthy of celebration, which would be dangerous. The best evidence of my work would be that no one remembered which piece I had added.

My everyday name would begin as a gesture made with one finger bending toward the palm. Perhaps my first footprint had shown a curled toe, or perhaps as an infant I had gripped people’s sleeves and refused to release them. Later the sign would change. The finger would bend, pause, and shift slightly sideways. In age, when my hands trembled, the tremor would become part of it: not an imperfection in the name, but its final precision.

Children would shorten it impolitely. Friends would make it with both hands when teasing me, as though the whole city were leaning and I were trying to hold it up.

I would have a bad habit of collecting objects whose purposes had expired.

Not precious things. A hook warped by too many years of tension. Half a shell cup with no sound edge. A message cord bleached until its color could no longer be read. A tiny antler wedge removed from a doorway after the doorway settled beyond needing it.

I would keep these in the wall niches of my crooked rooms. This would annoy my neighbors because every niche belongs partly to those who can reach it, and uselessness is not a private matter. When pressed, I would claim the pieces were awaiting reassignment.

Some would wait longer than I lived.

That habit would lead me to another duty, less official than my trade.

I would tend the signs of those who had gone above and not returned.

The city already keeps their hooks, inward-turned cups, and knots meaning *not yet*. But centuries are hard on uncertainty. Cords decay. Hooks are borrowed in emergencies and not replaced. New households inherit an old sign and, not knowing it, hang drying fungus from it. Absence slowly becomes ordinary storage.

I would notice.

Not because I knew the missing people. Almost none would belong to my generation. I would know only the shapes left around them: a worn patch no hand now used, a shallow socket where a cup had rested facing the wall, a bridge knot tied in a grammar spoken before the current arrangement of streets.

I would not preserve these signs exactly. Exact preservation would be another kind of death. I would translate them into whatever the city had become.

An old hook might be removed before it failed and its curve copied in willow root.

A vanished cord might become three pale stones pressed into a parapet.

A cup turned inward might finally crack and be replaced by a cistern shell whose spiral opened toward the limestone.

The meaning would remain: *A return has not been ruled out.*

People would sometimes ask how long such a sign should be kept.

I would answer by placing one hand against the nearest wall.

This would frustrate them, especially the young.

Perhaps that is why I would be asked, late in life, to spend a season at the black basin after all.

Not as a keeper of the water. I would sit beside the keepers and attend to the roofless buildings around them. Their walls are old, buried in moss, and made strong by arrangements that no living joiner fully understands. I would examine the places where streets arrive at the center. I would polish no stone and clear no fern. I would simply learn how the city distributes itself around the dark bowl.

There I might discover that the basin is not the hollow’s heart.

A heart pushes. It sends urgency outward.

The basin does nothing of the kind. It is the place around which urgency loses its force. Every street descends toward it, but no street ends there; footfalls turn aside, divide, climb again. The basin is less an organ than a withheld answer, and the city has built itself around the freedom of not receiving one.

I would be very old by then. My pelt would have gone patchy and white. Mineral pigments would stain the lines of my palms. I would climb with three feet: two broad ones and a cane made from a bird-bent willow stem.

Infants would be placed briefly in my lap because my body, once too cold to carry them safely, would have acquired the steady warmth of age. I would show them walls. Babies understand walls before adults do. They press their whole faces against them.

When I died, I would not want a bone placed beside the basin. Wanting that would feel too much like requesting an answer.

I would remain near my doorway while the moss took hold. Neighbors would touch my name against their chests when they passed. My collection of obsolete pieces would be sorted. Most would immediately reveal uses I had failed to see. The warped hook might secure a cradle. The half-shell might carry medicinal paste. The bleached cord might wick a week’s condensation into a bed of fern spores.

My bones would be cleaned and divided.

The wide bones of my feet would make excellent pressure plates beneath a threshold. Finger bones could become toggles for message cords. A section of forearm might be fitted inside the bridge above my old rooms, strengthening the rail where generations had leaned to look down into the avenue.

But I think the city would use my ribs best.

They would be set, one by one, into the leaning wall I had greeted every day. Not displayed. Buried among limestone flakes, root fiber, and old mortar. Their curve would fill a troublesome hollow without forcing it straight.

For a while, those passing overhead might feel the bridge tremble differently.

Then moss would thicken. The wall would settle. The difference would become part of the ordinary structure, impossible to isolate from the work of dead joiners, growing roots, patient stone, and the weight of households still awake behind their rounded doors.

So that is where I would find myself in this world:

not at the center, though near enough to descend to it;

not entirely a craftsperson, though my hands would know the speech of walls;

not a keeper of what is lost, but a translator of the shapes that loss leaves behind.

And eventually, if I had lived properly, I would be difficult to distinguish from what I had helped to make.

Someone would rest a palm against the parapet.

Beneath limestone, beneath moss, beneath the minute pressure of their hand, one of my ribs would take a little of their weight.

The wall would hold.

No one would need to know why.
