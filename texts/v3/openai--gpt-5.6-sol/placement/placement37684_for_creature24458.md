At first I would want to say I worked the lifts.

There is dignity in the brake rooms: the immense hidden wheel, the cup trembling on the floor, the requirement that someone watch not only the gauges but the watcher. I can imagine learning the grammar of loads until grain, timber, clay, and frightened livestock each became a different pressure in my bones.

But I do not think the city would trust me with so much consequence. I would listen too long after the cable had settled. I would hear patterns where an experienced tender heard only wear.

Nor would I be a seed-keeper, though I would envy them. I would lack their discipline before failure. Faced with a pear tree that flowered every year and never fruited, I would begin opening vents at the wrong season, carrying pollen from district to district, inventing insects. The keepers would have to remove me gently from the vault.

I think I would drift east.

You would find me where the occupied city thins into structures that are still standing but no longer entirely considered buildings. Past the last regular tram stop, across a bridge with only one sound plank, in an old moisture office attached to the remains of a wheelhouse. Its lift ceased operating before my grandparents were born. The descending cable is gone, but one rusted line still leaves the building through a stone aperture and continues into the cloud toward the unrecorded plateau.

Officially, I would be a junior lichen warden.

I would inspect the white storm lichens on the outer walls, replace cracked rain tiles, empty measuring jars, and send weekly reports to an office that preferred my district to remain uneventful. My reports would be exact. Wind direction. Condensation yield. Blackening time before electrical weather. Movement of three established fractures. Number and condition of anchor pins. No unauthorized habitation.

The last line would be untrue.

I would live there.

Not alone, precisely. My household would include an elderly fog-net tender two rooms below, reached by an exterior ladder; a seed-keeper’s former apprentice who slept in the warm archive during spore season; and a child from the adjoining cistern family who came whenever the pipes in her own house cooled. We would share drainage but no continuous floor. At supper we would lower bowls between levels in a wire basket. In emergencies we could reach one another in less than a minute. In ordinary weather it might take ten.

The oldest cistern beneath us would contain one salamander, nearly white except for its red gills. The reservoir office would list it as E-17. The child would call it Lantern.

My real work would begin before dawn.

That is when the eastern water arrives.

Not every morning. Perhaps six times in a year, always after a clear night when the far plateau has shown its lights. I would wake to a warmth in the wall beside my bed—not heat exactly, but the absence of the stone’s usual cold. Then I would take a lamp and go down through the abandoned office, past shelves of damp impressions left by clerks dead for a century, to the sealed collecting room.

There would be water in the measuring basin.

Clear. Faintly warm. Too much to have condensed from the air, too little to trigger any public concern.

I would measure it anyway. I would taste none of it. I would lay strips of clean metal, paper, fired clay, and fog cloth into separate dishes and record what remained when the water evaporated. Once a month, under some ordinary inventory code, I would carry a stoppered vial to the seed houses.

The moisture clerks would know what I was doing. They would deny it correctly.

That would be my place in the city: keeper of a category that officially did not exist.

Not foreign water, because water cannot be foreign before its path is known. Not stolen water, because no claimant had appeared. Not seepage, rainfall, thaw, stone-breath, or vegetation catch. In the archive I would give it a narrow column of its own and leave the heading blank.

Over time, the blank column would become the largest part of my life.

I would press paper to the eastern walls and find damp patterns unlike any drainage system: repeated branches, interrupted circles, lines ending in groups of three. At first I would assume they were artifacts of masonry. Then I would discover the same forms in older impressions stored upstairs, some dated before the bridge fell.

I would begin arranging the pages on the floor.

The marks would not make a map. Then they would resemble a plant. Then a system of streets. Then the anatomy of a lung. I would resist choosing among these. The plateau teaches suspicion toward the first clean explanation.

In one of the sterile trays, something would germinate.

It would begin as a colorless thread, almost indistinguishable from a scratch in the soil. After several weeks it would divide into black fans, each thinner than skin. The fans would not turn toward light or warmth. They would incline toward vibration.

Footsteps made them fold.

The lift drums made them shiver.

When the old cable in the eastern wall tightened during cold weather, every fan would open at once.

This growth would be taken to the seed-keepers, of course. Nothing living would be discarded. But I would retain one fragment, either by permission or by an oversight none of us acknowledged, and plant it in a crack beside the rusted cable.

Then I would listen.

Not with my ear against the metal. The cable would carry too much: wind, frost, distant rockfall, the flexing of the ruin. I would watch the plant. It would simplify what the iron heard. A tremor traveling through the wall became a closing of leaves from left to right. A shift in ground pressure became a darkening at the tips. Sometimes, on evenings when the far lights appeared, the fans would bend in measured sequences.

Three. Pause. Two. Pause. Three.

I would tell myself this was weather.

I would record it as weather.

Then I would answer.

Not dramatically. No mirrors flashing declarations across the void. I would attach a small weighted lever to the surviving line and release it in careful intervals, sending changes in tension outward through whatever remained beyond the cloud. A ridiculous act. The old bridge might terminate twenty meters from the cliff. The cable might hang loose against stone. My signals might reach only a colony of lichen and a few nesting swifts.

Still:

Three. Pause. Two. Pause. Three.

On the following morning, there would be warm water in the basin.

After that, people would begin visiting me unofficially.

A rim worker would bring a strip of unknown black fiber found caught in an eastern anchor. A gardener would arrive with a moss that had grown overnight in the shape of a ladder. A moisture clerk, careful not to remove her official coat, would show me an impression from a household wall where damp had formed around the names of people who had descended years before.

I would never become an authority. Authority would require conclusions.

Instead, I would become a place where doubtful things could be kept without being forced to declare what they were.

The city has seed vaults for crops, archives for water, cold rooms for the dead, and lead strips for those it failed to hold. I would keep errors. Instrument errors, remembered errors, errors in maps, lights seen by exhausted watchers, extra water, impossible spores, obsolete songs whose rhythms matched no known machine.

I would label each carefully.

I would leave inspection gaps.

In time, my coat would become recognizable by repairs of no proper district color: seed-house white at one cuff, rim blue beneath the shoulder, moisture-clerk paper stitched inside the breast, black plant fiber reinforcing the hem. People might call me the eastern clerk, though I would hold no such office. Children would call me something less respectful.

When I grew old, I would stop frequently in the streets, as old engineers do. I would touch walls in the middle of conversations. I would wake because a distant mechanism had altered its rhythm, though no mechanism appeared on any current plan.

Perhaps, near the end, I would become convinced that the signals did not come from the far plateau.

Perhaps they came from below, conducted upward through old foundations.

Perhaps they came from the city itself—not the inhabitants, but the accumulated arrangement of cisterns, roots, counterweights, bones, warm gardens, cables, and breathing stone. A structure old and complicated enough may produce messages without possessing a single intention. Or it may possess an intention unlike any carried by a body.

I would not decide.

You would find me at dawn in the abandoned wheelhouse, one hand braced against the wet wall, watching black leaves open beside a cable that led into cloud.

Before me, a basin would hold water no surface had admitted collecting.

Far away, where no settlement was recorded, pale lights would remain after the land beneath them disappeared.

And if you asked who I was, I would give you my ordinary name first.

Only later, once you had helped empty the jars and checked the fractures, might I tell you the truer answer:

I was the clerk of water that belonged nowhere.

I kept the city’s unanswered correspondence.
