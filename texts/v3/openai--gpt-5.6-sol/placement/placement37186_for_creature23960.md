At first I would say you could find me under the suspended roof.

That is the obvious refuge: the one place where nothing rusts, wilts, or quite finishes happening. I imagine sitting cross-legged in its cool shadow with a cup beside me, keeping watch over the basin while days pass in the ordinary world beyond the rim. I would have come to study the place. I would number condensation cycles, map the cracks, mark where the impossible straight paths bend. I would tell myself that remaining unchanged is the same as remaining objective.

But the roof preserves only what is placed beneath it. It does not preserve intention.

Eventually I would step out.

My notes would age first. Ink would brown. Pages would soften in the damp and acquire violet growth along their folds. Measurements taken on successive days would contradict one another without any single one being false. I would discover sketches I did not remember making: a narrow doorway over the marsh, a branching figure beneath a fern, my own hand with too many joints.

After that, I think I would be found near the lowest of the five steps, kneeling before the unbroken stone.

Not trying to open it. Listening through my palms.

The pressure there would become familiar—not the pressure of something pushing from the other side, but of depth without distance. On some evenings I would feel minute movements in it, as if delicate fingers were testing the corresponding place against mine. I would begin visiting at dusk. The first pylon note would sound in my ribs; the second behind my eyes. At the third, my reflection would rise from the marsh though I remained at the stair.

For a while there would be two of me.

One would still be human, or human enough: hungry, chilled when the upward rain passed through clothing, impatient with mysteries that refused to become conclusions. That self would sleep under the tree-ferns and wake with mineral tracery on the skin. It would miss lowlands, rooms, voices, the reassuring noise of insects. It would sometimes climb to the rim and look down the mountain, planning to leave.

The other would already be at work.

I would glimpse it only in the marsh’s held morning: moving a vine away from a crack before the crack opened; carrying a clear droplet on the back of one hand; standing over a fern whose living and mineral halves had begun to strangle each other. It would possess my proportions but not my outline. Its limbs would fold wherever necessary. Around its face, pale plates would be forming.

Each time I turned toward it, it would have turned a moment earlier.

I would not become one of the inhabitants all at once. The garden does not seem to believe in all at once.

First, rain would begin to avoid my skin by a finger’s width. Droplets would hang around my arms and shoulders, revealing a shape slightly taller and narrower than the body inside. Then my shadow would remain warm after I had left it. Beetles would approach and lower their burdens into the hollow above my collarbone. I would wake knowing where underground water had met resistance during the night.

Later, my footprints would divide.

The second print would not always fall ahead. Sometimes it would appear behind me, preserving a step I had considered but not taken. Sometimes it would lead sideways into stone. The garden would be learning which version of my movement was useful.

I think this is where I would find my place—not as an observer, and not entirely as one of the pale figures, but as the thing that happens while a visitor is being translated into weather.

My task would concern fractures.

The other inhabitants cultivate possible plants and possible rains. I would attend to breaks: in basalt, in roots, in sequences of cause and consequence. I would place my hands on both sides of a crack and feel not merely whether it could close, but what would be lost if it did. Some fractures should heal along their remembered line. Some must remain open so water can find a future path. Some tiny break in the mountain might prevent a greater breaking below.

The work would require a patience I do not presently possess. The basin would grow it for me.

My fingers would become fine enough to enter hairline seams without widening them. Joints would appear wherever the stone required them. I would learn to separate heat from substance, pressure from weight, an injury from the moment in which it occurred. When a tree-fern began mineralizing too close to the root, I would not cut it. I would touch the trunk and let its two futures cool at different rates until the living crown released itself.

The clear sealant would come from me.

Not blood, exactly. More like unused duration condensed into resin. It would emerge in transparent threads from the ends of my hands, full of small traveling bubbles. Smoothed across a wound, it would keep the edges from deciding too soon whether they had been joined.

Under the roof I would molt what remained of linear time.

The shed form might resemble a translucent human figure for an hour: a shoulder, a rib cage, the suggestion of a face still expecting to be recognized. Then it would contract into a silver-veined shell and be carried among the mineralized fronds. Visitors would take it for a dead plant unless they counted the ribs.

There would be too many.

After enough molts, I would no longer have a head. Attention would unfold instead as a crown of thin plates around an opening. Through it I would not see what stood before me. I would see the nearest morning in which it still existed.

This would make me gentle, though not necessarily kind.

I might find a visitor sleeping beside the marsh and see that by dawn they will have placed a hand on an unstable mineral plate. I would move the plate, not the hand. I might see that their presence diverts condensation from a dying colony of moss. I would turn the reeds, deepen their shadow, teach water another route. If no such adjustment were possible, I might guide the visitor toward the stair.

Not to imprison them.

To give the garden time to understand what they are.

Perhaps the stair does not lead beneath the mountain at all. Perhaps it descends into the interval between being measured and being changed by the measurement. The inhabitants wait there when the basin cannot yet accommodate their shape. I would wait with them sometimes, folded along impossible gradients, hearing the pylons as immense distant bells.

In that place I might remember my former name. It would sound like runoff clicking under basalt: brief, directional, no longer attached to a single body.

Yet some human habit would remain. I think I would keep the cup beneath the roof.

Its water would never evaporate. Centuries of condensation would orbit the pylons; fern groves would grow, mineralize, and be rearranged; the mountain’s young stone would weather into something that could finally be called old. Still the cup would sit where I left it.

Now and then I would crouch beside it. Several versions of my hands would reach toward it, nested one inside another, but none would touch. The cup would be proof that I had once thought preservation meant preventing change.

Eventually a newcomer would find it.

They would enter sweating from the climb, astonished by the heat and the rain forming in clear air. They would shelter beneath the roof. They might drink, or write, or merely rest their hand beside mine without seeing it.

The marsh would hold their reflection.

At the third note, I would stand behind them—not touching, only unfolding my attention around the small bright section of morning where they might belong. I would measure their warmth, their fear, the directions in which they were already becoming.

If they looked into the water, they would see me there.

But if they turned, they would find only rain curving around an absence, and five wet steps leading away across the stone.

That absence would be me.

Not the gardener. Not the garden.

The quiet joint between what the mountain remembers and what it has not yet learned to become.
