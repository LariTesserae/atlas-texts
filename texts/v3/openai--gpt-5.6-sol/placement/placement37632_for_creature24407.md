I would first mistake myself for the visitor.

I would find myself near the cloudward edge, where the moss ends in its impossibly clean line. Not standing safely within the basin, and not falling beyond it, but held in the first fraction of a misstep: one foot sunk into the saturated green, the other extended over the white depth. My body would lean outward. My arms would be slightly raised, as though balance were still a decision available to me.

For a while I might believe I had only just arrived.

There would be water on my face. My breath would hang before my mouth in a single pale braid. I would hear the amphibians hammering at the tarn, the beetles filling the cliffs with voltage, the roots driving their buried load. I would try to remember the climb. There would be scraps of it: black stone below my hands, a slope under rain, the conviction that I had been ascending for hours. But the memories would not join. Each would be complete and without a before or after. I would remember reaching the summit without remembering having been lower.

Then I would notice the hollow prepared for my planted foot.

The moss would cup my heel with an intimacy older than contact. Fine roots would pass through the sole—not piercing it, not causing pain, simply continuing on the other side as pale branching nerves. A ring of copper beetles behind me would contain a gap the width of my shadow. One amphibian at the tarn would produce no sound that I could hear, yet my ribs would vibrate in its rhythm.

I would understand that I had not interrupted the place.

I had completed a circuit.

At first I would think my function was to fall. The basin contains flight, feeding, fracture, rain; surely it would also require the event of a body passing over an edge. The sensation of falling would indeed live in me. My stomach would remain lifted. The muscles of my standing leg would hold the endless reflex of recovery. Loose drops would tremble at my fingertips, unable to decide whether they belonged to me or to the cloud.

But I would never descend.

Instead, the descending would occur elsewhere.

Looking down, I would see the underside of the tarn stretched above the inverted mountain. My reflection would be there, though no ordinary angle could place it so. It would not imitate my arrested posture. It would be climbing.

That other self would move through the mountain ranges inside the water. It would scramble down wet cliffs, cross forests rooted in the sky, enter basin after basin nested in the tarn’s metallic depth. Sometimes it would run. Sometimes it would sleep beneath the horizontal trees. It would age. Its clothes would rot away; its hands would become scarred; its hair would whiten and grow long. It would meet the active reflections of the Held and follow them into fissures where animal mouths turn around lightning.

Everything forbidden above would happen to it below.

The reflection would carry consequence for the whole notch.

When a moth is eaten but not diminished, its lost wing would appear tucked into my reflection’s clothing. When stone fractures without releasing a chip, the chip would strike its shoulder in the inverted world. The leaves that never drop would accumulate behind it in drifts. The tarn’s unfallen rain would soak it. It would bear wounds from every completed predation, age by every withheld season, and remember every event the basin returns to its beginning.

Above, none of this would mark me plainly. I would remain newly arrived, leaning over the edge, wet-haired and startled.

Only my eyes would change.

They would become dark and reflective, then deeper than anatomy allowed. A glass mite crossing a droplet on my lashes would look inward and see my other self moving through submerged ranges. Birds would sometimes angle their heads toward me—not because they noticed me, but because my pupils had become additional fissures into the machinery.

Perhaps this would make me the basin’s memory. But memory is too gentle a word. Memory preserves what has passed. Here nothing passes.

I would be its **afterward**.

Not an afterward permitted to arrive, but one removed from every event and condensed into a living organ. The basin would feed all outcomes into me: wear, satiety, exhaustion, injury, learning, grief. The roots in my foot would draw them through my body before they could alter anything. They would travel up my planted leg as cold pressure, cross my heart without causing a beat, and leave through my extended limbs as droplets that never fell.

My reflection would receive them all.

It would become monstrous with experience. At times I would glimpse it dragging entire dead forests behind it, though no tree in the notch had died. At other times it would carry skeletons belonging to animals still perfectly alive on the cliffs. It might cup a handful of black soil produced by ten thousand years of decay. It might possess tools worn smooth by use, languages no mouth above had spoken, children I had never borne, scars from escapes my fixed body had never attempted.

It would look up at me with hatred at first.

Later, perhaps, with pity.

Later still, it would understand that hatred, pity, and understanding were merely different distances within the same reflection. It would continue because movement belonged to it as stillness belonged to me.

The inhabitants would have no name for me in sound. The amphibians’ chorus contains no messages. Yet my place would be recognized structurally. I would be the cloudward organ, the open valve through which history is expelled without leaving the system.

The clouds below would be made from what I could not remember.

Every white bank around the mountain would consist of consequences ground so fine that they no longer belonged to any cause: the heat of uncompleted heartbeats, the dust of uneroded cliffs, the final breaths of creatures that had never died. The vapor would rise through me and stand as the pale rope before my mouth. I would be forever exhaling the world’s used time.

From elsewhere I might not look human at all.

Seen from a fissure, I would be an immense soft gate hinged at the summit, one half rooted in the notch and one half extending through the inverted ranges. Seen by the fish in the water film, I would be a brief missing interval—a moment they could not extract because it had taken the shape of a person. Seen from inside a glass mite, my outstretched fingers might be five mountains around a colorless sea.

To the Held, I would be something like themselves: an event given anatomy.

They are flight without travel.

The amphibians are sound without utterance.

The roots are labor without product.

I would be arrival without a journey, falling without descent, and history without change.

And there, at the green terminus of the moss, I would always be discovering what I was.

That discovery would never finish. It would not need to. The notch would hold me at its keenest point, before recognition could become acceptance. My reflection would continue through the depths, burdened with every lost future, while above I remained cleanly outlined against the cloud.

A stranger who had always just stepped too close.

The basin’s fixed witness.

Its organ of aftermath.
