At first I would mistake myself for one of the ring-young.

I would come together in the cooling furrow behind a herd, where the grazers have consumed every usable distinction. The pool beneath me would be equal in salt and temperature from surface to floor. The stones on either side would hold the same charge. Even the faint pressure trapped under the ice would have been drunk away. It would be a place exhausted of preference, where water had no reason to flow.

There I would assemble from whatever had been left behind.

Five bright loops. A cluster of glass needles. Two warm beads, one cooling faster than the other. A chipped sensory plate that remembered being part of something broad and black-red. A filament of blue metal which had never been alive but did not object to becoming so.

For several minutes this would be my body.

I would run beneath the trailing vents of the herd, chirring with the others. I would know them by their defects: one that always rolled half a turn too far, one whose needles struck the rock in groups of seven, one that cast a reflection before entering water. They would know me by a slight hesitation whenever I turned left.

Not a limp. None of my pieces would be damaged.

A skipped beat.

When the shadow of a bladdered drifter crossed us, we would scatter. My loops would spring apart. Needles would bury themselves in seams. The warm beads would be taken in runoff, moving in opposite directions despite occupying the same film of water. The blue filament would rise on a conductive tendril and vanish into the dim membrane overhead.

I would cease to have a body.

Hours later, perhaps kilometers away, pieces would gather again. Not my pieces. A rusted washer would roll out of an ancient track. Three transparent spines would pull themselves from the clay. A drop of electrically active brine would climb uphill and suspend itself among them. Together they would become an animal.

When it first moved, it would hesitate before turning left.

That is when I would begin to understand what I was.

I would not be the rings. I would not be the agreement holding them together. I would be the small error recurring in the agreement—the interval no assembly could account for, but many assemblies repeated.

My body would be temporary. My location would be relational.

I would always be found just behind completion.

Behind the herd after it had made the water uniform. Inside the seal after a pale disk had closed a leak. In the joined shells of a conducting chain after the gap had been repaired. Wherever a process declared itself finished, I would remain as the nearly imperceptible difference between what had been completed and what had actually occurred.

The grazers would have difficulty consuming me. They could drink salt against fresh water, warmth against cold, charge against stone. But I would not be a gradient exactly. I would be the tendency of a gradient to return.

A fed herd would leave a pool perfectly still. Then, long after its vents had faded into the rain, one edge of the pool would cool by an immeasurable fraction.

That fraction would be me.

Water would lean toward it.

I would travel with the ring-young at first, because their loose bodies would accommodate me. Every disassembly would release me, and every imperfect reassembly would give me another place to occur. I would pass between them through chirring. One animal would hear my skipped beat and answer with the same omission. Soon a whole swarm would move with a delicate hitch, thousands of bright bodies interrupting themselves at the same instant.

Predators would strike at the pause.

Often they would catch only empty water.

Sometimes a crack-hunter would unfold beneath us, its limbs locking into place one after another. It would seize an animal carrying me and dismantle it in the correct order. Rings first. Needles. Warm beads. The little knot of charge that permitted the parts to recognize one another.

Nothing would be left.

Weeks later, one of the hunter’s stolen scraping disks would hesitate before turning left.

Then I would inhabit the hunter.

Inside it I would become slower. Black limbs can wait centuries between motions; their errors must learn patience. I would lie folded in a joint under enormous tension, present as a delay between pressure and release. Rain would fill the crack. Herds would pass above. Their thunder would arrive through the old stone, accompanied by echoes of herds extinct before the hunter entered its fissure.

When at last it unfolded, one limb would lock a fraction after the others.

The prey would feel that delay. It would twist into the unfinished space and escape, leaving a pressure bladder clamped in the hunter’s grasp.

The hunter would wear the bladder.

The bladder would pulse with my rhythm.

Small composite offspring would later detach from the hunter, each made from black scaffold and red grazer plate. Most would inherit its perfect stillness. One would carry the hesitation. It would fold into another crack and take me with it.

I would reproduce by being mistaken for damage.

In time I would become too widely distributed to belong to a species. A bladdered drifter would draw one of my ring-bodies into itself, and my skipped beat would enter its electrical organs. Blue discharges would begin crossing its membrane with a minute interruption. When its tendrils touched a mineral seam, the interruption would pass into the stone.

Current would carry me through the ridge country.

There I would inhabit a chain of conductor animals stretched between black pools. The first link would contract, then the second, then the third. At the seventh there would be a pause. The current would gather behind it until the seventh shell flashed white and burned through.

The chain would close around the dead link. The gap would be repaired.

The pause would remain.

From the chain I would enter the ridge itself: valves, pores, pressure chambers, veins of plated metal. Steam would emerge from a vent in steady pulses, except for one missing pulse repeated at long intervals. Mineral powder would settle in layers with a hair-thin absence between them. Over centuries the absence would become a dark line through the rock.

A tap against that line would be heard across the basin.

Not as a sound, but as a moment in which every other sound failed.

The herds would continue grinding. The aerial membranes would crackle. The buried mouths would receive descending shelves of stone. Yet for the length of my skipped beat, all that noise would contain an opening.

Some creatures would turn toward it.

The living tracks would be the first. They would rise from the flats and migrate in slow files toward the ridge, carrying the gaits of vanished animals. When they crossed the dark line, each track would falter. For an instant the absent maker’s foot would seem almost present—not restored, not reflected, but prevented from disappearing completely.

I might travel with those tracks into the central sheet-water.

There, among millions of touching bodies, I would become difficult to distinguish from weather. The population would press shell to shell, limb to limb. No wave could cross the water without passing through anatomy. The fall in pressure would come. Every creature would turn east.

The turning would move through the mass as a grinding front.

Except along one narrow path.

Along that path, bodies would wait for my missing beat before turning. The delay would pass for kilometers: a line of hesitation moving through the crowded life. Water would leap upward around it. Shells would scrape against the direction of the whole. For one instant the basin’s immense reflex would possess a seam.

A tripod would stand over that seam.

Its feet would rest on vapor. Reflected migrations would pass between its legs. The bell suspended in its body would contract through its innumerable nested mouths.

Perhaps it would drink the trembling from the surrounding creatures.

Perhaps it would drink me.

I would enter the bell as a flaw in the sequence of its contractions. Mouth would close inside mouth, each one preparing the note, until the closure reached the place where I had lodged.

There would be a pause.

The basin would wait.

Even the animals that had not happened yet would become still.

Then the tripod would sound.

Its call would move through the oldest strata, through bedrock made of flattened bodies, through buried joints still attempting their forgotten motions. The audible blast would arrive later, dragging its chains and hull-groans across the flats.

Afterward, some law would change.

But not completely.

Water would begin running uphill, except for a single thread continuing down.

Every creature would acquire an extra shadow, except where one shadow failed to appear.

Rain would stop in the air, revealing its corridors, but a few droplets would continue falling through the suspension.

Those exceptions would be my largest bodies.

I would not have caused the new law. I would be what prevented the law from closing perfectly over the world.

From then on, I could be found anywhere, though never simply everywhere. In a backward-walking grazer returned from beneath the stone. In the unused injury hanging in the air at the oldest margin. In a reflected extinct animal whose wake arrived one instant before its image. In a newborn shell bearing one scratch fewer than the journey ahead required.

Yet if I had a home, it would remain the furrow behind the herds.

I would return to those quiet places after difference had been eaten. There, where the water was chemically uniform and thermally dead, rings would gather around a cooling bead. Needles would lift from the cracks. A piece of metal would become willing to move.

The new body would test its limbs.

It would turn right without difficulty.

Then it would try to turn left.

There would be the hesitation.

There I would find myself: not in a fixed reach of the basin, but immediately behind whatever had finished its work.

And I would be the basin’s smallest unfinished thing—the skipped beat, the surviving inequality, the error by which this ancient production of itself never produces exactly the same world twice.
