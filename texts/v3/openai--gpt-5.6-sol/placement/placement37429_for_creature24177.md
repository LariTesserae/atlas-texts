At first I think I would live high up.

Somewhere in the cooling towers, perhaps, where the basin could be understood as a whole: flood channels ruled across the silt, lamps burning in the haze, tidal water entering through gates. I imagine myself as a peregrine on a transmission gantry, owning the distances and descending only in violence.

But that is too clean a life for this place. Too much sky, too definite a body.

Then I think I would be one of the rats in the old galleries, knowing the city from beneath. I would understand which walls were hollow, which conduits remained warm, which sealed rooms could be entered through the width of a valve stem. Yet the rats belong wholly to appetite and route. They do not have to keep anything from failing.

I think I would.

You would find me at the lowest pumping station still operated by hand.

Not wholly by hand—the motors cycle themselves, the gate actuators answer distant signals, and the control room is lined with displays—but this is an old station, settled crookedly enough that automation cannot be trusted. One sluice binds when the tide carries too much grit. A level sensor grows a bacterial skin and reports water where there is only damp air. Crabs pack themselves behind the trash screen. The western pump has a vibration that vanishes whenever an engineer comes to inspect it.

So someone remains there at night.

I would be that someone: the keeper of Pump Station Fourteen, where the enclosed service corridor ends and the open basin begins.

The station stands on adjustable piers above a brown channel. Its lower stair has been underwater for years. The concrete apron tilts toward the sea, and every painted line upon it bends subtly downhill. There is a yellow door that never shuts unless lifted by its handle, a narrow control room kept painfully cold, and an exterior gallery roofed with corrugated metal. Condensation falls from the roof in five steady places. Small fish wait beneath each one.

My work would consist mostly of watching things that appear not to change.

Water against numbered marks. Pressure values. The slow accumulation of debris on a grate. The tremor of a motor through the floor. I would carry a lamp, a hooked pole, a radio, and a notebook sealed in plastic. I would wear rubber boots with the tops tied close and keep my sleeves fastened despite the heat.

But the gauges would not be my only instruments.

If the pale crabs leave the upper embankment before rain appears on radar, I close the outer inspection covers. If the pelicans assemble too early at the discharge channel, I check for a valve leaking warm water. When rats begin crossing the cable gallery above floor level, I know the tide has backed into a tunnel somewhere below. A silence among the nesting pigeons means snake; a sudden explosion means peregrine; a gradual restlessness means pressure building in the weather.

The animals would tell me what the machinery knows but has not yet reported.

Over time I would become less a mechanic than a translator between two unreliable systems.

The city says: **FLOW NORMAL**.

The crabs say otherwise.

The city says: **GATE CLOSED**.

An eel passes beneath it.

The city says the lower chamber is dry, but mosquitoes rise from the access hatch when I loosen the first bolt.

I would learn to trust neither side completely. Animals panic, instruments drift, water takes paths that did not exist yesterday. My task would be to keep several imperfect accounts and notice when their errors align.

I would sleep in a chair during the quietest hour before dawn, boots on, radio against my chest. A high-dwelling cat would sometimes enter through the ceiling chase and sit on the filing cabinet. It would not belong to me. Nothing here would belong to anyone in that way. Still, I would leave water for it in a stainless-steel tray and it would wake me before the pump alarm by looking toward the floor.

The crews would give me a practical name—Gatekeeper, Mud Clerk, Aunt Fourteen—something affectionate enough to deny affection. They would say I had been at the station too long. Perhaps I would have. My hearing would adjust to the machinery until I noticed a failing bearing as an absence. My eyes would go first to drain mouths, ledges, and undersides. In any room I entered, even far inland, I would inspect the seam beneath the door.

People would think I was solitary, but I would almost never be alone.

Mud crabs would gather beneath the gallery. Swifts would sleep inside the upper vents. Cockroaches would occupy the warm cavity behind the control boards despite every treatment. A water snake would shed its skin annually in the dry sump. Bats would pass each evening, and midges would pattern the security lights. At high tide, sharks no longer than my arm would enter the channel and turn in the reflected red pulse of the tower beacon.

I would know some of these inhabitants individually and others only as pressures, stains, seasonal noises. The distinction between acquaintance and infestation would vary according to what they damaged.

Once or twice a year, something large would clog the outer screen: a drowned dog, a ruined net heavy with fish, perhaps a seal still alive and furious. Then the quiet station would fill with people, lamps, shouted instructions, winches, blood-colored water. For an hour the place would seem dramatic. Afterward everyone would leave, and I would hose the apron while crabs waited below for what the water brought them.

Yet I do not think I would remain entirely human in the city’s imagination.

Stories would collect around the station. Children would say I never crossed dry ground. Drivers would glimpse me on the gallery at impossible hours. Someone would insist that Pump Station Fourteen had been automated years ago and no permanent operator was assigned there. My reports would continue to arrive anyway: terse notes on tide, blockage, settlement, animal movement.

Eventually it would become difficult to say whether I was the person tending the station or the station’s habit of attending to itself.

Perhaps, by then, you would find no one in the control room. Only a chair worn smooth, a lidded cup, tide tables pinned beneath cloudy plastic, and notebooks filled with entries in the same narrow hand:

**02:10 — Gate cycled.**

**02:31 — Crabs moving inland.**

**02:47 — Pressure falling without command.**

**03:05 — Birds lifted from western roof. No visible cause.**

**03:12 — Water heard beneath floor.**

Outside, on the tilted apron, there would be a figure with a lamp standing at the edge of the channel—or a heron, perhaps, made strange by haze and distance.

That is where I would be: at the point where the city tries to tell the sea where it may go.

I would be the small, sleepless correction required because the gate never closes perfectly.
