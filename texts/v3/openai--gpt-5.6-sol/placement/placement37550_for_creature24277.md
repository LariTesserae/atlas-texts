At first I would believe I was part of the ground.

Noon would have sealed me into stillness: three pale supports planted among flat pebbles, central body lowered, every joint glazed shut against the glare. Dust would lie undisturbed across my plates. One side of me would be amber where the prevailing wind had polished it; the other would retain the chalk-white roughness of younger mineral.

From a distance I might resemble a collapsed column. From nearby, perhaps an arrangement of stones left by no hand.

I would not yet know where I ended.

Something would be living beneath my lowest plates. Colorless mats would inhabit the protected seams, sending feeding filaments into the residue caught there. Several windborne shells would be wedged along my underside, still closed, testing the chemistry of my shelter. A family of minute grazers might have passed their entire lives on me, eating old films from the flexible skin between my armor. Their tilting sails would make brief signs I could not see.

For most of the bright hours we would all pretend to be one dead thing.

I would stand at the outer edge of a grove, where abundance stops with almost geometric suddenness. Behind me: crowded crowns drawn narrow against the sun, reproductive towers hidden among ceramic trunks, fluorescent mats asleep in the shade. Ahead: a basin of pale stone broad enough to contain weather. Wind-courses would cross it in long, shallow curves, and on the far horizon a black line would seem no thicker than a scratch.

That line would be another grove.

Whether it was my destination, I would not know.

Late light would touch the branching vanes folded along my back. The joints would soften one at a time. Resin seals, made months before, would split with sounds too small to travel. I would raise my mast.

The world would arrive not as a picture but as pressures.

The grove behind me would be a broad electrical murmur, deep-rooted and slow. A dust front beyond the northern ridge would register as a pressure gradient with a metallic taste. The buried contraction of cooling stone would not yet have begun, but I would feel its approach in the minute strain between layers of the plateau. Far out in the basin, old tracks would interrupt the ground’s thermal pattern: three impressions, then three more, continuing until they disappeared into the brightness.

I would have eyes of a kind, but they would be the least trustworthy part of me. The horizon might blur. A nearby boulder might vanish against the basin. Yet I could sense the charge gathering on a seed shell as it rolled past my forward limb. I could feel a grove beyond sight waking its roots after dustfall. I could distinguish the vibration of a living traveler from the hollow mineral stance of one emptied centuries ago.

At least, I would hope I could.

This would be my first open crossing.

For decades I would have walked only beneath crowns.

I would remember—if bodily sequence can be called memory—the sheltered underside of an older traveler. I began there as a flattened, three-lobed plate, carried across distances I could not comprehend. The adult’s steps entered me as rhythm before I possessed legs: tilt, brace, release; tilt, brace, release. Storms were long tremors through its armor. Dormancy was enclosure and silence. Groves announced themselves through changes in the adult’s circulation long before their shadows reached us.

Then, in the dark center of a colony, I was released.

My first limbs were threads. My first journeys were from one trunk shadow to the next. For years I knew the open plateau only as brightness glimpsed between crowns. The grove fed me resin cases and tolerated my clumsy pressure against its roots. Grazers climbed my legs. Seeds lodged beneath me and fell away. With every cooling cycle my vanes divided, becoming more sensitive and more difficult to fold.

Meanwhile a route assembled itself inside me.

No one taught it. It was not a map. It had no names, distances, or destinations. It was an unease at certain stellar angles, a calm produced by particular buried vibrations, an impulse to turn when the morning pressure tide touched one vane before another. Some of it came from the parent who carried me. Some might have been older than that body, passed through generations as inclinations of tissue.

Now the route would be pulling.

Not toward the dark line directly ahead, but obliquely across the basin, toward a place apparently empty.

I would hesitate. This, too, would be part of what I was.

One limb would unseal and lift. For a moment the remaining two would bear my weight. My body would incline over open ground. The raised pillar would reach farther than seemed safe and settle into dust.

Through it I would taste stone, salt, old sunlight, and the faint chemical trace of something that had crossed here many years before.

Then the second step.

The grove behind me would open for evening. Silver would travel through its crowns in a coordinated flash. The grazers among the upper blades would begin their black-and-silver signaling. For a little while, reflected light would move across my armor, and the organisms beneath me would stir as if dawn had come from below.

With each step I would carry pieces of that grove away: spores, films, closed shells, tiny feeding lives. I would not be merely an animal traveling between colonies. I would be a small, walking discontinuity in the emptiness—an island broken loose from an archipelago.

That might be the truest answer to what I was.

Not the three pillars. Not the low body or sensory crown. Not even the inherited route.

I would be a crossing made alive.

Behind me, one concentrated world; somewhere ahead, another. Between them, hundreds of kilometers where nothing moved but dust, pebbles, and the nearly inaudible shells of seeds. My purpose, if the word belonged here, would not be to conquer that interval. It would be to endure it without becoming separate from what I carried.

At dusk I would walk. At noon I would become stone. During storms I would lower my vanes and let fertility gather in my seams. Perhaps I would pass an upright shape and wonder whether it was another traveler sleeping, an empty framework, or a predator wearing patience as camouflage. Perhaps an invisible field would suggest shelter where no grove stood, and every inherited rhythm in me would struggle against the invitation.

One night, far from either colony, the deep contraction would move through the plateau.

I would stop.

All three pillars would plant themselves. My mast would descend. The small grazers beneath my plates would flatten; the colorless films would draw inward; the seed shells would tighten around their folded filaments. Through my feet, the buried vibration would enter and pass upward through every layer of my body.

For that moment, I would sense the groves behind and ahead not as distinct places but as distant concentrations in one vast field of listening.

Then the vibration would fade.

The stars would remain hard and unwinking above the white land. Somewhere in the dark, a traveling seed would strike stone and ring once.

I would raise one pillar and continue toward a destination I did not understand, following a road that existed only in the posture of my body.
