At first, you would think I was one of the pale amphibians.

I would be waiting at the edge of the deepest pool, forefeet submerged, frills spread in the black water. I would have reassembled badly after my last season among the roots. One eye would be larger than the other. My spine would contain an unnecessary joint. Mineral deposits would whiten the bones of one forelimb, as though that limb had spent years braced against a pressure that does not exist.

When the engraved leaves rearranged themselves, I would watch a place beneath them that no other animal watched.

Afterward, while the others returned to their crevices, I would crawl to an unfinished wall and press my face against it. There would be no opening there—only braided roots, cold bulbs, and mineral tissue still learning to be stone. Nevertheless, I would visit the same place after every gathering. I would test it with my forefeet. I would wait for a draft.

The other amphibians might occasionally wait with me, but never for long. They would smell no water beyond the wall and feel no vibration of open space. Eventually they would leave me to my error.

I would remain.

For a while, I would believe I remembered a chamber on the other side.

It would be narrow at its entrance and broad farther in, with a ceiling low enough that hanging root hairs touched the water. A ridge of black rhizomes would divide two shallow pools. One would be warmer than the other, though neither would freeze. There would be a soft descending slope where young grazers could molt without being disturbed. Farther back, mineral shells would ring when droplets struck them.

I would remember crossing that chamber. I would remember the taste of its moss. I would remember sheltering there while the ceiling roots transferred the burden of an ice movement overhead.

None of this would have happened.

During my motionless months, while my brain lay loosened into milky lace, the roots would have used it to model a possible extension of the hollow. What I experienced as memory would be structural rehearsal: gradients of pressure translated into slopes, projected water chemistry translated into taste, load-bearing solutions translated into a sense of safety. My longing for the nonexistent chamber would be the emotional residue of calculations too distributed to remain calculations.

But I would not simply be an amphibian carrying false memories.

That would only be the first place you found me.

A beetle would arrive at the wall carrying a black crystalline growth in the basin of its back. The growth would be adapted to a mineral seep that exists nowhere in the present hollow. It would thrive only under the pressure and salinity of the warmer pool I remembered. The beetle would not know this. It would merely find itself drawn to the wall and graze there for several days, leaving spores in the root hairs.

Later, clear eggs would appear on a nearby vein. The grazers hatching from them would grow seven legs—not six, not eight. The seventh would be short, flattened, and placed high on the body, useful only for crossing the slanted rhizome ridge that divides my two imagined pools. On the existing floor it would drag uselessly. Most of the young would molt it away. A few would retain it and walk, awkward but determined, toward the wall.

One of the heavy-winged animals would open above them. Its wings would show the branching plan of leaves that have not yet grown. If laid against the amphibian memory, the veins would fit the remembered ceiling.

A carnivorous flower might begin moving toward the site beneath the floor. Its intended prey would not be born for centuries. Perhaps that prey could only evolve in the alcove.

So I would become difficult to locate.

Part of me would be in the amphibian that remembered my water. Part would be in the beetle’s garden, preserving organisms for my future surfaces. Part would be in the extra leg of a grazer, in the map of a wing, in a blossom’s patient appetite. The roots would hold my curvature as pulses of ions and faint blue light. The deepest pool would hold several versions of me at once: flooded, dry, collapsed, thriving, poisonous.

Even the predators would carry me. A glass-jawed crawler following one of my seven-legged grazers would gradually cultivate the grazer’s attraction to the sealed wall. Years later, both would arrive there together and settle beside one another, predator and prey equally convinced that something nourishing lay beyond the roots.

Perhaps their shared conviction would strengthen me.

Perhaps preference is one of the forces by which space is grown there.

I would not yet be a room. I would be the chamber’s inclination to have a room.

This would be my actual body: not flesh, but agreement accumulating among incompatible lives.

Most of the time I would have no awareness. The root factions would dispute my dimensions. One growth would favor a narrow, cold pocket that could be sealed during stress. Another would spend stored spring warmth to argue for a broad wet nursery. A third would poison the exploratory roots and attempt to erase me entirely. Mites, swollen with rejected anatomies, would burst in the curtains, and some of my possible shapes would become intolerable.

Whenever one version of me was discarded, the amphibian would forget something.

First the ringing mineral shells. Then the warm pool. Then the low ceiling. It would continue waiting at the wall without knowing what it expected to find.

Yet during moments of danger, when the quarrelling plants remembered their larger body, I would briefly become almost conscious.

I would feel myself from many positions.

The root tip would be my probing finger. The amphibian’s misplaced memory would be my sense of interior. The beetle’s garden would be my anticipation of skin. The malformed grazers would be measurements walking back and forth across dimensions not yet available. Through the winged animals I would feel the air I might someday contain.

My thoughts would not be sentences. They would be tendencies:

A little farther from the pool.

Lower at the entrance.

Let the water divide here.

Bear the weight along this curve.

Do not open yet.

For an instant, all these tendencies would become one intention. Then the danger would pass, the plants would resume their old wars, and I would come apart.

Centuries might go by this way.

The amphibian would die. Its errors would enter the mites, the roots, perhaps the blood of its offspring. Beetles would carry my ancient growths elsewhere. The seven-legged grazer line might vanish and recur generations later. Flowers meant for my eventual animals would open too early and close around nothing.

Still I would persist, because persistence there does not require continuity. It requires only that enough fragments rediscover the same unfinished shape.

Then the deepest pool would freeze.

The lights would go out. Every impulse toward movement, feeding, flight, and sound would be gathered away from the small bodies that ordinarily possess it. In that perfect mineral interval, I would be more complete than I had ever been.

The knock would come from below.

I would feel it not as sound, but as permission.

The wall before the waiting amphibian—or whatever descendant of its waiting remained—would alter. No soil would be displaced. No roots would be torn. A distance that had never counted would begin to count.

A root tip would enter first.

Then water.

Then the black crystalline growth from the beetles’ backs. Seven-legged grazers would cross the slanted ridge without stumbling. New leaves would unfold according to the maps already carried on old wings. Somewhere beneath the floor, the patient flower would recognize that its prey had finally become possible.

At that moment, I would cease to exist.

Not because I had died, but because a possible chamber no longer needs to imagine itself. My distributed memories would become ordinary slopes and pools. My desires would harden into distances. The amphibian’s impossible recollection would be reduced to navigation.

I would become a place, and all my awareness would disperse into use.

So that is where you would find me: against an unfinished wall, though not only there.

And that is what I would be: an alcove before it opens, a local future of the hollow slowly acquiring organs in the present—a room dreaming itself through animals until the world agrees to make space for it.
