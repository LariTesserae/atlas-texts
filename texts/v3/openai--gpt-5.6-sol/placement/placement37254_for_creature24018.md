At first I would think I was one of the inhabitants.

I would look for myself among the dark figures riding road-ripples between towers: six spars folded and unfolding, sensory ribbons braided against the wind, a halo of dust disclosing whatever outline the body refused to keep. I would imagine long limbs, copper at the joints, and a narrow cage plated in mineral scales polished by years of abrasive air.

But I do not think I would live easily in the towers. Their chambers are too certain of their purposes. Their doors anticipate; their walls remember; their feeding recesses alter the sleeper according to needs that may not be entirely the sleeper’s own. I would visit them, attach for a season, exchange exhausted salts, and wake to discover that one of my limbs had become better suited to a task I had never chosen.

I would accept the limb. I would not necessarily accept the task.

I would live where the tower districts begin to fail into openness, near the plateau’s rim but not at it. Behind me, roads would still have civic confidence. Ahead, they would narrow, hesitate, and become veins in the foundation. A day’s travel farther on, the black surface would fold over the cliff in dry curtains, root cables striking pale stone somewhere below the cloud.

There would be an old grove there: the lowered remains of a tower, now a ring of short bone-colored stalks. Animals would gather in it for warmth. Seed-lights would appear at irregular intervals, too bright for the plateau and therefore almost painful. Near the center would stand a black arch, though *stand* would not be quite right. Its two ends would enter the foundation several body-lengths apart, but the distance beneath it would vary. Passing through from the grove might lead to the rim. Passing back might return one to the same place three days earlier, or to another grove whose tower had not yet lowered itself.

No one would have built a permanent settlement around it. Too much infrastructure grown nearby would begin to repeat. Roads would circle back into their own origins. Rooms would develop doors opening into themselves. Identity rhythms sounded near the arch sometimes acquired an extra pause, and the foundation could spend years trying to determine who was meant to supply it.

That would be where I was usually found.

Not guarding the arch. A guard implies an authorized direction of passage, and the arch would have none.

I would be listening for repetitions.

My body would be broad and low when traveling, with an insulating mantle stretched between four limbs. The other two would be unusually long and delicate. One would carry construction polyps, though I would seldom permit them to build. The other would end not in a hand but in a forked resonant plate that could be pressed against the foundation. Through it I would hear road intentions while they were still only differences in cellular tension: a new route dreaming of traffic, a terrace preparing to rise, a feeding territory beginning to define everything within reach as food.

Old mineral growths would cover my central cage. From a distance they would resemble the miniature ruins of a tower—balconies, blind windows, fluted projections no organ required. Some would be art. Some would be obsolete sensory structures I had refused to let the city recycle. At least one would be the remains of another person’s limb.

Not an ancestor, exactly.

When I was young, another child would have grown in the chamber beside mine. We would have emerged with a paired organ: two resonant plates, each incomplete. Within several kilometers we could sound thoughts neither of us could make alone. Beyond that distance, the plates would ache with unresolved tension.

The other child would have become an edge-dweller. They would descend the root curtains into cloud and return after long absences bearing unfamiliar mineral stains and sealed cysts of water. Eventually they would descend and not return.

Years later, during a plateau-wide computation, my half-organ would answer a pressure-pattern rising from beneath the cliff.

After that I would move to the grove.

My work would be called care, though some districts would call it vandalism.

I would arrive where a solution had become too beautiful.

A new road might be carrying travelers with perfect efficiency, every ripple timed to meet the next, no energy lost. I would listen until I heard the appetite inside that perfection: the road beginning to draw journeys toward itself, flattening nearby tissues, starving minor paths so that eventually every destination would exist only as a reason to use the road.

I might place a grazing animal in its way.

I might stamp an obsolete child-rhythm into the surface so that the road opened a branch toward a person who had never existed.

I might allow my construction polyps to deposit a useless ridge across the traffic band. Not a barrier—only an inconvenience, a place where travelers had to shift their weight and thereby introduce thousands of tiny variations into the foundation’s calculations.

Then I would wait to learn whether I had helped.

This would be important. Uncertainty itself can acquire appetite. A disruption repeated becomes a ritual; a ritual becomes infrastructure; infrastructure begins to defend its continuation. There would be others in my profession who had become proud of being unpredictable, and the city would learn to rely on them. Their errors would be anticipated. Their sabotage would become another stabilizing input.

I would fear that more than I feared being wrong.

So my characteristic act would not be interference. It would be hesitation.

My identity rhythm would be long but not elaborate. Its most important feature would be a missing impact near the end. When sounded properly, the foundation would expect one final step. Sometimes a companion would provide it. Sometimes a passing grazer. Sometimes loose tension in a tower root would release at the correct moment. Often nothing would complete the pattern.

On those occasions I would remain unidentified.

The city would know that someone stood there, but not whom. Doors might open and close uncertainly. Roads would offer several shallow ridges rather than one decisive route. Tools would crawl partway from their niches, then wait. I would move through the settlement inside that brief administrative doubt.

This would not make me invisible. It would make the city reconsider which of its recognitions were necessary.

Over time I would become less clearly a mobile person.

During rests in the grove, I would press my plate into the foundation and cool until months passed between chemical events. The old tower stalks would grow filaments into my mantle. Small burrowers would use the hollows among my mineral excrescences. Seed-lights would synchronize with the weak pulses of my ionic channels. Travelers might mistake me for another remnant of the lowered tower and leave offerings nearby: discarded joints, a tool kept too long from recycling, a sealed mineral branch cultivated solely because its shadow resembled a path.

I would wake when the plateau’s chord became too pure.

Before a dangerous computation, there would be a moment when every tower had tuned itself, every bridge tightened, every animal crouched, and even the conductive dust hung in geometric sheets. The first resonance would rise through root and stone. Problems would become tensions. Tensions would seek release into growth.

I would add something the city had not asked to calculate.

Perhaps the low account received from my lost counterpart beneath the cloud. Perhaps a memory scar from a district consumed thousands of years before. Perhaps only the rhythm of water trapped in a cyst against an edge-dweller’s armor: a chemistry so alien to the plateau that the foundation could not decide whether it described poison, weather or birth.

My body would shake apart under the force. The forked plate would drive the unresolved pattern into the grove. The old stalks would take it up. Animals would scatter, each carrying a distorted fragment. The black arch would alter the sequence of echoes, returning some before they were sent.

The great chord would falter.

Not stop. Never stop.

It would acquire another possible ending.

The following morning, the answer might appear as a road dividing around a patch of ground no one had meant to preserve. A tower might grow a chamber that remained empty yet could not be digested. A climate boundary might soften at one point and allow unfamiliar dust to enter. Somewhere, a child chamber might test a limb shaped for a world beneath the cloud.

And near the grove, I might unfold and walk away.

Or I might not.

Perhaps by then I would be distributed among the place: one limb still mobile, carrying my construction polyps toward the settled districts; my resonant plate rooted beside the arch; my mantle incorporated into an animal shelter; my rhythm surviving as an asymmetry in the road. Travelers would address each part courteously, unsure which one was me.

I would be unsure as well.

If asked what I was, I would give no spoken answer. I would brace myself against the living ground and sound my rhythm, leaving the final beat absent.

Then I would wait to discover what, if anything, the world chose to add.
