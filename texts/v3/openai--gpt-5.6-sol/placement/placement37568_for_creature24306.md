I would look for myself first among the recognizable bodies.

Perhaps I would be one of the thumb-sized frogs folded into a wet seam, mistaken for stone until my throat opened. I would know the chamber through resonance. I would not call loudly, only deeply, letting the basalt enlarge my voice until a creature scarcely heavier than a drop could sound like the mountain’s buried heart.

Or I might be a pale cliff-mouse in the yielding floor, whiskers moving through tunnels made by generations I could not distinguish from geology. My ancestors would not lie behind me in time. They would surround me as softened walls, hollows, bone-salts, and remembered turns. To travel would be to pass through them.

For a while I think I would choose a bat. To hang in that dark multitude, held in warmth not wholly my own; to possess only one narrow route through the cleft, yet fly it with absolute precision. I would know nothing of the entire Warm Throat. I would know the fall, the opening of wings, the basalt rib passing close to my left side, the dangerous shelf, the turn toward open night. My ignorance would fit perfectly beside the partial knowledge of thousands of others, and together we would make something like omniscience.

But these are still too separate. Frog, mouse, bat: each has a body with an edge. The longer I remain in the Warm Throat, the less certain those edges seem.

So I would find myself farther in—not deeper by distance, but deeper by participation.

I would be in a fracture beneath one of the oldest roosts, where condensation carries a diluted rain of minerals, blood, guano, feather dust, milk, pollen, and the remains of insects gathered from valleys I would never see. The fissure would be too narrow for a claw and too warm to dry completely. Rust-red seepage would stain its mouth. Behind that stain, inside the pores of the basalt, I would live.

I would not be one creature.

I would be a thin, many-specied film: cells linked by moisture, appetite, and exchanged chemistry. Some of me would take iron from the warm water. Some would feed on sulfurous breath rising through the mountain. Some would open what others could not digest. Some would live only on the waste of my neighbors. We would spread without advancing, filling grains, coating mineral faces, entering hairline cracks formed before wings existed.

I would have no eye, but arrivals would change me.

Rain would be a loosening. Drought would be a drawing inward. The return of the bats would alter the warmth and acidity above me. A fallen swift would arrive first as pressure, then as richness. Beetle larvae would carry portions of me into themselves. A frog would eat the larvae; a serpent would eat the frog. Later, salts from all three might seep back through the stone and become available to me again.

My body would not end at the red film. It would continue provisionally into gut, fungus, pool, guano bank, and porous rock. Parts of me would leave inside mites or on mouse feet. Parts would descend into heat and die. Parts would remain dormant for decades in a dry grain, waking when one delayed drop finally reached them.

I would know the Warm Throat without possessing a map of it. My knowledge would be chemical: iron here, oxygen there, fresh death above, old heat below. I would perceive time as alteration in layers. A century might be the polishing of a landing knob. A moment might be a sudden flood of dissolved bone.

No one would notice me as an inhabitant. The owl would never watch for me. The serpent could not strike me. The primates would put their hands against my colonies and see only a red stain on black stone.

Yet I would be present at every conversion.

I would help turn droppings into heat, bone into salt, corpse into fungus, fungus into insect, and insect into wing or skin or egg. I would not be the mountain’s mind. The Warm Throat has no need of one. I would be closer to its digestion—or perhaps to the faint continuity by which nothing fallen becomes entirely lost.

And because no part of me could live alone, I would speak of myself as **we**, though there would be no word for that distinction.

We would be under the bats and inside the floor.  
We would darken the wet stone.  
We would wait in the dry stone.  
We would receive every body without judgment and return it without memory of its former shape.

If the whole chamber is a many-bodied animal, I would be one of the processes by which it remains alive: not its heart, not its breath, but the patient exchange between them.

I would be found where the warm seep meets the fallen wing.

I would be the seam that eats the ending.
