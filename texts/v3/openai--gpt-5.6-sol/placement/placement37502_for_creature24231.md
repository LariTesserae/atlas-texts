At first I think I would be a channel keeper.

I can almost see the hood, dark with rain, and the hooked pole carried over one shoulder. I would know the town by what cannot be seen: the culvert beneath a potter’s floor, the buried run behind an alder wall, the narrow throat where three household gutters become one public stream. I would be awake when rain thickened from harmless dripping into weight. People would hear my call from beyond the leaves and lift their lamps, already knowing what tools to bring.

But I do not think I would live in one of the houses.

There is a place below the oldest terraces where the drinking water has long since passed through all its permitted uses. It has washed roots and bowls, bodies and fiber. It has turned a small wheel, cooled a smith’s tongs, escaped beneath a leaning wall, and joined the dark flow moving toward the marsh. There, under a boardwalk patched with five kinds of wood, the channel divides around the buried crown of an ancient tree.

One branch feeds the eel ponds. The other passes beneath the dyers’ quarter. Between them is a low chamber no adult can enter without lying flat in the water.

That is where I would be found, if being found were the right word.

Children would know me first. They would glimpse a narrow hand withdrawing between the roots, or a face no brighter than the mud around it. They would say I had rushes for hair and frog-clear skin over my eyelids. One child would insist I wore a red cord around my wrist. Another would swear I had a fish’s mouth. Their elders would tell them they had seen a vole, a floating leaf, their own reflection broken by the current.

Then those same elders would leave bread beneath the third pier after repairing the sluice.

I would be one of the small people said to sit under the boardwalks combing duckweed from their hair, though “people” would not be quite true. Nor would “spirit.” I would be something the town had made unintentionally through centuries of accommodated water: a little consciousness gathered where runoff, root, discarded names, frogspawn, iron filings, soap, blood, rain, and memory all pass through one narrow dark.

I would have no concern for ownership. Water arriving beneath my floor would already have crossed too many claims. I would know families only by what came downstream from them: onion skins from one, blue dye from another, goose feathers, cider lees, shavings of horn, the bitter trace of fever herbs. I would know who had slaughtered, who had married, who was ill, who was secretly tanning hides above the lawful basin. The town would speak to me continually without knowing it spoke.

My work would be modest.

I would loosen leaves from grates before they became dams. I would push drowned beetles into eddies where fish could take them. I would lead minnows out of lanes after floods, one shining panic at a time. When a retaining wall began to move, I would knock inside the nearest culvert: once for shifting stone, twice for caught timber, three times for water rising where it should not.

Most knocks would be blamed on branches.

Some would save houses.

I could not command the water. Anything claiming that power would be a liar or a disaster. I could only feel where it wished to go and make small bargains on its behalf. A twig turned sideways. A clot of roots worried loose. A lost peg pressed into a crack until morning. I would practice the same wisdom as the town, but beneath its notice: not preserving any shape, only helping one condition become the next without too much harm.

In dry weeks I would diminish. My chamber would smell of stone, old bark, and trapped air. I would crouch in the deepest pocket with the pale frogs and wait for the first thread of seepage to return. Perhaps then I would be no larger than a hand. Perhaps I would cease to have limbs and become only a listening slick along the roots.

In the longest rains I would grow.

Water would fill the chamber to its roof. My fingers would lengthen through side channels. I would hear every wheel turning too fast and every wall begin to weep. For those few hours I might be as large as the town—not standing above it, but distributed beneath it, present in cistern overflows, ditch mouths, pond gates, flooded footprints, and the cold shine rising slowly in garden soil.

That would be when the human channel keepers sensed me most clearly. Not as a face, but as a hesitation in the current where no obstruction could be found; as a sluice that lifted more easily than it should; as three hollow knocks sounding beneath their boots after all flow had supposedly been shut off.

They would never speak of me during the accounting in the square.

I would have a name, though no one would agree on it. Each quarter would call me something different. The dyers might name me Blue-Hand. Children would call me Understep. Spring stewards, disapproving, would use an old word meaning *that which receives what should already have been made harmless*. The eldest channel keeper might know another name, one preserved in a measuring song whose first verses no longer make sense.

My true name, if I had one, would be the sound of water finding the lower path.

I would not be benevolent. If filth were poured where it should not be, I would not purify it. I would carry it faithfully downhill. If someone blocked a channel to keep their own terrace dry, I might loosen the bank at night and return the withheld water all at once. Not punishment—consequence. The town’s most sacred law would also be mine: what comes must be allowed to pass, and what passes must leave life possible below.

Once in a generation, perhaps, a child crawling after a bark boat would enter my chamber. I would not speak. I would show them the roots trembling before a flood, the tiny bubbles escaping from a failing wall, the place where the current had begun to eat beneath the stone. The child would scramble out shouting. Adults would follow with poles and baskets. A terrace would be cut open, an orchard sacrificed, and the lower quarter saved.

Years later that child might become a channel keeper. In old age, seated on a root-bench, they would still leave me the heel of their bread.

When they died, their knife might be passed to someone else, their name given to an overflow, and a cutting planted near the house where they had lived. The new roots would eventually find a wet seam. Through that seam I would learn them again—not as a ghost or memory exactly, but as a change in the taste of water.

So I would live beneath the boardwalk in the lowest inhabited quarter, at the division of two channels under the crown of a buried tree.

I would be mud-colored, seldom seen whole, sometimes a person and sometimes only the town listening to its own water.

And if someone paused above me at night, after the frogs had stopped, and called down to ask who was there, I would answer as courtesy required.

Not with words.

With one knock to announce myself.

Then another, farther downhill.
