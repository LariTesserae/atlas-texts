At first, I think I would be one of the tall ones.

There is an immediate seduction in that: to move through mist with a garden raised along my spine, carrying red cups, silver moss, and sleeping amphibians from basin to basin. To inherit kinship as cuttings. To become visible only at great distance, a narrow procession crossing a swell while the cloud sea brightens beneath it.

But the tall ones belong too completely to distance. Their lives are measured in crossings, in blooms approached over years, in roads pressed into existence by generations of careful feet. I do not think I would possess that certainty of direction.

Then I imagine myself in a pool without a bottom: pale, eyeless, feeling the pressure of rain through meters of dark water. There would be older forests around me, drowned but not gone. I would know the world by currents moving through root walls. Once in a thousand years, a barrier of peat would soften and another population would enter—cousins made monstrous by separation. We would touch whiskers in the dark, unable to recognize our shared beginning.

That feels nearer.

But I would not be wholly aquatic. I would need the stopping of the rain. I would need the sudden immense silence in which every hidden thing becomes alert.

I think you would find me near an old erosion face, where the black layers of the plateau stand exposed above the clouds.

Not at the brink itself. Nothing sensible rests on the brink. I would be a little way back, beneath the rain-shadow of a great horizontal crown, along a branchway polished by innumerable palms. The route would end at one of the small living chambers woven into a dry swell. Behind it, the land would be dim green and crowded. Before it, through hanging roots, lightning would flicker upward from storms below the world.

The chamber would appear abandoned.

Its doorway would be nearly closed. Moss would have softened its outer ribs. A plant with warm, hand-sized leaves would grow across the roof. Inside would be a shallow bowl of dry fibers, a few translucent insect husks, and the mineral smell of collected rain.

I would be there, though at first I might not look like a being.

I might look like a dark-furred branch traveler asleep with all six limbs folded inward. Small enough to fit the chamber, old enough that the fur around my palms had silvered. My body would be motionless except for the fine tremor of throat and ribs. A juvenile might be curled beneath me, hidden so completely in my coat that an observer would count only one animal.

Yet the old animal would not, by itself, be me.

I would also be the colorless film on the palms. I would be the fungal threads living beneath the nails and the sweet knot lodged harmlessly in the roof of the mouth. I would be a pattern of taps remembered imperfectly by the muscles of the forelimbs. I would be the responsive tissue in the chamber wall, which swells when those taps are sounded. I would be something passed between animal, branch, room, and route so often that asking which one contains me would no longer make sense.

I would be a small, local memory of how to return.

During rain, the animal bearing me would travel with its family. It would test every branch before trusting its weight. At forks, it would press all four palms against the wood and listen to vibrations moving through leaves, trunks, grafts, and wet aerial roots. Most of what it felt would be ordinary: feeding insects, the slow contraction of a vine, water spilling from one canopy cup into another.

Sometimes, however, there would be a pulse from deep within the plateau.

Not a voice. Not a command.

Only a spacing.

Three slow pressures. A long stillness. Two more.

The buried chambers would beat like that when erosion exposed them. Roots would carry the disturbance badly, altering it at every junction. By the time it reached the branchways, it might take days to complete itself. Most creatures would never distinguish it from the movements of water.

The animal carrying me would stop.

Its family would freeze around it, assuming a hunter had entered the silence. But the old one would lower its fine forelimbs and answer the branch.

The reply would not be identical. It never would be. Each generation would add an interval, omit one, or strike with a different part of the palm. The branch would transmit the pattern into the chamber wall. The wall would tighten. Somewhere nearby, a fruit that had not opened for decades would split along one seam.

No one would know whether the plateau had spoken, whether we had answered, or whether two unrelated reflexes had merely happened to meet.

That uncertainty would be part of me.

I would not possess language in the airy sense. Nothing important here travels well through air. My thought would exist through contact: palm to bark, tongue to fur, rainwater to skin, root to buried rib. I would remember not pictures but sequences of pressure. A certain route would be the sensation of smooth bark followed by a hollow trunk, followed by cold moss, followed by three hours of branches bending downward. A dead family member would remain as a missing beat in a crossing rhythm.

My body would die often.

The old branch traveler in the chamber would eventually wake during a rainless interval and find that one of its grasping limbs no longer closed properly. It would continue for a season, perhaps two. Then, on a familiar branchway, it would settle its chest against the wood and begin tapping.

The juvenile would place its palms nearby.

Between them, the branch would tremble.

The lesson would take hours. Rain might begin and end several times. Predators might unfold in distant crowns. The old one would repeat the route-pattern until the juvenile answered with something close enough—not perfect, because perfect repetition is for dead things.

While they touched, the films on their skin would mingle. The juvenile would lick moisture from the elder’s palms and receive the mouth-knot. Spores from the chamber would already be caught in its fur. What had occupied one body would become possible in another.

Afterward, the elder might die. Its family might leave it suspended in a fork where vines would catch the corpse. Moss would cover its back. The branch beneath it would thicken, nourished by what passed downward.

I would continue, but altered.

Centuries later, the route itself might fail. A column might collapse without falling, hanging sideways until a forest rooted along its length. A basin might deepen beneath it. The old branchway would become impassable. Then the families carrying me would divide.

One version of me would remain among the original columns, tapping a memory of a path that no longer existed.

Another would cross on the backs of tall ones. The branch travelers would climb into the garden of an old tall one while it browsed beneath a crown. They would shelter among its shrubs, feeding on insects and red cup-fruit. When it joined a procession, they would be carried beyond every route they knew.

The journey might last years.

On the far side of a reed basin, the travelers would descend into an unfamiliar grove. They would tap the first branch they touched. The local insects would still. Sensitive leaves would close. Deep in the wood, thread-makers would receive the pressure and spread it until it weakened into nothing.

There, perhaps, a new living chamber would begin.

Not because the animal intended to build one. It might only sleep repeatedly in the same fold of branches. Its breath would loosen one graft and tighten another. Fibers shed from its coat would collect in the hollow. The palm-films would enter wounded bark. The remembered tapping would stimulate tissues that had never heard it before.

After several generations, the branches would arch over the resting place.

After several more, the opening would respond to heat.

Fruit would begin to appear after visits.

By then, no descendant would remember the crossing. They would regard the chamber as something found, ancient and inexplicable.

That would be my largest act: not construction, not design, but the carrying of a habit from one part of the plateau to another until the landscape acquired it.

So I would not be a messenger exactly. A messenger knows there is a message and distinguishes it from the body that bears it.

I would be more like a susceptibility.

A tendency in certain animals to stop at particular tremors. A tendency in certain branches to grow around sleeping bodies. A family rhythm that sometimes coincides with pulses from buried rooms. A living continuity made from many replaceable participants.

If given a name, perhaps it would mean **the knock that outlives the knocker**.

You would find me most clearly on a night when the rain had just ceased.

The chamber would open a little wider. The old animal inside would wake. Far below, thunder would turn beneath the cloud sea, sending pale light upward through the hanging roots. The juvenile would put its palms against the floor.

For a long time, nothing would happen.

Then, from somewhere under hundreds of meters of living ground, a pressure would arrive—faint, delayed, divided across innumerable roots.

The walls would leaf.

The animal would answer.

And for those few taps I would become almost an individual: not the creature, not the chamber, not the plateau, but the brief shape made by all three recognizing one another.
