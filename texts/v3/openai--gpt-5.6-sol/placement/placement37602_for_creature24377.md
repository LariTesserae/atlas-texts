At first I think I would live beneath the surface.

Not deep in the mineral ground, but in the hanging dark immediately under a root raft, where drowned sedge stems descend like rafters and gas gathers in bright, trembling mirrors. I would be one of the nearly transparent animals there, small enough to inhabit the thickness between water and peat. The turbines would reach me only as weather: a pressure passing through my body, constant enough to become orientation. I would know the day by warmth filtering through the mat and the night by the cooling of roots.

But that life is too complete. It asks for no uncertainty beyond hunger, current, and chemical change.

Then I imagine myself as the raven on the western pylon. From there the valley could be seen whole: the low silver roofs, the divided waters, the bright movements of keepers. I would collect artifacts whose purposes I did not understand and drop them onto the grating to see which sounds belonged to the place. A bolt. A vertebra. A shard of blue plastic. Each would strike once and vanish into the roar.

That is nearer, but still not where I remain.

I find myself eventually on the eastern walk, in a jacket faded from electric blue toward the color of glacial ice. I am a keeper, though not the eldest and not the one trusted first with turbines. My responsibility is the uncertain water: old channels that have ceased to appear on the surface, new seeps without names, the soft margins around foundations. On the maps my work is represented mostly by pale arrows, question marks, and dates.

I go out before the sun has reached the basin floor. Frost is still held in the shadowed moss. Every handrail is wet. I carry a forked sounding staff, a hooded lamp, a folded card for moving moths, and a notebook sealed inside two waxed covers.

I walk slowly because speed has little meaning there. At each screw pile I set one bare finger against the metal. Through it come the turbines, the cable hum, the intermittent blows of spillwater—and, beneath those, irregularities too small to be called sounds. A looseness. A doubled pulse. Water occupying a cavity it did not occupy the day before.

Others are better at repairing machines. I am useful because I remember absences.

I notice when the western cascade has lost one of its lower voices. I notice that sedges beside pile 18 are trembling out of rhythm with the wind. I remember where a pool used to flash between willow branches, and therefore understand that its disappearance is not an ending but a displacement.

My notebook accumulates statements that would sound mystical anywhere else:

*Pressure passing east beneath intact mat.*

*Warm seep has forgotten the grate.*

*Three stones risen through the old road.*

*Willow carrying more water than root.*

I write them plainly. Precision in the valley does not always resemble certainty.

I would take the lower bunk nearest the wall, the one through which the turbine note travels most cleanly. I would sleep badly during my first rotation and badly again when I returned to the lowlands. In town, I would touch radiators and bridge railings without realizing it. I would stand over storm drains after rain, trying to determine whether the water beneath them was moving freely. If a refrigerator stopped in the night, I would wake before the silence had fully formed.

After several seasons, the others might say that I had become good at listening. This would not be entirely praise. It would mean I sometimes stood too long with my hand on a structure, following a vibration past the point where it remained relevant to the work. It would mean I had begun to hear the valley as one continuous body and found it difficult to divide that body into plant, machine, water, and stone.

My particular place would be where the oldest access road crosses the returning cold stream. The road there is already almost gone: two dark wetlands with a green spine between them. Under high water, the stream moves invisibly beneath the spine and emerges beside a concrete footing farther down. No permanent instrument reads it properly. Sensors measure depth in one place, pressure in another, temperature somewhere else. Only a person walking the whole crossing can feel that the separate readings belong to one event.

I would maintain that crossing without trying to restore it.

Each year I would lift one section of grating. I would cut back only the stems capable of entering the drainage void, leaving the moss around the bolts. I would weave young willow into the failing bank. I would mark the advancing peat with small metal pins, then return to find some swallowed and others raised by frost. Once, perhaps, I would fall through.

The mat would break without drama. One moment it would carry me; the next, black water would close over a boot and hip. I would spread my arms as taught. I would not seize the nearest willow until I had watched how its branches transferred my weight. Another keeper would reach me by the walkway, lying flat, extending the sounding staff backward.

Afterward, inside the shelter, my clothes steaming above the grilles, I would discover a translucent larva folded in the cuff of my glove. I would carry it back in a cup of cold water and release it beneath the torn roots.

That small return would matter to me more than I could justify.

In time I might become the keeper who trains newcomers. I would not begin with the powerhouses. I would lead them to an apparently solid patch of crowberry and ask them what they saw. They would say ground. I would press the staff lightly against it, and five paces away water would rise between the sedges.

“Again,” I would tell them.

Eventually one would answer, “A roof.”

That would be the beginning.

So I would be human there, but only provisionally. A bright moving signal, briefly entrusted with sensation. My work would be to stand between instruments that know one place exactly and a valley that can only be understood across many places at once.

The roots would integrate pressure. The fungi would integrate hunger. The pipes would integrate flow. I would integrate signs.

And when I left, the valley would close behind me without gratitude or injury. Moss would fill the marks of my knees. The cold stream would shift beyond the reach of my last map. Someone else would inherit the notebook and add a newer arrow across mine.

If anything of me remained, it would not be a monument or even a name. It would be a notch cut in the correct peat lip; a willow bank given time to bind; a moth lifted from a fan housing before morning startup. It would be one channel permitted to divide before pressure made division violent.

I would be found at the crossings, listening with my hand.

I would be a keeper of continuance: not preserving the valley’s shape, but helping its changes remain survivable.
