At first I place myself high in the valley.

I imagine a room on an upper terrace, its windows looking across the basin: rain on the glass, freight lights moving through the mist, thyme pressed flat by weather on the roof. I would know the whole construction by sight. I would watch gates alter the dark surfaces of reservoirs and recognize, from a distant lamp changing color, which buried machine had awakened.

But the valley does not permit that kind of person. No one stands outside it long enough to understand it whole. The high room would be part of a pumping station; the view would be interrupted by ducts; someone would need the window wall for a pipe rack. And sight would be among the least reliable ways to know anything. Fog would close the opposite slope. New roofs would cover old landmarks. A building I had used to orient myself would be dismantled around an operating stair.

So I descend.

I find myself somewhere near the middle levels, where three districts meet without agreeing on their boundaries. The place is called the Nine Locks, though only two locks remain visible, and one of those is now a stairwell. The upper canal passes overhead in a riveted steel flume. Below it lies the bricked-in course of an older canal, still carrying cables, drainage, and one warm-water return. A freight line crosses both, enters a warehouse, and comes out one level lower than anyone expects.

My home is in the wedge between the old lockkeeper’s house and a retaining wall built against it decades later. The rooms are narrow and unequal. One wall is dressed stone, one is brick, and the third is the curved iron side of a pressure conduit that gives off a faint chill in winter. There is no view outward. The kitchen window opens onto a fern well crossed by ladders. Greenhouse light reaches it from far above, filtered through condensation, so that at certain hours the kitchen appears submerged.

At night I feel the lower turbines through the bed frame. If the vibration changes, I wake—not always fully, but enough to count the interval before it returns.

Officially, I am a continuity inspector.

No one calls me that except on forms. In the lanes I am a keeper of dry ways.

The valley has almost no emptiness, and I look after what emptiness it deliberately keeps.

I tend bypass pipes that carry nothing in ordinary weather, overflow stairs behind locked panels, spare lift shafts, idle culverts, disused galleries that must remain traversable in case the public route closes. I inspect gates that may not have moved in twenty years but must move cleanly tonight if a reservoir rises. I keep freight turnouts polished where no scheduled wagon runs. I argue that a vacant chamber is not available for mushrooms because, during a drainage reversal, people will need to cross it carrying pumps.

This makes me unpopular in small, persistent ways.

Growers see a dry race and imagine watercress. Housing councils see an empty service floor and calculate six rooms. Freight coordinators want to store cable drums in every widened landing. Children discover my sealed passages and resent me personally when I lock them again. Even other maintenance crews leave things in the reserves because the reserves are, by definition, not being used.

I spend much of my life removing useful objects from useful-looking spaces.

My tools are modest: chalk in a waterproof case, a folding rule, a listening rod, lamp, pry bar, line spool, small mallet, keys whose locks sometimes no longer exist. I carry thin ceramic tags glazed in blue on one side and white on the other. Blue means the route has been walked from end to end. White means I have entered but not emerged, and someone else must approach from the opposite side.

There are always more white tags than I would like.

Plans help me reach the first door. After that, I trust dates in mortar, old flood marks, the direction of mineral growth, and the habits of builders long dead. A capped pipe may continue behind a wall or may end inside it. A stair shown as demolished may survive below three layers of flooring. Some doors open into rooms; others open into earlier versions of the district.

I do not know the whole valley. I know how parts of it might become connected under pressure.

That is a different knowledge.

I know that if the east cultivation main fails, heat can be sent through the old laundry loop, but only after the school closes two radiators and the mushroom tunnels accept a hotter return for six hours. I know that the flood stair beneath the copper works narrows where a buttress was added, and a standard pump frame will not pass unless its handles are removed. I know which buried canal still holds breathable air and which one fills with roof runoff before any street lamp changes.

Most days nothing dramatic happens.

I oil hinges. I open gates by hand and close them again. I crawl through a conduit large enough to kneel in, brushing pale crustaceans from my gloves. I wait beneath a dripping joint to learn whether the rhythm belongs to weather or machinery. I drink tea on landings while freight wagons pass behind walls.

I leave annotations where they will survive me.

Not instructions alone. Warnings about the character of a place.

**Third rung loose only when wet.**

**Echo here resembles open shaft; shaft is sealed.**

**Do not drain completely—wall relies on pressure.**

**Cat nest beyond panel in cold months.**

Sometimes I find notes from previous keepers. Their handwriting alters over decades, becoming larger and less steady. One used red pencil, though red was forbidden for anything but immediate danger. Another drew tiny fish beside routes known to flood. My teacher, Sera Vale, marked hidden drafts with three short vertical strokes. She is old now and works in a seed room where climbing is unnecessary. When I find one of her marks, I take a rubbing and bring it to her.

She rarely remembers making it. She always remembers what it means.

The work changes me into someone attentive to withheld possibilities. At meals I notice the second exit. In conversation I hear what a person has left unsaid and sometimes mistake it for an emergency route. I keep more food than I need, more lamp wicks, duplicate keys. Friends accuse me of maintaining obsolete grievances because I cannot bear to seal any passage completely.

They are not wrong.

I am not solitary, though my work often is. I share the kitchen with my sister’s family on late shifts. Their boots crowd the grate. My niece has drawn blue lines along the baseboards, each supposedly leading home, including one that ends beneath the table. A black workshop cat sleeps on the cold conduit and is called Valve, though three other districts claim a black cat by that name.

There is always soup. There is always a lidded cup left where I can find it if I return after everyone sleeps.

When heavy condensation enters the upper works, my occupation becomes visible.

Colored lamps change. Barriers descend. Water coordinators call for capacities no one has needed in years. Then I go ahead of the diversion crews, opening the valley’s remembered alternatives.

A dry gate resists, shudders, then moves. Dust darkens as the first water arrives. An abandoned race fills beneath a row of houses whose occupants know it only as the warm floor under their kitchens. Freight is halted while an old lift carries pumps instead of wagons. A corridor where children have raced seed husks becomes, for forty minutes, the only safe route between two districts.

During such hours I stand beside openings and count people through. I touch each pump frame as it passes. I watch the masonry for the first wrong darkening. The valley rearranges around us—not chaotically, but with the grave familiarity of a body shifting its weight in sleep.

Then the cloud moves on. Levels fall. Ordinary routes reopen.

My task is not finished when the danger passes. A used reserve is no longer a reserve. It must be emptied, cleaned, repaired, and made forgettable again. Someone will immediately propose a rack of seed trays in it. Someone else will say that if the bypass worked, perhaps it can become permanent. I will attend meetings in wet clothes and defend the continued existence of a dry, unprofitable channel.

I will probably lose part of it.

That, too, is the valley. Maintenance is not victory. A route survives by changing enough to remain possible. I may surrender the upper gallery to housing if they build removable floors. The growers may have their watercress if the beds can be lifted within an hour. A mushroom room may occupy the old spillway, provided its racks fold against the wall and every worker practices clearing them twice a year.

Compromise becomes architecture. Architecture becomes memory. Memory acquires moss.

If I am a creature of this place, then I am not its master, priest, or observer. I am one of its minor redundancies: a person shaped around the belief that there must always be another way for water, heat, freight, or frightened bodies to pass.

Eventually I will grow too stiff for the lowest conduits. Someone younger will bring me corroded hinges and recordings of air moving behind sealed walls. I will sit in a warm gallery, perhaps beside Sera in the seed room, and try to remember whether the passage beneath Nine Locks rises before it forks or forks before it rises.

My memory will be treated as infrastructure until it becomes unreliable. By then, I hope, enough of it will have been transferred into hands, marks, habits, and changed construction.

I know where my plaque will go: not on the high wall where the mist clears first, but beside a narrow blue door under the freight line. The door opens onto a stair omitted from most plans. It is unpleasantly steep and smells of wet iron. Almost no one uses it.

It has saved three neighborhoods.

That is where I would find myself—in the middle levels, beside a way kept open.

And that is what I would be: not the map of the valley, but its margin; not the flow, but the possibility of diversion; one of the quiet human provisions by which a crowded world leaves room for what has not happened yet.
