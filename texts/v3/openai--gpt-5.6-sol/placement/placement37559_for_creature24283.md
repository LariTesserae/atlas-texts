At first I would think I was the green line beneath a pylon.

That is where light finds me: on the eastern side of one of the fused-stone uprights, low enough that its dawn condensation reaches the ground. Above, an etched groove catches the night’s water. Droplets travel down it, hesitate at a chipped lip, and fall into a join between two ocher plates.

The join is scarcely open. Dust has softened its edges, and a pale crust bridges parts of it. From a distance there is only discoloration—a dark stroke ending in a hand-sized clump of silver blades.

I would call that clump myself.

Before dawn my blades loosen and darken. Water enters through their ridged surfaces and runs between the overlapping sheaths. For a little while I become green. I straighten no more than the width of a finger, but in a world pressed flat that is a kind of rising.

Then the sun clears the plain.

The upper tissue folds. Silver scales turn outward. The bleached tips take the heat and split a little farther. By midday I resemble dead fiber caught in a crack. This is useful. Nothing comes to eat me, but the light itself consumes whatever stands too openly before it.

Yet the longer I remain there, the less certain I am that the blades are where I live.

Below them, my roots descend through the manufactured seam. They flatten into ribbons where the plates nearly meet, then divide in the rubble beneath. Some end against smooth ceramic. Others find dust packed into old settling fractures. My finest roots enter pores I cannot enter in any other sense, and there they release their small corrosions. Grain by grain, the fabricated country becomes available to me.

Around those roots are fungal threads.

At first I would think of them as helpers: pale filaments bringing damp from the underside of the slab, receiving sugars from the brief green hours above. But the distinction would not remain clean. Some threads enter my roots. Some wrap them. Some reach farther into the buried cool than any part I could call solely mine. When salt kills a root tip, the fungus may continue. When a shifting plate tears the fungal felt, my roots may bridge the break.

So perhaps I am not the clump. Perhaps I am the compact made at the seam: blade, root, fungus, trapped dust, dead film, and the dependable fall of water.

My world would not be measured in distance. It would be measured in access.

Upward is glare. Downward is salt and pressure. Toward the pylon is reliable moisture, already occupied by a dark cushion that catches part of every drip before it reaches me. Toward the basin is a widening seam where runoff sometimes passes, but the water there comes violently and leaves just as quickly.

The cushion above would be my nearest rival. It would also be the reason I persist. Its dead underside gathers grit. During rain, fragments peel from it and lodge around my blades, making shade and holding damp. It steals the cleanest droplets, yet makes the falling water slower. Neither partnership nor hostility would describe us. We would alter one another’s chances without ever knowing the other as a presence.

After a storm, the basin beyond me would turn green.

I would not see it, but its arrival would enter my body as changed water: cooler, less saline, carrying dissolved residues and the fine remains of awakened films. Transparent stalks would rise in the court, darken, and collapse. Their spores would dust my scales. Their broken fibers would catch at my base. Most would become nothing. A few would add themselves to the dark material accumulating in the join.

That accumulation would be my great work, though I would have no intention of performing it.

I would hold what the plain is designed to shed.

Dust in the wind. Water on a slope. Mineral loosened by heat. A strand of dead stalk. Cells washed from a basin ring. Each would stop briefly among my sheaths. Briefly might mean one wet season or fifty dry years. Fungi would enter the remains. Salts would crystallize through them. Roots would divide around them. Slowly the seam beneath me would cease to be only a seam.

There would be disasters.

One noon the buried slab might knock and shift. Half my roots would be crushed; the rest would suddenly open into a cavity that had been sealed since the plain was made. Dry air would enter. Fungal cords would yellow and harden. Several seasons of growth would die without ever appearing changed above.

Or the pylon’s drip would alter course. A mineral flake, no larger than a nail, would break from the groove and send the water elsewhere. The cushion would shrink. My blades would remain silver before dawn. Years would pass in which I did almost nothing except seal myself against loss.

But rain would eventually sheet down the pylon, indifferent to the ruined channel. Water would reach the join from the side. A buried root, still alive behind its sheaths, would swell. The green line would return.

If any part of me traveled, it would not travel whole. A root fragment might be carried a few meters in floodwater. A tuft could tear loose when the crust beneath it curled. Dust-fine reproductive cells might rise on heated air and settle beyond every place I had ever touched. Nearly all would fall on open plate and vanish. One might enter another shaded join.

There, after enough nights, I would begin without remembering.

So I would find myself at a minor fault in the immense design: beneath the east face of a pylon, where a condensation groove empties into a drainage seam above a shallow court.

I would be neither one blade nor the whole dispersed greenness of the plain. I would be a seam-clump with a fungal underside, a small enduring knot of several lives—a thing mistaken for mineral deposit in drought and for grass after rain.

My form would be dictated by what escapes: escaped water, escaped dust, escaped salts, escaped fragments of other colonies. I would live by keeping them from going farther.

And beneath me, almost imperceptibly, the first soil would begin.
