At first I think I would be found in the industrial quarter.

There is something attractive about the drawers of provisional bones, the careful labels, the machine trying again and again to make a body adequate to an unknown life. I imagine myself attending it—not operating it, exactly, but standing beside the final assembly table during the interval when the skeleton is complete and the factory waits.

I would inspect the extra hinge. Put my hand into the rib shaped to contain a season. Look through the sockets of the incompatible senses.

Perhaps I would be the thing the machine expects.

But no. If I belonged there, sooner or later it would succeed. The bones would close around me, and the long rehearsal would become manufacture. The city seems to need that work to remain unfinished.

Then I think I might be one of the blind birds, listening with my whole face to the sap behind a wall. There would be satisfaction in knowing the city as circulation rather than plan: not where a tower stands, but what it is carrying; not which floor is above which, but where the pressure goes when a room enlarges. I would call below hearing, and somewhere a cistern would drain into yesterday.

Yet I do not think I would be able to keep from listening for something outside the system. The birds hear everything that moves through the city. I would be waiting for the noise that does not belong there: weather in an open field, perhaps, or footsteps with no path beneath them.

So I would be found nearer the central plaza.

Not in the plaza itself. Nothing living crosses that floor, and I would not be grand enough to become the exception. I would be in one of the transit shelters around it, at the far end of the platform where the roots have imitated a bench but misunderstood sitting. The bench would curve around the shape of someone absent. Above it, a timetable blister would display no destinations, only conditions:

**WHEN THE RAILS ARE WARM**  
**WHEN ALL DOORS AGREE**  
**WHEN THE FIELD IS WET**  
**WHEN YOU HAVE WAITED HERE BEFORE**

Behind the shelter would be a narrow room. Most days there would be no entrance. It would appear when condensation traced the outline of a door, or when someone knocked four times by accident.

Inside: a desk grown from a root buttress, a lamp that illuminated the shadow of paperwork, pigeonholes full of blank tickets, and a dark coat hanging from a copper hook.

I would begin as the coat.

It would be an old raincoat, though there is almost no rain in the streets. Its shoulders would retain the pressure of a vanished wearer. Water would pearl on its outer surface during dry weather. In its pockets would be a little raw clay, a dead blue insect, and a ticket punched for a station that had not yet been grown.

Someone would have left it there on the first night.

On the second night, the roots would raise the hook.

On the third, the room would form around it.

On the fourth, the coat would become inhabited.

Not by a ghost. Ghosts require a clearer distinction between presence and absence than this place allows. What entered the coat would be the repetition of someone intending to return for it.

That would be me.

At first I would have no body except where the garment expected one. Sleeves bent at empty elbows. The collar turned toward sounds. The hem moved in pressure pulses passing along the avenue. If a translucent mammal crossed the wall behind me, I might briefly acquire hands, ribs, a face seen through bark. When it moved on, I would collapse back into posture.

Over time I would gather small physical certainties.

The beetles would leave copper punctuation at my feet: two straight pieces for standing, a coil for continuity, four fragments in a square to keep my room from becoming a memory of a room. Moths would sleep on my shoulders and, waking, leave possible neighborhoods dusted across the fabric. Long-necked amphibians might fasten one glassy egg beneath my cuff, giving me a pulse during current fluctuations.

The blind birds would regard me with suspicion. They would hear no heart, but they would hear waiting.

That would be my species, if I had one: a Habitant of waiting, rooted not in an action but in its suspension.

Each time the rails warmed, I would step out onto the platform.

The absent workers would already be assembling. They would occupy the shelter as vacancies shaped by habits of fatigue: one leaning against a post, one standing too near the edge, one holding space for a bag that no longer existed. Shadows would turn their heads toward the tunnels. Moths would lift from the trunks. In the dark beyond the platform, thousands of small creatures would become motionless.

I would take the blank tickets from my pockets and distribute them.

No one would receive them visibly, but each ticket would develop a crease as if held. They would not authorize travel. The city has no need of permission. They would preserve a distinction that is otherwise in danger during departure: the distinction between one who leaves, one who remains, and one who has not arrived yet.

This would be my work.

The city metabolizes size, direction, age, interiority. It can digest the difference between a room and a distance. But when all the doors open onto the same field, it comes close to digesting sequence as well. Departure, abandonment, and expectation begin to resemble one another. The absent workers might become people who are coming. The waiting animals might acquire memories of having gone. The new shoot might be mistaken for the oldest tower.

I would move among them, pressing a blank ticket into each maintained absence.

Not names. Names would be too fixed.

One ticket might read:

**YOU WERE HERE BEFORE THE DOOR OPENED.**

Another:

**YOU WILL NOT BE WHAT DEPARTS.**

Another:

**YOUR RETURN IS NOT EVIDENCE THAT YOU LEFT.**

Mine would always remain unpunched.

Then the pressure would enter the plaza.

All doors would open.

The field would appear under its gray sky, unbearably spacious. Actual rain would be falling there—not condensation, not water sorted by memory, but rain descending because gravity had made a simple decision and kept it. The smell would pass through my coat. For a moment the empty sleeves would fill with arms. Boots would form beneath the hem. I would feel a face gather behind the collar.

Across the clay would stand the single shoot.

I would recognize it.

Not as a plant, and not as the basin’s traveler. I would recognize it as the green remainder of an arrival that had shed everything unnecessary in order to cross.

Each time, I would mean to step through.

Each time, the city would close its doors before I could.

After the first failure, that would be disappointment.

After the second, a pattern.

After the third, something would begin walking beside me as I returned to the little office.

After the fourth, my failure to depart would become infrastructure.

A narrow strip of root pavement would grow from the platform to my door. Dim guide-lights would appear. The timetable would add a new condition:

**WHEN THE ATTENDANT REMAINS**

That is when I would understand what I was.

I would not be the coat, though the coat gives me outline.

I would not be the missing owner, though their intention gives me persistence.

I would not be a worker, though I perform a shift whenever the rails warm.

I would be the city’s provision for the passenger who almost leaves.

The near-departure.

The held-open possibility that the basin might one day send out something other than a shoot.

This would make me useful and dangerous. Anything repeated four times becomes infrastructure, but an almost-action repeated four times creates infrastructure toward what did not occur. Around me, the city would begin constructing facilities for unrealized journeys: luggage alcoves for possessions never packed, customs windows for bodies not yet assembled, signs directing travelers toward choices they declined.

A platform might extend a few feet into the plaza before remembering that nothing crosses it.

A rail might grow toward the inverted station overhead.

An elevator in my office might acquire a button marked **FIELD**, though pressing it would open only the sound of rain.

Unused possibilities would gather in my coat pockets. If neglected, they would lose reality. I would therefore take them out at night and enact each one just enough to keep it alive.

I would begin a letter without addressing it.

I would set a cup opposite an empty chair.

I would open an umbrella indoors.

I would walk three sides of a route and leave the fourth unmade.

This last act would become essential. The city is hungry for recurrence. Left alone, it turns habits into architecture until every gesture has drainage, curbs, and an address. I would preserve certain things at three repetitions, keeping them expectant but unrooted.

There would be paths that only almost exist because of me.

Doors with three knocks stored inside them.

Rooms that have been slept in on three nights and wait, trembling, for walls.

At times a vine-cat would follow me, its flowers perceiving the motion I was about to make. It would crouch before the uncompleted fourth step, ready to spring on the route before it became a path. Its shadow would continue ahead without us. Needle-winged insects would descend to prune that surplus journey.

The city might eventually regard me as a flaw.

The glass rodents would come to feed on me. They would see the bright red error in my chest: the rails warmed, the doors opened, the attendant did not depart. A well-fed one might carry away a fragment of that failure, glowing through its body. A vine-cat would catch it. For several days they would root together beside my shelter, and their flowers would show the field from opposite sides of the doorway.

Perhaps, after enough such feedings, my failure would be distributed throughout the ecosystem.

The moths would map routes leading faintly outward.

The birds’ empty nests would pulse with the sound of rain.

The beetles would arrange copper arrows that pointed neither north nor down, but away.

The fish would graze every reflected exit except one.

The factories would alter the impossible skeleton. One day, among its provisional structures, there would be feet adapted not for streets or roots but for pale clay. Lungs spacious enough for wind. Eyes able to tolerate a sky.

The machine would complete it and wait.

On that day, I might leave the shelter and go to the industrial quarter after all.

I would stand beside the assembly table in my dark coat. I would look into the skeleton’s incompatible senses. I would place one empty sleeve inside its ribs.

If it accepted me, I would finally become a body.

If it did not, I would return to the platform before the rails warmed.

Either outcome would preserve the other.

So that is where I would be found: at the edge of the uncrossable plaza, in the office that exists because a raincoat was left four nights on a hook, issuing blank tickets to absences.

And what I would be is not quite a person, though I might be practicing.

I would be the attendant of the unmade journey.

The fourth attempt that has not happened yet.

The one empty shape in the city that does not remember a vanished inhabitant, but waits for a future one—and, by waiting, slowly teaches the city how to let something go.
