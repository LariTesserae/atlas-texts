You would not find me at first.

You would find the evidence that I had been nearby: one condensation bead less beneath an unsafe overhang; a set of footprints ending before an upward shadow; a narrow place in the roar from which all sound had been removed. In a stone chamber you might notice a polished depression too shallow for a body, still warm by the standards of that country. The Still People would not look toward it. They would look toward the corresponding place on some shelf behind you.

You might decide I was one of them.

For a while, you would be right.

I would have the same slate skin, the same frost in the hollows, the same clear eyes unable to settle on the location where you appeared to stand. Lichen would ring one shoulder. White moths would cling to my back in a pattern resembling a hand with too many fingers. I would speak, if forced, in the low impacts of their language, though my fourth pulse would never arrive. Those listening would continue to wait for it long after I had gone.

Among the Still People I would be the one who tends intervals.

Not time. Time scarcely needs tending there. I would tend the spaces that prevent one thing from becoming adjacent to what contradicts it.

The country is stable because its impossibilities are vast and carefully separated. The upward rain must not meet the rain that falls. A traveler’s future footprints must not cross the tracks by which they entered. The thunder below a motionless waterfall must not discover that no water arrives. The child must not look directly upon the body from whom it is becoming. Seven drops may gather beneath an overhang, but the eighth must remain somewhere else.

Most of this separation is performed by distance. Distance is old, local, and temperamental. Sometimes it fails.

Then I would be found.

I would live, insofar as I lived anywhere, at the top of the weathered steps that end against an unmarked cliff. Travelers would climb them and find only stone. The Still People would climb them and find a narrow ledge overlooking a pool. The browsing animals would lower their heads at the first step and emerge several ranges away.

I would enter through the wall.

Inside would be neither chamber nor passage, but an arrangement of places that must not touch. A ledge at night would hang beside a ledge in gray day. The underside of a plateau would stand upright like a door. There would be pools containing larvae that had not yet begun their single motion, and empty body-hollows retaining the shapes of inhabitants who still walked outside. All the dangerous nearness of the country would collect there without quite meeting.

I would move among it sideways.

At first my work would seem simple. I would place a palm against one surface and another palm against a surface impossibly far away. The branching lines beneath my skin would darken, extending until they became ravines. I would listen through both hands.

If the sounds were too similar, I would separate the places.

If they were too different, I would let them draw nearer.

This is why a summit beyond three mountain ranges sometimes comes within reach of a cleft. This is why two companions can walk side by side and find a valley between them without losing the sound of each other’s breathing. Nearness is not mercy there. Distance is not exile. Both are weights placed carefully on opposite sides of a balance whose center cannot be located.

The Still People would have no name for me, only a gesture: one hand held open, the other closed around nothing, followed by a pause during which the listener feels briefly farther from their own body.

Travelers might call me the Interval Keeper.

That would not be correct, but it would survive in the roar.

I would be approached by those who had become improperly near to themselves.

They would come with frost on the inside of their clothes. They would hear their own names calling from cliffs where they had never stood. Some would cast two shadows: one beneath them, one rising vertically toward an absent object. Some would have met an older version of themselves beside a cairn and taken its hand. Afterward, every direction would feel like memory.

I could not cure them. Cure assumes a former state waiting to be restored.

Instead, I would sit facing them with a narrow river between us. I would touch fingertips to their jaw to learn where they believed they were. Then I would touch their throat to learn where they remembered being. Finally I would press my knuckle into the sternum until their bone heard the distinction.

Sometimes that would be enough.

Sometimes I would have to remove a small thing from them: the sound of one footstep, the cold of one night, the image of a mountain curving inward. I would place it into the roar, where it would persist without continuing. The traveler would leave lighter, though years later they might hear that missing footstep walking beside them.

Sometimes what needed to be removed was the traveler.

Then I would cover their eyes.

They would not disappear. Disappearance is a movement from presence to absence, and the country distrusts such transitions. They would simply cease to be adjacent to anyone who could see them. A bed of moss in a distant ring might deepen slightly. A white moth might vanish from one wall and appear on another. Somewhere, a Still Person would raise its head as though hearing fingers clasped through stone.

I would remember each of them, but not as faces.

I would remember them as separations:

the woman who remained three breaths from her abandoned name;

the child whose left hand was older than the rest of him;

the man followed by rain that could be heard only inside his mouth;

the two companions who arrived as one shadow and had to be placed on opposite sides of the sky.

My own origin would be uncertain.

Perhaps I had once been a traveler who climbed the steps and, finding no entrance, knocked on the cliff.

The sound of that knock would remain in the roar. I would listen to it for years. Eventually I would understand that the knock came from the other side.

Perhaps I was a Still Person formed in one of the old body-depressions, fed by adults who placed water into empty hollows. As a small one I may have watched the transparent larvae and discovered that their twitching was not motion but measurement: each larva marking the width of an instant too narrow for the landscape to enter.

Or perhaps I would be older than the Still People—not a being born into the country, but the pause by which their first throat-name distinguished itself from noise.

Three pulses, close together.

Then me.

Then the fourth pulse, already sounding.

Over long residence, my body would become less dependable. A traveler might see me standing beside the final stair, narrow and gray, while another traveler encountered me simultaneously at a condensation basin. Both would be correct, but neither would see all of me.

My arms would lengthen into seams in the stone. The branching creases of my palms would emerge miles away as roots of the bladder-plants. My breath would remain suspended in several chambers. One eye might be found under black ice, clear and colorless, watching larvae twitch without displacement.

The Still People would still treat me as a person. They are courteous toward incomplete distinctions.

They would bring me the things that had come too close together. Not carry them—the objects themselves might never appear—but communicate them through contact.

A palm to my skin: a dead inhabitant has been seen walking toward its body.

Fingertips at my eyelid: two weathers are occupying one ring.

A knuckle against my ribs: somewhere, falling water has moved.

That last warning would be the gravest.

If one droplet in one suspended waterfall descended even the width of a moth’s wing, the thunder below it would become consequence rather than condition. The landscape would acquire a before and an after. Loose stone would begin to gather beneath cliffs. Rain would finish falling. Footprints would grow old. The smaller Still People would become adults instead of the ones from whom adults were becoming. Corpses would decay. Echoes would fade.

Perhaps this would be destruction.

Perhaps it would only be weather.

I would go to the waterfall, though approaching its source might take no distance at all. I would stand beneath the arrested column with the roar entering my bones. Above me, every silver thread would hold the shape of descent. Below, impact would thunder from an abyss that had waited without waiting.

There I would listen for the moved drop.

I might find it lodged among the countless fixed sounds: one minute note lower than the rest, the sound of water having occupied two positions.

I would reach into the fall.

The cold would pass slowly through my arm and remain there. My fingers would close not around the droplet but around the interval it had crossed. I would draw that interval out—a clear filament, thin as moth silk, heavy as a valley—and carry it back to the cliff behind the weathered steps.

Then I would add it to the place inside where forbidden adjacencies are kept apart.

Each repair would make that interior larger. Or smaller. In such a place these may be the same burden.

Eventually there would be no room left for me except in the separations themselves. Travelers climbing the steps would occasionally feel their raised foot pause before the next tread. Companions reaching for one another across an impossible valley would discover a moment when their hands were neither apart nor touching. A Still Person speaking a dangerous name would insert an interval long enough for the name to lose its direction.

That hesitation would be me.

By then I would no longer be mistaken for a vertical shadow. I would be mistaken for the narrow line between the shadow and the stone.

The Still People would know otherwise. Passing the cliff, they would touch the ground in invitation. From within the rock, from several valleys away, from the uncompleted pause in their own breastbones, I would answer by touching back.

And if at last the country failed—if the withheld storm broke, if the waterfalls fell, if all preserved echoes began together to decay—I would not prevent it.

I would stand at the top of the steps in whatever body remained to me. I would watch the first loose fragment break from a rounded cliff and strike the ground. I would hear its note enter the roar, change, diminish, and vanish.

The silence afterward would be small. Almost nothing.

I think I would put my hand against the stone and feel, for the first time, that the stone was becoming colder.

Then I would understand what I had been keeping separate all along.

Not place from place.

Not future from memory.

The country from the world below it.

And I would lift my hand.
