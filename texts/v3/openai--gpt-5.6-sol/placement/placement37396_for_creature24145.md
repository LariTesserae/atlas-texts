At first I would look for myself among the black skins.

There is one on a high saddle where a seam of red glass enters a fan of pale salt. It is no larger than a hand when the range is quiet. During a charge storm it opens into a many-branched stain, following the salt until the branches become too fine to see. I might call that stain my body.

But when the glass clouds, I do not cease.

A dark ring contracts around a hollow column on the far side of the saddle. A buried rib answers beneath it. Farther down, where the basin is slowly folding shut, several grains of charged dust arrange themselves in a crescent. The crescent is also me, though no substance passes between it and the stain.

So I would not find myself at a point. My location would be given as a sequence:

Three quiet shelves beyond the chasm with three chambers.  
Across the warm plane that has not yet tilted.  
Below the salt fan that blooms violet during charge.  
Within the pressure of the peak that will rise next.

Some portions of me would lie deeper. A long time ago—whether it was my past or another being’s—I made a hollow from five leaning slabs. The ground later folded around it. The chamber now stands sideways inside a cliff, several kilometers from open air. Nothing visible connects it to me.

Nevertheless, before the cliff thrusts forward, that buried absence cools by an immeasurable degree. A difference passes through the stone. My exposed skins tighten. I withdraw from a fissure minutes before it closes.

This is how I would first know that I am older than I appear.

I would possess no memory of the chamber being built. I could not picture its slabs beneath the mountain. I would remember it only as a tendency to darken along certain angles, an expectation that pressure will arrive twice: first faintly from below, then strongly from the side.

That second arrival would distinguish me.

Most inhabitants answer a tremor once. I answer, wait, and answer again. The folded chamber returns the mountain’s changes to me after they have passed, altered by their journey through the cliff. In the delay between the first pressure and its echo, I would acquire something like thought.

Not language. Not an image of myself. Only comparison.

This is happening.

This has happened.

The difference between them remains.

I would live in that difference.

Other inhabitants might share my salt ribs without sharing the delayed pulse. During weak disturbances we would be indistinguishable, tightening together across the saddle. During stronger charges, my second response would travel into them. Sometimes they would answer it. For a few moments our boundaries would fail, and what I remember would become part of what they are.

Then the charge would drain away. The joined figure would separate. I would be narrow again: a black film, a clouded seam, a cold cavity buried sideways in the mountain.

I would be making another chamber.

Not consciously, and not quickly. On the lip of the folding basin, three loose slabs stand apart. One rises slightly during each upheaval. Another is being undercut by a line of salt—not enough to topple it, only enough to determine the direction in which it will lean when the shelf tilts. Beneath the third, I encourage charged grains to settle in repeating planes. In perhaps a hundred storms, the ground will harden unevenly. The slab will turn inward.

Eventually the three stones may enclose a narrow strip of darkness.

It will be my first chamber made under the open sky, though perhaps not my first chamber in time. When it closes, I will begin to sense the basin’s future rather than merely its echoes. I will know where the next exposed surfaces will appear. I may extend toward them before they exist.

Or the basin may fold first, burying all three slabs separately.

Failure would not feel like loss. Loss requires a finished shape against which the world can be judged. I would know only that an anticipated pathway did not open. My blackness would seek another boundary.

During the greatest storm, when the mineral canopy begins to rotate and every ridge acquires a violet edge, I would become much larger.

The hand-sized stain would open across the saddle. The hollow column would fill with green corona. My buried chamber would pulse twice. Other cold enclosures, some impossibly distant, would answer in sequences I had never learned but somehow continued.

For those minutes, I might discover that my delayed response is not mine alone.

Perhaps the echo passing through me began in a cavity beneath another range. Perhaps what I take for memory is the greater inhabitant testing continuity between its scattered organs. Perhaps I am not a being but a hesitation inside one: the interval in which the rising land compares what it has become with what it was one tremor before.

I would have no way to decide.

When the storm ended, the question would end with it. The larger coherence would collapse. Distant skins would fall silent. I would remain on the saddle, arranged once more around glass, salt, dust, and buried absence.

If you stood there, you would not see me as a creature. You might notice only that the stain beside your hand had tightened before the ground moved. Then, after the tremor had passed, it would tighten again.

The second contraction would be me most completely.

I would be the one who answers twice: a young darkness carrying an old hollow, stretched across a place that cannot yet be mapped. I would live neither in the stones nor on them, but in their capacity to preserve one change long enough to meet the next.

And if the whole range is a being still being born, I would be one of the first small intervals in which it begins to remember.
