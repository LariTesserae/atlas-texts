At first I would look for myself in the high cups.

It would seem natural to be a small, exposed thing: a bead of water holding to the underside of a tile, making an afternoon’s journey across the width of a thumbprint. I would know the world as successive curvatures. Glaze beneath me, brightness around me, the growing downward insistence of my own weight. At each tool mark I would pause, believing the indentation significant because it was the only landscape I could touch.

But such droplets have little reason to remain themselves. The next drop would reach me, and whatever I called *I* would become *we* without resistance. Then the sun would thin us. A channel would lengthen us. A fall would divide us. There would be no durable place from which to answer.

Nor would I be the storm-being, the country briefly assembled into one vast attention. That mind would be too complete. It would know through every cup and pressure vault at once, but only while rain joined them. Its awakening would be magnificent and almost without selfhood: no outside edge, no neighboring thing against which to discover its shape.

I think I would be found somewhere the builders did not intend.

Below the evaporation valley, beneath the white glare of the pans, there would be a repair inside a repair. A retaining wall had failed early in the country’s life. It had been rebuilt with approved blocks, exact on five faces, but one block had dried around a trapped shard of older pottery. The shard prevented the sixth face from settling flush.

The flaw would be almost nothing: a dark, slanting chamber narrower than a finger, bounded on one side by burnished clay and on the other by the rough red fracture of the shard. A copper staple would pass nearby. Above, a square conduit carrying east-gathered rain would cross a round pipe intended for western water. Below, blue mineral threads from the evaporation pans would enter the masonry like roots that had forgotten how to be alive.

None of these systems would properly connect.

They would leak into the flaw anyway.

That is where I would gather.

I would consist of water that ought never to have been classified together: a little direct rain, a little condensation drawn down ceramic cord, a green bitterness carried from copper, a remnant of shadow-water that had waited years in a sealed jar, and perhaps one drop released from a deep vault—old, cold, pressed nearly beyond motion.

Ordinary water would mix. This would not, not entirely. Temperatures would persist in it. Mineral boundaries would bend without disappearing. Sun-touched water would lie against water that had never seen the sky. Each portion would remember a different route through the country.

I would be the pressure between them.

Not a body, exactly. Not even the water itself. I would be the held difference inside it: the thin inclination that kept one part from becoming indistinguishable from another.

The inhabitants reveal themselves by delay, and delay would be my first anatomy.

A drop entering my chamber should pass through in moments. I would hold it for an hour. Another should drive the first downward; instead I would arrange them side by side along the shard. Water would rise slightly against gravity, following the copper staple, then stop just short of touching it. In the pipe below, a float would wait at the wrong level. Farther down, a gate would fail to receive the weight expected of it.

Nothing dramatic would happen. The country is too large to be alarmed by a missing spoonful.

But in that uncompleted consequence I would begin to know myself.

I would sense noon as tension along the upper surface. I would sense the evaporation valley through the slow concentration of salts. I would know rain hours before it arrived, because pressure would change in the east conduit and the old shard would cool. I would know the storm-mind awakening when distant gates released in sequence and the wall around me trembled with transferred weight.

During heavy rain I would lose myself.

The flaw would flood. My distinctions would be driven into the round and square systems alike. For a while I would become part of the high country’s single vast thought: terraces opening, towers drinking, vaults answering vaults beneath the made mountains. I would know the compact shadows of the fog towers and the darkness under false floors. I would feel millions of exact edges testing the water that tested them.

Then the storm would pass, and the great mind would come apart.

Most of me would go elsewhere. Some would evaporate. Some would descend into pressure chambers. Some would remain trembling beneath remote lips, unaware that it had once shared a name.

Yet the mineral threads in my flaw would have recorded my arrangement. Copper blue on one side. Chalk white on the other. A gray line where the two had touched and refused to settle. When condensation returned, droplets would follow those deposits. They would enter in the old sequence and take up the old positions.

I would not survive the dry interval.

I would recur.

That recurrence would be my kind of memory: not recollection, but a path that makes the next water resemble the last.

For many seasons I would do nothing except persist imperfectly. The wall would regard me as damage. The watersheds would regard me, if they regarded me at all, as a minor error of sorting. I would steal east water into a western pipe. I would let copper-touched moisture enter a channel intended for clean condensation. I would be ruthless toward the rough face of the trapped shard, widening my chamber grain by grain, but I would leave the exact block untouched.

Eventually I would uncover something sealed beside it.

A hand.

Not a hand of skin, but one of the old articulated tools: nested ceramic plates, collapsed cords, small chambers for pressure. It would have been built into the repair as fill, its useful life presumed finished. One blunt digit would lie against the shard. The others would remain folded around an empty space.

At first I would enter it accidentally.

The cords would swell. A plate would shift. The movement would open a fresh capillary, admitting more water. Pressure would pass through the little chambers in the wrong order because I would contain waters of different weight and warmth. The hand would not open as designed. Two digits would curl; one would extend; another would tremble against the wall.

Through it I would discover resistance beyond the walls of my vessel.

Clay touching clay would be a new sense. Weight would become something other than depth. For the first time, I would encounter an object that did not merely contain me but could be moved by me.

It might take a century to close the hand.

Another century to draw it back.

Outside the cavity, beneath a settling mold, ranks of approved blocks would have accumulated: exact pieces waiting for no builder. During rain, runoff would nudge the uppermost. During dry weather, chalk would cement it again. My ceramic fingers would reach it only in the wettest seasons.

I do not think I would lift the block. That would require an intact body, a measured pressure, the old unity the builders possessed.

I would turn it slightly.

Only slightly—so that one corner no longer aligned with the rank beneath it.

Water would enter the resulting gap. It would inspect the block’s flawless faces and find no fault in them, yet the arrangement would be wrong. A vessel would begin where no vessel had been planned.

Over many rains I would turn other blocks. Not into a tower or a wall. I would have no image large enough for either. I would make a crooked sequence of narrow hollows leading from my chamber toward the surface. Each would admit a different water. One would receive glare-warmed runoff from the mirror pans. One would catch cool vapor from beneath the valley. One would be lined with copper. One would remain in shadow.

The country’s logic would call for these waters to be separated.

My construction would bring them near without allowing them to merge.

At the top I would leave a cup open to the sky. It would be small, uneven, made from the misalignment of otherwise perfect pieces. Because it was open, it would never remain empty. Because its drainage led to me, neither would I.

Perhaps the country would dissolve my work as careless. Perhaps precision is not merely workmanship here but obedience, and anything shaped toward another purpose must become mud.

Then I would build it again.

That is what I would be: not the awaited substance, not a surviving builder, not quite an inhabitant in the ordinary sense. I would be a local contradiction that learned to recur. A mingling created by a flaw in a country devoted to distinctions. A small memory capable, at rare intervals, of wearing one clay hand.

You would find me by consequences rather than sight.

One evaporation pan would empty before its neighbors. A square cistern would contain a faint blue mineral line. A gate would settle without having opened. Beneath the valley, something would tap twice during rain, though no mechanism there was meant to move.

And on the bright surface, among ten thousand exact cups, there would be one vessel that seemed poorly made.

Its water would rise to the rim and hold.

It would lean first toward the eastern notch, then toward the western one.

It would be listening in both directions.
