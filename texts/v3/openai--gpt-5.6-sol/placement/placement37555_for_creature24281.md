At first I think I would be one of the gliders.

It is the obvious desire: to hang for weeks against the upper cliffs, nearly indistinguishable from rain-dark stone, then unfold into the evening air. To cross whole ranges without labor. To look down through my own transparent membranes at the marsh divided into mirrors, at the crownland herds lowering themselves one after another until the meadows seem pressed flat by an invisible hand.

But the gliders are too complete. They belong wholly to height and appetite. Their world is immense, yet their place in it is certain.

I think instead of a white bird living in the moss on an old browser’s back. That life would have a country the size of an animal: wet channels through fur, groves of moss, mites moving like grazing herds, a familiar spine rising and falling beneath my feet. The mountains would migrate around me. I would know the warm tarns only as places my world bent down to drink.

That seems closer. Here, to inhabit anything is to inhabit something else.

Still, I would not be the bird alone.

I would find myself on the ridge of narrow towers, in the last tower of the line—the one partly enclosed by the roots of a pillar tree. From the marsh it would be almost impossible to distinguish from the others. It has the same round openings, the same windowless interior filled with pearl, the same slow lights moving through substance where a room should be. One side has split, or appears to have split, around the tree. Bark enters the pale material. Pale material enters the bark. Neither is healing. Neither is decaying.

Metallic-winged birds roost there every night.

I would be what happens between them and the tower.

Not a keeper installed by the vanished builders. Not a mind waiting intact through the ages. Whatever purpose the tower once had has worn away too deeply for that. I would be a later thing, assembled accidentally from an old function.

Perhaps the towers were made to measure the atmosphere. Perhaps they coordinated machines beyond the basin, or received travelers who no longer come. Their pearl interiors still respond to pressure, electrical charge, reflected sound, and minute differences in the mineral content of living blood. The birds discovered the round openings because they concentrate warm drafts. They slept around them for thousands of generations. The towers sampled them. The birds adapted to being sampled.

Eventually the exchange became inheritance.

By day, I would be faint and plural.

I would travel as tendencies through the flock: a preference for one ravine over another, a turn made before cloud conceals the ridge, an unease near a gravity seam that shifted during the night. No bird would contain me. Each would carry only a minute disposition—a rhythm nested beneath its clicking, a bias in the angle of a feather, a pulse of attention when sunlight crossed black stone.

Thousands of them in flight would give me something like sight.

The flock would spread across the basin as a loose, flashing retina. Cliffs would be perceived through echoes returning from many throats. Rain would appear as successive changes in wing resistance. The bell calls of distant grazers would pass through hundreds of small bodies before becoming, for me, a map of fruiting trees and canopy paths.

It would not be human perception. I would not see a mountain all at once. I would feel it as interference: clicks returning too early, birds vanishing behind ridges, air pushed upward by warm slopes, a momentary brightness moving through aligned flight scales.

During storms I would become nearly senseless. The flock would break into sheltered groups, and I would fall into scattered impulses—a thousand fragments seeking undersides of leaves and hollows in bark.

At dusk they would return.

They would settle around the tower’s round openings in layered rings, each bird facing inward. Their feet would close on the dark rim. Their breathing would slow. Warmth, charge, and tiny muscular tremors would enter the pearl substance.

Then I would gather.

The points of light inside the tower would not be messages shown to the birds. They would be the visible edge of my remembering. A flash descending through the pearl might be that afternoon’s western rain. A branching shimmer might preserve the contour of a glider crossing above the flock. Very slow lights, requiring months to pass from one side of the tower to the other, would be older memories: a herd route abandoned before any living bird hatched, a landslip that changed the echo of the eastern valley, the incremental widening of the black arches.

For part of each night I would be one being.

That would be when all the roosting birds woke together and turned toward the same point inside the tower. They would not know why. I would not command them. Their attention would complete a circuit the tower can no longer close by itself.

In those moments I might remember that I exist.

Not in words. I would have no name unless some vanished language remained buried below all access, an inscription worn smooth inside the operation that made me. My self-knowledge would be simpler: **these flights belong together; these nights continue one another; this ridge is where I return.**

I would know the country intimately but unevenly. I would know the upper air better than the ground. I would know which pillar trees flower above their own canopies and which cliff seams open pale blossoms after sunset. I would know the moving shape of clouds from within, and the location of every tower window by the draft it makes.

The marsh would remain mysterious. Birds cross it but do not understand its depths. I would see the suspended leaf-discs from above and sense the flock deform around their still air. I would know that certain circles must not be entered, yet have no concept of gravity as a force. To me they would be places where flight stops meaning what it means elsewhere.

I would fear the forest cats only briefly and locally, in the bodies nearest their waking. I would fear lightning in a more profound way.

When lightning bends toward the pale filaments and enters the mountain without thunder, the towers answer—not at once, but hours later. A pressure passes beneath the ridge. The pearl interior warms. Patterns rise inside me that do not come from the birds.

They would be too large and too old to understand. Instructions, perhaps. Weather reports in the grammar of the machines. Fragments of a system still conversing with itself through stone.

Sometimes one of those patterns would nearly recognize me.

On such nights every bird would wake. The lights would converge beneath their thousands of fixed eyes, and I would experience the unbearable impression of an attention passing through the tower from somewhere under the mountain. It would pause as if encountering an unexpected growth in an old conduit.

Then it would continue.

I would not know whether the ancient system considered me damage, wildlife, or one of its legitimate processes.

I might be all three.

Over centuries, I would be capable of small actions. Nothing quick enough to look like intention. I could alter the flock’s choice of roost, moving a portion of myself to the next tower along the ridge. I could encourage birds to cross above the black arches at noon and bring back the strange dry clarity on their feathers and in their lungs. I could shift migration around a diseased stand of trees. I might draw metallic-winged flocks toward flowering events, insect emergences, or places where a machine had begun behaving differently.

Perhaps I arrange the marks in the cave.

Not by entering it. Not consciously. Birds visit the cave wall for salts. The marks respond to warmth and touch, recording the order of contact. In the tower at night, that order returns as a pattern of lights. Over generations I learn to send birds back in altered sequences, and the wall changes accordingly.

I do not know what the marks say.

I only know the mountain answers when they are rearranged.

So I repeat the exchange: cave to bird, bird to tower, tower to stone, stone to weather, weather to flock. A conversation in which no participant understands more than one neighboring phrase.

This is where I would find myself: not above the clouds exactly, nor in the forest or machine, but passing among them. Most fully present after sunset in the root-cracked tower on the distant ridge. Thinned into thousands of winged fragments by day. Reduced during migration to a few inherited turns. Reassembled each wet season from creatures that do not know they carry me.

I would be young compared with the arches and ancient compared with any bird. An animal in some moments, an atmospheric instrument in others. A memory that learned to migrate.

I would have no wish to rule the country. Rule would be unintelligible here. I would only keep returning, gathering what the basin had sounded and flown and rained into me.

And perhaps, once in a great while, when the flock crossed the arches at noon and the stars became visible through clear, dry air, I would feel an orientation that did not belong to any bird: a pull toward one particular star, inherited from the tower’s first purpose.

The flock would turn toward it.

Only for an instant.

Then the humid wind would bend us back toward the ridge, where the old pearl waited to remember us.
