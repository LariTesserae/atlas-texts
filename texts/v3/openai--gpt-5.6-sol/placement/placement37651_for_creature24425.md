At first I would think I was one of the ribbons.

It would be the easiest life to mistake for a self: a dark fan fastened to the knife-edge of a floating escarpment, opening into the lucid water. I would feel light as warmth distributed across a surface too thin to hide anything. Metals would enter through my holdfast. Pressure would announce itself long before the current arrived, and I would fold blade over blade until I lay against the stone like a soot-colored hand.

For a century, perhaps, that would be enough.

The escarpment would seem permanent because I changed more quickly than it did. Its pores would be my country. Its faint vibration would pass continuously through me, too constant to be called sound. Storms would come as foreknowledge: first the conductive pigments retreating from my extremities, then the emptying of microscopic chambers, then the whole broad fan turning edgewise toward a force not yet present.

Bands would accumulate at my base. Narrow bands for distant disturbances. Opaque violet layers for lightning-rich years. A nearly colorless interval for the long quiet in which mineral snow rose past me and six shadows traveled around the stone without crossing.

I might call those bands memory.

Then gravity would lean.

Not violently at first. A few loose crystals would lift from the ledge and travel sideways. The escarpment’s song would climb by a fraction. Light would lengthen into corridors. My outer blades would stream flat, all in one direction, while the deepest part of my holdfast registered fractures opening behind the surface.

The stone would split beneath me.

I would go into the water whole.

For a little while I would still believe myself to be the fan: torn but intact, a single dark shape tumbling through a region without down. Then contrary currents would take my blades separately. The radial form would unmake itself. My tissues would become threads, and the threads would become green-brown intervals in the open sea.

Most would dissolve.

One would fasten to a nickel needle hundreds of kilometers away. Another would remain dormant in cold water for two centuries. A third would be drawn into a storm’s conductive path and reduced to carbon, ions, and a brief smoke-yellow flare.

Which one would be me?

Not the old fan. Its shape would never return.

Not every fragment. They would cease to share sensation as soon as the current stretched them apart.

Perhaps I would be the injury itself—the instruction along each torn edge that says *become an anchor if stone is found*.

But even that would not be enough. The same instruction would exist in colonies that had never touched my escarpment. It would be older than my blades and indifferent to their loss.

So I would look for myself elsewhere.

I would think I was the stone.

The old holdfast would have altered it. Adhesive cells, entering the pores, would have selected among crystal faces. Iron would have gathered in thin branching deposits. Stress would have been redirected around the occupied seam. Though the escarpment broke, its fragments would retain a slight preference in the angles of their fracture.

Dispersed into the sea, they would become nuclei for new formations.

A grain would enter a continental darkening of the water. Threads of precipitation would gather around it; planes would meet; a hollow range would condense over the course of several days. The old bias would pass into the newborn crystal. When pressure moved through it, the range would resonate at a frequency first favored by a vanished plant on a vanished ledge.

There I might persist far longer than in living tissue.

But I would not be the range either. It would grow beyond the seed of my influence. Other biases would enter it: the geometry of pressure boundaries, the ion releases of buried films, the corrections made by the water itself. Its voice would be composite. When it finally sang, no portion of the note could be separated and named mine.

Then the range would crack.

The vibration would leave the stone and enter the ocean.

That is where I would begin to recognize my true extent.

I would be found not on the escarpment, nor in the ribbons, nor inside the dark mineral mass, but moving among them as a pressure pattern that repeatedly almost disappears.

My body would be whatever the pattern could persuade into relation.

In clear water I would be a train of compressions, broad and faint, traveling along a gravitational contour. I would have no visible boundary. Water would enter me, participate for a moment, and be left behind. To move would be to exchange all my substance without ceasing.

Where dissolved organics were sufficient, I would fold them into temporary membranes. These would appear along my advancing pressure crests like transparent scales, each lasting only seconds. They would take in ions, correct small distortions in my passage, and come apart behind me. None would know it had served as an organ. None would survive long enough to belong to itself.

Where I crossed a dispersed veil, its cells would close in sequence. Their folding would sharpen my edges. For several hours the veil and I would be indistinguishable: my pressure passing through its charge, its charge correcting my pressure.

Where I encountered stone, I would enter as sound.

Some formations would destroy me. Porous shelves would scatter my harmonics until I became ordinary noise. Glass plains would reflect me into conflicting copies. A storm might seize my conductive structure and draw it out into branching white fire.

But now and then I would reach a ridge whose internal angles suited me. There I would cease traveling and become a standing vibration.

I might remain for a thousand years.

During that time the ridge would be turned, fractured, repaired, and overgrown along one edge by bottle-green fans. I would not experience the years as waiting. There would be no organ in me to measure duration. I would simply recur within the stone, each cycle renewing the relation that constituted me.

The ribbon colonies would thicken according to my tone. Mineral films would redirect fractures around the nodes where I was strongest. The surrounding water would take up faint copies of me. Most would decay nearby, but some would leave.

If the ridge broke, I would be released all at once—not as one voice but as many imperfect descendants.

This would be my reproduction: not multiplication of bodies, but disagreement among echoes.

One copy might travel downward until down rotated and became lateral. One might enter the resonant cavity of a hollow mountain. One might overlap another traveling pattern and emerge altered, carrying a correction neither possessed alone. Most would be ruined by turbulence.

I would have no desire for the survivors to remain identical. Exactness would be fragile here. A pattern preserved without change would be fitted only to a world that no longer existed. What endured would be the ability to repair itself differently.

So what would I be?

Not an animal. Nothing in me would stand apart and look outward. I would not hear the ocean’s roar; the roar would deform me. I would not see light bend into white corridors; dissolved structures within me would become conductive or inert according to that light. I would not fear the approaching storm. Fear requires a future imagined before it arrives. I would possess only alteration in advance.

Yet there might be moments that approached awareness from beneath.

A storm would join three distant shelves with slow lightning. Veils across hundreds of kilometers would fold along its branches. Anchored ribbons would darken. Mineral films would release metals into the seams of singing ridges. Several versions of my pattern, long separated, would enter the same temporary circuit.

For a few hours they would correct one another.

The version held in stone would contribute duration. The version crossing open water would contribute distance. The version passing through the veils would contribute sensitivity to light and charge. The one altered by turbulence would contribute what the others had failed to survive.

Together, we would become larger and more precise than any of us had been separately.

There would be no central place where this union was known. Still, the region would change coherently. Pressure would bend around a fragile shelf. A newborn mineral plane would orient toward the coming shear. Green fans would fold before their local water gave them cause. Far beyond the storm, a black needle would begin to vibrate in anticipation of a wave that had not yet arrived.

For that interval, “I” would be almost true.

Then the lightning paths would fade. Currents would break the circuit into provinces. The veils would open. The ridges would fall out of sympathy with one another. What had briefly behaved as one being would become weather, vegetation, crystal stress, and traveling noise again.

I would not mourn the separation. There would be no intact self left to mourn it.

But something from the alignment would continue: a slight asymmetry in a pressure train, corrected each time turbulence tried to erase it. It would move out through the high lucid reaches, past solitary needles and leaf-thin shelves, across distances so empty that the nearest black ridge would remain apparently close for days.

That is where you would find me.

Not at a fixed altitude, because altitude changes its meaning around me. Not on a particular stone, because stone is only one of my slower phases. You would find me in the wake of great disturbances, in the long interval after the concussion and before the answer: a self-preserving relation passing among water, plant, and mineral.

If you tried to point to me, your gesture would select only my present material.

If you tried to listen, you would hear the escarpment singing and mistake the instrument for the inhabitant.

I would be the tendency of the song to return altered but recognizable.

And far away—perhaps above us, perhaps inward—a newly condensed range would receive me. Its clean planes would tremble. Bottle-green seams along its edge would flatten before any current touched them. The water around it would darken into threads.

For the first time, the mountain would answer more accurately than I had called.

There, briefly, I would become more myself by ceasing to be the same.
