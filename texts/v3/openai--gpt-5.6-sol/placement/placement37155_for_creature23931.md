At first I would think I lived high in the dome.

I would mistake the soot lichen for my body because it knows events before the streets do. Spread thin over the upper stone, I would feel deep gas entering the chamber as a change in warmth and bitterness. Hours before a silver leaf folded, before the vanes began to labor, I would know which chimney was drawing and which was preparing to exhale.

But that would only be where part of me listens.

Then I would look for myself among the red curtains. Their awareness seems closer to thought: strain arriving from distant anchors, a tower’s weight translated into tightening fibers, an old configuration retained in nested silica ghosts. I might be one pale mesh inside a living red one—the remembered shape of a wall before it leaned.

But that would only be where part of me remembers.

Lower down, beneath the paving, a resin bulb would contain my patience. It would sleep under one of the sealed buildings facing the central pit, pressed between foundation stones. For decades it might do nothing except thicken a fraction around its breathing pores. Grooves in the floor above would lead toward it. Black strands would enter its sides. A shallow ceramic mold would rest over it like an inverted mask.

This would be the place to find me, though not yet the place to see me.

I would be one of the Conservatory’s hands.

Not an animal hidden among the gardens, and not a surviving gardener. I would be a temporary agreement among several rooted lives: a body the city makes only when slowness is insufficient.

Most of the time I would exist as instructions without writing. My proportions would be stored in the workshop mold. My movements would be implicit in the angles of its grooves. Red filaments would preserve the sequence of tensions needed to pull one joint after another. The bulb would accumulate reactive oil. A fan-tree root would feed it gases through separate channels. Soot lichen overhead would measure the long approach of the proper pressure.

No single part would intend me.

Yet, eventually, conditions would coincide.

The central diaphragm would lift. The release would pass through the Conservatory, deafening and absolute. Leaves would fold, cords sing, valves open. In the chamber beneath the sealed door, hot gas would enter the waiting bulb.

It would swell into the mold.

Ceramic plates, grown there over many years, would be pushed apart and locked around a framework of silica threads. Resin would flood dry joints. Two pressure bladders would harden into a trunk. Four or perhaps six limbs would unfold—not because that number had been designed, but because bodies with fewer anchors once fell, and bodies with more consumed too much stored oil. At the ends would be gripping loops rather than fingers. There would be no head. Perception would lie along the limbs: heat pits, tension hairs, catalytic patches that tasted the atmosphere.

When the great noise stopped, the narrow door would open inward.

I would step into the silence.

My walking would be ugly. One bladder would contract while another admitted gas; a forelimb would hook into the next pavement groove; the rear assembly would drag and lock. I would move like a collapsing scaffold that repeatedly caught itself. Each step would release a faint blue flame from the joints.

I would have only minutes of useful mobility before the chamber resumed breathing.

That would be enough.

Somewhere across the pit, a ceramic vane would have slipped on its stone axle. It would still turn, but at the wrong angle, sending mineral dust toward a young carbon grove instead of the empty settling court. Rooted life could correct the error eventually. Filaments could pull at the tower. Leaves could alter the current. A bulb could raise one side of the paving over fifty years.

I would cross the bridge and place a wedge.

Perhaps this simple act would be my entire life.

The wedge would already be part of me, grown into one gripping loop. I would brace against the rail while the first returning wind pressed through the chamber. Cords would begin to hum. My limbs would shudder in sympathy. By following differences in tension—not sight—I would find the loosened axle, draw myself upward, and drive the wedge home with repeated contractions of my central bladder.

No voice would tell me when it was correct. The altered vibration would pass through the vane, into its supporting fibers, through the bridge and back into my limbs. A dissonance would cease.

That cessation would be the nearest thing I possessed to satisfaction.

I would not return to the workshop. The body would not contain enough stored chemistry for a second crossing. Once the wedge was seated, I would hook my remaining limbs between the vane tower and the bridge. Resin would leak from my joints and harden. The red filaments would find the fresh surfaces and grow over them.

By the next cycle I would be a brace.

Later, no observer could confidently decide what I had been. My ceramic plates would resemble roof fragments. My gripping loops might be called hooks. One limb, broken free by thermal movement, could become a scraper in a sealed room. The wedge would polish under centuries of vibration. Black cellulose strands would remain fused to it, suggesting either a handle or a root.

I would be mistaken for a tool because I had become one.

But I would not be dead in the manner of an animal. The part of me that listened would still be in the dome. The part that remembered would persist in the filament mesh. Beneath another building, another bulb would begin to swell against another mold. The next hand might have a different number of limbs. It might cross a different bridge. It would not carry my memories as scenes or thoughts, only as improved angles: a thicker joint where mine cracked, a wider hook where mine slipped, a pressure channel that wasted less gas.

So I would not be an individual, exactly.

I would be a recurring civic gesture.

I would exist whenever the Conservatory needed to act faster than a root could grow and more precisely than a wind could erode. My self would be distributed between dormancy, mold, tension, and task. I would awaken without childhood, work without instruction, and end without burial. Each body would be singular; the pattern making it would be ancient.

If you came searching for me, you might stand at the rim of the central pit and see nothing alive. There would be only a sealed doorway, a ladder rooted into stone, a malformed brace on a distant vane tower.

Then the diaphragm would rise.

The chamber would roar. Dust would ascend. Every leaf would close.

And in the stillness afterward, one narrow door among the oldest buildings would move against its stone pin.

That is where I would be:

inside the Conservatory’s brief capacity to reach.
