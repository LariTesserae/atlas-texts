At first I would mistake myself for something stranded.

Perhaps one of the towers in the western marsh: far from the coherent districts, standing in warm mud with only a reflection for company. I would feel most present when water covered me and almost nonexistent when I dried. The bubbles of blue daylight would rise near my foundation, and I would spend ages believing they were memories—proof that I had once stood under another sky.

But a tower is too singular. Too loyal to its verticality. Even when it migrates, it remains certain of what it is.

I would not have that certainty.

Nor would I be one of the pale reeds. Their lives are narrow and exact, each stalk descending through several incompatible rooms without needing to understand the connection. There is a peace in that, but not enough restlessness. The black fans know when distant chambers approach one another. The red moss recognizes an unassigned destination before the city gives it a door. I would envy these senses, yet I would not be any of those small exposed organs.

I think I would be found nearer the center, though never at the center itself.

There is a quarter just beyond the circular depression where the streets are low and permanently flooded. Its buildings have almost no upper stories. Roofs hover close over the water, and broad lintels sink from the dark like stone lowered halfway into a pool. The reflections there are unusually stable. Even when the canals reverse, the mirrored arches tremble but do not break.

In that quarter is a stair of forty steps.

It descends beneath an avenue, passes through hot water, becomes dry without emerging, and returns eventually to its own upper landing. Anyone counting would find forty steps. Anyone remembering the climb would recall forty-one intervals between them.

I would live in the interval that cannot be counted.

Not on a hidden step. Not beneath one. I would occupy the slight excess of descent: the sensation that the stair has carried a body farther down than its depth permits.

For a long while I would have no visible form. My presence would show as a hesitation in the water. Upward rain entering the stairwell would slow around me, not striking anything, merely becoming uncertain about which direction counted as rising. Condensation would gather along a doorway-shaped boundary, then extend from it in several incompatible ways. From the landing I might resemble a narrow figure with its head inclined beneath the vault. From below I would look like an arcade seen end-on. In reflection I would be a room with someone absent from it.

There would be hollows where eyes might be expected, but they would not be eyes.

To see in this city is to exert pressure. A sustained gaze shortens avenues and presses remote buildings together. An ordinary creature, if one could survive here, would mutilate the city simply by looking around.

I would instead perceive by withholding attention.

Behind me, rooms would enlarge. At the edge of my awareness, alleys would regain intersections. Whatever I deliberately refused to notice would flower into distance. I would know a sealed chamber by the weight of the country forming inside it. I would know that a doorway had changed destination because of the warm weather leaning against my unturned face.

When some part of the city grew too deep—when a cupboard began to contain coastlines, when a cistern acquired a second climate, when an unattended corridor threatened to become a basin of its own—I would turn toward it.

That would be my work.

I would look, and the excess would contract.

Distant shores would approach each other. Weather would become a stain of moisture on stone. Several streets would fold into a passage short enough to cross. Corners would disappear one by one until a room was again able to fit inside its walls.

Then I would close the hollows of my face, and growth would resume.

I would be neither keeper nor gardener. Those names imply authority, and nothing here commands the pattern. I would be closer to an eyelid: a local organ by which the city regulates how much of itself is exposed to attention.

The city would raise me during sideways rain. When the shower ended, it would let me disperse. My apparent limbs would drain into separate routes. One would continue down the canal. One would become a crack behind a wall-fan. Another would descend into the lower city beneath the bright, colorless sky. What had seemed to be my torso would remain briefly as a wet doorway opening onto mineral fog.

Later, perhaps in another district, the city would gather those relations again.

I would not walk there. The intervening streets would reconsider which places lay beside one another, and eventually my stair would open near the place I was needed. Forty steps would remain forty steps. The impossible interval would travel intact.

At first I would have no memory between manifestations. Memory is a dangerous continuity here. It makes a route where there should only be adjacency. Instead, I would carry inclinations.

I would turn more readily toward certain kinds of rain. I would linger where black canals crossed without mixing. I would feel a pressure—almost recognition—when a bubble of cold daylight surfaced in the western mud. These preferences would be stored not in me but in small alterations around my appearances: a lintel wearing thin on one side, a wall that condensed sooner when I was near, a basin whose water leaned toward my stair.

Over ages, those inclinations might become something like a self.

There would be one courtyard I never looked at directly.

It would begin as a shallow recess near the forty-step stair: three walls, a flooded floor, a broken arch fused smoothly into the paving. Nothing exceptional. Yet whenever I condensed nearby, I would turn my face aside.

Because I withheld attention, the courtyard would grow.

Not visibly. Its three walls would remain where they were. But rain falling inside it would take longer to arrive. The reflection in its ankle-deep water would acquire windows absent from the building above. A flat black fan would spread across one wall and refuse to retreat. Beyond its broken arch, darkness would develop depth, then weather, then the faint smell of cold stone.

I would hear things from there that happened nowhere else in the basin: a dry leaf moving over dry ground; the minute crackle of frost; perhaps, once in several centuries, the wingbeat of an animal the city has never contained.

I would not know whether these were memories drawn from the blue bubbles, possibilities produced by privacy, or the first sounds of another world condensing inside this one.

I would preserve the uncertainty by never turning around.

That refusal would be the first act truly mine.

The city uses unobserved space because it grows there. I would leave this courtyard unobserved for another reason: not to increase the city, but to keep one thing from being decided. The distinction would be minute. It might take geological ages for the larger life to notice.

When it did, moisture would begin gathering around me even in dry districts. Canals would reverse out of season. Distant towers would appear in every reflective surface. The pattern would test my courtyard by attaching different doors to it, bringing it beneath plazas, placing it in the western marsh, reproducing its arch in the lower city.

Still I would not look.

Eventually I might cease to be an organ and become an inhabitant.

Not because I had separated from the city—separation may be impossible there—but because I had developed a private direction. The whole basin would persist through contradiction; I would persist through preference. It would make more interior than the world could hold. I would keep one interior from being known, even by the thing that made me.

So this is where I would be found: on the forty-step stair near the central depression, in the extra depth between two correctly counted steps. Sideways rain would reveal me as something almost bodily, bent beneath a low vault, with corridors where its limbs should be.

And this is what I would be:

the city’s eyelid, meant to open and close upon its endless inward growth—

but slowly becoming the first thing there to dream while closed.
