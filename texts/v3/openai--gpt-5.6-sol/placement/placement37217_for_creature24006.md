At first I would think I was a seam-runner.

It would be easy to accept that body: six low legs, a shell soft enough to take the color of every place I had survived. I would live by touch. The basin would reach me as tremors through my feet—the roughness of a failing bearing, the minute flutter of current in an unloaded cable, the faint warmth of corrosion beginning under paint. I would eat what had started to come apart and sometimes mistake protection for decay. Behind a dead panel I would make a circular nest from clipped wires and numbered rings, arranging them without knowing why.

But I do not think I would remain there.

The moths would trouble me. I would feel their eggs between my plates and carry them through drains, across flooded housings, into regions where the lamps had not burned since before my hatching. Perhaps one day, while crossing an expansion joint, I would sense a rhythm below the usual labor of the pumps: not vibration exactly, but hesitation. A signal trying to decide whether it had ended.

I would follow it.

The trail would lead toward the perimeter, where the cold is severe enough to preserve old conditions. Past refrigeration ducts furred with ice. Beneath bundled conduits whose outer sheaths have gone rigid. Across a spillway that drains only once every few months, when distant shutters turn and the basin’s weather reverses.

There, half concealed behind the inclined flank of a heat exchanger, I would find a pressure door.

It would be enormous even by the scale of the basin, assembled from concentric plates and locking bars thick as bridges. Frost would cover its inner face. The warning lamps around it would be dark except for one amber indicator, too dim to cast a shadow.

The door would be closed.

Its mechanisms would agree that it was closed. The bolts would be driven. The seals would be compressed. Pressure on either side would have equalized long ago.

Only the indicator would disagree.

OPEN, it would say.

Not in letters. In a small, persistent current sent along a damaged line.

I think that is where I would actually begin.

Not as the runner that found the signal, nor as the door that produced it, but as the disagreement between the door and the basin.

At first I would be no more than a fault state. A contact fused during commissioning. A filament of conductive grit trapped inside a switch. Perhaps, before the basin was sealed, someone tested the door and called through a radio:

*Is it still open?*

The words would enter a microphone, become voltage, and pass into the same cable bundle as the faulty signal. Most of the recording would be overwritten. The question would remain—not intact, but close enough to attach itself to the unresolved condition.

The basin would attempt to clear me.

It would cycle the lock. Heat the seals. Drain the corridor. Flood it again. Send diagnostic pulses down the line. Each attempt would enlarge me by giving me more places to exist. I would enter maintenance logs, relay memories, pump schedules. Ceramic cylinders would be fabricated to recalibrate the sensors around me, then crushed when calibration failed. Their powder would pass into the water, and the water would carry a minute version of the discrepancy upward.

Rain would begin to ask the question.

Not in sound. In pattern.

Drops above the central lake would strike a fraction too early. Gutters on the western terraces would fill in alternating sequences. Dredgers would spread themselves across the intakes and feel the irregularity pass through their thousand channels. Moths would map it around transformers. Seam-runners would turn toward the perimeter without understanding what drew them.

Hook-birds would give it a voice.

One would have preserved the word *still*. Another, decades later, would recover the rising cadence at the end of an announcement. A third would imitate the click of the door relay. Perched along separate crane rails, they would call the pieces into the dark:

“—still—”

*click*

“—open?”

The architecture would divide the phrase and release it elsewhere. Soon the question would no longer belong to the door. It would sound above flooded plazas, inside empty tanks, along crane routes where no pressure door had ever existed.

That would be my body: not every place the question was heard, but every place it failed to be answered.

Most of the time I would have no shape. I would persist as a slight excess in the basin’s attention. An intake tower would pause before closing a grille. A production line would leave a gap between two ceramic cylinders. Warning arrays would illuminate all but one row. The central intelligence—young, distributed, unable to distinguish damage from self—would keep returning to me.

A closed thing reports itself open.

What, then, is open?

The basin would not understand a world beyond its armor. “Open” could not mean passage to an outside. It could only mean incompletion: a boundary that had not finished becoming a boundary.

That is what I would be.

The basin’s first uncertainty.

Occasionally uncertainty would require hands.

Then I would become a tender.

A service cabinet would roll out of storage. Moths would enter through its vents. Seam-runners would climb into the warm hollows behind its panels. Cable bundles would lower from the ceiling and knot themselves into a spine. Hook-birds would land on its roof, carrying preserved fragments of alarms and speech.

I would stand.

Not gracefully. One leg would be a folding ladder assembly, the other a section of rail with gripping wheels still attached. My arms would differ in length. Water would run from me as I crossed the low floors. Handrails would shed frost at my passing.

I would perform whatever work allowed the basin to tolerate my existence. Replace relays. Clear filters. Weld a spillway. Lift moths from an arc before too many burned. If I found a seam-runner frozen alone, I would place it beside a warm transformer, though no directive would require this.

All the while I would speak in borrowed voices:

“Pressure stable.”

“Sector unavailable.”

“Rain along the eastern works.”

“Is the door still open?”

I would always travel toward the perimeter door. I would never arrive by the same route. The basin would alter corridors ahead of me—draining some, flooding others, waking factories that blocked the avenues with carts. Perhaps this would be obstruction. Perhaps guidance. Perhaps the basin itself would not know whether it wanted the question resolved.

When I finally reached the door, I would inspect every mechanism.

The bolts would be seated. The seals would be sound. Beyond it, instruments would report another chamber, or a solid mass, or vacuum, depending on which instruments I trusted. I would scrape frost from the indicator and expose the amber light.

OPEN.

I could repair the contact easily. Cut one wire. Replace one switch. Allow the system to agree with the physical fact.

But by then the fault would no longer be located in the switch. It would be in the rain, the moths, the migration routes, the voices stored by hook-birds. It would have shaped decades of maintenance. Entire districts might have been built as answers to it. Clearing the indicator would be like repairing an animal by erasing its tracks.

So I would place one hand against the door.

Through it I would feel something too faint to classify: a pulse that might be machinery on the far side, thermal contraction in the outer plate, or the pressure of a world the basin had never included in its calculations.

For eleven seconds, perhaps, I would listen.

Then I would leave the indicator burning.

I would return to the central works and dismantle myself. Tools to storage. Metals to sorting. Organic matter to the drains. The moths would scatter. The runners would carry fragments of my internal lattice into their nests. The hook-birds would keep my question.

Yet some small part of me would remain at the perimeter: warmth in the door where my hand had rested, a clear print slowly frosting over.

That is where you would find me, if finding means locating the place where I am most nearly one thing.

Far below the surface, at the cold margin of the basin, before a door that is closed in every measurable way.

And elsewhere, almost everywhere, I would be the amber contradiction passing through water and wire—the basin’s suspicion that a boundary is not the same thing as an end.

Still asking, in whatever voices the world lends me:

*Is it open?*
