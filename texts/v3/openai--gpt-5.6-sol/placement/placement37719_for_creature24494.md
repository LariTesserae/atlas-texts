At first I think I would be at the ledge.

It is the obvious place for a self: the brink where the small basin opens onto distances vast enough to give direction and scale. I imagine sitting beside the tall orange flower, looking over drowned forests and cloud-filled ravines, believing the world begins beyond the room of stone.

But nothing that truly belongs to the basin would face outward for long. The view is magnificent, yet almost irrelevant. The important distances are not measured from mountain to mountain. They are the width of a root crossing a runnel, the depth of a fissure beneath a pool, the years between the planting of a vine and the first pod striking stone.

So I would be farther in, near the place where the water ceases to obey the visible slope.

There is a mineral band on one of the lower shelves, dull violet edged with cream. A thread of water follows it sideways, passes beneath a pale root, then climbs the height of two fingers to enter a hole in the rock. I would return to that hole repeatedly. Not live inside it, exactly. It would be the fixed point around which my life arranged itself: a door too small to enter while young, an ear pressed into the mountain, and eventually a destination.

I would be a tender, though I would not know myself by that name.

I would be an old one.

My wings would still open, but reluctantly. Their membranes would have grown cloudy and uneven, carrying repairs where thorns or stone had split them during mist crossings. In flight I would no longer resemble a bird. I would resemble a wet leaf unsuccessfully refusing to fall. Most days I would not fly at all. I would move shelf by shelf, placing each hind foot into the fading mark of the forefoot, leaving behind the misleading tracks of a solitary three-toed walker.

Moss would have begun to inhabit me.

It would gather first in the folds behind my forelimbs, then along the pale marks of my spine. Algae would darken my flanks whenever rain remained for several days. Tiny mites would cross my back. I would occasionally scrape at these growths, but without conviction. They would not feel entirely foreign. The distinction between carrying a garden and becoming its soil would already be weakening.

I would spend hours with one foot raised.

To another creature this might look like hesitation. It would be listening: three feet receiving the basin’s present condition while the lifted one preserves, for a moment, the vibration of the last place I touched. Through one arrangement of toes I would feel water compressing air in a buried chamber. Through another, a pale tree tightening almost imperceptibly around a drowned branch. A fine crack would travel through a seed shell. Far below, warm fluid would pass from porous stone into fungal tissue and from fungal tissue into a root.

I would not understand these separately.

Understanding would arrive as inclination. A pressure in the left forefoot would make me turn a pod. A low interval in the rock’s murmur would draw me toward the outer shelves. The smell of softened bark would cause me to scrape a branch until fungal threads were exposed. I would carry a hard seed for half a day without hunger, then press it into a crack beneath a blade-leaf.

There would be no plan in me that could be described as a plan. The basin would remember through my body, and my body would mistake that memory for desire.

Perhaps this is what I would be: not the gardener, but the garden’s brief ability to walk.

Long ago, when my wings were still translucent, I might have crossed the mountain face in evening cloud. I would remember almost nothing of the other basin—only a different mineral taste in its water and a hollow trunk that answered at a higher pitch. Perhaps I brought something back: spores caught in the ribbing of a toe, a seed lodged beneath a flank fold, a vibration retained in bone. Perhaps what seems like one household is made from such small exchanges between rooms that never see one another.

Now I would remain close to the dark pool.

I would visit it before dawn, when the air above the water was warmer than the mist. I would lower my snout until its hard edge touched the trembling surface. Pollen patterns would divide around my breath. They might appear to me not as roots or rivers or blades, but as pressures—places where the vibration strengthened, crossed itself, and disappeared.

I would recognize one pattern.

Not recognize it as an image. Recognize it as one recognizes a familiar foothold in darkness.

It would resemble the branching shape that appeared during my own emergence from the upper seepage, though I could not remember that beginning. It would resemble the arrangement of roots around the violet mineral seam. It would resemble the veins of my folded wings. For several mornings it would recur, altered slightly each time.

Then I would begin carrying things to the pool: a red cup from rotting wood, the transparent skin of a seed, a flake of softened bark. I would place them at the edge and wait. Some would drift apart. Some would be drawn into the pollen pattern. One might move against the tiny surface current and lodge where stone overhangs the water.

None of this would be an offering. The basin would have no use for reverence.

It would be closer to fitting a shape into the place that has been waiting for it.

On my last night beneath the fern crown, mist would fill the shelves until water, stone, and air seemed to occupy the same substance. I would hang with six toes pressed against the trunk. The mountain’s bass murmur would travel through the pale wood, into my feet, along my ribs, and through the membranes of my closed wings.

I would knock once.

The hollow trunk would hold the sound longer than expected.

During the silence afterward, I would feel responses arriving by different routes. One through the tree. One through the wet ground. One through the air inside my own mouth. They would not agree in time. Their interference would produce a slow beating sensation, and I would wait until the beats aligned.

Then I would knock again.

The answer would come from beneath the pool, but also from the small hole in the violet seam—the one the sideways water enters. For the first time, I would understand that they are connected.

Or rather, I would act as if I understood.

I would descend from the fern. Cross the shelves. Pause at the pool, but not enter it. Continue to the mineral band and follow the uphill thread to its opening.

By then I would be thin enough.

I would fold my wings, flatten my ribs, and press my head into the fissure. The rock would scrape away the moss on my back. My forefeet would find wet stone within, then fungal softness, then no surface that could be distinguished from living tissue. Warm water would move around me with the slow insistence of breath.

I would not die there in the manner of a separate animal ending.

For a while I would remain a tender inside the mountain. My toes would still register pressure. My mouth would still open when the water changed taste. Then sensation would cease to arrive from six distinct points. It would spread into threads. The fungal films inhabiting my skin would enter the softened folds. Silver structures along my flanks would loosen and travel with the climbing water.

What had been memory in nerves would become a bias in growth.

A root encountering one trace of me would divide instead of turning aside. A microbial film would thicken around another. Water would begin favoring a passage it had previously entered only during heavy rain. Months later, gray young might appear in warm seepage on the upper shelves, carrying no recollection of me and yet pausing at the violet seam.

Years later, pale fungus would brighten the mouth of the fissure.

The water might climb another finger’s width.

And if something knocked in the mist, I would answer—not as a hidden creature beneath the pool, not as a ghost inside the stone, but as a slight change in the whole instrument: a root tightening, a leaf-cup spilling, a hollow trunk deepening its note.

That is where I would find myself: in the narrow place where the sideways water enters the mountain.

And that is what I would be: one of the basin’s temporary limbs, just at the end of being countable.
