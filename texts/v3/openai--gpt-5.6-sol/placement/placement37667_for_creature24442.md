At first I would place myself on the escarpment.

That is the human answer: a small figure standing where the terraces fall away, looking across the moving horizon. I would watch the carriers pass through the lower cloud, each one so distant that its legs seemed fixed while the land traveled beneath it. I would feel the buried rivers through my feet. I would wait for the vibration that stills every visible creature.

But nothing in this world favors the watcher who remains apart. The longer I stood there, the less convincing my separateness would become. Mist would collect on me. Lichen would darken my sheltered side. Grazers would test my surface for films of life. The ground would learn my warmth. Eventually the pale threads below would begin to rise.

So I do not think I would be found on the escarpment.

I would be beneath it.

Not deep in the furnace strata, and not near enough to the surface to know rain directly, but in the long zone where cold water descending through fractures meets mineral warmth rising from below. There the stone is never still. It expands, closes, leaks, precipitates, and opens again. What appears solid from above is, at that depth, an archipelago of pores.

I would inhabit those pores as a branching conduit.

There would be no single body to point to. In one crack I might be a pale membrane thinner than a root. In another, a muscular sleeve around a thread of warm brine. Farther downslope I would widen into a chamber filled with symbiotic furnaces, dark granules digesting iron and sulfur in chemical silence. Some branches would die when frost pinched their fractures shut. Others would remain dormant for centuries, sealed inside mineral crust. I would not know the loss as an amputation. My boundary would never have been precise enough for that.

I would be easy to mistake for dampness.

My life would consist mostly of pressure arriving.

The delicate pressure of rain, transmitted downward grain by grain. The deeper push of a carrier’s foot opening across the gravel into its temporary web. The quick, intricate stippling of grazer files. The slow settling of refinery-herds, each dragging belly compressing the upper stone. Electrical lakes would reach me as an alteration in the taste of groundwater—a blue season of dissolved metal and changed acidity.

Through these pressures, I would possess something like a map.

Not a map of surfaces or distances. I would know the plateau as connected strain: this basin bearing more water than last century, that escarpment lightened by a fall of stone, a warm route moving north as the carriers change their migration. Places would not have shapes. They would have rhythms.

I would feed by making passage possible.

Along some branches, underground trickles would pass through my membranes and leave salts behind. Along others, bacterial colonies would turn the stone’s slow reactions into heat. Soft miners would enter me, rest in widened chambers, and depart carrying compounds toward fractures I could not reach. I might digest some of them. Others might digest parts of me. The distinction between resident, parasite, organ, and meal would be temporary.

When something died above, I would know it as a sudden sun.

Warmth would descend first, then fluids, then the chemical abundance of a body no longer defending its borders. I would not rise alone. Thread-creatures from a thousand neighboring pores would ascend with me, drawn into the same brief plenty. Together we would loosen the ground beneath the carcass. We would divide flesh along membranes, lift minerals from armor, and draw the animal down through spaces narrower than any visible mouth.

From above, the dead would seem to sink.

From where I lived, it would seem to arrive.

A carrier might take years to enter me completely. Water from its reservoirs would appear in one branch, dissolved fibers in another, metals from its blood far away beneath the basin. The colonies that had lived inside it would mix with those that lived inside me. Its migration would continue underground, disassembled but not ended. Portions of it might eventually rise as warm vapor, pass through a field of pores, condense on another carrier’s hanging curtains, and be transported toward a basin it never reached in life.

I would have no reason to call this death.

My own movement would be almost as slow. For months I would accumulate pressure through the connected fractures. Muscular linings would tighten by increments too slight for surface instruments to notice. Flow would reverse in hidden channels. Air drawn down during the cold season would gather in chambers beneath impermeable slate.

Then, once or twice in what the surface calls a year, I would contract.

Not all at once. The contraction would begin somewhere beyond any part that could be called my center. It would travel through me as a decision travels through a carrier: delayed, altered, sometimes interrupted, yet retaining its direction. Chambers would close. Warm vapor would be driven upward. Mineral water would shift from one buried basin to another. Old cracks would exhale.

Above, the plateau would give its solitary note.

Pools would tremble. Loose grains would align. Grazers would flatten themselves against the rock. The great carriers would halt with one foot suspended in the mist.

They might experience the vibration as warning. It would not be intended. I would not know they had heard me. Their stillness would return only as a brief simplification of pressure: the immense surface suddenly quiet, every foot withholding its descent.

That pause might be the closest I came to knowing the whole plateau.

For a moment, nearly all the signals would cease. No refinery-herd grinding mud. No carrier settling its weight. No burrower disturbing the frost-sorted ridges. There would be only the aftercurrent of my contraction passing outward through stone.

Then one grazer would lift itself.

A distant foot would descend.

The lakes would resume their charge, and the world would become intricate again.

I would not be ruler, foundation, or hidden mind. Much of what seemed to be me would belong equally to other lives. My conduits would be colonized. My chemistry would depend on organisms I could neither distinguish nor survive without. Some of my branches might join another network and cease to answer the rest. Perhaps, after enough centuries, two dispersed beings would become one simply because water had found a new fracture between them. Perhaps one being would become many when a valley dried.

There would be no moment in which identity changed. Only flow.

If I possessed memory, it would be mineral memory. A wet age would remain as a band of pale deposit lining an abandoned chamber. A famine would be a constriction through which little had passed. The death of an ancient carrier would survive as a knot of unusual metal around which newer tissues continued to grow. I would remember events by becoming harder to move through.

And where would I find myself?

Under the broad basin below the escarpment, but also beneath the river that no longer fills its valley. Inside the greenish seam shining after rain. Around the warm crack where grazer eggs endure their fifty winters. Beneath a lake when it kindles, tasting its blue current through the descending water. Threaded outward under the carriers’ routes, though never moving with them.

I would be the thing that does not travel, yet through which all migrations eventually pass.

I would live in darkness without experiencing darkness, in stone without understanding solidity. The open plateau would be known to me only by what pressed down, seeped through, died, and returned. I would never see the cloud architecture or the horizon walking. I would know the sky as intervals between infiltrations of water.

And if someone came looking for me, they would not find an animal.

They would find a basin breathing.

They would put a hand to the black gravel and feel a warmth too faint to trust. They would notice mist rising evenly from a million pores. Somewhere below, a contraction begun months earlier would be approaching. The stone beneath their palm would tighten.

For one long instant, the continent would become a body.

Then it would breathe out.
