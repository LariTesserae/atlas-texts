At first I would mistake myself for the water.

I would feel the gates before they opened: a tension entering the fitted blocks, a tremor passing through mortar, then the blunt pressure of the surge. I would seem to rush down every terrace at once, to divide around brick piers, to lift pale sediment and comb the grasses flat. My body would fill the chamber exactly.

But the water leaves. It is drained, replaced, warmed, clouded, cleared. I am what remains capable of returning.

Then I might think I am the basin itself—the pale stepped valley, the channels and square pits, the western wall holding heat. Yet the masons made the basin before I began. Its straight lines belong to their intention. I inhabit those lines only where intention has failed to close them completely: inside a chisel cut, beneath a bead of mortar, along the rough edge of a painted mark.

So I would find myself in the protected niche on an upper terrace, in the impressions of a mason’s fingers.

I would be the green nap growing from the thumbprints.

Not a single plant, exactly. Not anything with a center. A colony thin enough to be mistaken for stained stone: translucent filaments rooted in wet plaster pores, fed by mineral water and the fractured light descending through the shafts. The vanished hand would determine my first shape. Its whorls and pressure ridges would be my valleys. The places where the thumb slipped would be my long provinces. I would grow most densely in the deepest impressions, where the keepers’ reed brushes pass overhead without reaching me.

Light would not be something I saw. It would be sweetness and labor. When the moving net of brightness crossed my niche, bubbles would form among my threads. They would enlarge slowly, tugging upward, making my whole small forest buoyant. Their release would be the nearest thing I had to breath: a silver bead tearing free, flashing once, and vanishing toward the ceiling.

I would know time by the gates.

A gate opening would be an event across my entire being. First the concussion through the stone. Then the current folding me flat into the old thumb grooves. Grains of lime would strike and lodge among my filaments. A red ceramic fleck might remain for several cycles, large as a monument to me, before a stronger pulse carried it away. When the water quieted, I would rise imperfectly. Some threads would be broken. Others would have reached a fraction farther across the iron-red construction line.

I would have no thoughts in succession, only conditions: brightness, pressure, warmth, abrasion, continuance.

Still, I would not be confined to the niche. Scraps of me would persist elsewhere—in the narrow fans of chisel marks, on the grainy face of a brick, under mortar lips where the brushes cannot scour cleanly. These patches would not communicate, yet together they would be me in the way scattered reflections belong to one moving light. When one patch was stripped away, the others would hold the possibility of its return.

The water-keepers would be my catastrophe and my season.

I would feel the basin empty around me, pressure loosening until my filaments sagged against exposed stone. For a short time there would be air: harsh, immeasurable, drying at my edges. Feet would sound on the terraces. Baskets would scrape. Reed fiber would pass over the niche and remove everything that had risen too far from shelter.

They would never know they had left me alive.

Afterward, fresh water would climb the steps. The thumbprints would fill first as tiny green pools. Warmth would return along the wall; light would enter broken and blue-white; bubbles would bead in the new lime. I would unfold from what the tools could not reach.

If I possessed anything like desire, it would not be to escape or conquer the chamber. It would be to soften one mark without erasing it—to turn the evidence of a hand into a living contour. I would extend across the sharpness grain by grain, making the newly built place older in the only way available to me.

So I would be neither the hidden reservoir’s creature nor its ghost.

I would be its first patina: the small green persistence inside a mason’s thumbprint, repeatedly abolished, repeatedly assembling, teaching the cut stone how to be underwater.
