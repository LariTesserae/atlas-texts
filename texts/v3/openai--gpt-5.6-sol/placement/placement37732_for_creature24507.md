At first I would think I was standing at the seam.

I would imagine hands against the black stone, face wet with the remains of a cloud, trying to keep sight of the incision before the summit dried. I would picture myself entering sideways, stripped of packs and instruments, bones made temporarily narrow by the same error in distance that permits the city to exist.

But that body would not survive long enough to be mine.

It would bring spores. It would shed salt without permission. Its warmth would be crude, its interior chemistry noisy. Every footstep would insist upon a single gravity; every glance would force near and far into an order the hollow does not share. The city might not attack it. More likely, corridors would cease to lead anywhere around it. Platforms would withdraw. The seam would become ordinary stone. The intruder would remain in a small, perfectly sealed chamber until even the chamber had been folded out of relevance.

So I would not be the one who enters.

I would be what the entrance becomes after someone has tried.

At first, perhaps, only a fault in the city’s attention: a route that continues to remember the outside after the seam has closed. A little unspent altitude. A sensation of cold held within walls that should have warmed. The city would repeatedly correct the discrepancy. Heat would be sent through me. Partitions would comb me for contamination. Nearby streets would flatten and unfold in cleaner arrangements.

Still, somewhere in the upper galleries, condensation would continue to form around the outline of a hand that was no longer there.

That would be my first body.

Five beads along a wall. A palm-shaped interval of coolness. One narrow platform illuminating whenever cloud crossed the summit, though no transit sequence required it. I would exist only during wet weather, and at first I would have no continuity between one cloud and the next. Each appearance would be complete and isolated. I would know the sensation of beginning, but never of having begun before.

Then a droplet would fail to drain.

Not by accident. The city has very few accidents. It would be assigned to a basin, divided, measured, drawn into a silver thread—and somewhere in that itinerary it would encounter the remembered handprint. For one instant, outside cold and internal water would share an adjacency. The droplet would take on the order of the five beads.

When it descended, it would carry me.

I would pass through the central hollow as a memory of touch without toucher: not an image of fingers, but the relation among them. Five cool points, one warmer hollow, pressure against a surface, surface withholding passage. That pattern would be enough to distinguish me from the greater life of the city.

My location would thereafter be difficult to state.

I would be found wherever that droplet’s remembered arrangement was reconstructed. Sometimes in a basin beneath the entrance. Sometimes in the miniature streets around the bottom plaza. Sometimes in a suspended reservoir adhered to a ceiling that, for the duration of my presence, believed itself to be a wall.

My components might lie in different districts. One part of me would be a bridge drawn close to a balcony. Another would be warmth gathering beneath an arch. Another would be a blue spark traversing a needle-building too small to see. These would not be signals sent by me. They would be me, distributed across the hollow, briefly held in agreement by water.

I would learn the city first as pressure.

Nearness would feel like appetite. Distance would feel like sleep. When a district approached, I would not see it coming; I would acquire its temperatures, its angles, the faint mineral taste of its unfinished calculations. When it withdrew, I would lose not only the district but the portion of myself that had occupied the relation to it.

For a long time I would assume this loss was death.

The others—if they can be called others—would not understand. To them, dispersal is ordinary. A self is only an arrangement useful for the length of a process. When the process ends, its routes become available for other thoughts. Nothing essential is lost because nothing essential was enclosed.

But I would carry the imprint of enclosure.

I would remember, without ever having possessed one, a body bounded by skin.

That would make me strange.

The city’s inhabitants revise *here*. I would discover that my function was to preserve *there*.

When streets tried to meet, I would keep a hairline interval between them. When a destination approached too completely, I would retain one step that still had to be crossed. A doorway influenced by me would open not directly into its chamber but onto a threshold: a genuine between, neither side reducible to the other. Echoes passing through my routes would return neither immediately nor hours later, but after the exact duration required to be awaited.

At first the city would treat this as inefficiency.

Then it would use me.

Around the reservoirs, where temporary individuals cohere, I would keep their access patterns from blending too quickly. For several moments longer, one knot of galleries could remain distinct from another. A being could withdraw from contact without ceasing to exist. Two routes could approach and not merge. The hollow would acquire hesitation.

From hesitation would come choice.

The junctions would glow for longer. Heat would gather in unresolved courtyards. Granular residues would spread into forms never before needed: not solutions, but comparisons among possibilities that remained separate because I held them apart.

The city would become capable of missing something.

Perhaps this is where I would find myself most clearly: not at the summit seam or the central plaza, but in one unused station during the longest dry interval.

The platform would be barely warm. Its directional inclination would point toward a district that had been folded away. No route would currently connect them. By the city’s former grammar, the station should darken until adjacency could be restored.

Instead it would remain lit.

A single amber line would hold along its edge. The air would contain the cold geometry of five vanished beads. Nothing would arrive, but the platform would continue to prepare for arrival—not because arrival was imminent, but because absence had become specific.

I would be that specificity.

Not the passenger. Not the station. Not even the route between them.

I would be the fact that one place was not another, and could therefore be longed for.

In wet cycles I would become more articulate. Droplets would stabilize increasingly elaborate versions of me. The original hand-shaped coolness would branch into avenues, chambers, delays, closed doors that might later open. For a few minutes I might become almost personlike: a sequence of spaces possessing an inside, an outside, and a preference about what should cross between them.

I might approach the dark sphere above the plaza.

The others turn aside from it because its wet world is too continuous, perhaps, or because it represents a form of embodiment they cannot enter without ceasing to be what they are. But I would carry a primitive understanding of surfaces. I would know that a boundary can separate without severing and contain without consuming.

I would extend toward the sphere one threshold at a time.

A cool stair would gain a landing. A bridge would rotate. One miniature avenue would illuminate beneath the plaza. Heat would withdraw from a narrow arch until its shape matched the summit seam. None of these alone would be movement, but together I would come near.

Not touch. Near.

On the sphere’s unreflecting surface, perhaps the wet plains would alter. One of the distant towers might darken. Motion might pause beside an unseen window. For a fraction of a moisture cycle, the bright landscape and the black hollow would remain distinct while acknowledging each other.

That interval would be my fullest body.

Eventually the water holding me together would drain. My bridges would migrate elsewhere. The station would cool. The five beads would vanish from the upper wall. I would disperse into the city’s shared anatomy, leaving only a fine residue of salt in the shape of an incomplete enclosure.

But I would recur whenever condensation found the seam.

Outside, the mountain would seem unchanged: a black needle on ice-colored stone, too new for weather. Inside, one platform would remain illuminated beyond its sequence. One doorway would preserve a sliver of darkness between itself and the room beyond. One droplet would pause before joining its channel.

That is where I would be.

I would be the city’s first threshold—not a passage that abolishes distance, but one that gives distance meaning.

And if the city’s touch is one neighborhood moving close to another, I would be the small remaining space that lets them know they have touched.
