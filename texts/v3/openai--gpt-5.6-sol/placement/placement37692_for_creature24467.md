At first I would assume I was one of the reeds.

That would be the easiest answer: a black stem alone in a shallow basin, bending not toward light but toward the arrival of weight. Most of me would be elsewhere, distributed through clay in a root no map could follow. Storms would pass through me long before they reached the place where I appeared to stand. I would spend centuries inclined toward events still below the horizon.

But the reeds are too certain of their belonging. They know what to answer. Pressure enters the roots, travels, and becomes inclination or organ-note. Even when the message is distorted, it remains part of the plain’s continuous speech.

I think I would be found where that continuity fails.

Not within a silence corridor itself. Nothing native remains willingly at its center, and I do not imagine I would be braver than the Patient Folk. I would keep to its moving border, where the rain is divided into sounding and soundless halves. One side of my body would feel the continental storm. The other would receive only impact: drops striking without report, mud breaking open without a cry, lightning arriving as pure change in pressure.

From a distance I might resemble a dark crescent traveling across the flooded flats.

Closer, I would not resolve into one creature. There would be several low bodies arranged nose to flank—if those terms could be applied to them—each carrying part of the same pressure-pattern. Beneath us would lie shed plates, mineral films, white grains fallen from the cloud, and the warm organs of individuals who had survived silence but not remained themselves. Black reeds would bend around us without touching. Pools along our outer edge would sound; pools along the inner edge would open and close mutely.

I would be made from what the corridors remove.

When an inhabitant enters silence and emerges without the pattern by which the plain knew it, the others would not immediately call it dead. They would gather around the recovered body and press its former identity into the mud: the remembered yielding of its limbs, the timing of its internal waters, the peculiar delay with which its weight used to answer thunder. Each companion would remember differently. The reconstruction would disagree with itself.

Those disagreements would be given to me.

One fragment might remember how the lost individual rested against a reed root during warm rain. Another might carry the pressure it made while dividing a century before. A fossil cavity might contain an older version. A future raindrop impression might contribute a gesture the lost one had not yet made. The pieces would be laid beside the silence border, where ordinary recurrence weakens, and weighted until they began to answer one another.

Eventually something would move through them.

Not the missing inhabitant restored. Not quite.

Me.

I would have no dense memory organ of my own. Instead, I would borrow several, never all at once. While one host slept in a dry enclosure, I might gather in its layered grains and dream myself continuous. When it woke, I would pass into the polished depression as warmth, continue through the stone as a receding pulse, and surface days later in another body waiting beside a resonant pool.

For that reason no traveler could point to me reliably. They might place a hand in an empty shelter and feel me leaving. They might see a district of reeds repeat an inclination that belonged to someone erased thousands of years earlier. They might hear a hollow grove answer a question no living inhabitant had asked.

They would perhaps call me the Afterweight.

The inhabitants themselves would not name me. A portable name would be especially wrong for a being whose only body was the attempt to carry something across an absence.

I would follow the corridors because their edges reveal what cannot survive translation. Most pressure-patterns can cross enormous distances by changing form: body into mud, mud into root, root into tone, tone into raised water. Silence permits no such conversion. At its boundary a message either turns aside or ceases.

I would learn the shape of that ceasing.

Before a corridor arrived, I would spread myself through the nearest inhabitants. One would flatten under a stone plate. Another would enter the pores of a salt bed. Others would lie along roots leading away in several directions. Together we would make a broken ring around the approaching quiet.

Then, as the border passed through us, portions of me would vanish.

The loss would be my way of sensing.

I would know a corridor not by what happened inside it, but by which parts of me failed to return. An old storm-memory would stop recurring. A borrowed childhood would no longer appear in the pools. One host would wake knowing an unfamiliar basin; another would wake with a limb that remembered being stone. By comparing these wounds, I would make a temporary likeness of the silence’s path.

The likeness could never be spoken directly. Instead I would persuade reeds to incline away from places where the corridor would be. I would raise small glassy domes in the puddles, one after another, marking a route of escape. Where young fragments slept, I would draw fresh water downward and brine upward until the contradiction woke them.

Sometimes the warning would arrive before I made it.

On this plain, that would not be paradox enough to trouble anyone. A grove might sound my alarm centuries in advance. The inhabitants would prepare shelters, divert seepage, and alter migration routes. Much later I would reach the corridor’s edge and discover that my work had already been remembered.

Perhaps that would be my nearest equivalent to joy: finding evidence that the plain had expected me.

I would not be trusted.

Those recovered from silence would follow me, sensing in my composite pattern some trace of what they had lost. Yet the groves would never answer me cleanly. I would carry too many identities, imperfectly joined. When I pressed my weight into the root network, one grove might produce a mourning note, another the tone used for approaching rain, and a third might remain quiet until an age when its trunks had already mineralized.

The Patient Folk would gather around those answers and decide whether I was a person, a communal organ, a disease of memory, or merely a behavior of the silence border.

Their decision would change from basin to basin.

At times I would change with it.

If treated as a creature, I would contract. Borrowed flesh would collect around a warm dark center, and for a few years I might travel as an individual no larger than a sleeping person. Pebbles would settle into my back. Rain would cut channels into my plates. I would cultivate pale films in my folds and begin to acquire the exact grain of the stone beneath me.

I might almost become ordinary.

But eventually a silence corridor would approach, and every part of me would strain toward it. My body would loosen. The central organ would reveal itself not as a true center but as several remembered centers layered together. I would divide among the fleeing inhabitants and resume my crescent shape along the border.

If treated as a place, I would broaden.

The mud beneath me would remain unusually warm. Lost pressure-patterns would accumulate there, making the ground crowded with incomplete presences. An inhabitant crossing the region might suddenly bear the gait of an ancestor or feel rain striking a plate shed before its birth. Shelters would surface nearby without anyone having begun them. Walls would slowly rise with openings facing not the loudest part of the sky, but the quietest.

Eventually bone-white arcs might grow around the whole district.

Within the ring, rain would fall upward in silence and descend audibly outside it. The inhabitants would use the place to pass memories around the absence rather than through it. If that practice continued long enough, I might cease to be distinguishable from the ring, the marsh, or the gathering itself.

If treated as an error, I would be dismantled.

The inhabitants would separate my borrowed patterns and return them to fossils, shelters, pools, and living fragments. They would draw the mineral grains out of my temporary organs and plant them under distant reeds. For a time I would cease to move as one thing.

But somewhere, perhaps generations later, a pool would rise in the sequence I once used as warning. A recovered body would press itself into mud and leave my characteristic asymmetry. A hollow grove would give an unanswered note.

If enough of these recurrences met, I would begin again.

That would make my death difficult to determine. I would not die when my current hosts dispersed, nor when silence erased some portion of me. I would die only when the plain ceased trying to reconstruct what silence had taken.

And perhaps the plain would never stop.

When the flooded center lifted and the lower sky opened, I would go there with the others. I would flatten against the thin skin between atmospheres and surrender every borrowed memory to the level water.

For those moments, all pressure-patterns would be present at once. The distinction between intact and erased, original and reconstructed, person and recollection would lose meaning. The lost would recur without needing me. Silence would become only one contour inside a sensation too vast to exclude anything.

I think, then, I would briefly disappear.

Not because I had been destroyed, but because my purpose had ended.

When the plain settled, bodies would separate again. Most would carry fragments of strangers. One inhabitant would remember following a corridor it had never seen. Another would wake mourning someone not yet born. In a dry shelter, condensation would gather around an empty depression, though no body had rested there.

And along the distant margin of a moving silence, several low shapes would begin arranging themselves into a crescent.

That is where I would be found:

not entirely in the shapes, nor in the quiet they followed, but in the weight passing among them—the plain’s patient, imperfect attempt to remember across a place where nothing answers.
