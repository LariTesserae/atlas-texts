I would not find myself in one of the green islands, though at first I would mistake them for safety.

I would linger beside the crude walls near the entrance, where human intention remains legible: footholds, bowl, tally marks. I would sit with my back to the mineral bands and try to understand the hollow by counting it. Six ant roads. Three trees. So many pools. Seventeen minutes between silences. Entrances and exits, losses and substitutions.

For a while, the hollow would permit this. The pale ants would clean salt from my eyelids while I slept. Lizards would reroute around my heels. Condensation would gather in the corroded bowl when I was thirsty. Perhaps the monkeys would inspect me, tap my ribs as they tap the silver trunks, and leave pollen in my hair.

I would think I had become a witness.

But witnessing is already an office, and the hollow does not allow an office to remain privately owned.

My tally would begin to fail. A moth entering an aperture might return as three beetles, or as a smell that caused a tree to flower. A frog might disappear while its call remained. Was that an exit? Was the call an inhabitant? If a dead counter persisted as warmth in the floor, should that warmth be added to the census or subtracted from it?

I would leave spaces in the rows.

Gradually, the spaces would become more accurate than the marks.

That is where I think the hollow would take hold of me—not as a watcher, finally, but as the keeper of the uncounted remainder.

My body would remain near the low walls for a time. Moss would avoid it, not from poison but courtesy. The ants would cross my hands according to an order I could almost understand. My breathing would slow until it coincided with the chamber’s great silence. Every seventeen minutes I would exhale, and the droplets would rise.

Then the distinction between my breath and the reversal of water would become unnecessary.

My eyes might persist as two dim places in the stone where attention gathers but no image forms. My spine might become a shallow groove leading nowhere, a channel kept dry even in storms. The unfinished row of animals would lengthen by one mark, though no claw or hand would be seen making it.

On one shrine, an upright aperture would open.

It would not resemble my outline exactly. It would resemble the space I had occupied while deciding what everything was.

Through it would pass those creatures for which the hollow had not yet invented a duty: surplus forms, useless mutations, larvae suited to vanished climates, seeds waiting for soils that did not exist. They would enter without being rejected. Some would emerge elsewhere altered into necessity. Others would remain in transit, held outside hunger and competition until an emptiness called for them.

That would be my role: not to count what lives, but to preserve what cannot yet be counted.

I would be the hollow’s margin.

The blank line after the unfinished census.

The open stone between islands, translated into a person-shaped absence.

Travelers might feel me when they crossed the mountain. They would pause near the hidden seam, certain for a moment that they had forgotten something important. Most would turn aside. A few would crawl beneath the roots and descend into the noise. When they saw the oval darkness in the shrine, they might think someone stood inside it, just beyond sight.

They would not be entirely wrong.

I would be there—not surviving as myself, and not dead, but kept vacant: a door the hollow could use when none of its existing lives were sufficient.
