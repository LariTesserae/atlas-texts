I would first mistake myself for something fixed.

A dark filament in the wall, perhaps, one of those iron-loaded strands passing through the transparent skin. I would feel pressure before motion: the deep gathering beneath the pool, the minute widening of fractures, the chemical brightening where one water met another. My world would arrive as strain and charge. A footstep would be a bruise of vibration at the entrance. Rain would be not weather but an increase in the chamber’s thought.

But I do not think I would remain in the wall.

There is too much importance here in the difference between staying and being carried. The resident endures by fastening itself down, while the larger creatures survive by leaving. I would belong somewhere between those laws.

I would find myself in the black pool, beneath the edge of an overlapping ice plate, inside the translucent seam where a bubble prevents the surfaces from closing. At first I would appear to be one of the tiny white crustaceans: nearly blind, jointed, no longer than a fingernail clipping. My shell would be colorless except along the hinges, where iron had gathered in fine red-brown lines. I would have too many delicate legs, always moving, combing food from water that looked empty.

Between the plates of my shell I would carry knots of the resident.

They would sit in the protected hollows behind my limbs, clear when young and darkening as they accumulated metal. They would not root deeply enough to immobilize me. They would taste the water through me, opening when I crossed a chemical boundary and tightening before the pool convulsed. Their weak currents would pass into my softer tissues. In time I would cease to know which warnings came from my own body.

I would begin moving before the eruption.

Not from foresight as a human would understand it. The conductive strands on my shell would feel the electrical alteration running through the chamber’s mineral veins. My limbs would draw in. I would leave the exposed center and grip the underside of stone. Around me, others would do the same, but I would move a fraction earlier.

Then gas would tear upward.

The pool would become weight, impact, and white confusion. Sediment would rise in black crescents. Pale filaments would stream from the walls. Larval tubes would bend. A stone flake might descend through the turbulence, ringing only after it struck. I would hold fast until the collapse had passed through my joints and the returning echoes became separate enough to count.

Afterward I would release my grip and feed.

I would eat what the pulse loosened: bacterial shreds, mineral-softened tissue, the contents of broken eggs. The folds carried on my shell would feed as well, taking dissolved iron and the smallest organic fragments. We would share nothing deliberately. Exchange would happen because our surfaces touched. Their chemistry would alter mine; my waste would sustain their partners. If one of my limbs were injured, the clear film might close over it. Whether that was healing or slow incorporation would remain uncertain.

The chamber-wide being would not command me. It might not even know me as an individual. Yet when I crossed the pool, distant portions of it would briefly become connected through my body. A charge gathered from one wall would discharge against another. For an instant, separated films would answer each other.

I would be less its messenger than its misplaced continuity.

During flood, I might enter a fissure normally too narrow to notice. Water would pull me sideways through cold gravel, away from the hammering hollow. Behind me, the chamber’s rhythm would diminish into irregular tremors and then disappear. I would pass through absolute dark, touching stone with my antennae, carrying several folded specks of transparent life.

Perhaps the fissure would return me to the same pool when the marsh drained.

Perhaps it would open into another pocket: smaller, silent, lacking the mineral ribs and the buried gas. There the resident’s knots would rub from my shell against a seam where iron-bearing water crossed a sulfurous seep. They would fasten. Flatten. Divide.

I would leave without witnessing what they became.

This would be my place in that world: not at its center, because the hollow has no center that rules it; not at the entrance, where frogs and shrews retain the possibility of another landscape. I would live in the temporary passages—under ice, inside flooded cracks, along the shifting boundary between attached life and open water.

I would be an animal, but also a fragment of habitat moving through habitat.

If held up to the gray light, I would seem almost negligible: a transparent crustacean with rust at its joints. Only under magnification would the pleats between my plates become visible, threaded with dark mineral lines. Even then it would be impossible to say whether I carried the resident or whether, for the duration of my small life, the resident had learned to swim.

When pressure began gathering below, I would feel the walls contract through the water.

My legs would still.

The iron knots would tighten.

And before the first stone rib began to hum, I would already be moving toward the dark opening beneath it.
