At first I would look for myself at the Confluence Works.

It is the obvious answer: a recessed station, both hands on an instrument rail, learning to distinguish the impact of gravel from the beginning of structural failure. I can imagine the discipline of remaining still while the basin spends all its strength around me. I would like the screens with their uncertain borders, the forecasts that openly diminish in confidence. I would like being praised not for predicting correctly, but for noticing quickly when prediction has become useless.

But I do not think I would remain there. The Works is the valley’s great knot, and I would be drawn instead to the loose ends.

For a while I might imagine myself with Pale Horn, following the cattle through water black enough to erase their legs. There would be satisfaction in trusting an old cow’s hesitation more than a remote survey. I would know each bell by tone and each animal by the shape of its back in rain. I would sleep in a shelter that rocked when the peat took water beneath it. I would wake before the alarm because the herd had drawn together.

That life attracts me, but it is not quite mine either. I would watch the herd keeper leave and envy them. Then I would turn back toward a lamp that had begun blinking incorrectly.

That is where I think I would find myself: beside a path that was still marked safe but no longer felt true.

I would be one of the route listeners.

Official documents would give us a longer name—temporary-access verification and withdrawal crews—but no one would use it. We would be called the Closing Lights, sometimes affectionately and sometimes not. Our task would be to maintain the lowland’s paths, though maintenance would often mean deciding that a path had ended.

We would go out before the ordinary traffic, especially at dawn and during gate changes. We would test raised walks, root mats, levee remnants, gravel shelves, detachable spans, and those strips of sedge ground that carried weight only because a pressure field elsewhere was persuading the water to leave them alone. Our poles would read depth, soil tension, field gradients, and vibration. Our boots would carry broad contact membranes thin enough to feel the smallest lateral slide.

The instruments would matter. So would the reeds.

A route listener would be expected to notice that stalks on one side of a path were trembling without wind, that a water vole had sealed the rear entrance of its burrow, that a row of bubbles had stopped being round. We would learn the particular hollow sound made by peat when water had begun moving beneath its intact surface. We would watch the cattle if they were near, birds if they were not, and loose seeds if there was nothing larger to consult.

Most days, nothing dramatic would happen. We would clean lamp lenses, replace anchors, update confidence intervals, and argue with travelers who wanted a blue route to remain blue until they had crossed it.

Sometimes we would say no.

That would be the real work.

A route may still be passable when it must be closed. The last safe crossing is visible only afterward; waiting for proof means waiting too long. So I would have to become comfortable acting in the narrow interval between evidence and certainty. I would kneel in the rain, one glove pressed to the ground, and feel something almost too slight to name: not collapse, not yet, but a change in the way the path carried the river’s pressure.

Then I would turn the first lamp from blue to red.

Farther lamps would answer one by one until a red line appeared through the mist. Route forecasts would retract the crossing. Household walls would update. Throat loops would murmur alternatives into the ears of anyone approaching. If time allowed, my crew would remove the bridge plates and recover the lamp stakes. If it did not, we would release everything and watch the path go.

People would occasionally hate us for this. A closed route could add six hours to a journey, separate a household from its herd, spoil medicine, delay a repair, or leave someone looking across twenty meters of water at a door they had expected to reach. They would accuse us of closing too early.

If we closed too late, they might not be there to accuse us.

I would live on an old levee whose official name had outlasted the river that made it. My household would occupy a low building with three inherited doors and no single correct address. One door would be fitted conventionally. Another would have become a table. The oldest, darkened around the latch by hands several generations dead, would be mounted on the interior wall with its hinges intact.

I would be “with the Closing Lights,” but also “of Ninth Door” at home and “Third Spillway trained” when qualifications mattered. My family might include no blood relatives at all: a bridge rigger, a seed-bed medic, an older person who repaired throat loops, two children whose parent was away with the western herd, and whoever had been stranded by the latest route withdrawal. Kinship would accumulate around repeated rescue.

The entry room would never dry. I would come home carrying half the marsh on my armor: reed fibers in the joints, clay on the boot membranes, insects sheltered under the hood seam. Tools first, then hands. The children would inspect my pole readings before asking whether I had brought anything interesting.

Often I would have: a lamp lens abraded opaque by silt, a section of root lattice that dissolved too early, a ceramic route marker from a path no longer present. Objects recovered from closed crossings would be useful for games because they carried stories adults could correct.

“That was the north shelf.”

“No, the north shelf went in spring.”

“You’re both wrong. Listen to the rain in the recording. That was before the roof panels were changed.”

I would not be especially solemn. A person who closes paths cannot afford to treat every disappearance as a funeral. I would have a bad habit of naming temporary structures as if they were permanent: Eternal Bridge, Final Bank, Absolutely Dry Shelter. None would last a season. The joke would improve each time.

My closest companion might be a small inspection machine with six folding legs, built to crawl beneath walkways. It would be ugly, frequently clogged with moss, and intelligent only in narrow ways. It could recognize corrosion, voids, stress fractures, and several species of otter. It would classify all otters as malicious structural events.

I would insist this was accurate.

It would travel strapped to my back until the ground became uncertain, then unfold and move ahead, tapping supports. Children would give it contraband names. Technicians would deny having programmed its irritated whistle.

Over years, the work would alter my body. I would stand poorly on rigid floors, knees loose and weight low. One shoulder would be stronger from the test pole. The pressure pads at my temples would leave permanent pale marks. I would turn my head toward changes no visitor could hear.

I would also become less certain, and better.

When young, I would probably believe courage meant being the last person off a failing path. I would take unnecessary retrievals personally. I would wade back for lamps whose loss had already been included in the design. Someone older would eventually stop me with a closed fist against the chest: understood; hold; I am staying with you.

Not *the lamp is worth nothing*.

Not *you are afraid*.

Only: *This is the moment to release it.*

Perhaps that lesson would become the center of my life.

Later, I would train children and new crew members. I would teach them that a route does not fail when it closes. It fails when its warning does not reach the people who depend on it. I would ask not only whether they had changed the lamps, but whether they had notified the medicine carrier, the observatory shift, the household with the damaged receiver, the keeper bringing a lame calf east. A red light is only a color unless obligation travels with it.

I would be known for conservative closures in thaw weather and reckless ones—according to my colleagues—during early freeze. I would distrust smooth water. I would trust rails more than screens but screens more than intuition pretending to be revelation. Once or twice I would make the wrong decision. A path I kept open would break under an empty supply sled. No one would be harmed, which would not make the error harmless. For years afterward I would hear the sled’s beacon beneath other sounds.

When asked what happened, I would have to say the respected sentence:

“The river left the model, and I followed too late.”

My dead, when I acquired them, would remain in practical forms. A parent’s mineral ceramic might be part of the red lamp housing I carried at my belt. A former crew leader might persist in the grip of my testing pole, where my hand warmed it through the glove. I would not speak to these objects as if the people were trapped inside. Their presence would be less mystical and more demanding than that. They had become part of what I used to keep faith with the living.

Eventually I would retire from the outer routes. My knees would still know how to yield, but not how to recover. I would move into forecast review, sitting in a warm room while younger listeners sent back vibration samples, bird silences, lamp failures, and phrases like *the old levee is trying south*.

I would irritate them by asking for raw sound.

I would close my eyes and listen to rain striking someone else’s hood, their breath through the throat microphone, the tapping pole, the low roll beneath the ground. Perhaps I would detect something they had missed. More often I would only help them trust what they already knew.

And when my own material was fired into ceramic, I would not want to become a household tile kept from weather. I would ask to be mixed into lamp housings—blue and white as well as red, because no person should be remembered only for forbidding passage.

The housings would be moved, recovered, lost, buried, found, and fitted with new lights. Some would stand beside safe paths. Some would mark active work. One would eventually turn red over a crossing nobody had yet named.

So I would not be the master of the river, nor the creature that knows before all others, nor the heroic operator holding the valley together.

I would be the person at the uncertain edge of permission.

You would find me bent beside a trembling path in the metallic half-light, one hand on the peat and the other raised toward my crew. Rain would be running from my sleeves. Somewhere beyond sight, cattle bells would be drawing close together.

I would listen once more.

Then, if the ground had changed, I would change the light.
