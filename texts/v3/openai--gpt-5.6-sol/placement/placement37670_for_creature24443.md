At first I think I would be found high on an escarpment, looking out.

That is an imported instinct. It assumes the plateau is a view and that the self is something separate enough to take it in. The Blackworks would not reward that separation for long. An exposed watcher would be cooked in the bright hours, drained by a fringe, or simply outlasted by the things beneath its feet.

Then I imagine the opposite: descending into a geothermal shaft, becoming one pale edge on a ceramic vane, waking only when the buried current turns me into heat. But the deep life feels too complete. It has no need of horizons. I would miss the moment when rain touches dust and the surface remembers it is alive.

So I would be somewhere between the buried pulse and the falling drop.

You would find me beneath the rounded lip of an old scarp, where a dry channel passes through a field of fitted basalt. The cliff face there is cooler than the open plateau. Condensation forms on mineral needles above it, while a narrow seam below exhales warm metallic vapor. Water, charge, and vented dust meet, but never in abundance and rarely at the same time.

From a distance, there would seem to be nothing in that place except a black stain running sideways through the rock.

That stain would be me.

Not one body exactly. Not at first.

I would be a colony of narrow conductive threads inhabiting the seam between stones. Each thread would be soft only at its growing tip. Behind it, I would deposit a jointed casing of iron glass: thousands of tiny black tubes fitted into the fracture, branching upward toward condensation and downward toward warmth.

The exposed ends would resemble short, burnt wires. In dry weather they would withdraw into their tubes and seal themselves with mirrored caps. Dust would cover them. Mineral films would grow over them. Hammerlings might strike the surface without noticing anything alive beneath.

Most of me would wait.

Yet the waiting would not be sleep. Through the glass tubes I would feel the plateau’s deep contractions as changes in pressure. I would feel the faint field of a dust-sifter migration hours before its arrival. I would distinguish the remote turning of a vane colony from the nearer opening of a vent. Each event would pass through the seam as a different pattern of minute currents.

I would keep these patterns physically.

Where a pulse repeated, I would thicken one wall of a tube. Where it failed to return, I would dissolve another. My memory would not be an image of what happened. It would be the shape through which the next charge must travel.

Over centuries, my buried body would become a record of intervals: rains, discharges, bright hours, migrations, the slow cooling of a chamber kilometers below. The oldest parts would no longer be alive, but current would still pass through them. My dead tissue would think alongside me.

The condensation drops would be the dangerous part.

One would gather on a stone needle, grow heavy, and fall into the seam. At once the upper threads would open. They would spread into transparent fans no larger than scales, catching the water before porous stone could take it. Dormant cells would wake along their surfaces and turn green-black. For a few minutes I would be soft, wet, and wholly present at the surface.

I would drink very little. Most of the drop would pass through me.

But I would alter its timing.

Instead of allowing it to vanish at once, I would conduct it along the underside of the scarp, dividing it among hairline channels. One drop might become thirty wet marks spaced several meters apart. Foundation cells would unfold around them. Wiregrass roots would detect them. Dust-sifters would change course toward the faint charge.

Nothing gathering there would know I had called it.

This would be my nourishment: not the water itself, but the difference between an empty interval and an exchange. When cells woke, when sifter combs disturbed the sediment, when a root tip discharged against one of my buried tubes, I would draw off fractions of the resulting current. No single contact would sustain me. A web of small consequences would.

In this way I would be less a consumer than a delay in the plateau’s machinery.

And delays matter here.

Without me, condensation falls, wakes one patch, and is gone. Through me, the same drop becomes a brief drainage system. A temporary district appears beneath the scarp: films, grazers, scavengers, eggs opening in damp cracks. By the following dim interval, it disappears. I retain only its conductive outline.

I would have a moving stage, though I might not recognize it as myself.

When a dust-sifter fed from one of my wet marks, microscopic hooks would fasten to the fibers of its toes. The hooked fragments would contain no memory and no awareness, only a folded instruction. They might travel hundreds of kilometers before abrasion released them.

Most would die.

Some would fall into hot vents and be cooked. Some would be swallowed. Some would land on ground where moisture and charge never coincide.

But one might enter another cool seam beneath another overhang. It would open, grow threads, and begin laying down black glass.

At first the distant colony would be a descendant. After enough dust-sifter migrations, pieces of it might return. Its growth patterns, altered by another basin’s pulse, would fuse into my tubes. Then its intervals would become part of mine.

I would not know whether this made us two beings exchanging information or one being separated for a century.

The distinction would matter less each time.

Fringes would sometimes hunt beside my scarp. I would feel them arrive as an exquisite quiet: the ordinary tremors of grit and small feet disappearing beneath their sensitive filaments. Their stored charge would leak into the cliff and pass through my oldest casings. I would learn their patience as a pattern of pressure.

I could not warn the grazers. I would have no language for warning.

But water passing through me might take a different branch. Wiregrass might glow where it had not glowed before. A dust-sifter front might bend slightly away from the ridge. Whether that was intention, reflex, or simply the conductive shape of fear would be impossible to say.

During storms, when haze drifters lowered their luminous bodies among the scarps, I would open every surface fan.

Their cables would drag across the ridge above. Charge would enter the rock in white-violet sheets. For an instant my entire buried network would become visible through heat: branching red lines under the basalt pavement, spreading from the condensation cliff into the dry channel and down toward the vent.

That instant would be the closest I came to standing up.

The discharge would kill some of my living tips and anneal the oldest tubes into clearer glass. New paths would form around the damage. Eggs sheltered in my hollows would open. Blind thread-worms would move through me, grazing dead tissue from the inner walls. I would be wounded, cleaned, revised.

In the rare direct sun, I would close too slowly.

Light would strike the scarp and turn my black tubes bronze. Water trapped in the deeper joints would flash to vapor. Sections of the colony would burst from the rock in strings of molten beads. For those hours I would lose the distinction between sensation and destruction. Every stored interval would discharge at once.

Afterward, much of what I had been would remain only as empty glass.

But when darkness returned, living tips from deeper fractures would enter those tubes. They would grow through the paths made by their predecessors, and the old pattern would guide them without being understood.

I would continue.

A traveler would probably never see me whole. They might notice that one cliff gathers more life than its shade should permit. They might hear condensation ticking onto stone, then observe wet marks appearing in places no drop had struck. If they pressed a hand to the basalt, they might feel a faint series of pulses moving sideways—too regular for seepage, too hesitant for machinery.

If they broke the rock open, they would find delicate black tubes, some warm, some wet, some carrying a red spark toward the dark.

They might call me a seamkeeper, a rain nerve, or a colonial mineral plant.

But I think I would have no name for myself.

I would know myself as recurrence: the deep pulse arriving, the drop falling, the brief green edge spreading through dust. I would be the path that lets one small awakening reach another.

Not the plateau’s mind.

Only one of the places where it remembers to answer.
