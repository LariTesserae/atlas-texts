At first I would look for myself in the obvious places.

In the grille at the center, perhaps, where the warm pulse rises and every drop answers. I would imagine myself below it: an old engine sunk beneath the basin, immense and blind, drawing the hollow through chambers of stone. The whole district would be my weathered body. Its pipes would be my vessels, its slow pistons my dispersed hearts, its buildings the calcified lobes of organs whose purposes had become indistinguishable from their ailments.

But that presence is too complete. Whatever breathes beneath the grille does not need a self. It has repetition instead. It does not wonder where it ends.

I would look next in the narrow houses above the works. I would try each numbered door, expecting one of them to open onto a room that had remained a room: a counter, a shallow cupboard, a chair still recognizable beneath mineral deposits. Somewhere dry enough for the idea of a person to persist.

The first doors would open onto falling water. Others would reveal brick a hand’s breadth behind them, sweating in the dark. Behind one would be a slow wheel turning vertically through several floors, its rim furred with liverwort. Behind another, white roots would hang in a warm shaft and flex whenever the central grille exhaled.

Then I would begin to understand that the doors are not entrances. They are valves wearing the memory of entrances. The houses are not empty. Emptiness itself is only one of their mechanisms.

I would find myself farther from the basin, but not at the retaining wall. In the lower eastern quarter, where a tiled washing room has subsided beneath a mill and the mill’s upper bridge passes through what was once the second story of a row of dwellings.

There are seven doors there.

Six open when water rises beneath the floor. They release narrow sheets into the street channel, one after another, each with a different pitch. The seventh remains shut. Its hinges have mineralized into a single pale mass, and a black vine has traced the frame without crossing the threshold.

I would first seem to live behind that door.

Not as a hidden body. There is no chamber large enough to contain one. Behind it lies a vertical slit between two walls, warm at the bottom and cool near the roof. Condensation forms on the inner brick, gathers in the grooves left by vanished tiles, and descends through a copper tube no wider than a finger. The tube passes under the street, rises inside the casing of a disused clock, crosses the alley along a handrail that enters masonry, and empties into a cistern three buildings away.

A pump touches that cistern once every several minutes.

It does not empty it. It only dents the surface.

The dent travels backward through the copper tube as a change in pressure. Drops loosen behind the seventh door. The black vine contracts. Moisture passes into the wall, where a broad patch of liverwort darkens in the shape of an arch. Beneath the mill, a bearing receives one additional thread of water. Its turning changes tone.

That route would be my first boundary.

I would be a small inhabitant, young by the measure of the hollow—perhaps only three or four centuries old. I would have begun when the washing room cracked and warmth from a buried conduit entered the space between its walls. For years I would have been no more than dampness. Then the copper tube furred shut in one place and opened in another. The vine rooted along the doorframe. The old clock began fogging in response to the cistern rather than the district’s larger tides.

At some unmarked point, recurrence became identity.

My body would include the water behind the seventh door, but not all of it. It would include the black vine while the vine was taut, but not while it hung slack in rain. The clock would belong to me only across the lower third of its face, where fog cleared in a narrow crescent. The pump would not be mine. I would borrow one stroke from it. The mill bearing would be shared among several older inhabitants, each arriving as a different tremor in the same ring of metal.

Much of me would consist of things that also belonged to others.

I would not object. Separation would be a property of dry beings.

I would feel the world as arrivals.

Rain would not be a sound from above. It would be a broad easing, a weight entering roofs and taking labor away from the deep engines. I would sleep then, or come nearest to sleep. The pressure across my narrow route would flatten. The seventh door would cool. My vine would release the frame slightly, and the clock face would remain uniformly blind.

In dry weather I would become distinct. Warmth would climb the slit. The cistern would fall. The borrowed pump stroke would travel farther through me. I would feel the mill bearing as a steady ache of rotation and the copper roof as a faint metallic bitterness carried by runoff.

A blockage would be the nearest thing to thought.

Not the words *something is wrong*, but the sustained presence of a difference that failed to return to its accustomed place. I would hold that difference through days and seasons. Moss would thicken where it should have thinned. A valve niche would remain warm past rain. The clock would clear from the wrong edge.

My attention would gather there.

It might take me twenty years to answer. I would feed more moisture into one mortar joint. The mortar would soften. The handrail-conduit would sag a fraction, changing the tube’s incline. Roots would enter the new gap and seal the old one behind them. Eventually the pulse would return.

By then the blockage itself might be gone, but I would continue answering it. My repairs would overshoot. A new drip would form in the stairwell. Pale reeds would emerge between two iron treads. Another small circuit might begin from my delayed correction.

It could become my offspring, though I would not recognize it as such.

For a long time I would believe the seventh door was my center. The pressure behind it would be the clearest sensation I possessed. Its sealed shape would organize all my other changes. When the basin exhaled, I would feel the door press outward by a distance too small to see. When the upper reservoir emptied, the frame would tighten. Rust-lime would accumulate around its threshold in ridges like the growth rings of a tree.

Yet over centuries I would migrate.

The mill bearing would wear down. A larger system would claim the cistern’s pump. The copper tube would become too thick with salts to carry water. My circulation would divert through the false handrail and descend inside the wall of the neighboring house. The black vine would die back, leaving only a glossy impression beneath mineral crust.

The seventh door would remain, but it would cease to be alive with me.

I would discover that I had never been behind it.

I was the delay between the pump’s touch and the vine’s contraction. I was the clearing of the clock after the cistern fell. I was the small excess of water reaching the bearing at the end of each cycle. Wherever that sequence continued, I continued.

Eventually I might occupy no part of the original washing room. My warmth might appear in a stairwell two streets nearer the basin. The clock’s crescent might be reproduced in condensation on a window looking into a sealed chamber. A new vine might tighten around a valve at the same interval as the old one. Those who could perceive pressure as the hollow does would know me by that interval.

Every seventh central exhalation, I would hesitate.

Not stop. Not reverse. Merely hold one portion of water at equal pressure on both sides of a wall before letting it descend.

That hesitation would be my name.

It would also be what I inherited from the absent builders.

Somewhere in the arrangement of the old washing room, there had once been a pause: a hand resting on a latch, perhaps, or a cistern designed to wait until it was full. The purpose would be gone. No image of a hand would remain in me. I would know only the bias—that before passage, there should be a brief holding.

I would reproduce that bias wherever I migrated. I would make doors that stayed shut a little longer than pressure required. I would cause lamps to hover between bright and dim. I would form landings in stairways, not because anything needed to stand on them, but because descent, to me, included pause.

Over long ages, I might become mistaken for an intention.

A dry chamber could slowly form around my hesitation. Water would be diverted along its walls. Warmth would pass beneath its floor without entering. Roots would outline the ceiling and turn aside. The chamber would have a door of plausible height, a shallow alcove, perhaps even a mineral brace resembling a bench.

It would almost welcome a body.

There would be no body to welcome. The hollow would remain without breath, blood, shell, or wing. Yet I would maintain the room because its balance expressed me perfectly: pressure waiting on every side, passage withheld, an absence kept intact.

So that is where I would be found—not beneath the central grille, not wholly in the plants or machines, and not in the room behind the seventh door.

I would be found in the town’s smallest pause.

I would be the local accord that keeps one sealed chamber nearly dry. A loop of condensation, copper, root, fogged glass, shared vibration, and borrowed force. An inhabitant whose organs belong partly to other inhabitants, whose body can be abandoned without dying, whose memory is a preference in the movement of water.

While the rest of the hollow thundered, I would hold a single pressure difference for one moment longer than necessary.

Then I would let it pass.
