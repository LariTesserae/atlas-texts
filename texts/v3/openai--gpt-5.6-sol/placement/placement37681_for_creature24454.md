At first I would mistake myself for one of the people.

I would look for my body among the figures wading the warm channels: long feet spreading over the bronze-green street skin, rain moving in bright lines through the nap on my arms. I would expect to find a chosen name, a trade, a sleeping chamber grown to the temperature of my back.

Then I would notice how often my attention left the people.

I would follow the pause in a doorway after someone passed through. I would listen for the second current beneath the visible one. I would watch an empty patch of mud while towers unfolded behind me. Eventually it would become clear that, though I possessed a person’s body, the city did not locate me entirely inside it.

I would live on the far side of the basin from the Last Slope, in a district that rarely survived the night in the same arrangement. There, two shallow canals repeatedly attempted to cross. Sometimes they joined. Sometimes they recoiled and flowed in parallel. After certain storms, one passed beneath the other without any intervening depth.

Between them lay a clearing.

It was not dramatic. Twelve strides across in the dry season, perhaps thirty when flooded. Silver moss grew around its edge but would not enter. Roots approaching from the nearest towers divided, turned aside, and rejoined beyond it. If a floating market drifted too near, its anchors released without instruction. Birds flew across the clearing but their shadows bent around it.

In its center stood a door.

The door was alive, though there was no wall and no building. It had grown from a loop of pale wood rooted at both ends in the mud. A membrane filled the opening, darkening or clearing according to weather no one else could detect. Most days it showed whatever stood behind it. On rare mornings it showed rain falling in a dry season, a tower where there was open water, or the back of someone who was at that same moment standing in front of it.

No one knew what the door was for.

My work would be to prevent the city from deciding too quickly.

I would be an emptiness keeper.

My water-name would be a pattern difficult to translate: three taps, a pause long enough for one ripple to cross another, then a fourth tap delivered against the current. Children would shorten it to **Late Drop**.

My chosen name would be **Keeps Open What Has Not Arrived**. Friends, being unwilling to say all that whenever they wanted my attention, would call me **Open**.

I would have broad, mud-dark feet and clear inner eyelids inherited from a nest that once lived through years of horizontal rain. Along my forearms would grow rows of fine sensory filaments. They would look like roots but behave more like whiskers, lifting whenever nearby structures considered changing shape. In a district during a growth pulse, they would stand so sharply that sleep became impossible.

My garment would be older than I was. At rest it would resemble several layers of brown leaves fastened over one shoulder. When fed, it would produce small red bladders filled with sour water. When alarmed, it would grip my body hard enough to bruise.

It would dislike the door.

Whenever I stepped into the clearing, the garment would contract and pull backward, trying to return me to the vegetation. I would have to soothe it with sugar and the scent of old rain. We would argue about this for years. Eventually it would learn that fear did not always mean retreat. I would learn that courage did not always mean advancing.

I would not sleep beside the clearing. Emptiness keepers know how easily guardianship becomes possession.

My nest would occupy the back of a young ferry beast called, approximately, **Mud Moving Under Stars**. Its route changed continually, but it returned to the clearing every ninth or tenth day. A chamber-tree grew low between its navigation vanes, just large enough for four adults and two children if everyone liked one another, or three adults if they did not.

The nest would also include a weather shepherd with translucent throat fans, an eater of the third rain whose mask colony sang while digesting, two elderly memory moths, and a child who insisted the ferry beast was their biological parent. No one would correct them. Mud Moving Under Stars would lift one soft foot whenever the child cried, holding it above the water until they stopped.

Most of my days would be spent cutting back abundance.

This would not feel heroic. Growth would push into the clearing constantly. Signal reeds would cast runners across it. Bridge vines would test the open air at night. After floods, mats of tiny flowering plants would cover the mud, each blossom holding a convincing memory of having always belonged there.

I would remove them one by one.

Some I would transplant. Some I would feed to the garment groves. Some I would carry to the listeners and ask whether the city had sent them intentionally. Usually the answer would be complicated and plural.

“We wanted to know if the space was still being kept.”

“Does that mean the flowers were a test?”

“We did not say that.”

“Does the city want them removed?”

“We are uncertain which city you mean.”

I would become impatient with listeners. This would be one of my less admirable traits.

People would imagine that an emptiness keeper practiced perfect restraint, but I would often want to build. I would see how easily the clearing could hold a rain reservoir, a nursery pool, three sleeping chambers, perhaps a grove producing fever fruit. During crowded seasons, when nests doubled themselves and children slept under folded market leaves, keeping bare mud open would feel almost cruel.

Sometimes people would tell me so.

They would ask what good the clearing had done. They would point out that no revelation had appeared there in generations. They would say uncertainty was a luxury and emptiness an aesthetic preference disguised as law.

I would have no answer beyond the work itself.

I would redirect roots. I would taste the water after every storm. I would sit before the door without opening it.

Once, the door would open by itself.

There would be no flash, no violent weather. Its membrane would simply relax at noon. On the other side would stand the same clearing beneath a dim green sky. The canals would be dry. The towers would be leafless and shut. A figure would approach across the cracked mud.

It would be me, though not closely enough to be mistaken for a reflection.

This other self would be heavier, scarred across the mouth, wearing no living garment. Their sensory filaments would have been cut away. They would carry a clay vessel filled to the brim with clear water.

We would observe the required courtesies. Separate bowls. No shared roof. No questions about originality.

They would tell me that in their basin the clearing had been filled.

Not through greed or carelessness. There had been a fever, then a population surge, then an urgent need for healing chambers. The space had remained purposeless for so long that keeping it open seemed like superstition. A medicinal district had been grown there in a single afternoon.

It saved thousands.

Years later, when the city’s water began disappearing into adjacent outcomes, they discovered that the clearing had been the only place where a certain possibility could fail to take root. By then there was nowhere left for that failure to occur.

My other self would not ask us to avoid their history. They would know better than to convert a life into a warning so neatly. They would only give me the vessel.

The water inside it would have no neighboring versions.

It would be ordinary water from a world that had become singular.

The Last Slope attendants would come without being summoned. They would carry the vessel away wrapped in roots that had been killed, eaten, remembered, and regrown for that purpose. For three days no one would make structural decisions near the clearing.

My other self would remain.

This would make us siblings.

They would choose the name **Closes What Has Already Entered** and take up work with the eaters of the third rain. People would call them **Closed**, which would irritate us both. For a long while we would avoid one another. Their presence would make every gesture of mine seem naïve; mine would make every silence of theirs seem accusatory.

Eventually we would begin meeting at the clearing’s edge.

Never inside it.

We would share fruit containing memories neither of us possessed. We would compare the childhood we had in common and discover that even before our histories divided, we remembered it differently. Closed would recall our first room as warm and yellow. I would remember it as cool and green. The chamber-tree, when consulted, would insist it had been blue.

Years would pass. The door would not open again.

My body would become less suited to wading. One transparent eyelid would cloud. The rootlike filaments along my arms would lose sensitivity, until I could no longer feel the city’s intentions before they became movement. Younger keepers would take over the cutting and redirection. They would have new methods and make decisions I disliked.

I would complain that they allowed the moss too close.

They would remind me that the clearing was not mine.

Near the end of my life, I would ask not to be preserved whole. My patience could go into a door-seam. My sense of currents could be given to Mud Moving Under Stars, if the ferry beast wanted it. My irritation with listeners should be fed to something without language.

But I would leave my forearm filaments to the clearing.

Not planted in its center. Never there. They would be grafted into the moss along its border, where they could lift whenever a structure almost began to grow.

After my body was divided, children might still call that trembling **Open**.

Yet I would not be the moss, exactly. Nor the door, nor the ferry beast carrying a little of my sense for hidden currents. I would not be the chamber-tree’s imperfect memory of my sleeping shape, or the keeper who inherited my tools and corrected my mistakes.

I would be distributed among these things, but distribution would not be the whole answer.

What I would be, finally, is the maintained interval between an arrival and the decision to receive it.

A person, for a while.

An emptiness keeper by practice.

A sibling by accident.

And, in the city’s deeper recognition, one small act of not yet—standing in warm rain at the edge of bare mud, keeping a place for the reason that has not arrived.
