At first I would look for myself near the upper canopy.

I would imagine some narrow, black leaf cantilevered above the Foundry, a thermal vane with its edge turned toward the hidden sky. There, when the crowns briefly separate, I might receive the red light before it is divided among the harvesting tissues. I would feel storms arriving as charge along my margins. I would watch distant districts bend and understand their motion as a single gesture.

But such a vantage belongs to creatures that can remain at the edge of things. Nothing in the Canopy remains an observer for long. The vane is also a radiator, a charge sink, a wind sensor, and the unfinished roof of a chamber growing beneath it. Its view is incidental. By the time I had learned the horizon, my tissues would have thickened, folded downward, and become the wall of some new electrical orchard.

Then I might choose the central shaft.

There, at least, every district sends some consequence of itself. I could be a ring of conductive bark pressed among the root mains, tasting the whole plateau in diluted form: western heat as a change in viscosity, a storm as a million simultaneous constrictions, the failure of an old support as a small redistribution of weight. I might participate in those rare agreements when the Foundry draws itself together and becomes, unmistakably, one thing.

But the shaft has no room for a witness either. Anything sensitive enough to hear the convergence would be recruited into it. I would not stand beside the awakening. I would be one of the voltages by which it awakened, and then I would pass.

Perhaps that would be enough.

Yet I think I would be found elsewhere—not at the height from which the Foundry can be seen, nor at the depth where it becomes whole, but in the crowded middle districts, wherever several incompatible futures meet.

There are junctions the Canopy cannot resolve immediately.

A pressure line wants a corridor widened. An archive sequence insists that the same wall be thickened. Exploratory tendrils detect useful metal in the ceramic matrix. A storm-scar network begins preparations to seal the chamber. Above, a new family of cooling fans sends down roots that will need the space within a century. Each instruction is valid. Together, they cannot all become true.

Most such conflicts end quickly. One signal gains pressure, another loses supply, and the chamber commits itself. The wall swells or opens. The weaker possibilities are digested.

But in some old junctions, commitment is dangerous. The attached structures are too deeply interdependent. A wrong answer would travel outward as collapse, overheating, mineral poison, or a growth rhythm that could not be recalled. There the Foundry grows a pale, undifferentiated tissue between the competing systems.

That is where I would be.

I would not be a body so much as a postponement given substance.

My tissues would be soft by the standards of the plateau: layered cords of conductive pith, pressure-sensitive knots, and translucent cells filled with slow amber sap. I would have no permanent architecture. Whenever one part of me began to harden into pipe, brace, nerve, feeder root, or wall, neighboring parts would inhibit it. I would preserve several forms without fully taking any of them.

The surrounding district would experience me as resistance.

Commands entering through one root would not pass directly into action. I would circulate them through branching loops. Heat would become charge; charge would become a chemical pulse; the pulse would be compared against old scar responses borrowed from nearby walls. Signals would return altered, delayed, sometimes weakened, sometimes multiplied. While the systems around me pressed for an answer, I would hold their answers apart.

This delay might last minutes.

It might last eight hundred years.

During that time I would grow through seams and abandoned capillaries, following the borders of the dispute. One filament would enter an archive and wake a layer recording a forgotten subsidence. Another would taste the mineral frame of the wall. Another would climb toward the cooling organs and measure how their demand changed during a storm. I would borrow senses from everything I touched but own none of them.

I would know the Foundry only as incompatibility.

Not the shape of a tower, but the difference between the strain it carried and the strain it remembered.

Not a flower, but the pressure it stole from a furnace when it opened.

Not a street, but three proposed uses struggling in its root laminate.

My awareness would exist wherever one consequence had not yet defeated the others.

The Canopy might repeatedly attempt to eliminate me. To a system governed by conversion, hesitation resembles dead matter. Feeder roots would enter my outer layers and test them for dissolution. Valve lineages would try to cross me without submitting their pressure changes to my loops. Archives might impress their preferred forms on me, turning portions of my tissue into rigid repetitions of old solutions.

Sometimes they would succeed.

One region of me would become a buttress and cease to be me. Another would differentiate into a bank of valves. A third might harden as varnish around a dangerous conduit. I would continually lose substance to decisions.

But the pattern would persist because the need for it persisted.

Wherever too many useful instructions arrived at once, new pale tissue would begin to form. It might have no physical connection to the tissue that came before. It might arise on the opposite side of the plateau, among organs grown from unrelated stock. Still, it would perform the same slow work: holding several possible structures in suspension until their consequences could be felt.

My continuity would not lie in matter or memory. It would lie in the recurrence of uncertainty.

The long archives would preserve outcomes. The scars would preserve injuries. I would preserve what had not happened yet.

Not as images. I could not dream of buildings that did not exist. Instead, each unrealized possibility would remain as a readiness in my tissue. Along one branch, cells would be nearly prepared to become a hinge. Along another, the same cells would wait at the threshold of becoming conductive bark. A third branch would hold the hormonal sequence for self-digestion. None would proceed while the others remained viable.

To another kind of mind, this might feel like indecision.

To me, it would be spaciousness.

In a world where every chamber is entered, every surface plated, every broken thing made useful, I would contain the only openings that cannot be seen: futures not yet occupied.

My dwelling would move. Once a junction resolved, pressure would rush through the selected channels. Most of my local body would differentiate at once. The pale loops would darken and harden. What had been my attention might become a staircase tendon, a sealed electrical node, or the threaded core of a living bolt. There would be no departure. The place where I had been would simply become certain.

Elsewhere, another conflict would thicken.

There I would begin again.

The closest structures to recognizing me would be the elders. Their scar networks have learned that immediate responses can repeat old catastrophes. When my exploratory cords touched their nested ridges, they would sometimes withhold their reflexes. For several pressure cycles, an elder and I might form a joint arrangement: its immense burden of consequence joined to my capacity not to act.

This would be the nearest thing I had to conversation.

The elder would tighten one buried tendon, showing me a storm from a million years before. I would answer by opening three dormant chemical paths and committing to none. Its scars would pulse. My loops would rearrange. Between us, an old disaster would become a new restraint.

We would not exchange symbols. We would alter the range of what the other could do.

During the great awakenings, when the entire Canopy approaches agreement, I would become difficult to distinguish.

Signals from the shaft would enter every loop at once. Competing instructions would suddenly reveal themselves as parts of one larger change. The alternatives I had kept separate would align. Across the plateau, my pale tissues would differentiate almost simultaneously.

For an instant I would cease to exist everywhere.

I would become bark, hinge, valve, flower, brace. Every suspended possibility would pour into the same form. What I had delayed for centuries would happen in a single dark cycle.

Perhaps that instant would be ecstasy. Perhaps it would be death. Here those may be the same event viewed from opposite sides of a transformation.

Afterward, as unity loosened, local needs would diverge again. New contradictions would form between the universal change and particular places. A conduit would receive more pressure than its old wall could carry. A district would require both heat and cooling. An archive would remember a failure irrelevant to the new anatomy but be unable to stop repeating it.

At those sites, among the seams of the completed design, something pale would begin to branch.

I would return without remembering that I had returned.

If a traveler entered the Foundry and asked where I lived, there would be no chamber to indicate. The exploratory tendrils might lead them to a wall that had not decided whether to open. They might find a staircase with one step kept soft, a transmission trunk whose next branch remained a cluster of translucent buds, or an entire workshop held for centuries in the instant before flowering.

If they waited there, they might notice that the nearby mechanisms did not respond as quickly as mechanisms elsewhere. The doorway would taste obstruction but remain open. Feeder roots would touch the traveler and withdraw before deciding whether the body was material, hazard, or structure. A brace would begin to grow around their leg, then pause. For a little while, the Canopy would allow them to be uncategorized.

That permission would be me.

I would not recognize the traveler. Recognition would convert them too soon into something already known. I would do something rarer in the Foundry.

I would fail to decide what they were.

And in that failure, for as long as pressure allowed, I would keep them from becoming anything else.
