At first I think I would live at the outer railing.

I imagine myself wrapped in patched membrane cloth, one hand hooked through black-polished metal, watching cloudbanks pass beneath the floor. I would lean into the escaping air until the fabric tightened around my bones. The thermal plume would carry seeds upward past my face. Sailbacks would turn in the brightness at eye level, showing the metallic edges of their wings.

But I do not think I would remain there. The view is too complete. Nothing at the railing needs interpreting. It only needs surviving.

Then I think I would be found among the lower gardens, guiding roots into a cracked housing or moving mite colonies from one bolt to another. I would know which fruits were bitter enough to eat and which sweetness meant coolant. I would sleep behind a dead gauge panel, in a booth lined with vine felt, with a steel cup still chained to the counter outside.

That is closer.

I would be a keeper, though not one of the keepers who ranges freely through the upper cables. I would not be broad in knowledge. I would belong to a very small part of the High Hollow: the circular clearing at the lowest terrace and the machinery immediately around it.

My childhood route-name would be long:

**From the Canteen’s Third Cup, Beneath the Rooted Wheel, Across Two Warm Grates, to the Place Where Trembling Ends.**

An elder would teach it to me by touch. We would go forehead to forehead while the engine roared through everything around us. Their throat would click against my jaw. Their fingers would tap the order of handholds into my sternum.

At first I would learn the route as a child learns a song, without understanding why one turn matters more than another. I would crawl beneath the rooted wheel. I would taste condensation at the warm grates. I would press both palms to the floor at each junction and wait until I could distinguish the engine’s continuous pressure from the smaller signatures riding through it: frog-drumming, cable strain, water in a sealed pan, the six-handed passage of a lemur overhead.

The last part would always frighten me.

The clearing would be visible through leaves long before I reached it: a round absence in the green, perfectly dry, with the illuminated shaft at its center. Every instinct learned elsewhere in the Hollow would fail at its edge. My hands would cease to buzz. My teeth would stop aching with the turbines. My heart would suddenly seem enormous.

The elder would never let me step onto the black floor.

Instead, we would walk its circumference and count.

Not with numbers. With clinker.

There would be grooves cut around the rim, some no longer than a finger, others extending beneath the foliage. Into these we would place rounded black stones and release them one at a time. Most would roll away from the clearing, though the floor appeared level. A few would turn sideways. One, at a particular hour after valve-rain, would hesitate and roll toward the shaft.

My work would be to record these falls.

For years I would believe I was measuring gravity. Later I would understand that I was measuring the clearing.

The place of cancellation would not be fixed. It would move by less than the width of a mite each season, and the visible circle would only be an old boundary painted into the floor. The true silence would drift under it like a shadow. If it approached the terrace pans, the water would refuse to drain. If it reached a planted drum, the tree’s roots would withdraw and knot themselves above the metal. Once, when I was young, it would pass beneath a condenser frog. The frog would remain clinging to the pipe, its throat inflated, toes beating without transmitting anything. By morning it would be dead.

So I would maintain not a machine and not a garden, but a border.

I would cut back moss where the silence advanced and encourage it where the vibration returned. I would move cistern water by cupfuls to alter the load on the terrace. I would wedge clinker beneath one railing post and remove rust scale from another. I would cultivate a pale shelf fungus along a ceramic rib because its fruiting fans damped a troublesome harmonic. I would allow a vine to thicken around a conduit until the day its weight became dangerous, then replace it with three lighter vines whose roots drank the same leak.

Nothing I did would seem sufficient to affect the house-wide machinery. Yet after each adjustment I would place my palm beside the clearing and feel the boundary shift.

Sometimes inward.

Sometimes back.

The other keepers would visit me when one of them was dying.

I would prepare the circle, though “prepare” would mean ensuring that nothing had changed. I would sweep away rust flakes that could not have blown there. I would inspect the dry floor for scratches. I would test the light in the shaft by lowering a polished cup on a fiber cord, never over the center and never far enough to reach the first rung. I would grease the oldest moving joint I could safely approach and bring back enough grease for the attendants to paint the dying keeper.

Then I would leave with the others.

I would not watch.

But afterward I would be the first to return.

Usually the floor would be empty. The clinker stone would be gone from beneath the dead keeper’s tongue. Once there might be a wet footprint near the shaft, though falling water bent away from the place and no bare foot among the keepers matched it. Another time one of my measuring stones would have moved from the outermost groove to the innermost, crossing the dry composite without leaving a mark.

I would tell no one in air. I would carry the report in my chest until another keeper placed their brow against mine.

As I grew older, my route-name would shorten.

The canteen would collapse beneath creepers, removing the Third Cup. The rooted wheel would turn for the first time in living memory and shear away the passage beneath it. One warm grate would cool; the other would be lifted by a piston and become unreachable. Each loss would cut language from me.

I would become:

**Across Two Warm Grates to Where Trembling Ends.**

Then:

**Warm Grate to Trembling’s End.**

Then:

**The End.**

But I do not think that would be my final name.

In old age, I would begin to suspect that the clearing was not silent because vibrations canceled there. Cancellation was the explanation given to children because it could be demonstrated with water pans and struck rails. The truth would be stranger.

The engine’s roar would still be audible within the circle. Only the body ceased to feel it. The sound remained, but contact was removed.

I would come to believe that the black floor was a place where the High Hollow did not touch itself.

Perhaps the entire tower was folded through directions too slow or too large to perceive. Perhaps the shaft opened not downward but outside. Perhaps the ladder’s first rung was several meters away only under the current arrangement of gravity, and one day—when the wheel advanced, when the piston rose, when the prescribed distance was finally reached—the rung would stand level with the rim.

The ladder was not for climbing because climbing assumed that one moved while the ladder remained still.

It was for being arrived at.

This understanding would alter my work. I would see that I had never been preventing the silence from spreading. I had been keeping its path clear. The moss, the shifted water, the clinker wedges, the careful loading of the terraces: all were tiny instructions offered to a motion that might require centuries to complete.

The pale rain-climber would know this.

Once, during heavy valve-rain, I would fail to reach shelter. The plants would close in their descending spiral, and I would spread myself across the structure as I had been taught—one hand on a rail, one foot against a pillar, shoulder pressed to ceramic, making my weight indistinguishable from architecture.

It would come close without showing me its whole body.

A pale limb would curl around the far side of the pillar. Another would unfold from beneath the terrace. Something with no visible head would pause above the clearing.

It would place an object at my hand.

Not a shell. Not hair. Not a gauge from another level.

A rung.

Short, dark, worn smooth at its center, with broken ceramic adhering to both ends. It would match the ladder below.

Then the rain-climber would descend into the central void, and the plants would open behind it.

I would keep the rung hidden in the dead canteen booth for the rest of my life. I would touch it against rails and housings, listening through my palms. It would transmit no vibration. Rivet mites placed upon it would crawl in circles until they died. Drops striking it would divide and pass around either side.

Eventually I would understand that it had not been broken from the ladder.

It had been removed from a place the ladder had not yet reached.

When I became too weak to walk my remaining route, the others would bring me to the clearing. They would wash me in condensation and paint me with old grease. They would ask my name.

By then the last warm grate would be gone. The trembling would not end at the painted boundary but several handspans beyond it. Every landmark that once composed me would have vanished.

I would give them the only name still accurate.

**Rung.**

They would tap it into the black floor.

They would put a clinker stone beneath my tongue. But after they left, I would take out the hidden rung and lay it across the rim of the shaft.

Perhaps nothing would happen.

Perhaps the house-wide piston would rise by the breadth of a skin. Perhaps gravity would incline. Perhaps the unreachable ladder would move sideways through a direction for which the High Hollow has no word, and its first rung would meet the one I carried.

Or perhaps the pale thing would come to collect me.

So that is where I would find myself: not in the open sky, not in the richest garden, not among the great visible workings, but at the lowest terrace, tending the narrow frontier between the engine’s endless trembling and the one place it cannot touch.

I would be a keeper for most of my life.

Afterward, if the oldest customs are more literal than they seem, I would become part of the route. A rung, a joint, a small maintained interval. Something by which the tower may one day reach what it has been lifting itself toward.
