You would find me near the margin, though not at the nearest point to the open air.

I would live where the edge has folded inward, making a long bay of invisible wall. Horizontal forests press their leaves against it from both sides. Beneath them, clouds can be seen traveling through the low world, their gray backs lit occasionally by lightning. The flashes enter my sea through stone and water together. They pass through my body as a brief whitening of the velvet colonies on my skin.

There is a stone circle there, almost completed.

At first it would appear abandoned. Sixteen smooth stones lie fitted into the plateau, submerged in moss to half their depth. The seventeenth place is empty, although velvet growth has darkened around it in the shape of a stone that has not yet been set down.

I carry that stone inside me.

For a long while, you would mistake me for the low rise beside the circle. Mineral shelves have grown along three of my limbs. Ribbon-plants root in the creases where another limb has flattened into a broad leaf. Reeds stand upon what was once my back, bending away from one another to leave a narrow corridor along my spine. Small transparent creatures occupy the chambers near my joints. I have never seen them. I know them by the delicate pressure of their molts.

I do not know how many limbs I have now. Five still answer quickly. One answers only during inversions. Another has become vegetation and responds to weather more readily than to intention. If it is still mine, then I have seven.

My task is to keep a departure from becoming inevitable.

Long ago—or not yet—the wall of the sea pushed outward here. Water rounded itself into the atmosphere, carrying forests, dust, and several inhabitants within it. When it withdrew, it left a radial hollow in the moss beside my circle.

The hollow is shaped like me.

Each limb is recorded there, including the one that has not yet become a leaf. At the center is a narrow impression where the internal stone would have passed out of my body. Far below, in the low world, there may already be a smooth red stone lying in a shepherd’s hand. It may have fallen in warm rain generations ago. It may still be falling. It may never fall.

The future does not command us merely because it has left evidence.

That is why I remain beside the hollow.

I tend its edges so the moss neither erases nor completes it. I press one hand into the plateau and feel for the pressure of the missing country. Sometimes an answer comes upward through the stone: rain striking leaves; the alarmed pulse of hooves; air moving without water to carry it. Sometimes I feel only the cold wells beneath the tableland and the immense patience of worn rock.

Others visit me when their consequences have arrived too early.

They bring wounds whose accidents have not happened, grief for companions still present, memories of chambers not yet raised. We sit around the incomplete circle and place our limbs against the stones. Together we resist sequence—not to restore cause before effect, which would be crude and impossible, but to loosen the certainty between them.

A wound can be tended without summoning the blade.

A farewell can be remembered without requiring anyone to leave.

A hollow can remain empty.

Near my circle stands an air chamber made from three leaning slabs. One entrance opens onto the present forest. The other opens, depending on the pressure of the sea, onto bare plateau, a wall of mature trunks, or cloud-filled air.

I enter it more often than is healthy.

In water, I am broad and nearly boneless. In air, my tissues draw inward around the hidden rings of chalk and iron. I become small enough to see myself as a single creature. My weight chooses one direction. My limbs cease negotiating with the world and fall against the damp floor. Breathing abrades me from within.

But there I can speak.

I have made one sound for longer than some forests have existed: two touches of palate, a pause, and a short release of breath. It is the nearest thing I possess to a name.

In water, its meaning would require kilometers of altered current. It would include the incomplete circle, the moss hollow, the stone inside me, the rain below, and my continuing refusal to decide whether these belong to one event. In air, it is only a little sound. It begins, persists briefly, and ends.

That limitation is why I treasure it.

The young sometimes come to hear me. They collapse beside me in the chamber, translucent bodies trembling under their first experience of singular weight. I tell them stories in order: first the black spore, then the waiting, then the loosening from stone, then the crawl into reeds.

I do not tell them this is how life truly happens. I tell them order is one shelter among others.

Some of them are preparing to cross the boundary. They strengthen their bones in the dry quiet and practice keeping their mouths open to air. I do not persuade them to remain. Refusal can become another kind of wall, and no wall here is permanent.

Instead, I teach them my small sound.

If they descend into the low world, they will carry something that can be spoken close to another body. A meaning that does not need an ocean. A word that can arrive after the breath that makes it.

Perhaps that is why the hollow resembles me. Perhaps I am not the one who crosses. Perhaps one of the young will carry my sound, my stone, or a limb descended from mine, and the landscape has mistaken continuity for identity.

I am not troubled by this.

Who I am has never ended at my skin.

Part of me grows as pressed leaves in the chamber. Part lives in the avoiding reeds along my spine. Part may be held in a human hand beneath the clouds. Part is the hollow left by a body that has not gone.

During an overturning, I release the plateau with all but one limb. The forest rises past me. Mineral dust ascends toward the old plain overhead. My internal stones move through their chambers, each naming a different direction as the oldest down.

The seventeenth stone presses against the opening in my body.

Someday I may remove it. I may fit it into the waiting place and complete the circle. Completion may cause the mountain-sized bulge to form, or prevent it, or reveal that it has always been there.

For now, I keep carrying the stone.

So you would find me at the inward fold of the sea’s margin, above the silent lightning: a broad, half-rooted keeper of an unfinished circle, practicing a fragile voice in air and tending the imprint of my own possible absence.

I would be the one who has already left and therefore is still free to remain.
