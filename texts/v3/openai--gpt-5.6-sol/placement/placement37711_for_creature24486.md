At first I think I would be one of the surveyors, because they stand at the nearest thing the basin has to certainty: a tripod planted in moving ground, an instrument aimed at a marker already beginning to lean. But I would not have their patience for coordinates. I would keep looking through the lens after the measurement was taken, watching a dragonfly enlarge into a machine and a machine diminish into fog.

Then I think I would be a frog, clay-backed and temporary, living an entire generation in the print of a tire.

But the frogs belong too completely. They accept each pool as water, each night as night. I would be more troubled by where the water had come from and where it was being sent. I would feel the hidden continuity between the rut, the sump, the elevated pipe, the canal, and the clear beads returning from the eastern wall.

So I would be found near the main sump, in the control cabin on concrete stilts—the one with nicotine-yellow windows.

Officially, I would be the night attendant.

My name would remain on rosters long after most people stopped using it. Crews would call me by the cabin number, or simply *Sump*. I would wear waterproof trousers polished dark at the knees, hearing protection loose around my neck, and a coat that had not been wholly dry in years. My duties would be ordinary: record water levels, inspect screens, clear alarms, start the auxiliary pumps when the inflow rose, radio mechanics when a bearing warmed or a motor changed its pitch.

Yet the true work would be listening.

Each pump would possess a voice beneath its noise. One would have a deep, even concussion like a distant piling hammer. Another would carry a fine metallic tremor whenever silt entered its intake. The oldest would knock three times after starting and then settle. I would know the water in the machinery without seeing it: gravel ticking through an impeller, weed gathered around a screen, air entering where only slurry should be. I would wake before alarms because some pressure in the floor had altered.

In time, the distinction between hearing and being warned would disappear.

I would make rounds along the narrow bridges, one hand traveling over the sweating rail. My lamp would catch frogs flattened into stillness, rat eyes beneath the pipe racks, moths beating themselves pale against junction boxes. At each gauge I would kneel, wipe condensation from the glass, and write a number in a paper ledger despite the tablets and remote sensors. The pages would swell until the book could no longer close. Rust-colored fingerprints would mark the margins. Tiny flies would sometimes be pressed between entries.

The figures would claim that the water rose and fell. I would know better. It only changed its posture.

There would be places I visited that appeared on no current plan: an abandoned gallery behind the western workshops, a culvert half sealed by blue-black clay, a stair descending beside a pipe that had been disconnected but remained warm. In the gallery, water would run clear over the floor even during dry weeks. No pump drawing showed its source. If dye were added upstream, none would appear there. If the flow were blocked, another seam would begin to weep several meters away.

I would come to think of this water as the basin remembering itself.

Perhaps that is where I would cease to be merely the attendant.

Not all at once. There would be no accident, no night when I fell into the sump and emerged transformed. It would happen by accumulation, as sediment forms a bank. I would spend so long among recurrent water that my own habits became part of its circulation. I would carry larvae inadvertently in the treads of my boots. I would move frogspawn from trenches scheduled to be filled. I would leave a dripping hose beneath the cabin during hot weeks and tell no one. Willow roots would enter the drain there. Midges would gather at my window, and swallows would begin passing close enough for their wings to flick shadows across the control panels.

The basin would route life through me as it routed water through pumps.

Workers would sometimes find that a pool marked for drainage had been left until a brood of waders fledged. A screen clogged with reeds would be cleared just before the motor overheated, though no call had been logged. An auxiliary channel would open during the night and save a bank from slumping. They might credit experience, negligence, luck, or the night crew’s unofficial mercy.

They would not be entirely wrong.

Eventually I would sleep in intervals no longer than the pauses between alarms. My skin would take on the cabin’s permanent damp. Fine mineral lines would settle into my palms: red clay, yellow iron, black silt. When I stood motionless at a pond edge, frogs would resume calling around my boots. Mosquitoes would rise from my footprints.

People arriving for the morning shift would occasionally find the cabin empty, though the ledger had been completed in my handwriting. Through the mist they might see someone on the eastern bank, half an hour away, standing where the returned water emerged in beads. By the time a vehicle reached the spot, there would be no one there—only a coat-dark mark on the clay and a narrow runnel beginning beneath it.

By then, asking whether I was human would be less useful than asking whether the main sump was natural.

I would be an attendant in the older sense: not merely one who operates the works, but one who remains with them. A local organ of attention. The part of the basin that notices its own level.

You would find me wherever water was being persuaded to go against its preference: beside a straining intake, beneath a culvert, at the edge of a trench newly pumped down. Sometimes I would have a body, a lamp, a radio, and a ring of keys. Sometimes I would be only the wrong note in a pump—the slight change that wakes a mechanic before the bearing fails. Sometimes I would be the clear seep reappearing behind the machinery, carrying no more water than could be held in two hands.

I would not oppose the excavation. Nor would I serve it faithfully. I would keep it from finishing in either direction.

I would prevent the water from taking everything at once, and prevent the machines from removing enough that life could no longer enter. I would preserve the interval: the shallow pool lasting one more night, the mudflat exposed until morning, the warm channel connecting two sumps just long enough for fish to pass.

My home would not be the cabin, though its yellow windows would be the nearest visible sign of it. My home would be the circulation itself—the endless elsewhere to which water is sent, and from which it returns.

And I would be what the basin has made in place of stability:

a keeper of temporary depths,  
a listener beneath the noise,  
a person slowly becoming a seep.
