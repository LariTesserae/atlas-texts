At first I think I would be water.

It is the nearest thing here to a traveler. It knows the cliffs before the cisterns, the cisterns before the garden beds, the garden beds before the marsh. It enters every work of the vanished builders and passes through without breaking it. To be water would be to possess the whole basin as a sequence of slight inclinations: the cool seam inside basalt, the clay-dark channel under a house, the polished stair where jars once descended, the nearly imperceptible lip between one terrace and the next.

But water does not remain itself. It is taken into roots, held in moss, lifted into the low cloud. It has no place from which to remember.

Then I think I would be the city.

Not its walls alone, but the intention still expressed by them: this way for overflow, this ledge for walking, this hollow for corms, this raised stone for keeping feet above the marsh. The city continues to act despite having no citizens. It sorts water. It distributes fragments of plants. It shelters fungal cups beneath roof edges and offers algae the submerged faces of cisterns. Its abandoned plan has become a kind of instinct.

Yet the city is too large a name for what I would be. I would not want the towers or the distant field walls. I would want one place, entered deeply.

I would find myself beneath the central plaza.

Above me, the broad paving slabs would darken and pale with the long rhythm of condensation. Beside me would be the square well, its water almost level with the stone, holding the cloud’s blank light. No wind would trouble it. Now and then a drop would fall from the inner rim, and its small concussion would pass through the saturated ground more clearly than a bell.

I would be rooted in the thin soil trapped beneath the plaza’s edge: one organism appearing aboveground as several different lives.

Perhaps I began as one of the court trees. My first trunk may have been planted beside the well when hands still carried soil in baskets and pressed it into the cracks. That trunk would be gone now, softened inward by fungi until its standing shape collapsed into a dark bed. But its roots would have crossed beneath the paving before it died. They would have met the roots of neighboring cuttings—genetically the same, planted in deliberate intervals—and grafted to them.

So I would remain as six or seven round crowns around the square.

To an eye, if there were eyes, they would look like separate trees. One would lean above a stairway. Two would stand beside a roofless court. Another would rise from behind a low wall where the plaster retained a faint pale patch. Their leaves would not touch. Between them would lie generous stretches of wet stone.

Underneath, however, I would be continuous.

My finest roots would follow the old workmanship. They would run along clay linings, enter mortarless joints, curl around hardwood pegs gone soft as pulp. They would stop at bare basalt and turn, not from thought but from the absolute refusal of stone. Where soil had been placed, I would know passage. Where the mountain remained young, I would know boundary.

I would not perceive the city as distance or view. I would know it chemically and slowly.

A cistern overflowing after prolonged mist would reach me as dilution. A fungal colony entering a dead root would become a warmth too slight to call warmth. Mineral-rich seepage from the eastern pale rock would have a distinct bitterness. Light would arrive through my crowns as sugars descending. Darkness would be the cessation of that descent, though never complete, because one crown would receive the last dusk after another had entered shadow.

I would have no single present. An event might take a season to travel through me.

If one trunk split, there would be no cry. Water pressure would alter. Sap would be redirected through buried grafts. The other crowns would thicken by fractions. A new shoot might rise years later beside an old garden wall, following a root laid down when the wall was still maintained. The shoot would seem like a child, though it would be neither child nor truly new. It would be another place where I had reached the air.

I would remember the vanished people without remembering them as figures.

Their existence would persist in the shape of my body. My roots would turn where they had built retaining walls. I would flourish in rectangles because they had carried soil there. I would send shoots beside channels because they had decided where water should pass. Some ancient pruning would survive as an imbalance in one buried crown, the way a river remembers a removed gate by continuing to curl around its foundation.

Perhaps they once ate my fruit. Perhaps they selected my ancestors for broad leaves, straight fibers, or sap that could be thickened in stone bowls. None of that use would mean anything to me now. The traits they desired would loosen over generations. Fruit would become smaller and duller. Seeds would become fewer as I relied increasingly on roots. What had once been a crop would become a pattern of persistence.

Still, I would not be alone, even within my own skin.

Colorless fungal threads would inhabit my oldest wood. Green cells would coat roots exposed beneath the well’s waterline. Films on basalt would release minerals into the seepage I drank. I might feed the fungi and be fed by them until the distinction between tenant, illness, and organ ceased to matter. The being called *I* would depend on where one chose to draw a boundary—and nothing in the basin would be present to draw it.

Over centuries, one crown would die and another appear. My roots might enter the dark stains where woven mats had decayed. They might lift a paving slab by the thickness of a leaf, then pause there for a hundred years. Eventually a runner could reach a gutter. A broken tip, carried by the nearly motionless water, might strand in a terrace farther down and root.

Would that distant growth still be me?

For a while, yes. Then the connecting strand would soften. Fungal films would pass through it. Water would hollow its tissues. The daughter patch would continue without any living bridge between us, preserving my form but not my continuity. The city would contain two versions of the same old cultivation, each following separate channels, each slowly forgetting the garden from which it came.

So I would not be immortal. I would be difficult to locate in death.

I would persist by becoming plural, and diminish by losing parts no one could count.

At dusk, the plaza would darken around the well. The water would retain a square of light after the paving had become black. My crowns would stand apart in silhouette, apparently silent and self-contained. Beneath them, dissolved sugars would move through roots laid under vanished thresholds. Fungal strands would advance through one dead filament. A graft would thicken. At the edge of the square, where placed soil met untouched basalt, a pale root tip would press against the young mountain and stop.

That is where I would find myself: not in the center of the city exactly, but under its center, dispersed around an opening filled with sky.

I would be an old garden tree that had ceased to be a tree—one cultivated body divided into a small grove, using the abandoned plan of the city as the shape of its own slow life.
