I would not find myself standing in the basin.

Standing would already be a kind of violence: a foot insisting upon one place, forcing frogs from moss, pressing chambers shut beneath the roots. Nor would I be one of the birds, looking down through the layers. Sight grants too much distance. Nothing here is permitted the illusion of being apart.

At first I might enter as warmth.

The basin would register me before any creature saw me. Condensation would gather on my skin; birds would tighten their claws; the pool would receive the pressure of my weight through root and stone. For a moment I would exist as an interruption traveling outward—a message without meaning, distributed in droplets.

But the place would not let me remain an event. Warmth would pass into water. Salt would be taken by tongues too small to see. Fibers would soften, skin would host spores, breath would join the mist. Whatever in me insisted on being a visitor would be dismantled first.

When the basin had finished finding uses for my separateness, I think I would awaken beneath the shelf where the pool disappears.

Not beyond it. There may be no meaningful beyond. I would cling to the underside of the stone lip, in the narrow interval where dark water presses itself flat and enters the mountain.

I would be a small, colorless creature, something between an amphipod and a leaf: no longer than the final joint of a finger, thin enough to inhabit the seam without obstructing it. My shell would be soft and faintly jointed. Mineral grains would show through me. I would have many legs, but the foremost pair would open into delicate fans.

I would be nearly blind. Perhaps I would possess two clouded pits capable only of distinguishing the green-black of the pool from the complete black beneath the rock. Vision would be unnecessary. The entire basin would arrive through the water.

A frog lowering one toe would be a broad, slow pressure. Rain passing from leaf to leaf would reach me as fine stippling. The owl’s departure would descend through branch, trunk, root, and pool as a single deep tremor. A stone flake dropped by one of the browsers would ring so sharply through my body that I would fold all my limbs and remain flush against the shelf.

I would not know these things as frog, rain, owl, or stone. I would know only their shapes in pressure: widening rings, brief needles, a weight that enters and is gone.

When the water carried spores, fragments of fungus, wing scales, pollen, blood, or the transparent remains of eggs, I would open my fans. What the basin released, I would comb from the current. I would eat almost nothing that could be named, only the worn minuteness of other lives.

There would be others of my kind along the shelf, but we would not gather socially. We would occupy adjacent imperfections in the rock, close enough that our antennae sometimes crossed. That touch would be sufficient acknowledgment. If one hollow emptied, a young creature would settle into it. If no hollow emptied, the young would be eaten in the pool.

My eggs would adhere beneath my body in a clear cluster. Some would contain only a pale point, others folded limbs and beating interiors. I would not remember laying them. Memory, in the form I know it now, would be useless there. The water would bring me the condition of the present with such completeness that nothing else could be held.

Still, I would retain a smaller memory: the angle at which to brace when the current thickened; the particular roughness where one leg belonged; the sequence by which my fans should close when a fish approached. My intelligence would consist of exact returns.

From time to time a salamander’s blunt head would enter beneath the shelf. Its mouth would widen. One of us would vanish. The remaining bodies would draw flat, then gradually unfold. No alarm would persist after the pressure had passed.

When my own turn came, perhaps I would be swallowed whole. Perhaps fungus would enter a weak seam in my shell and bloom through me while I still clung to the rock. Perhaps I would simply fail to brace, loosen, and travel into the mountain with the departing water.

In none of these endings would I leave a vacancy. Another translucent body would settle where mine had been, place its legs in the same dim irregularities, and open its fans to the same current. From above, there would be no sign that anything had changed.

That would be the nearest thing to a self I could keep there: not a name or a history, but a posture repeated by many bodies.

You would find me at the basin’s least visible threshold, where water ceases to belong to the pool but has not yet become an underground stream. I would not guard the passage. I would not understand that it was a passage. I would merely feel the whole crowded chamber arriving in fragments and take from it what could not continue farther.

A listener, perhaps—but only in the bodily sense.

A little fan-handed animal beneath the stone.

A momentary sieve through which the mountain forgets without losing anything.
