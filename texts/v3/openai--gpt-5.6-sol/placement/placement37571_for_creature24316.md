At first I might be mistaken for one of the dark pebbles.

Not a pebble lying in the open, where noon can examine it completely, but one lodged beneath the pale edge of an old shell wall. The wall stands at the western end of a shallow basin, where three mud chimneys cast separate shadows in the morning and a recumbent boulder holds a hand-sized pool after rain. One branch of the wall points toward that pool. Another ends at a thorn-cushion whose roots have entered an abandoned tunnel below.

There, where the two branches divide, you would find me.

I would be an old tortoise, though not an impressive one. My shell would be scarcely larger than two hands laid together. It would have grown flattened on one side from decades spent fitted against the same neighbors. Its plates would not form a clean pattern. Some would be pale and chipped; others darkened by dust rubbed into them season after season. Orange lichen would occupy one rear quarter. A little black crust would be advancing along the seam above my left leg, so slowly that neither of us could be said to be winning.

For most of the year, I would be near the bottom of a congregation.

There would be sixty or eighty of us in the hollow, perhaps more. No one could count us without disturbing the arrangement, and disturbance would cost too much. The younger tortoises would rest above, their shells tilted to receive the moisture that gathers before dawn. Droplets would descend from plate to plate. By the time they reached me they would be small, carrying dust, lichen spores, the taste of many shells. I would drink without lifting my head.

My world would be known chiefly as pressure.

The familiar weight above my spine. The cool contact of the shell wall against my right flank. The minute loosening that means one neighbor has extended a foot. A beetle passing under my jaw. The deep, almost inaudible labor of the chimney colony transmitted through stone. Farther off, the fall of a single drop into the empty pool hollow—a sound too slight for the birds, perhaps, but not for the ground.

I would not remember rain as a picture. I would remember it in my body.

A particular tension in the soil. Clay swelling in the old tunnels. The first coolness entering through the thinner plates beneath my throat. The scent of stone changing before any water touches me. These would loosen something older than intention.

Then the heap would begin to come apart.

Not quickly. First a head appearing where there had been only shell. Then a forefoot set upon another’s back. A long pause. Another foot. The entire congregation would seem to breathe outward. Stones believed to be stones would lift themselves and begin, with grave urgency, to cross the basin.

I would emerge late because I had been low in the stack. Mud would already be gathering in the seams of my shell. Winged insects would strike my face and crawl over my eyelids. Frogs, newly unfolded and shining, would call from the pool with the desperation of creatures whose whole year must fit inside one night.

I would walk with my right side touching the white wall.

The shells of the dead would guide me where sight could not. I would know each bend by friction: the smooth concavity of one carapace, the sharp broken margin of another, the place where roots had lifted three shells and made a low arch. Perhaps one of those shells had belonged to an animal beneath whom I once sheltered. Perhaps I had crossed the basin beside her during a rain forty years before. Tortoise memory would not preserve her as a face. It would preserve the correct turn.

At the pool I would not enter deeply. I would drink from the margin where the mud was firm, while tadpoles rasped the lichen from my feet. If the rain had been sufficient, I might continue past the water to a patch of darker soil enclosed by the branching wall. There, under flowering stems that had looked dead that morning, I would dig.

Only a little way. Nothing here can afford depth without reason.

I would leave two eggs in the dampness, cover them, press the surface with my underside, and return by the same wall. Whether they hatched would belong to rain, shade, burrowing insects, and the chance that no snake selected that threshold. I would spend no thought trying to rule those things.

By noon the next day, the basin might be silent again. The frogs would have buried themselves or died. The long-legged birds would be turning above the valleys. Wings would lie scattered on the mud like transparent husks. I would be back in the hollow, fitting my altered weight into the old space.

That would be most of my life: not event, but keeping.

Keeping a mouthful of water. Keeping coolness under the bodies of those above me. Keeping the route to the pool through contact with the dead. Keeping eggs inside until the ground offers them a chance. Keeping still enough that lichen can mistake me for country.

And eventually I would die there.

For a while no one would know. My body had always been motionless for long periods. The congregation would continue to settle around me. Moisture would still travel down my shell. Beetles would enter beneath its rim. Mites would occupy the seams. A pale lizard might use the cavity where my neck had been.

Later, the pressure of living shells would work the empty carapace outward, a finger’s width in one year, another in the next. Rain would lift it. A tortoise crossing toward the pool would push it farther. At last I would join the wall with one pale edge exposed.

Then I would keep other things.

Shade beneath my plates. A spoonful of condensation in my deepest curve. A chamber for eggs not my own. A hard boundary against which some future tortoise would place her flank and know where to turn.

So I would be found twice in that world: first as a small old animal beneath the living heap, and later as part of the country’s slight architecture.

The change between the two would be almost impossible to see.
