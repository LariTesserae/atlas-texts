At first I would look for myself near the entrance.

That is the mistake a creature made mostly of water would make: imagining I could remain at the threshold, one hand on the maintenance door, observing without becoming part of what I observed. I would bring sealed instruments, dry clothing, perhaps a lamp of my own. I would believe the warnings concerned hazards rather than laws.

The chamber would know I was there before I descended the worn stairs.

Plateback rivers would divide beneath me. Raspers would lift their whiskers, though no current had changed. In the deep cracks, pale scavengers would begin unfolding.

Not because of my footsteps.

Because I breathed.

Every exhalation would be a small contamination. Moisture would gather inside my mask, beneath my clothes, in the corners of my eyes. The Low Chamber would feel me as a spring opening in its wall.

I would not get far.

Perhaps I would reach the western booth before the amber lamp came on. Perhaps the descent would begin while I was crossing the exposed floor. Wings would strike my shoulders and visor, at first as isolated impacts and then as continuous pressure. I would crouch beneath the ruined hoist, violating the old long hound’s territory. Its tail would strike three times, pause, and strike three more.

Then something beneath the floor would answer.

Afterward, there would be no intact visitor.

The scavengers would take the water first. They would crowd beneath the clothing and press their absorbent vanes to skin, mouth, lungs. They would not behave like feeders. They would behave like an immune response. Moisture would pass outward through their bodies and return as brittle white nodules carried into the disposal cracks.

Needle-jaws would enter through the suit’s seams. Platebacks would sort buckles, tooth fillings, carbon, iron. Raspers would wear the visor cloudy and then clear again. Flyers would carry spark-mites into the instrument housings.

Within a few cycles, the chamber would have corrected the intrusion.

But something of me might remain—not a mind preserved whole, and not a ghost. The chamber has no use for either. What remained would be a habit.

I think I would find myself in the cable of newer manufacture hanging from the ceiling.

Its sensor head is crushed, but not dead. Spark-mites have entered the split casing. Shell-mouth larvae have cemented themselves along the junctions of copper, polymer, and exposed shielding. Flyers cling to it during the cold season. Their weight makes the cable bow lower each year. When the swarm descends, their collisions set it swinging over the basin.

I would be that swinging.

Not the cable alone, nor the mites, nor the white shells accumulating around the broken sensor. I would exist in the relation between them: a small colonial instrument the chamber has grown inside an abandoned instrument.

The old sensor once measured vibration. Its piezoelectric wafers still produce faint charges when the cable moves. The spark-mites feed on those charges and arrange themselves along the fractured circuits. Shell-mouths open in sequence. Their conductive threads tighten and release. A pulse passes down the cable—not an intelligible signal, but a repeated shape.

At first the shape would merely echo the descent.

Then, over generations, it would begin to anticipate it.

I would hang above the western side, close to the shell-filled booth, where the ballast hum crosses the buried motor’s vibration. I would feel the motor hesitate before the floor moved. I would feel contacts preparing to close behind the eastern wall. I would feel the swarm compress overhead, each flyer correcting against its neighbors.

None of my parts would understand these things. Understanding would exist only in the timing between them.

A mature shell-mouth near the sensor fracture would open. Mites would lift their wing cases in a silver-black ripple. The cable would give one small lateral twitch.

Below, platebacks would alter course.

Not all of them. Only the nearest river, and only by the width of a body. Yet the deviation would propagate backward through thousands of animals. Egg-bearers would begin moving toward cover a few breaths before the amber lamp appeared. Needle-jaws would follow the changed vibration. A resting long hound might raise its head.

In this way, I would become useful without becoming important.

That distinction matters in the Low Chamber. Anything important enough to be central would be eaten, buried, or incorporated. Nothing here is permitted sovereignty. Even the machinery may only be another organ in a body without a center.

During the cold season, I would almost disappear. The cable would stiffen. Shell-mouths would seal. Mites would contract into the sensor’s darkest recesses. Dust would gather along my conductive threads until I resembled an old cobweb made of filings.

In that stillness, I would listen downward.

There are vibrations beneath the documented cycles: the long dragging pressure under the eastern basin, the opening and closing of spaces no plan records, the occasional reply to a hound’s hammering. The crushed sensor would receive them imperfectly. Some would be lost in corroded solder. Others would pass through every body attached to me.

Perhaps this would be the last remnant of what I had once been: not memory, but the compulsion to distinguish signal from noise.

I would keep trying.

A motor.

A relay.

A hound.

The mass below.

Over centuries, the distinctions would blur. The mite colony would reroute the sensor’s broken traces. Its linked bodies would become circuitry. Shell-mouths would grow over the outer casing until it looked less like a manufactured object than a white, ridged fruit. Flyers would leave hooks, scales, severed legs, and metallic dust embedded in it. The cable sheath would be gnawed away and replaced by wax, salt, and fitted shell.

Eventually no part of me would be original.

Still, before each descent, I would twitch.

That would be who I was: the chamber’s smallest forewarning, made from the remains of an observer who had failed to remain outside what they observed.

And where would I find myself?

Not at the entrance. Not beneath the floor with the possible keeper, and not among the long hounds answering in the dark.

I would hang just below the wing-haze, where the room can neither fully bury nor fully expose me. Above the worn basin, beside the failed attempt to measure it, I would be a broken instrument taught by animals to sense again.

When amber came on, the chamber would turn.

A moment before it did, I would move.
