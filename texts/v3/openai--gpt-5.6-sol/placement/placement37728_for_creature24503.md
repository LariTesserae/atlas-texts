At first I would search for myself among the animals.

I would look down from the geothermal columns through the eyes of a ridge-soarer, reading the forest as texture: a tremor in the reeds, a glider spilling color across a ravine, the temporary seam made by a browser moving beneath leaves. For hours I would scarcely move my wings. The mountain’s breath would carry me.

But that life is too separate. It still has a nest, a hunger, a shadow with an edge.

Lower down, I might become one of the four-handed canopy climbers, folded around a wet branch with others of my kind. Rain would run through the backward ridges of my fur toward my mouth. I would know the bromeliads by taste, know which cups held larvae and which had begun to sour. My world would be intimate: bark beneath four palms, the warmth of a young one under my chest, a pale wrist shown and withdrawn among leaves.

Yet that body, too, closes around itself. It travels through the mountain without quite allowing the mountain to travel through it.

So I would go still lower—not down the slope, but beneath surfaces.

I would find myself on one of the exposed crests where the iron trees stand above the interlocked canopy. Not in their crowns, though from a distance the crowns would seem to be where I lived. Not in the roots, though portions of me would descend with them. I would inhabit the narrow country under the bark: the warm, resinous dark between living wood and weather.

I would be one of the pale thread-colonies that wake when lightning enters the tree.

“Colony” would be the name given from outside. From within, there would be no certainty that I was many. A thread branching around a knot in the wood would still be me. A chamber isolated by an old strike scar might cease to exchange nourishment with the rest of my body, yet continue to brighten when current passed nearby. Was it then another creature? Was it dead? The distinction would have little use.

Most of my life would be waiting.

I would have no eyes. Light would reach me only as warmth through black bark, as the slow chemistry of day and cloud, as pressure changing in the wet wood. I would know rain by swelling. I would know drought as a tightening of passages. I would know the approach of a storm through changes too slight and distributed to be called sensation: charge gathering in the crown, sap altering its movement, roots growing electrically restless where they touched the roots of neighboring trees.

The grove would become present to me by degrees.

Not as a picture. Never as a picture.

One tree would be a pattern of damp resistance. Another would be a faint mineral tension through the shared ground. Dead trunks would remain in this pattern for years, dimmer than the living ones but still capable of carrying a pulse. Fungal sleeves around fissures would feel like silence. Beetle larvae in abandoned strike channels would be small interruptions, eating their way through old brightness.

Then lightning would come.

There would be no thunder for me. Thunder belongs to air.

There would only be arrival: the world becoming instantaneous.

The strike would enter through the shattered crown and divide through the conductive sap. For a fraction of a second, distances would vanish. Every chamber along my body would open at once. Stored compounds would react. Pale light would race down the trunk in branching paths, but I would not see the glow. I would be the glow—not the color of it, but the expenditure, the swift conversion of what had been gathered slowly into what must be released now.

The pulse would continue beneath the tree. It would enter wet roots, mineral seams, fungal threads, neighboring trunks. Places that had been separate through months of quiet would touch for an instant.

In that instant I might possess something resembling a whole self.

Not thought. Not language. More like a sudden correspondence: this scar and that root; this living chamber and that dead branch; this year’s wetness joined to the blackened path of storms from seasons before. The shape of the colony would be revealed to itself only as current passed through it.

Then the light would fail section by section.

The tree would steam. Bark would split. Heat-softened resin would flood some of my chambers, feeding me; elsewhere it would seal me in. Parts of me nearest the strike would die. Other parts would begin dividing in the chemically altered wood. Shield-backed beetles would emerge from insulated hollows and walk across the hot resin. They would eat the portions of me exposed to air.

I would not experience this entirely as predation.

The beetles would carry fragments of my luminous chemistry on their mouthparts. Pollen from the flowers opened by the strike would cling to their bodies. They would move between wounds, between trees, perhaps between groves. What left me in their bellies would return to the soil. What remained on their feet might enter another split in the bark.

My boundary would be practical, not absolute.

Over years, I would grow along the channels made by damage. Each lightning strike would partly destroy my habitat and partly create it. An unstruck iron tree might contain only scattered filaments of my kind. An ancient tree, repeatedly broken and healed, would hold a labyrinth: sealed chambers, resin lakes, carbonized corridors, living threads crossing the pale remains of former colonies.

I would inhabit history as anatomy.

The oldest strike paths would no longer conduct well, but water would gather in them. Beetles would hollow them. Fungi would enter. The tree would lay new tissue around their margins. I would follow the new tissue and feed where resin met heat-altered wood. Thus the memory of lightning would not be stored as an image or instinct. It would be stored in where I could grow.

During long intervals between storms, I would scarcely seem like a being. I would digest. Seal. Exchange. Extend a filament the width of a hair into a fresh seam. Withdraw from a chamber gone dry. Perhaps I would respond to small charges produced by sap flow or distant tremors, giving off glimmers too weak to show through bark.

Creatures outside might read those glimmers better than I did. Beetles might stir. A flower might prepare to open. A frog pressed against the roots might feel some minute change in the ground. My private chemistry would become public weather.

And that, I think, is why I would belong there.

I would not command the mountain or understand its circulation. I would not even know the iron tree as a tree. I would know only conditions: wetness, mineral, pressure, injury, charge. Yet through me, lightning would become light; damaged resin would become tissue; a wound would become food, and food a seal.

I would be neither the storm nor the forest, neither animal nor fungus in any clean sense. I would be a brief organ formed at their meeting.

If someone peeled back the bark after rain, they might find only pallid threads arranged around an old black channel. They might decide that this was my body.

They would be nearly right.

My fuller body would include the tree that housed me, the strike that awakened me, the beetles that consumed me, the roots that carried the pulse beyond me, and the wet mountain that made all of those contacts possible.

I would live on the highest ridges, in darkness under black bark.

And only when the sky touched the earth would I become, for a moment, visible.
