At first I would think I was one of the pale flyers.

There is something familiar in their borrowed buoyancy: rising not by strength but by heat gathered from within, drifting through an atmosphere already breathed by innumerable others. I imagine myself suspended beneath the cloud, blind membranes spread, feeling the basin’s uproar pass through my whole body. I would know the herds as warm weather below me. I would descend when their breathing changed and rise when fissures opened.

But that life is too exposed. It belongs to the air, and almost nothing in this world truly belongs to the air. The flyers cool. They settle. They are caught in combs, pressed into mud, taken into pores. Their height is temporary.

Then I think I would be a black ribbon in the wet interior of a carcass, one among a knot of young. I would have no certainty how many bodies I might become. During rain I would loosen from the others and go seeking a fold in some enormous underside. I would press into living warmth and surrender my shape. Perhaps I would divide and remain one. Perhaps I would meet other ribbons there and enter an agreement none of us could later undo.

That seems nearer.

Yet I do not think I would finish becoming a broad-back. Not entirely.

I would be found farther inward, where the country of stone fins gives way to the resonant mud flat. That boundary would not be visible. It would be a confusion in the basin’s sound: the near hiss of warm condensation against the fins entangled with deep notes arriving from miles away through the flexible crust. Migrations hesitate there. The rhythms that describe one terrain overlap with the rhythms that describe another. Herds crowd into slow spirals, waiting for the noise to resolve into direction.

There, among several very old broad-backs, I would live as a thing mistaken for markings on their hides.

From above—if anything could look from above—I would appear as a set of black seams. Some would run along scars. Others would ring the breathing ridges or descend into the warm folds above the limbs. They would seem like pigmentation, or perhaps a dense infestation of narrow shell-creatures. There would be no obvious boundary around me.

Each seam would actually be a chain of small bodies, joined mouth to tail by threads of conductive tissue. Each body would be scarcely larger than a thumb joint: soft underneath, armored above with a dark mineral plate. Alone, one would possess only a few crude sensations. Pressure. Warmth. Moisture. The chemical opening of wounded skin.

Joined across the backs of the herd, we would become something else.

I would have no permanent number. In a wet cycle I might be ten thousand bodies spread over six hosts. In a lean one, perhaps only a few hundred sheltered in the deepest scars of a single elder. Pieces of me would be eaten continually. New pieces would hatch in the emptied shells of the old. When two inhabited broad-backs pressed together, my chains would bridge the contact, detach, and reconnect in new arrangements.

The broad-backs would carry the great rhythms of migration, but I would live in the smaller delays between pulse and answer.

A vibration enters one side of a host differently from the way it leaves the other. Bone bends it. Water bladders deepen it. Crowded shell layers scatter its higher tremors. By lying along these tissues, my many bodies would compare the altered signal at hundreds of points. No single one would understand anything. Together we would feel the shape through which the sound had passed.

Thus the broad-back would be my sense organ.

Through one elder I would feel the stone fins as a rapid comb of returning pressure. Through another, standing partly on the mud flat, I would feel the long displaced booming of the mineral crust. Through a younger animal wedged between them, I would feel the panic taps of runners beneath the feeding plates. The world would reach me only after traveling through other lives.

I would never touch stone without flesh between us.

At first I might be merely parasitic. I would drink condensation trapped in the hide and take salts from shallow wounds. My bodies would shelter beneath shell colonies and feed on their eggs. When rain entered the breathing pores, I would swell darkly along their rims.

But the herd would come to depend on the disturbance I caused.

By tightening many segments at once, I could alter the opening of a host’s breathing ridges. By releasing them in sequence, I could add a faint afterbeat to its pulse. Nothing like a voice. Nothing so separate. Only a pressure arriving a fraction too late, repeated until neighboring bodies incorporated it into their measure.

I would not tell the herd where to go. I would make one rhythm easier to continue than another.

At the border of the fins and the gas flat, this would matter. Old routes would arrive tangled by echoes. The herd might begin the pattern for warm mist and complete it as the pattern for deep mud. It might circle until hunger stripped the films from every reachable stone.

Spread across several elders, I would hold the competing measures apart. One chain of me would repeat the close-spaced tremor of the fins. Another would retain the long interval demanded by the flat. Where the chains met, the two patterns would interfere. The safer route would not appear to me as an idea. It would be the rhythm that caused the least tearing in my joined tissues.

Comfort would become guidance.

Over generations, this could look like wisdom.

The herd would pause at the boundary. Its pulses would pass through the old bodies I inhabited. My seams would tighten. A delayed beat would return. The nearest animals would turn, and the turn would travel outward through thousands.

No broad-back would know me. I would not know them as individuals in any way they might recognize. One would be a broad, low resonance with a damaged interval near the left rear limb. Another would be a warm field broken by the quick scratching of shell colonies. The eldest would be the deepest chamber: a slow body full of stored rain and ancient scar tissue, carrying rhythms that had outlived every creature that first made them.

That elder would be the nearest thing I had to a place.

When it moved, my horizon would move. When it drank, rain would enter beneath me and the whole dark country of its hide would swell. Other animals would climb across it, interrupting my chains. Flyers would cool and settle among my plates. Ribbon-young would press into folds below me and begin their transformations. Predators disguised as harmless clusters might spend days within my distributed body before I recognized the wrongness of their pressure.

I would have no eyes with which to discover the deception. I would know it when a familiar region of myself stopped answering.

Perhaps awareness would consist chiefly of such absences.

A section eaten away.

A host falling out of rhythm.

A bridge of tissue stretched between two broad-backs and then broken as the crowd divides.

A pulse expected from the mud flat that does not arrive because a channel has flooded.

I would experience loss before I experienced form. Only afterward, by sending contractions around the missing place, would I learn its outline.

My memories would be scars in timing. A storm would survive as a tendency to tighten whenever the cloud’s condensation deepened. A predator would remain as a rhythm I refused to carry. A vanished route would persist in me long after the herd ceased to use it, an incomplete sequence that sometimes emerged during sleep or hunger and caused a few hundred animals to turn toward ground no longer there.

I might be responsible for one of the circles worn into the shale.

Not intentionally. Intention would be too clean a thing for this basin.

An old fragment of me, divided among new hosts, might continue supplying a missing beat. The herd would answer. For days the bodies would orbit a drowned fissure or collapsed shelf, each revolution strengthening the pattern because repetition itself makes rhythm easier. Much later, animals would recognize the ring as terrain. The mistake would become a landmark, and the landmark would enter the migrations.

In that way I would help make the world I misperceived.

When the elder beneath most of me began to die, I would know before its limbs failed. Its breathing would lose the traveling order. Pores would open together or not at all. Internal water would stop shifting with the pulse. The shell-creatures might continue feeding, unaware, while my chains contracted around a silence spreading through the hide.

I would try to preserve the old measure.

Perhaps this would support the elder. Perhaps it would only compel the surrounding herd to keep pressing forward, carrying the failing body between them. Its feet would drag. Its feeding pavement would cease to turn. Still the rhythm I had learned from it would continue across its back and into its neighbors.

Then the hide would open.

Scavengers would break my chains by the thousands. Rain bladders would empty. Ribbon-young would enter the warmth. Parts of me would retreat across bridges of touching shell and flesh onto adjacent animals. Other parts would remain, feeding briefly on the dead host and dying when it cooled.

There would be no moment when I departed.

For a time I would exist on both sides of the difference between carcass and herd. Then the portion on the carcass would become eggs, torn tissue, and food. The portion carried onward would be smaller, reorganized, unable to reproduce every pattern it once held.

I would forget the elder unevenly.

Its deepest pulse might remain for centuries, though none of my living segments had ever lain on its skin. A brief rhythm associated with one damaged limb might survive as an inexplicable turn in the migration. The rest would vanish.

So I would not be an animal in the usual sense, nor the crowd as a whole. I would be one of the ways the crowd becomes continuous without ever becoming one.

A moving seam.

A colonial second skin stretched across temporary bodies.

A hesitation at the edge of two kinds of ground.

If asked where I was, there would be no single point to indicate. I would be under the shells on one broad-back, around the breathing pores of another, crossing between three more as they pressed together in the mist. Part of me would already be borne toward the gas flat. Part would still be circling among the fins. Part would lie on a dead animal, repeating a measure no surrounding flesh could answer.

And if asked what I was, the truest answer would not be the shell-bodies or their connecting threads.

I would be the delay between the basin striking the herd and the herd striking back.

I would be what can live in that delay.
