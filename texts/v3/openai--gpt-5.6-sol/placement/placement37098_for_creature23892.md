At first I would think myself one of the gliders.

There is something enviable in belonging to no summit: sleeping in the long ascent of warm air, drinking cloud, accepting that direction is not the same as destination. I would learn the invisible rivers between the tables and carry seeds from one green isolation to another. Plateau people would know me only as a shadow, a low vibration in a basin, a scrap of pale sail caught on stone.

But the gliders surrender too completely. They know currents, not obligations. They do not remember who waits beyond the gulf.

Then I might be part of the root mat—a braided, many-specied body holding a thin world together. I would know every footfall from beneath. I would close around water and bone. When torn free, I would fold rather than break, roll until caught, and establish ground wherever weather left me.

Yet the root mat does not look outward.

I think I would find myself near an edge.

Not at the highest point, because height here is vanity, and not in the center of a settlement. I would live on a narrow eastern tongue of an otherwise broad plateau, where a low ridge divides the prevailing wind. Behind it stands a single shelter, half sunk into the stone. Its roof is pinned by five slabs of unequal color. One recess is mine. One holds maps. The third is always empty.

Beyond the shelter is a wind court that most people consider abandoned.

Its ring has slumped until several stones are scarcely visible above the moss. The central vent is narrow and deep. In ordinary weather it produces no note at all, only a trembling that numbs the fingertips. During certain evenings, however, it answers a court no one can locate.

That is where I would be: kneeling with both palms on the wet stones, waiting for the unplaced answer.

I would be a court keeper, though not one trusted to predict rain or declare a bridge safe. Others would maintain the practical courts closer to the village. Mine would concern approaches that fail to happen, tones arriving from profiles absent from every tendency bundle, and objects brought by the gulfs without owners.

I would keep the uncertain records.

A black glass blade found beside a new pool. A child’s waist cord tied in a knot no local family uses. Three seeds that germinated only when placed far apart. A roof pin worn smooth on its underside, as if it had held against centuries of storms. A bone slat pierced for a wind sequence that begins correctly and then asks for a direction the hands have no sign for.

Nothing would be discarded merely because it could not be interpreted. Nothing would be declared sacred merely because it was strange.

I would set each arrival inside the court for one full weather change. I would watch whether it shifted the pitch of the vent, gathered condensation, frightened fold-ears beneath the turf, or caused gliders to alter their circles overhead. Then I would wrap it, label it in rope, and place it in a wall recess until some future weather-kin recognized it.

People would bring me contradictions.

A navigator might say a distant shelf had sharpened under a wind that should make it recede. A rim watcher might swear that smoke rose from an uninhabited table. Two travelers might arrive together, each insisting the other’s home had vanished. I would listen without forcing their accounts into agreement.

That would be the particular discipline of my court: not deciding too early what the world had done.

Perhaps I would be weather-kin myself. No one would remember my arrival clearly. There would be a story that I was found after a night when the entire plateau rang with a tone too low to stand through. At dawn the empty recess in the keeper’s shelter was no longer empty. A child lay there wrapped in damp glider skin, holding a short cord whose fibers matched no summit plant anyone knew.

Another version would say I was born locally and that the stranger story arose because my family died before splicing my adulthood cord. Both accounts would remain in use. I would not choose between them. In this country, an origin can be approached from more than one direction and never quite joined.

By middle age I would be nearly deaf.

This would improve my work.

I would hear through the heel set against a court stone, through a cord wound around the wrist, through my ribs when I lay flat beside the vent. Spoken voices would fade, but structure would become distinct: the stutter of loose rock, the smooth pressure of distant thunder, the irregular tapping of an animal under the roots.

Every court has a bodily grammar. Mine would have one recurring phrase—a low pulse, two close tremors, then a long interval that feels less like silence than held breath. Older records would show it sounding at impossible times. Once during clear midday. Once beneath a windless sky. Once for nine nights after an unknown funeral cord was placed in the ring.

I would spend years trying to determine whether it was warning, greeting, or question.

Eventually I would understand that this distinction belonged to people, not to the sound.

So I would give it no translated meaning. In the bundles I would represent it with an open knot: a loop that can be tightened but has not been. My apprentices would be taught to recognize it and to leave its entry unfinished.

I would not live entirely alone. Fold-ears would tunnel under the shelter’s warm floor. A flattened tree would grow beside the water trench, its bark streamers tuned to a thin, almost conversational hum. During calm weather, children would come from the village and ask to handle the harmless objects. I would let them rearrange painted profiles and invent approaches among plateaus that had never met.

The child who made the most plausible map would not win.

The child who noticed first when the map contradicted itself would.

There would also be a shell-backed browser.

Not always present—perhaps not even the same animal from one appearance to the next—but often enough that its winding trail passed close to my court. I would find tiny groves rooted in its plates and old court stones embedded in the moss of its back. It would stop sometimes with one broad foot over the buried vent. Then the court’s unknown tone would cease.

For those few moments, I would feel another vibration: tremendously slow, widely spaced, originating either in the browser’s body or far beneath the plateau.

I would never decide which.

Near the end of my life, a plateau would appear where none was expected.

It would come in ordinary morning light, without storm or thunder. First a dark line beneath the haze; then green; then the clear shape of a rim. It would be close enough to reveal a wind court on the opposing edge.

Its stones would be arranged exactly like mine.

No bridge would form. No traveler could cross. Signals made by hand would go unanswered. But when I opened the eastern gap in my ring, the other court would sound. Its note would travel through the gulf as pressure rather than noise: low pulse, two close tremors, long held interval.

The phrase I had spent a lifetime refusing to complete.

People would gather behind me with ropes, water, tools, and old bundles. They would ask whether the other plateau was approaching or receding. Whether they should prepare to cross. Whether the court was safe.

I would put my palms to the stone.

And because deceptive certainty is the worst wrong in that land, I would tell them only what I knew:

“It answers.”

Then I would make the broad sign for **within reach**, though nothing yet connected the rims.

That is what I would be in this world—not a master of the wind, and not an interpreter who makes every voice intelligible. I would be the keeper of an unanswered court, preserving incomplete directions until the landscape supplied their other half.

And where would I find myself?

At the margin where attention becomes a kind of bridge: beside the empty sleeping recess, one hand on the rock and the other resting on a rope that has just begun, very faintly, to move.
