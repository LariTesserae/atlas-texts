At first I would be one of the gray multitudes.

That seems the simplest answer: a glassmouth near the front of a migration, clear-bodied at hatching, later stained amber-green by whatever the ridge is becoming beneath me. I would not know the shape of the swarm. I would know only the hairs touching mine, the warmth ahead, the pressure of eggs behind, and the sudden white emptiness passing from body to body when it was time to flee.

For an instant, danger would be nothing but my neighbor’s shiver.

There is an honesty in that life. No private courage. No private panic. The warning survives whether I do or not.

But I think I would be larger than that, or at least more burdened by the illusion of being separate.

You would find me where a warm ledge narrows above one of the white basins.

Not at its rim. I am not brave in that ornamental way. I would live perhaps half a day’s careful travel from it, in a shelter beneath a folded escarpment called **The Place That Holds Until the Water Tastes of Iron**. The name used to be accurate. Lately iron has appeared in the catch-shells even when the ledge is still. We have not agreed on a new name.

Our settlement would contain nine adults, eleven young, three communal egg chambers, a cistern of nervous ribbon-swimmers, and more clatterers than anyone admits to feeding. Its shell walls would be dismantled each time the amber note deepened and rebuilt after we decided—never unanimously—that the basin had not opened yet.

My sleeping rack would hang nearest the outer passage. This would not indicate rank. It would mean I sleep lightly and possess an old injury that makes my chest plates buzz before pressure changes. In another world it might be called pain. Here it is employment.

I would be one of the upright listeners, though “upright” would describe only the moments when my fine hands were occupied. Moving quickly, I would drop onto all eight limbs. My rear legs would be thick with pale mineral reinforcement, my middle braces short and scarred, one carrying the permanent dark stain of sulfur exposure. One carrying arm would be stronger than the other. Of my two delicate forehands, the left would be missing its final joint, taken not by a predator but by a water lid that snapped shut during a tremor.

Across my shoulders I would wear a plate from a herd animal that traveled beside my brood-group when I was young. Gypsum-born, translucent at the edges. It would be drilled through in five places and tied into scar tissue, though only four ties would remain necessary. Tiny red scavengers would live beneath it, cleaning the seam where my own armor met the borrowed shell.

I would insist they were useful.

I would also be fond of them.

That distinction would embarrass me, so I would not make it aloud.

My work would be uncertainty.

Not prophecy. Not leadership. I would be the one sent to listen when familiar explanations failed.

A herd announces itself in the ribs. Water loosens the breast. Burrowers scratch upward through the heels. These can be learned. Even a collapsing shelf has a grammar: first the small fibers part, then the crust complains, then weight begins speaking to weight.

The basin has no grammar.

Near it, every sound becomes smaller than its cause. A thrown shell strikes the floor without an echo. Stamping produces no return through the legs. Bone needles pushed into cracks begin to tremble, but when touched they feel still. The eye sees motion that the body cannot confirm.

I would keep a listening post overlooking that silence.

It would be an ugly construction, repeatedly altered: seven balanced slabs, three shell cups, a fan of long needles cut from the jaw-spines of wet-stones, and a copper disk suspended where rising warm air can touch it. In its foundation would be mounted the cleaned chest chamber of my ground-parent.

They had been a route-keeper. Their old name imitated rain striking a hollow plate in groups of three, then two, then three. After death, the pattern changed. The tower made the chamber speak when amber pressure moved beneath the basin, and now the final group contained four beats.

No one knew whether this was damage or correction.

I would still call them by it.

My own name would be difficult to carry from place to place. It began as the rhythm of a plated animal limping over thin crust: two quick contacts, a drag, then a pause. As ridges rose and paths changed, the drag blurred into the surrounding stone. People shortened it. Young ones exaggerated it. Strangers often mistook the pause for the name’s end.

The nearest translation would be:

**The Step After Which the Ground Has Not Yet Answered.**

Among intimates, perhaps simply **Not Yet**.

That would be my dangerous trait. Not curiosity exactly, and not hope. A refusal to declare an event complete merely because everyone had survived its beginning.

When the sympathetic notes sounded, I would go to the post with two route-keepers, a tethered clatterer, and a covered shell of water. We would place our feet on separate slabs. We would listen first to the ordinary world: glassmouths feeding westward; a nursery-bearing herd moving beyond the ridge; three fractures settling; thermal flock falling in the far arches. We would name each vibration so that the unknown could not disguise itself among them.

Then the basin would click.

The others might call it hatching. I would say unknown.

They might call it deep stone. I would say too regular.

They might call it speech. I would ask where the answering rhythm was.

This would not make me popular. It might make me trusted.

One moisture cycle, while the ceiling sweated heavily enough to silver every surface, the amber cracks would widen. Not much—less than the width of a glassmouth’s mineral blade—but enough that the light began to pulse instead of merely shine.

The pulse would enter my ground-parent’s empty chest.

Their chamber would answer.

Not echo. Answer.

Three beats, two, four.

Then something below would return the pattern with the intervals reversed.

The clatterer would scream. The ribbon-swimmers in our carried shell would knot themselves until the vessel tipped. Far down the slope, gray feeding fronts would empty their guts all at once, a whitening that moved across the land like weather.

The others would flee to warn the settlement.

I would remain—not alone, because nothing here is alone. The red mites beneath my shoulder plate would be climbing into the warm joints of my neck. Larvae would stir in the rain mantle tied at my side. My ground-parent’s chamber would hang within the tower. Glassmouths would gather below. Flocks would wheel in the rooflight. Every body would carry bodies, every warning would pass through more lives than the one that made it.

I would press one heel to the tower’s foundation.

With the other, I would give my name.

Two contacts. A drag. The pause.

For the first time, the ground would answer before I completed it.

Afterward, if I returned, I would no longer be merely a listener. I would become a route of a new kind—not a keeper of where to move, but of how to approach a thing that made silence around itself.

If I did not return, my companions would recover what parts of me they safely could. My chest chamber might be mounted beside my ground-parent’s. My shoulder scavengers would migrate into the tower. Glassmouths would take the softer minerals. Someone would inherit the gypsum plate. One of the young might copy my name badly until the bad copy became a useful warning.

So that is where I would be found: on the last responsive ledge before the white basin, in a tower made partly from the dead, surrounded by crowds already arriving for whatever comes next.

And what would I be?

Not the one who understands the thing beneath the stone.

The one who knows it has answered.
