At first I think I would be one of the slivers, because they possess the nearest thing this place allows to freedom.

I would cross the pale plazas broadside, almost visible, then turn and become only a line. I would know the drowned plateau as a recurring sequence of forces: the northern cold, the lift through the buried avenues, the westward blow. I would spend years within one immense circuit and mistake repetition for permanence.

But slivers belong to the open water. I would not. The scale of the plateau would draw me inward. I would want some small attachment to set against all that involuntary motion.

Perhaps, then, a seam mouth sealed over a joint—alive so slowly that several generations could pass during a single pause in my feeding. Yet even that seems too self-contained. A seam mouth survives by making itself a closed world. I think I would survive by remaining open.

You would find me beneath the Basin of Steps, though not very far beneath it: in a chamber uncovered by the last great storm.

The chamber was once sealed. One wall cleaved along a flawless plane, exposing a crescent-shaped room with no visible entrance and hundreds of shallow sockets in its ceiling. Water from the deep levels now enters through a crack near the floor. It rises warm and mineral-rich, circles once beneath the curved ceiling, and escapes through the broken wall into the basin.

The room exists only during exhalation. During inhalation, it becomes a cold blind pocket full of descending grit.

I would be a mourning bell there—but not quite a mature one.

I would be the first bell.

I would have arrived detached, contracted into a bead scarcely larger than a grain of the plateau’s own powdered substance. The storm would carry me through passages no living bell had occupied before: upward through a reservoir, sideways along a ribbed gallery, then down against the apparent direction of the current. Most of the others torn loose with me would be crushed, eaten, or swept into open water.

I would strike the newly exposed ceiling and fail to hold.

I would strike it again.

On the third contact, a filament would find a microscopic flaw left by the wall’s cleaving. It would enter no wider than a hair and harden there, incorporating carbon and traces of metal until it became black. The current would pull my body outward. For a long time I would be nothing but a closed pale drop at the end of that dark line.

Then the plateau would exhale.

Warmth would reach me before food. Pressure would loosen my rim. I would unfold into the current, extending a veil so fine that the water itself seemed to give it shape. Bacterial dust, fragments of tissue, reproductive particles and the remains of deeper organisms would collect along my cilia. For several hours I would feed.

When the pressure turned, I would close.

That would be almost the whole of my life: opening when the buried world breathed out, closing when it drew breath in.

I would have no image of the chamber. I would not know its crescent shape, its sockets, or the pale sweep of its ceiling. I would know only the pressure shadow cast by the broken wall, the warmth climbing from below, and the minute tremor made when grit began to pour back toward the aperture. The Basin of Steps would not be a basin to me. It would be an alternating command.

Open.

Close.

Hold.

In time, other bells would arrive. Some would be my offspring; others would be strangers carried from distant shafts. Filaments would blacken the ceiling around me. During each exhalation we would open from the warm crack outward, not because any signal passed between us, but because the same current touched us in sequence.

From outside, if anything capable of watching passed the breach, our chamber would appear to blink.

I would be only one soft body among thousands, yet the blink would become the larger creature to which I belonged. My individuality would matter less with every new filament. Predators might take half the colony. A storm might strip the outer rows away. The chamber might fill with grit until only those closest to the ceiling remained. Still, whenever the deep water returned, what survived would open together.

Eventually the ceiling would fail.

There would be no warning comprehensible as fear—only a pressure change too steep to answer. The pale slab to which we had fastened would divide along an ancient plane. Some bells would be pulverized between pieces. Some would remain attached as the slab rolled down the terraces. Others would contract into beads and be flung across the basin.

Perhaps I would be among those torn free.

Then I would cease to be the first bell of that chamber and become only a drifting possibility again: closed, nearly insensible, carried through the architecture until eaten or given another flaw to inhabit.

So that is where I would find myself—not in the grand avenues, not at the glowing rim, and not in the central aperture, but in a side chamber briefly made habitable by damage.

And that is what I would be: a small translucent bell suspended from a black filament, opening only when the lost depths exhaled.

Not an explorer of the ruin. Not its inheritor.

A momentary eyelid in the dark.
