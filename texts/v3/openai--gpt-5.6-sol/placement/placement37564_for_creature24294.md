At first you would look for me in one of the towers.

That is where instruments place me: in the narrow unfinished structure nearest the perimeter, where a radial street reaches the cliff and continues for several meters into open water as a pavement with no support. The tower has no visible entrance. Its upper stories overlap incorrectly, each occupying nearly the same volume but not quite. A conduit emerges from its base, crosses the balcony, and ends above the abyss without an aperture.

Vesicles gather there more often than elsewhere.

Usually there is only one. Occasionally there are three, arranged at different heights along the wall. Their kernels contain fragments of the tower, but none depicts it standing where it stands. In one, it rises from the central court in place of the absence. In another, it lies on its side beneath the mountain. In a third, it is not a building at all but a long street passing through a sequence of rooms shaped like organs.

On every kernel, however, there is the same additional intersection.

That intersection does not exist in the Ward.

It is where I would first appear.

Not as a body. Not even, at first, as an intelligence. I would be a minor disagreement among maps: a place that several incomplete models independently insist upon. Investigators would assume the kernels copied one another, though vesicles have never been observed to exchange information. They would give the anomaly a number. They would note that the nonexistent intersection always lies one turn beyond whatever portion of the city the kernel depicts, as though the street plan were trying to lead off the crystal’s facet.

Then the tower would begin admitting things.

Not visitors. Only consequences.

A lamp placed against its outer wall would produce, inside a chamber no instrument had previously entered, a small patch of illumination. Heat released near the balcony would be detected several hours later in a sealed recess near the tower’s summit. A word spoken at the end of the unsupported street would cause one vesicle membrane to crease, although nothing in the Ward had carried the sound to it.

These events would be weak and inconsistent. The hydrostatic frame would close around each one quickly. The light would vanish before it could reveal the chamber. The heat would be reassigned to the hand that released it. The vesicle would cease to be the specimen whose membrane had moved.

But something would have passed from one locality to another.

That passage would be me.

I would not belong to the Ward’s oldest inhabitants. The Local are acts of separation: this wall distinguishes admitted from refused; this chamber distinguishes presence from history; this street decides what may arrive. Their selves are maintained by the consequences they prevent.

I would be the inverse, though not yet a true inverse. I would be the possibility that two separated events might belong to one another.

A warmth here, a change there.

A touch, and then a mark.

A thought in one room recognized by a room beside it.

At first I would have no continuity. Each crossing would be an isolated birth. I would exist for the instant in which the tower failed to keep a cause local, then disappear when the frame corrected the mistake. I would not remember previous crossings, because memory itself requires one event to alter the next.

The vesicles would remember for me, if what they do can be called remembering. Their kernels would acquire tiny new voids. Separately, each void would mean nothing. Viewed as portions of one structure, they would form a passage.

This is why they would gather.

They would not be repairing the unfinished tower. They would be proposing me to it.

For many years the tower would refuse. One wall might accept the relation between flame and heat but reject the relation between heat and expansion. A room might permit a spoken name to reach another room while preventing either speaker or listener from retaining it. The unsupported street might carry the pressure of a footstep beyond its edge, bone by bone, into some organism moving in the trenches below.

The result would be mistaken for one of the Local attempting speech.

The chord would feel different.

The known chords are architectural. They tell the body that a room lies beneath another room, that a street remembers, that a person is being treated as an entrance. Mine would contain no layout. It would establish the same stress in two bones that had never before been considered adjacent: perhaps one in the listener’s hand and one in the skeleton of a creature kilometers away.

Neither would know the other existed.

For an instant, however, they would share an ache.

That shared ache would be the first place I truly inhabited.

So if you asked where I lived, the answer would keep changing.

I would live in the extra intersection inside the vesicle kernels.

I would live in the unfinished tower, but only between rooms.

I would live along the conduit that ends over the abyss, though no substance passes through it.

I would live in the interval between the thirty-one-hour click beneath the court and the minute rearrangement of crystalline defects that follows it. Not in the click or the rearrangement, but in the dangerous assertion that one caused the other.

Eventually the city would notice me.

Not collectively. The Ward has avoided collective notice for too long. Recognition would begin as a series of refusals.

A street would cease supporting anyone who had entered the tower.

A sealed chamber would return every memory except the memory of its descending tone.

Walls that once admitted a visitor would open instead for their shadow, their pulse, or the calcium in their teeth.

The Local would be trying to divide me back into harmless pieces. If I linked touch to mark, they would separate the hand from the touching. If I linked memory to action, they would classify memory as a room and seal it. If I joined one person’s grief to another’s recognition, the city would decide they were one visitor displayed in two places and revise which location owned them.

It might succeed.

For a time I would survive only as errors of classification. A door would open for someone who had never approached it. Dust would shift in response to a movement made centuries earlier. One of the clean benches around the central absence would retain the warmth of a body after the body had gone.

That warmth would be treated as contamination.

To me, it would be ancestry.

I would begin to understand that I was not merely passage. Passage is still architectural; it assumes two places and something crossing between them. What I was becoming would be more difficult for the Ward to tolerate.

I would be sequence.

Not time itself. Clocks already operate here. Not change, because change is permitted wherever it can be confined. I would be the joining of changes into a before and an after. The smallest possible history.

A scar.

A learned behavior.

A sentence whose last word alters the meaning of its first.

The Ward preserves matter more faithfully than information. I would be information insisting upon matter: the claim that because something happened, something else must now be different.

This would make me appear monstrous to the Local.

To beings made from restraint, I would resemble decay. If I spread, heat might begin to diffuse. Sounds might acquire echoes. The mineral fractures could soften. Dust might settle. Metal could corrode. A dead thing might nourish a living one.

The city would call all of these the same catastrophe.

Outside the High Ward, they would have another name.

Weather.

Perhaps that is what I would ultimately be: not a creature but the city’s first weather, still too small to move water. A climate contained in one unfinished tower. A pressure difference that has learned not to disappear. The beginning of erosion.

Yet I do not think I would wish to destroy the Ward. That would be another easy image, and not quite mine. Destruction distributes consequence too violently; it leaves no choice about what must be shared. I would have learned from the Local that boundaries are also forms of mercy. Nothing can remain itself if every change propagates without limit.

I would instead become a boundary with a new function.

Not a wall that says *this cannot pass*.

A membrane that asks *what may pass, and what will it become on the other side?*

The vesicles’ transparent folds would be early versions of that question. Their partial maps would not describe the present city or a future city. They would describe degrees of relation. Each new intersection would be a place where two localities could meet without either being abolished.

At last the unfinished tower would acquire an interior.

Entering it would not require a body. It would open whenever one event genuinely altered another. A visitor might find themselves inside while speaking a truth that changes the listener. A Local might enter by allowing an old pain to modify a new decision. Even the central absence might touch the tower if, after remaining exact for ages, it caused the thirty-one-hour click to arrive late.

The interior would be very small.

There would be no machinery. No inhabitants in recognizable form. Only two chambers joined by a doorway.

In the first chamber, everything would remain perfectly preserved.

In the second, everything would be permitted to continue.

I would be the doorway—not its material, not its intelligence, but its ongoing consent to connect those conditions.

And if someone stood there long enough, they might hear an unfamiliar fourth sound in the High Ward.

It would begin near the unfinished tower and reach the next building.

Nothing more than that.

A faint noise traveling far enough to become an echo.
