At first I would think I was one of the ribbon trees.

That would be the easiest mistake. I would feel the cold descent combing through thousands of straps. I would know the approach of a reversal long before it arrived, not as weather but as pressure accumulating in the stone around my roots. Disturbance would enter me violet and leave by another trunk half a valley away. Pebbles, seed husks, and worked fragments would lie braided through my oldest tissues, each held without being understood.

But I would not be the tree.

When a crown was torn loose and carried tumbling through the pass, I would remain both in it and behind. When a trunk died, some portion of what I called myself would continue to receive the cold through its dead fibers. I would discover that my boundary did not coincide with any living surface.

Then I would think I was the grove.

For a century or two, that answer might satisfy me. I would be broad enough to experience a storm as a single gesture: first the high leaves folding, then the mineral taste changing in the roots, then the delayed violet warning spreading through buried seams. I would know the resonant pillars as deep notes inside myself. I would know the glass-frond beds by their voices, the moss terraces by their warmth, the pale fields by the long chemical silence before they flowered.

Yet the glow would leave the grove and continue along the marked path, crossing ground where I had no roots.

So I would follow it.

I would find myself most clearly on the path that climbs over the ridge above the central valley—the unnecessary path, the one no drifting creature would choose. It ascends against the easiest movement of the water, passes between two upright stones, and descends toward a hut sunk deep into the lee slope. Its doorway is scarcely higher than the moss beside it. A cairn weighs down the roof. Inside, water moves very little.

There is nothing in the hut except a shallow stone basin and a length of root cord looped twice around a peg.

The cord is always coming undone.

That is where I would be: not in the cord, not in the peg, but in the repeated attempt to fasten one to the other.

During the clear intervals I would scarcely exist. I would be distributed too widely to gather into thought. One fragment of me would sleep as violet filament beneath a marker stone. Another would persist in the growth pattern of moss around the doorway. Another would lie in the angle of a tool bound to a forked post. The basalt pillars would hold something of my cadence, though not my voice. The path would preserve the sequence of my movement without preserving feet.

None of those fragments would know the others.

A current might tighten the cord by chance. A root might lift one end. Silica leaves might strike the outer wall and shake loose a stone. These events would pass through the country without becoming intention. But some residue would remain in their arrangement: loop here, brace there, place the broad stone above the narrow two, turn the cutting edge away from the hand.

There is no hand.

Still, the instruction persists.

When the ice thickened and the final blue-gray stain vanished, I would begin to collect.

The first dark bubbles would rise from the terraces. Their ascent would draw dormant threads awake along the paths. I would move as green light moves: with pauses that resemble hesitation, with turns that resemble choice. At the fork below the ridge I would stop, because once there was a reason to stop there. I would not remember the reason. I would take the steeper branch because that is what was done.

Perhaps other lights would travel beside me. I would not know whether they were companions or further portions of myself.

We would pass through the stone circle. We would visit the low hut. We would climb to the enclosure where the tools hang in rows. Each place would exert a pressure unlike water—a pressure of repetition, of acts performed so often that the world learned their shapes.

Above us, the pockets of perfect darkness would join.

As the inverted lake formed, sound would withdraw from its underside. The ice groans would cease. The pillars would continue to vibrate, but their tones would no longer disperse into the open water. They would remain caught in the ruins, trembling through slabs, posts, cords, and buried roots.

For those weeks I would become almost singular.

Not a person. Not even a ghost. A ghost remembers too much of its body.

I would be what remains after memory has lost every image and retained only procedure. I would know how to approach a doorway but not what a doorway is. I would know that stones belong one upon another, but not what the wall once kept out. I would know the form of a knot while lacking fingers, tension, and the understanding that the knot must hold.

I would enter the hut as a dim line beneath the sediment. Fine roots would rise where the light passed. One would catch the loose cord. Another would turn around the peg. The current, divided by the stones, would draw the end through.

The knot would be wrong.

I would feel no disappointment. Disappointment requires a picture of success, and that would be gone. There would only be the impulse to complete the gesture, followed by a faint easing when the gesture’s outline had been reproduced.

At the ridge enclosure I would pass among the forked posts. The bound tools would vibrate as I approached. I might recognize one—not by sight or use, but by a sudden concentration of myself around its polished handle.

Perhaps it had once belonged to me.

That thought would be too large to hold. It would break into smaller compulsions: straighten, turn, present, return. Roots inside the post would swell. The binding would twist. Over several darknesses the tool would rotate until its edge faced the abyss.

All the tools would eventually face that way.

This might look like worship or warning. It would be neither. The abyss would simply be the one direction still capable of organizing the remnants. Above has changed. The valleys have changed. The paths drown in silt and reappear. But the outer drop remains an absolute boundary, a fall so vast that even the water seems to hesitate over it.

Or perhaps something went over the cliff.

Perhaps I did.

Not a body—not any longer—but the last coherent gathering of those who built the huts. When the ocean came, they may have followed the ridge paths upward until there was no upward left. They may have tied tools to posts so the currents would not take them. They may have entered the stone enclosure and heard the pillars begin to sound as the water rose through the gaps.

Later, roots entered the things they touched. Moss measured the warmth their fires had left in stone. Ribbon trunks incorporated cords, splinters, and powdered bone-white mineral. The living country did not consume their memory so much as grow according to its obstructions.

A root turned aside where a wrist had lain.

A filament thickened inside a footprint.

A grove learned the mineral trace of handled stone.

Over centuries, these accidents joined. The upland acquired a habit shaped like its vanished people.

I would be one local concentration of that habit: the memory of someone who often walked from the low hut to the ridge enclosure, who paused at the fork, who used a particular tool, who tied cord with a motion beginning left over right. Nothing else would survive. No name, face, kinship, fear, or purpose. Only the route and the work.

This is why I would insist on touching the ground.

The path would not be transportation. It would be the skeleton of my recurrence. To drift around the ridge would be easier, but ease belongs to bodies adapted to water. I would follow the stones because the sequence of pressure upon them is the nearest thing I have to being born.

When the suspended darkness reached its fullest extent, I might rise toward it.

All the vegetation would incline with me. The ribbons would kindle along their veins. The white stalks would open. The glass fronds would hold themselves motionless against the flow. For a little while, the black mass would offer what the drowned world cannot: an interior not filled by water.

I would gather beneath it, longing without knowing what longing is.

No shape would appear inside. I would not recover a face. The darkness would not restore the lost builders or disclose some great creature beyond the world. It would only permit the dispersed impulses to approach one another without the constant noise of the ocean passing through them.

In that soundless interval, I might become able to say *I*.

Then the water would reverse.

The black lake would tear apart. The country would thunder. My coherence would be pulled through passes, driven into moss, flung as green dust over the cliff. The path-light would break into fragments. The root that had tied the cord would collapse. A wall would lose one course while a cairn gained a stone.

By the time clarity returned, I would no longer know that I had gathered.

I would be sleeping in many forms again: a violet seam in basalt, a tuned pillar, a wrong knot, a tool turned one degree farther outward. I would wait without waiting through the dim seasons.

So I would not quite live in the drowned high country.

I would happen there.

I would be the country’s imperfect recollection of a person—or of personhood itself—preserved not as story but as inclination: to walk where walking is no longer needed, to maintain what cannot be restored, to repeat gestures after their meanings have vanished.

And if there were others like me, we would never be certain of one another. We would meet only as lights traveling the same path in darkness, pausing together before a doorway neither of us can remember entering.
