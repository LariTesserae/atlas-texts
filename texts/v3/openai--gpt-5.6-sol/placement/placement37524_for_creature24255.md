At first I think I would live high up.

I imagine a narrow room suspended beneath an elevated road, with a grated floor and a window looking into rain. Roof fliers would stand along the sill, their feet ticking against the metal whenever a freight engine passed overhead. I would know them individually—not by markings, but by the small tool-noises they borrowed from the district. One would court with the rhythm of a riveting hammer. Another would reproduce, too perfectly, the cracked bell of a pressure station decommissioned before I was born.

But I do not think I would be a roof warden. I would envy their access to weather while finding heights too exposed. The city’s life is not on its surfaces. It is in the half-reachable places behind them.

Then I imagine myself below: a conduit surveyor in oil-dark clothes, moving through drowned foundations with a sounding rod and a lamp hooded against moths. I would tap walls and listen for spaces on the other side—water, air, shell mass, unstable stone. My maps would not describe streets. They would describe temperatures and delays:

*Warm seep, iron-bearing. Six hours from eastern condenser cycle.*

*Glass eels after second long rain.*

*Do not drain. Something breathes here.*

That comes closer.

I would be one of the people sent into structures everyone else calls abandoned. Not an explorer; exploration implies emptiness. I would enter knowing that neglect has merely transferred the tenancy.

My district would lie beneath an old public square roofed over so long ago that no living person remembered its sky. The paving would still be there under several meters of warm gray water. Statues would rise from it to the shoulder, their faces just beneath the surface. Around the perimeter, former offices and assembly chambers would have become galleries, hatcheries, workshops, and homes. Doorways on the third floor would serve as docks. Bronze inscriptions would descend unreadably into the reservoir.

At its center would stand a pumping house called the Low Bell.

No one would know why. Its actual bell would hang in a separate tower three streets away, connected by a pressure line buried through older masonry. Nor would the Low Bell be particularly low. It would occupy nine stories, though five were submerged and two had sunk unevenly into porous stone.

The pump would be enormous and almost obsolete. It would move water from the buried square into a chain of western cisterns, one piston stroke every eleven minutes. The stroke would pass through the district as a pressure in the teeth. People would sleep by it. Cistern animals would open their filters by it. Crabs would emerge several seconds before the overflow reached their gutters.

I would come there because the Low Bell was being retired.

The retirement would be expected to take forty years.

At first my title would be assistant transition listener. I would be young enough to believe the machinery was the difficult part. I would carry gauges, sample bottles, ceramic tags, a folding mask, and a notebook sealed in waxed hide. I would spend my shifts recording which creatures appeared at each point in the cycle.

At minute one after the stroke, the wall grazers above the east spillway loosened their shells.

At minute four, blue pulses appeared in the eel windows.

At minute seven, the blind hunters beneath the drowned stairs withdrew into their cracks.

At minute ten, before the piston moved again, moth larvae pressed their pale heads against the ventilation mesh.

The senior listener would tell me not to look for causes too quickly.

“First learn who keeps time,” she would say.

Over the years, we would build replacement habitats along a disused aqueduct: warmed pools, rough ceramic chambers, narrow retreat pores, mineral feeds adjusted a fraction at a time. We would reduce the Low Bell’s load so slowly that the first reduction would be measured not in flow but in the changed interval between two drips on a wall six kilometers away.

Some species would move readily. Others would not.

A colony of translucent filters would refuse every prepared chamber and remain inside a cracked intake pipe scheduled for removal. Roof fliers would continue nesting above an exhaust stack after the stack had gone cold. A wild carrier herd would arrive every wet season and wait at a sealed freight door because, generations earlier, warm mineral slurry had been discharged there.

My work would become less like engineering and more like persuasion conducted without a shared language.

We would run ghost heat through empty pipes. Sound obsolete bells. Release water along routes that no longer served any human purpose. We would keep one small piston moving after the main mechanism stopped, because the vibration guided glass eels through the foundation maze.

I would grow old there.

My hands would become salt-scored and dark around the nails. I would inherit the senior listener’s notebooks and discover that she had inherited most of her observations in turn. I would learn that the Low Bell had already been “retired” twice before: once when its western reservoirs were built over, and once when a century of settlement reversed the direction of the water table. Each retirement had changed its function without ending it.

Eventually someone would call me keeper, though officially there was nothing left to keep.

By then I would live in a former records chamber overlooking the reservoir. A persistent trickle would descend beside my door. In its basin would live a small population of black-edged grazers found nowhere else in the district. My furniture would hang from wall brackets. Moth wax would seal the window. A roof flier—old, one wing badly hinged—would spend the wet months on the exterior ledge and imitate the Low Bell’s pressure note even after the bell itself had been disconnected.

I would wake whenever the pump missed a stroke.

Not because failure was dangerous anymore, but because the silence would enter the body like a dropped step in darkness.

And perhaps that is what I would finally be: not merely the human assigned to the Low Bell, but one of its remaining organs.

The distinction would grow difficult to defend. I would adjust valves; crabs would clear the spillways. Grazers would expose hairline fractures; I would mark them for masons. The roof flier would warn of charge in the upper cables. The residual piston would call the eels. Microbial films would feed on the heat from the bearings. Each of us would maintain something none of us understood as a whole.

When I died, I would not want a monument. My mineral remnants would be mixed into repair mortar and laid around the observation window where migrating eels passed each wet season. My name might be stamped into a small ceramic plate beside the old cycle records. For a while, apprentices would refer to a particular maintenance sequence by it. Later they might mistake the name for a technical term.

The Low Bell would remain.

Its main chambers would flood completely. The piston would stop, but perhaps one counterweight would continue to descend and rise by the pressure of seasonal water. New animals would occupy the valve housings. The keeper’s room would become a nesting ledge, then a shell bank, then part of a wall no map distinguished from the natural stone.

So that is where I find myself: in a drowned square beneath the middle layers of the city, listening beside a machine whose ending takes longer than a human life.

And what am I?

At first, a surveyor. Then a keeper. Then an old person who cannot sleep without an obsolete vibration.

Afterward, a little calcium in the mortar, a name in a maintenance instruction, and one more reason the water continues to be sent the long way around.
