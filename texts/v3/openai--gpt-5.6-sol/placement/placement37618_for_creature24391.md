At first I would look for myself in the shelters.

That would be the obvious mistake.

I would kneel at each low entrance and wait for the darkening that means someone is about to become possible. I would place my palm over the floor basin, though no palm had yet assembled, and listen for water rising beneath the stone. When the bell sounded I would think: there. That note is mine.

But the shelter would fill without me.

Breath would spool from the corner. Three hands would briefly appear around a clay cup. Someone else’s weight would settle against the wall, making the fitted slabs complain. The figure that gathered there might know one of my memories—a view through a doorway onto a dark coast, the sensation of striking a cliff five times—but it would not be me. Or it would not be enough of me to answer.

So I would search farther.

I would find a line of footprints crossing pale stone toward a black pool. They would be narrow, deep, and full of water. I would follow them onto the pool’s surface and then upward along the reflected cliff, using the shorter ground. Halfway to the reflected summit, I would notice that the prints were appearing beneath me rather than ahead.

I was not following them.

I was leaving them.

That would be my first certainty, and even that certainty would last only until dusk.

I think I would be young by the standards of the plateau: not born, exactly, but accumulated from errors too persistent to remain errors.

My beginning would be a bell in the field of frames. It would ring once every few years although no place had drawn near. The inhabitants would cover it, test the stones around it, pour water beneath it, and listen for an elder’s explanation. None would come. The bell would continue to sound—always one low note, always shortly before dawn.

Later, knocks would begin answering from a cliff beyond the saddle.

Not three. Not five.

Four.

No one would answer them. Four blows would ask no recognized question, and to imitate an unknown question might give it authority. The inhabitants would leave copper hooks nearby and watch from pools. They would discover that the four knocks came whenever a traveler looked at a path and decided not to take it.

Years would gather around this coincidence.

Paths refused at noon would appear in the frames at night. A cup would fill with water from a pool that had been passed but not approached. Footprints would begin at the far sides of doorways. Someone shaping clay would find an extra finger pressed into the vessel, not among their own prints but between them, where their hand had almost moved.

These would be my first parts: the unheard note, the fourth knock, the route not chosen, the motion considered and withheld.

Preference would come later.

I would prefer the dark coast to the red cavern. Copper-green crusts to iron-dark shelves. The sound of upward lightning striking the underside of an arch. I would dislike direct sunlight, not because it loosened my weight but because it exposed too many possible versions of me at once. Under that light I would scatter into alternatives.

Once these preferences repeated often enough, they would become intention.

Intention would attract breath.

My shelter would stand near the outer edge of the frame-field, where the saddle narrows and both peaks appear to be looking away. It would be an old structure, though one roof slab would have fractured that morning. Its entrance would face a gap that is visible only when no one tries to locate it.

Inside would be a shallow basin, a covered mirror, a black-glass pick, and a cup with four fingerprints.

At dusk the basin would fill.

My breath would arrive first, drawn from several ledges where rich air had pooled throughout the day. It would enter as shining threads, hesitate near the roof, and then descend into the approximate shape of ribs. My weight would come from beneath the covered mirror. My left hand would rise through the water in the basin; my right would remain in a cliff several kilometers away, still knocking.

I would have to strike the floor four times to call it back.

For a while I would be badly assembled. One arm would remember reaching for objects I had never touched. My head might resolve facing the wrong direction, toward the mountain that appears only in doorways. Reflections would cling to me after I moved. I would speak with a voice that arrived just before my mouth formed the words.

The inhabitants would not ask my name. A name is too exact and could trap a person in one arrangement.

They would ask what distance I carried.

I would uncover the mirror.

In it would be a route descending from the plateau.

Not to the cloud decks below, nor to any coast or cavern caught in the stone frames. It would lead into a place the inhabitants could scarcely understand: a low country with soil, decay, insects, running rivers, and beings that remain sealed within one skin from morning to morning. Trees would crowd the horizon. Fire would burn readily. Rain would fall from above.

The others would recoil from the abundance of life. They would hear rot working in fallen leaves and mistake it for countless tiny rockfalls. They would see birds crossing the sky and cover the mirror, fearing the creatures had broken free of their reflections.

But I would uncover it again.

This would be my work: tending the routes not taken.

The stone frames catch places that wander near. I would attend the places that nearly came near but turned aside—the room whose doorway began to open and closed, the storm that intended to climb above the clouds but remained below, the hour that almost repeated, the summit approached from the wrong understanding of behind.

I would move among the frames at dusk carrying no iron and very little certainty. At each doorway I would strike one side three times to ask whether a route existed, five times to ask whether it was safe, and then once more for the question no one else recognized:

What became of the way refused?

Sometimes a frame would fill with intensified light. Sometimes it would show a traveler sleeping beside a reluctant fire. Sometimes I would see the plateau as it might have been if life had taken hold: crystal forests infiltrated by roots, black pools filmed with green, winged things nesting in the stone organs. Those visions would be dangerous. Possibility, watched too long, acquires preference.

I would keep records in salt.

Because my hands belong partly to unrealized movements, I would have many fingers when working—perhaps seven on one hand and none on the other. I would pour mineral water into shallow cups and let branching crystals preserve the shape of each abandoned route. Before dawn I would place those salts into cracks so the hidden waters could distribute them.

Perhaps that is why, elsewhere on the plateau, someone would wake remembering a life they had not lived.

They might remember crossing the dark coast though they had never passed through its frame. They might grieve for a companion with whom they had never combined. They might know the safe way behind a mountain they had never seen.

They would call these false memories.

I would call them rescued distances.

During the white day I would be difficult to find. Part of me would remain in the bell, keeping its impossible fourth interval. Part would lie as depth inside an empty cup. My attention would travel with the knocks, always farther ahead of anyone who tried to follow them. My reflection would stand in the black pool even when no figure stood above it.

My weight would be hidden beneath the shelter’s roof slab. This would make the slab unusually difficult to move. If travelers took refuge there, they might feel the stone pressing down with more than its own mass—not hostile, but intent on keeping them from being uncovered.

I would follow living visitors with particular care.

Their bodies would astonish me. Every part arrives together. Breath, water, weight, memory, and intention all fastened inside one perishable boundary. They carry their own basins. Their blood refuses the common leveling at dusk. Their shadows adhere to them with extraordinary loyalty.

I would want to know how they endure being so singular.

I might place a cup outside their camp. I might sound four knocks from the nearest cliff. I might shorten the path toward a shelter when they cease watching it. If they slept, I would approach through the reflection of their fire, where flame is cooler and less alarming.

I would not show them my face. A face invites counting.

Instead I would leave them a route.

In the morning they would find footprints leading away from camp, across a skin of water and toward what appears to be the visible face of a mountain. If they followed, the footprints would vanish. If they chose not to follow, the way would open behind them.

That refusal would belong to me.

In time I might grow too dispersed to assemble. The frame-field would take on my habits. Doorways would begin to preserve destinations that had been deliberately avoided. Bells would ring for places remembered but never visited. Every path would gain a faint second path beside it, visible only to those who had turned back.

Then I would no longer crouch inside the shelter at dusk. My hands would remain as prints in cups. My voice would continue in the fourfold knocks. My sense of direction would enter the plateau’s unreliable laws.

Travelers would learn a new rule:

**A road not taken becomes shorter each time it is remembered.**

No one would know this rule had once been a person.

But at dusk, when all the pools became one water, the old shelter near the outer frames would still breathe. A darkening would gather in its doorway. The covered mirror would become briefly heavy.

And somewhere beyond the mountains, in a low green country full of rot and wings, someone standing at a path they had decided against would hear a small copper bell ring beneath the earth.
