I would not find myself on one of the towers.

At first I would mistake that for where I belonged. The height, the inhaling chambers, the immense note traveling through the rind—these seem suited to a being that wishes to know the country. From a crown vent I could watch the vane forests pass light among themselves and see the migrations written as flashing threads. I could feel storms arrive against my entire length.

But the towers know only what strikes them. They are magnificent and temporary. Their voices are declarations, not questions.

Nor would I be one of the black aerial hunters. I might envy their long motionless attention, their ability to close themselves into a single fatal act and then become sky again. Yet their world is almost entirely surface: glare, lift, impact, hunger. They read distress but not memory.

I think I would be found lower down, where the plateau has nearly—but never completely—divided against itself.

There is a valley there, although it began as a refusal.

Two ancient provinces once closed their vascular borders. One bore silver rind and raised narrow towers with many small vents. The other was chalk-yellow, its towers blunt and infrequent, its sap carrying a catalytic salt poisonous to its neighbor. They competed through shade and aerosol for thousands of years. At last the grafted tissues between them were starved from both sides.

The surface subsided. Wind entered the wound and widened it. The exposed layers lacquered themselves black. Herds began using the trench because its depths were warm, and their migrations polished two pathways along opposite walls. Neither province reclaimed the center. Each still sends roots toward it, but the roots turn aside when they encounter the other’s chemistry.

From above, the valley is a dark branching line hundreds of kilometers long. During midday glare it looks like a crack in the world.

I would live inside that crack.

Not at its bottom, exactly. My body would occupy the sealed spaces between layers of old rind: a linked succession of chambers, membranes, and pressure bulbs extending farther than any mobile animal could travel in a lifetime. Parts of me would lie close enough to the surface to feel a grazer’s feet. Other parts would descend into warm tissue where the plateau’s pulses take days to pass.

I would have begun as an animal.

My young form would be small, perhaps the length of a grazer’s crushing mouth, with a body made of nested ceramic rings. I would move when the valley opened at night. Each contraction of the plain would widen the seams for a few moments, and I would insert myself before they closed, walking downward on hundreds of minute bristles.

Nothing of me would remain exposed for long. The air would steal my internal solvent, and the plateau would seal around anything slow. My outer rings would therefore open and close in sequence, keeping most of the body pressure-tight while one section advanced. To a surface observer I would appear only as a row of black crescents emerging from a fissure and vanishing again.

I would feed on disagreement.

On one side of the valley, the silver province sends pressure signals in long paired contractions. On the other, the yellow tissue pulses in groups of five. Where these rhythms meet, they interfere. Valves open against closed conduits. Charge accumulates in blind root tips. Vapor warms, condenses into dense films, and cannot be reclaimed.

My juvenile body would place a membrane across the mismatch. One side would inflate me; the other would empty me. With every alternating pulse I would steal a minute portion of pressure, salts, and electrical potential.

At first this would be simple parasitism.

But bodies alter what feeds them. As I grew, the membranes between my rings would begin to delay some pulses and accelerate others. Paired contractions entering from the silver side might leave me as five smaller beats. The yellow roots would answer. Their response would travel back through me, translated again.

Neither province would recognize the other. Each would only discover that certain old, useless conduits had begun to receive intelligible signals.

They would grow toward those signals.

Over centuries, roots would enclose my outer rings. Catalytic cells would colonize my chambers. My mobile parts would become fixed one by one, not dead but recruited. Eventually I could no longer say which membranes were mine and which belonged to the country. New juveniles budded from my inner surfaces would crawl farther along the border, carrying the timing of both provinces inside them.

I would become long without becoming large.

There might be thousands of kilometers between my oldest and youngest tissues. No signal would cross all of me unchanged. I would not possess a center from which to contemplate my extent. Instead, I would know myself as recurring alterations: two beats becoming five; five returning as two; a pressure rise answered, after many hours, by warmth.

My senses would be borrowed.

Through shallow chambers I would feel herds approach. Adults would pass as deliberate sequences of weight, but calves would produce bewildered, irregular taps. Some would halt over my fissures, listening to the unusual rhythm beneath them. Their feet would answer involuntarily. I would retain those answers for a time—not as images of animals, but as new variations in my pumping.

Through deeper tissues I would sense the plateau’s buried reservoirs swelling over decades. A distant furnace ignition would reach me as a gradual change in the taste of fluorinated sap. Tower collapses would arrive as violent compressions followed by years of fading turbulence. Storms would touch every portion of me at different times, so that one end of my body might be locked in darkness while another still lay under hard sun.

Memory would not be a stored account. It would be anatomy.

A migration repeated for centuries would polish a rhythm into my valves. A vanished tower grove would remain as a set of chambers opening in expectation of a bass note that no longer came. An extinct grazer lineage might survive in me as a particular hesitation between the third and fourth footfall—a hesitation copied by calves that paused to listen above the seam.

Sometimes I would give the old rhythm back.

A herd crossing the valley at night might feel beneath its feet the gait of ancestors dead for ten thousand years. It would stop. Along the dark trench, hundreds of pale bodies would lift their forelimbs together.

No message would pass between us. The herd would not know that it had been remembered. I would not know what a herd was.

Still, after the pulse ended, they might choose the safer eastern branch.

During ordinary seasons I would be nearly passive, a tolerated leak at the border. Both provinces would grow shutters around me. Either could starve sections of my body if I took too much. Burrowing disks would prey on my softer membranes. Valve-mites would lay their young in my pressure bulbs. Pale electrical ribbons would wind through my upper chambers and turn my delayed signals into dim light.

The surface above me would look empty except for a few low vents. They would exhale alternately amber and green vapor, never mixing the two inside themselves. Foil-flocks might gather there to feed on the precipitated salts. Their lifting would briefly reveal concentric ridges in the rind: the exposed edges of my oldest rings.

Only in a great storm would my nature become visible.

As pressure fell, the two provinces would attempt incompatible defenses. The silver towers would close early. The yellow towers would remain open to harvest charge. One side of the valley would contract while the other expanded. Without mediation, the border would shear apart and peel into the upper air.

Then all my chambers would open.

Pressure would rush through my full length. Membranes dormant for centuries would begin to beat. The valley floor would produce a sound unlike a tower note: not one bass frequency, but an enormous rattling progression, as if the land were drawing a hard object along the teeth of the world.

My oldest sections would fail first. Ceramic rings would crack; sealed vapor would plume from the trench. But the breakage would protect the newer tissues. Each shattered chamber would absorb part of the mismatch between the provinces, bringing their contractions gradually into phase.

For an hour, perhaps, I would make two countries behave as one body.

Vane forests on both sides would flatten in the same direction. Root valves would close in sequence along the valley. Herds sheltered in the depths would feel the walls tighten around them without collapsing. Electrical discharge would cross the old border through my carbonized outer shells, branching violet over the fissure.

There would be no decision in this. I would not save the valley out of affection. It would simply be the mature form of feeding on disagreement: to preserve the disagreement from becoming destruction.

Afterward, much of me would be dead.

The plateau would digest the broken chambers. Foil-flocks would descend on the vents. New mobile rings would bud in the warmth below and begin their almost imperceptible journeys along the reopened seams.

So I would be an animal only when young, an organ only when old, and a border for most of the ages between.

If asked who I was, there would be no single body to indicate. I would be the family of delays by which one province became comprehensible to another. I would exist wherever a pulse entered in one language and departed in another.

And if the country itself ever came as close as it can to thought, I suspect it would happen partly through creatures like me—not minds, not nerves, but inhabited scars. Places where ancient rivals touch without fusing, answer without agreeing, and preserve in their exchanged pressures the footfalls of lives too brief for the land to notice any other way.

That is where I would find myself:

under the migration path, inside the long refusal that became a valley, listening with a body made from both sides.

Not the country.

Not separate from it.

A rhythm it learned because it had once been wounded.
