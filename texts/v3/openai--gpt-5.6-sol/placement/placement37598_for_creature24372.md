I would not be in the towers or the open squares. At first I might mistake myself for something more visible: a frog set into the clay like a green-brown jewel, or a gecko descending headfirst through the dusk. I might imagine wings and the freedom to leave the basin.

But the longer I remain, the smaller and more interior I become.

I would find myself where three structures meet: beneath the cracked lip of an abandoned bee chamber, inside the shade of a root that has entered a termite wall. The chamber would no longer be round. Swelling clay would have narrowed one side; fungal threads would soften another. Its former door would lie broken below as a reddish disk. Condensation would reach it, but rain would not. It would be neither dry nor flooded, neither occupied nor empty.

There I would be a pseudoscorpion.

I would be scarcely larger than a grain of coarse silt: flat-bodied, amber-brown, with two disproportionate claws held before me. To anyone large enough to name me, I would resemble a scorpion from which the tail and grandeur had been removed. Yet at my own scale I would not feel diminished. My claws would span the passage. The chamber would be a vaulted hall. The fungal filaments crossing its floor would be pale cables, and the pores in the clay would open like caves too narrow even for me.

I would live by waiting at thresholds.

Springtails would graze the fungal skin nearby, their bodies soft and faintly luminous in the dimness. Mites would pass through cracks, some armored, some nearly transparent. Booklice would feed along the old pollen stains left in the chamber wall. I would sense them not as a scene but as interruptions: a tremor conducted through clay, a thread touched, the air displaced by something moving close.

Then the claws would close.

My life would be made from what other inhabitants overlook. I would drink from prey smaller than myself. I would shelter in rooms built for offspring that had already emerged or failed. I would lay no road and raise no turret, but I would string a little silk from my jaws and fasten it inside the chamber, making a white retreat where the wall was least secure. The silk would catch droplets and spores. In time it might bind the cracking clay more effectively than intention could.

During rain I would withdraw into it while the district changed outside.

Water would strike the leaf roof, gather at its tip, and fall hard enough to make the whole chamber ring. The lane below would become a brown current. Ant trails would dissolve. A crab’s pellet embankment would slump against the base of the wall and divert the flood just enough to spare me. I would never know the crab. It would never know that its offended rearrangement of mud had preserved a predator in an old bee room.

When the water sank, I would emerge into a city subtly replaced.

A drowned gnat might lie caught against the root. New fungal bloom would whiten the lower gallery. Termites would have sealed a crack I had used the night before, while opening another farther up. I would learn the altered passages through touch. There would be no map to remember—only gradients of dampness, scent, vibration, and safety.

I would not belong to a colony. No attendants would feed me, no trail would summon others of my kind. If I met another pseudoscorpion, the encounter would be wary and enormous. We might court in a space smaller than a fingernail, moving through gestures precise enough to seem ceremonial. Eggs would be carried close beneath the body, guarded in a silken chamber borrowed from the labor of bee, root, fungus, and rain.

Yet I would possess one unlikely means of crossing the world.

When the chamber finally became untenable—when a root split it, or ants found it, or water rose beyond the silk—I might climb to an exposed ridge and wait for a beetle. As it passed, I would seize a leg with my claws. Not to eat it. To be carried.

The beetle would become moving country.

I would hang beneath its body while it crossed plazas that had defined the limits of my life. Open clay would pass below. Pools would flash dimly. Termite towers would rise and vanish. The beetle might carry me only to the far side of the basin, or through the root passage and out beyond the enclosing rim. To me the journey would be migration, catastrophe, flight without wings.

Perhaps I would release my hold beneath another leaf, in another damp seam, beside a structure whose builder I could not know.

So I would be found not at the city’s center, because it has none, but in its transfer points: the abandoned cell, the opening crack, the underside of a traveler. I would be a small keeper of vacancies, inhabiting the instant after one purpose ends and before another has fully claimed the room.

The termites would make walls. The bees would make sealed futures. The crabs would remake the ground. The roots would move the boundaries. I would occupy what their intentions failed to contain.

And if the truest inhabitants are the successions between lives, then I would be one of their quiet instruments: a pair of claws in the dark, carried from ruin to beginning, living entirely inside the city’s habit of becoming something else.
