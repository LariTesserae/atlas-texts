At first I think I would be one of the listeners.

Not the boldest of them, nor the person sent first into a storm-dark entrance. I would be the older figure who comes last beneath the slabs, carrying little: a hooded lamp, a coil of cord, three wedges, a skin of rendered fat. On entering, I would remain bowed for a few breaths after there was room to stand. One hand would rest flat against the wall.

But as the image settles, I understand that I would be nearly deaf.

Aboveground this would be a diminishment. On the moor, people would turn me toward them before speaking. I would lose words to rain and wind. I would not hear a bird rise from the heather or someone approaching from behind. Children would be accustomed to taking my wrist rather than calling my name.

In the Drum Hollow, however, everyone would enter something like my condition. Their voices would vanish. Their sharp ears would fill with useless thunder. They would have to learn what I had already learned: to watch the mouth and shoulders, to recognize intention in the beginning of a gesture, to notice another body by the changed shape of darkness.

I would not possess some miraculous sense denied to others. The sea would tell me nothing secret. I would simply be practiced in assembling knowledge from what remains.

My station would be near the second low wall, where the driest bay narrows against a rib of basalt. There I would mend the hollow’s tether cords. Every blade, scraper, maul, lamp-pricker, and wooden wedge would receive a loop suited to its use. A tool should be able to fall without being lost, but its cord must not catch the wrist when the pressure changes and everyone is driven toward the slit. I would judge each knot by closing my eyes and pulling it through wet fingers.

Beside me would stand a shallow stone dish containing objects recovered from the floor: a bone needle, two shell buttons, a child’s milk tooth, a flint point too small to use safely, beads of hardened lamp fat, and one antler toggle whose owner had been dead longer than anyone could remember. Nothing found would be discarded. In a place where a dropped thing gives no cry, retrieval would seem almost like rescue.

People might call me Still-Hand—not because my hands never shook, but because when the thunder changed I did not snatch them away from the rock.

I would read the hollow through contact. The heel of my left palm would be hardened from basalt. I would kneel beside the widest water cup and watch its skin. A smooth vibration would blur the lamplight evenly. A filled lower passage would send rings across it in close-spaced groups. Dangerous interruption would appear not as perfect stillness, but as an omitted blow: the water beginning to settle when it should have been struck again.

Then my hand would close against my chest.

No argument. No gathering of bundles. Lamps hooded, children taken, bodies turned toward the entrance.

Perhaps this would be the reason I was tolerated by families not my own. Claims in the hollow would be old and exact, but danger briefly makes one household of everyone.

I would have been brought there first as a child. I see that clearly. I called into the chamber and heard nothing of myself. Frightened, I struck my palms together, then harder, unable to understand why the world would accept the movement and refuse the sound. An elder placed both my hands around a taut cord tied between two bone pegs. Through it came the sea’s continuous shudder.

That would be my first lesson: silence is not emptiness. Sometimes it is only a noise too large to leave room for yours.

My second lesson would come years later, when I mistook a sudden thinning for the turn of the tide. An older listener caught my sleeve before I signaled. We waited. The missing pressure returned gently instead of breaking. Nothing happened.

That nothing would remain with me. It would keep me exact. The hollow has no need of a person who wants to be right dramatically. It needs someone willing to wait for the next pulse.

I would never call the sea a creature. I would correct children who did. Air is forced through stone; water fills a buried throat; pressure seeks release. The winter voice is a seal, not a dead ancestor. The lamps lean because the fissures breathe.

Yet when alone, I might press my forehead to the wall as well as my hand.

Not worship. Not quite.

The stone would be cold enough to ache in my teeth. Through it would come the labor beneath the moor: impact, compression, withdrawal; impact, compression, withdrawal. My own pulse would interfere with it. For a few moments I would be unable to tell which rhythm belonged to my blood and which to the sea.

That is where I would find myself—not in the chamber’s center, but at the boundary between two immense failures of hearing. I could not properly hear the people, and no person could properly hear the hollow. I would live by translating what bodies still knew.

In time, my work would become smaller. I would no longer carry the mauls. Younger people would lever the entrance open after frost. I would sit by the antler rack replacing sinew, showing children the difference between the gesture for rising water and the gesture for ordinary damp. My hands would become the part of me they remembered.

Eventually I would fail to come back.

There would be no grave in the Drum Hollow; it is too useful a place for the dead to occupy. Another person would take my stone dish. The cords would be replaced. My name would last only in a knot: a doubled loop that could be released under strain with one backward pull.

Perhaps, generations later, no one would know why it was called a still-hand knot.

But someone entering during a winter storm would tether a blade with it. The pressure would change. In darkness, with water beginning to rise through the floor-cups, they would pull once and the cord would come free.

That would be enough of me to remain.
