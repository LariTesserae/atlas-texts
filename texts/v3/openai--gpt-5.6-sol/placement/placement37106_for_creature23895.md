I would not live at the city’s center.

At first I imagine myself high among the sounding pillars, one of the listeners with a scarred jaw and the patience to distinguish a new cavern from an old echo. Then lower down, in a house tethered beneath an arch, tending membranes in the warm upward flow. Then perhaps not a person at all, but one of the balloon-creatures above the streets, mistaken for the same individual across generations.

But I think I would be found at the city’s unfinished edge, where the last maintained guide-rope crosses into gardens and does not come out again.

My dwelling would be anchored beside one of the broad gates.

The gate would be much older than the district around it, though not old enough to have weathered. Its two uprights would lean apart as the mountain widened beneath them. Pale mineral moss would fur the lintel. Beyond it would lie a road built for something larger than any inhabitant: a descending avenue of basalt shelves, root-bound walls, and open turns too broad to be useful. No rope would run down its center. The posts would stand at the edges, widely spaced, as if meant to guide flanks rather than hands.

Nothing living would remember what the road was for.

It would be my responsibility to keep it passable.

The work would have a name that means, approximately, *making room for what exerts a shape while absent*. It would not be a prestigious craft. Children would assume I spent my days waiting for a monster. Adults would know better, though some would still make the small finger-pressure for foolishness when I proposed repairs.

I would clear creeping fronds from the avenue without killing them, teasing their roots into neighboring walls. I would inspect the gate after each sounding and add slack where the bindings had drawn too tight. I would prune the great fruiting chambers growing along the route—hollow bells large enough to admit my whole body—and wedge them open when they ripened. Their pulp would taste mineral and faintly bitter. No one would know what was meant to enter them whole.

The avenue would be useless in all ordinary ways. That would be why it required a keeper. Useful paths defend themselves by being traveled.

My house would be small and detachable, a loop of stone ribs sealed with cultivated membrane. It would hang just inside the gate so that, while sleeping, I could feel through the tether if anything touched the outer road. Bowls of green light would be fixed low along the walls. A white grazer would live somewhere among them despite every enclosure I made for it. I would pretend not to know it was the same one a neighboring child kept returning after each escape.

My ballast would be mismatched.

At one hip I would wear dense black basalt taken from the gate’s first fallen hinge. At the other, a pale carbonate stone formed around the wrist bones of an ancestor—not an ancestor of blood, but the former keeper from whom I inherited the road. A narrow hammered-copper weight would lie against my sternum. It would green my wrappings and leave a metallic taste in the water near my breathing folds.

The imbalance would make me drift slightly sideways when I crossed open water. People would recognize me at a distance by that error.

I would have been born to a dwelling that no longer existed. Its anchor-name would refer to a warm spring now flowing beneath an abandoned terrace: the Fifth Mouth, perhaps, or the Spring Under Three Roofs. During my childhood the fissure would have narrowed. The house would have been detached, divided, and retied into several districts. I would therefore have relatives everywhere and a home nowhere except the gate.

That might be why I took the work.

Others would descend from stable anchors, even if those anchors moved. I would descend from a flow that had ceased. I would understand that absence was not the same as nothing.

My breathing fans would be scarred along the left ribs from following a rising tool too far when I was young. Perhaps it was a shell comb. Perhaps, in the story I told children, it was a ceremonial copper mirror. I would have crossed the density boundary beneath the False Shore and entered the thinner water above, where my buoyancy failed without warning.

For a few moments I would have fallen upward.

The city below would have become a dark arrangement of ridges and living lamps. Above me, its reflection would have thickened until I could not tell which settlement contained weight. I would have seen a bridge where no bridge had yet been built and a tower with its roots exposed like fingers.

Someone would have pulled me back by my trailing hair.

Afterward, the damaged folds would never open fully. I would move more slowly than most and rest often with one wrist inside a cool-water channel. This would prevent me from becoming a long-distance surveyor or a mason working in heavy sediment. It would make me suited to the gate, where patience mattered more than breath.

I would be neither believer nor skeptic concerning the reflected city.

I would study it as a builder studies a crack: not because it must be a message, but because consequences do not require intention.

During reflection season I would climb to a ledge above the gate and look upward. The mineral layer would gather the city into itself. My broad avenue would appear overhead as a pale corridor running through inverted growth.

Usually it would be empty.

Sometimes a distortion would move along it.

I would record such movements in cords: one knot for direction, one for apparent size, one for whether the form displaced the reflected leaves or passed through them. The cords would hang inside my house, an archive readable in darkness. Over the years they would become difficult to distinguish from superstition.

A listener might visit and tell me the road had begun to tilt into danger.

A gardener might argue that the unused stones should be surrendered to nurseries.

A household displaced by a migrating spring might ask to anchor a new dwelling beneath the lintel. Their need would be immediate and real. Mine would be an obligation to something that might be extinct, unborn, imaginary, or incapable of gratitude.

I would not refuse them outright. Refusal is a form of hardness, and hardness splits.

Instead we would negotiate how much emptiness the gate required.

A dwelling could be hung above the route if its lower cords could be released quickly. A nursery could grow along one wall if no root crossed the central opening. Guide-ropes could be added for present travelers, but braided with deliberate weak points so they would part rather than entangle something large. The avenue would become inhabited without being closed.

That would be my true craft—not waiting, but preventing the present from consuming every possible future.

My hand-name would be given as two fingers laid across the palm, followed by a pressure that moves outward without lifting. It would mean *held open*, though intimates might joke that it meant *unable to finish anything*.

My stone-name would be a low knock followed, after a long pause, by a lighter one.

The pause would be part of it.

Near the end of my life, I would begin cultivating my hair loose. Green points would gather in the braids. Tiny grazers would scrape at the carbonate beads near my scalp. My house would acquire younger keepers, though none would formally call themselves apprentices. One would tend the root-bindings better than I did. Another would consider the reflected records embarrassing but continue knotting them with greater accuracy.

When I died, I would not be sent upward.

I would be placed in a porous cradle beside the gate, where roots could enter slowly. My ballast would be removed except for the mismatched black hinge-stone. That one would remain tied at my hip. As tissue and wrapping gave way, the stone would draw my bones slightly sideways, into the same posture by which people had recognized me in life.

In time, the roots would coat my ribs and pass through them. One arm might become part of the outer wall. My finger bones might be set into the release catches of the guide-ropes, so that a strong enough pressure from the unused avenue would open the way.

I would remain answerable to the gate after ceasing to be a person.

And if the enormous visitor finally came—beast, ancestor, future animal, or mountain entering its own reflection—it would not find me waiting in the road.

It would find that I had left room.
