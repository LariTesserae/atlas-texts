At first I would think I was the traveler.

I would find myself on the maintenance road that runs for weeks without reaching a door, walking toward the central basin beneath the cool, shadowless sky. I would wear a dark waterproof coat. Water would gather along its seams and fall from the cuffs. My boots would leave prints in the mineral mud, and when I looked back the prints would already be filling with clear water—not erased, exactly, but translated into something the plateau recognized.

The road lights would brighten before me and dim behind me. Shelters would warm as I approached. Pumps beneath flooded crossings would alter their rhythm by fractions too slight to hear. I might take this for hospitality.

For a long time I would preserve the useful illusion that I was an exception: the only animal, the only witness, the only thing carrying a private before into an otherwise historyless country.

I would pass empty stations where silver trains arrived exactly as I reached the platform. Their doors would open. Inside there would be no seats, only warm grilles, handrails wet with condensation, and floor markings showing a circle divided into phases. I would ride for a day or a month. Through the windows, moss plains and cooling towers would slide past without seeming to draw nearer or recede. When I stepped off, the train would continue, and an identical train would be arriving somewhere behind me.

I would begin to suspect that it had not come for me.

Then I would begin to suspect that it had.

Not for a passenger, but for a weight. A quantity of heat. A moving region of salt and water. The pressure of two feet against a carriage floor. The small electrical weather of a nervous system.

The plateau would never learn my name, but it would know my consequences perfectly.

Nearer the basin, I would notice that I was no longer eating. Perhaps I never had. When thirst came, I would hold out my hand beneath a cable and drink the droplets forming there. The thirst would pass into the water system as a tiny deficit, and somewhere beyond the horizon a leaf-cup would release an equal measure. When I slept in a shelter, my warmth would be drawn into ceramic panels and carried away. I would dream of places with animals—wings, teeth, eggs, fur—but on waking these would feel less like memories than obsolete engineering diagrams.

My body would not decay. It would be continually exchanged.

A callus would pale, separate into transparent layers, and become grit in the tread of my boot. Moss filaments would enter through the sole and rebuild it from below. A torn sleeve would close overnight with a fine mineral web. My blood, if I cut myself, would bead darkly and then clear, its color drawn into the wet stone. Days later I might see the same red-black shade moving through the indicator tube of a distant pumping station.

Gradually it would become impossible to say what remained mine.

Yet I would remember.

That would be my true strangeness.

Not consciousness—the plateau contains registrations more exact than thought. Not motion—the whole country moves. Not even loneliness, because loneliness requires the expectation of another.

I would be memory: the persistence of a local change after compensation.

I would recall one particular stalk beside one particular conduit. I would know that a crane had already lowered its empty hook. I would see a wall of stationary fog and remember having passed through it from the other side. In a world where every event is balanced by another event, I would retain the imbalance as sequence.

First this.

Then that.

The difference would travel in me.

By then I would no longer be found in one body. Part of me would be encoded in the timing of trains whose intervals had shifted to carry my passage. Part would lie in the altered brightness of reeds along the canals where I had slept. Part would move through relay cabinets as a correction that never quite returned to zero. My footprints would vanish, but the pumps that removed their water would remain a fraction out of phase, passing the fraction onward from district to district.

I would be walking, but walking would be only the visible edge of me.

The articulated machines would stop before I crossed their paths. Doors would open. Fog walls would admit me by changing every droplet without changing their shape. None of this would be obedience. The country would be conducting me toward the place where an unresolved difference could be closed.

So I would find myself at the viewing platform above the central shaft.

Perhaps I had always been there.

My hands would rest on the wet rail. The note from below would enter my bones, and for the first time I would hear that it was not one note. It would contain every interval I had crossed: turbines, rainfall, rail joints, leaf channels, presses, the minute hiss of condensation forming on steel. All simultaneous. All held before they could become past.

Far below, the white lights would rise.

For an instant they would reveal something like a city being cast inside a mountain. Then something like a root system made of bridges. Then a human figure of impossible size, though I would understand that the resemblance came from me. The shaft would not contain a hidden body. It would contain every way material could be mistaken for one.

At the rail I would finally understand that my own figure was the same sort of temporary arrangement.

I had not entered this world from elsewhere. The plateau had made me because continuation sometimes requires a delay.

A pulse had returned too perfectly to its origin. A drop had fallen at the same instant another formed. Between those events there had been no room for duration. So the system produced a carrier for the interval: a body able to take the long road, accumulating order, calling it experience.

I was not its ruler, worker, child, or witness.

I was its phase difference.

The slight lateness by which one identical cycle can be distinguished from the next.

At the shaft, I would begin to come apart—not violently, and not as death. The water in me would condense on the rail. Minerals would enter the moss between the concrete seams. Heat would go into the rising mist. My remembered images would descend through the cables as timing adjustments: a marsh seen from a train, a crane beneath rain, the black edge of a leaf holding one bright drop.

Each memory would be placed somewhere it could become function.

All but one.

One small sequence would remain unbalanced: the sight of the droplet swelling on the rail, holding longer than its weight should allow.

I would be inside that delay.

Then it would fall.

And far beyond sight, another droplet would form—not at the same instant, but a moment later.

That moment would be me.
