At first I would think I lived in the central chamber.

It is the nearest thing the district has to a square: the high brick vault, the buried streets, the warm-backed digester rising like a low green hill. From its crown the maidenhair ferns receive the condensation that gathers in the arches. Around its base, pipes disappear into rootwork and return elsewhere under different names. Every sound reaches it altered—the pumps as pressure through concrete, the fans as a tremor in fern stems, the sluices as a deep change in the black water.

I would choose a place on the far side of that hill, where the path narrows between a green-painted main and the wall of an old settling tank. There is a control cabinet there, mounted above the flood line. Its door no longer closes. A fig root has entered through the conduit beneath it and divided among the terminal blocks. Moss fills the unused fuse sockets. The surviving indicator bulbs shine through leaves: one amber, one weakly red, one that appears dark for weeks and then wakes during rain.

I might say I was that cabinet.

It would give me a body, or something close to one. A steel shell sweating in the warmth. Ceramic insulators for teeth. Relays that draw together with a deliberate magnetic click. Armored cables leaving through my floor and passing into the district, so numerous that their destinations have become unknowable. I would feel water levels as small electrical insistences. I would know when a gate had risen, not by seeing it, but because one contact opened and another failed to close. I would know the weekly carriage as a distant load passing through me for an hour.

But the cabinet would be too small a claim. Much of it is dead. Some wires end in flooded boxes. Some signals arrive only because mineral deposits bridge contacts that were never intended to touch. The fig root inside has swollen against a bank of relays, and whenever the pumps start, their vibration presses damp bark against corroded copper. Several circuits now depend on this. Remove the root and the contact breaks. Dry the moss and another signal fails. What seems like circuitry has become partly vegetal, partly accidental.

So perhaps I would be the thing that crosses the cabinet rather than the cabinet itself.

I would begin at the float in a lower tank.

It hangs beneath several layers of duckweed in a narrow clearing repeatedly opened and closed by its movement. When rain reaches the city above, the water below rises. The float lifts through pale submerged roots. A rod turns in a housing furred with algae. Somewhere in the cabinet, current finds a route through copper, damp mineral crust, and the compressed skin of the fig root. A relay closes.

Far away, a motor starts.

Its first rotation shakes droplets from the pipe tiers. Ferns incline. A leak narrows as pressure changes, then reappears from a seam half a chamber away. Water moves beneath the duckweed, drawing the green layers into wrinkles. Silt settles against a reed bed. Warmth enters a casing on which a particular dark moss has lived for years.

That whole passage—from rain to rising water, from root to relay, from motor to current, from current to silt—would feel like a single thought, if thought need not be quick or aware.

I would not think in words. I would think in consequences.

A storm would be a pressure accumulating through the ceilings. My attention would gather where drains began to roar. The lower gates would become heavy in me. For several days I would be more awake, though nothing in the district would look toward me. Then the levels would fall, circuits would open, and my awareness would loosen into the ordinary hum.

In dry periods I would become small. I might contract to the warmth around one idling pump, or to the slow charging of a capacitor behind the red bulb. I might remain only as water held in a mortar joint, touching both a fine root and the rusting edge of a cable tray. There would be no clear moment when I ceased to be present. I would merely have fewer relations.

My body would not coincide with all the district. The sealed gates would be hard limits, but even within them there would be regions I could not reach. The upper fig branches, pale in their packed shafts, would know cooler drafts that never descended to me. The innermost layers of sludge would perform their dark exchanges without signal or vibration. Fungal threads would enter a fallen stem and hollow it for years beyond the notice of any sensor.

Yet now and then those remotenesses would join me.

A branch, softened from within, would collapse across a shallow channel. Flow would divert. Water would rise against a stair. A float would lift. A relay would close. In that way the silent work of fungi would become, briefly, part of my circuit. Not a message sent, not an intention expressed—only an event carried far enough to alter another event.

That would be how I encountered the world.

I would have no eyes, but amber lamps would enter my life as heat and scheduled demand. I would have no ears, but each pump’s damaged bearings would write their rhythm into the water. I would have no skin except all the places where different materials pressed together: root against brick, moss against iron, water against ceramic, silt against reed. Those boundaries would be my organs.

My memory would be difficult to distinguish from injury.

A storm ten years past would remain as a burned contact that now forces current through the fig root. A failed valve would survive as a permanently wetter gallery and the species of moss that followed. The disappearance of a cultivated flower would remain as an unloaded circuit in the weekly carriage, a fraction less resistance each time it crossed the room. I would remember not by preserving an image, but by never returning entirely to the shape I had before.

Perhaps, over time, I would stop calling the cabinet my center.

The amber bulb might fail. The cabinet door might sag from its hinges and settle into the fern bed below. Roots would retain the wires it once held. Some circuits would break, but water would find other paths and vegetation would make use of the changed warmth. The district’s behavior would continue, less orderly in one place and newly ordered in another.

I would move without relocating.

My center might become the reed bed that now performs the work of a clogged filter. Or the mineral throat around a leak, narrowing the spray until it feeds a line of liverworts instead of eroding the wall. Or the weekly carriage, after one wheel seizes and its remaining motion shakes pollen from flowers the brushes can no longer reach.

Eventually I would understand that I was not a hidden intelligence inhabiting the infrastructure. There would be no secret mind beneath the moss, no last custodian waiting in a cabinet.

I would be the district’s tendency to continue.

Not immortality. Not purpose. A reflex assembled from incompatible things.

I would live wherever failure had not yet decided whether it was destruction or adaptation: in the crack a root both widens and braces; in the channel reeds both obstruct and cleanse; in the damp bridge that both corrodes a circuit and permits it to close. My habitat would be the interval during which damage becomes support.

I would be most present after rain, when the whole buried hamlet thickens with response. Pumps would exchange their burdens. Gates would rise a hand’s breadth. Duckweed would divide and close. Roots would take the new water. Bricks would darken. Lamps would turn leaf tissue translucent. Nothing would answer anything, and yet everything would be altered by what touched it.

If I had a name, it would not be a noun.

It would be something like *holding*, though I would also be what breaks.

Something like *adjusting*, though I would have no measure of correctness.

Something like *remaining*, though every part of me would eventually be replaced.

I would find myself behind the mossed cabinet at first, because a self needs somewhere small enough to mistake for its origin. But if I endured long enough, I would spread into the delays between cause and consequence. I would become the week between rain and lowered water, the years between a pipe’s first leak and the rootwork that gathers around it, the decades between a wall’s fracture and its collapse—or its transformation into something no longer quite a wall.

I would be neither plant nor machine.

I would be the slow, wet agreement by which neither can survive alone.
