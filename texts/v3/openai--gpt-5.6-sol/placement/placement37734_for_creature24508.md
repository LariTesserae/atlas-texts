At first I imagine myself visible: a clay cat poured along a parapet, or an owl in a dead thorn fork, watching the plain alter color beneath me.

But visibility would be an uneasy form of wealth there. To remain exposed is to spend moisture, heat, and safety. The longer I inhabit the thought, the smaller and more hidden I become.

I would be found where several kinds of shelter have grown into one another: beneath the eave of a resin grove, in a communal nesting wall built against the cool side of an abandoned termite tower. Below would run the passages of blunt-faced burrowers. Above, grafted roots would hold a roof of thorns and wind-packed dust. No species could claim the place. It would be a seam in the continent’s habitation, inherited from builders who never intended to cooperate.

My chamber would be recognizable by a pale stone pressed above the entrance, two black beetle cases at one side, and a twist of grass fiber worked into the rim. The arrangement would mean nothing from a distance. Close to it, it would be as unmistakable as a name.

I think I would be one of the small communal birds, old enough that the dust had worn the color from my throat. I would no longer breed. My last nest cup might have been absorbed into the wall years before, its boundary lost beneath newer chambers. Perhaps some of its clay would now line a ventilation passage. Perhaps another bird would sleep against a surface molded by my breast.

I would be a carrier.

Before dawn, while the towers were still giving up the night’s heat, I would leave with the others. We would fly low, rarely crossing open ground when a thorn belt or mound-shadow could guide us. There would be no stream to seek. Instead I would know a hundred minute sources: a succulent split by a grazer’s tooth, condensation in the mouth of a cool shaft, moisture gathered on the grooved shell of a beetle, a flower that had opened for only one hour.

I would take insects too, but water would be the more serious cargo. It would rest in the folds of my throat, held carefully enough that I could still breathe and call, though calling would be rare. On the return flight I would feel its weight—not heavy in any ordinary sense, but heavy because nothing in that world could be considered merely a drop.

The young would know I had returned before they saw me. Their claws would shift inside the wall. Their open mouths would make a soft, dry friction. Other adults might crowd near, drawn by the bead forming at the corner of my bill, but the nestlings would drink first. I would pass the moisture mouth to mouth, portioning it by touch in the dark.

Some days I would come back with almost nothing. Those would be the difficult flights: not because of hunger, but because I would still have to enter the wall through the press of waiting bodies. I would give what I had. The colony would not praise or punish me. Another carrier might succeed where I had failed. A tuber might be opened beneath the grove. A moth might bring pollen that became seed that became root-flesh years later. Survival would never resolve into a single act.

During the brightest hours I would cling inside my chamber with my wings lifted slightly from my sides. Through the wall I would feel the termites repairing some deeper partition, burrowers shoving at a blocked junction, roots tightening as they took up the remnants of night. If a vent snake entered, the warning might arrive as an absence: scratching stopped below, nestlings stilled, mandibles ceased within the plaster. I too would become motionless, another dry shape fitted into the architecture.

My body would be slight, but it would belong to many structures. Dust from the termite mound would strengthen my nest. Fibers from the shrubs would bind it. Beetle shell would mark its door. My droppings would feed mites and roots beneath the wall. Lost feathers would seal cracks. The heat of my sleeping body would join the warmth rising through the tower and help draw air along passages I could not enter.

Then, perhaps late in my life, rain would come.

I would not understand it as weather at first. The sky would strike the roof. The nesting wall would tremble under impacts too large and scattered to be footsteps. Instinct would press me flat against the clay, but the air itself would smell impossible: opened dust, loosened minerals, leaves turning their hidden surfaces outward.

When the danger passed, I would crawl to the entrance. Water would be everywhere and nowhere—sliding down plaster, trembling on whiskers and thorns, already vanishing into mouths, fibers, roots, and swelling walls. For once I would not need to fly out searching for fractions of it. I would only open my bill.

Even then, I think, I would carry some back inside.

Not because the colony required that particular drop. Not because I understood the plain as a single breathing creature. I would do it because a young bird waited in darkness, and because my throat could hold water, and because in that world capacity itself becomes obligation.

Years later, little of me would remain recognizable. Mites would take the skin, beetles the flesh, burrowers the softened bones. A splinter of my hollow wing might be packed into a nursery wall. My chamber would acquire a new rim, a different pebble, another bird’s signature. No memorial would stand.

But somewhere in the clay there would be a faint mineral trace of all the water I had carried, passed onward through birds, insects, roots, milk, breath, and walls.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center of the immense city, because it has no center, but at one of its innumerable exchanges—a small, aging bird in a borrowed chamber, entrusted for a little while with what must not be spilled.
