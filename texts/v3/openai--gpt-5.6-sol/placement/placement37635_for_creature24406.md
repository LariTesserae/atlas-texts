At first I imagine myself traveling.

It seems natural in a world that refuses permanence: a narrow sled under me, breath caught in a felt hood, one hand resting on the floor to feel whether it is truly still. I would carry little. A water case. A thorn hook. A route-cord wound twice around my ribs. Something mineral, perhaps—a blue grain sealed in resin, kept not for value but for the comfort of touching what does not answer.

I would know how to walk badly.

Three quick steps, a dragged heel, stillness. One long stride. A whispered word between the next two footfalls. I would never hum all the way through a tune. When frightened, I would become less regular, not more.

But I do not think I would be a traveler for long.

The distances in the White Undergreen are too enormous for me. The clean air between structures would work on me until I began to fear not what might emerge from it, but that nothing ever would. I would spend too much water looking back. I would become attached to each temporary ledge, each honest patch of black growth, each crack containing old teeth. I would want to learn one place beyond usefulness.

So I find myself instead at a margin called the Held Breath.

It is not a settlement, though it was one once. It lies where an ancient branch passes near a solitary pillar without touching it. The gap between them is less than the width of a village, narrow by the standards of the hollow, but the drop beneath has no visible end. During strong brightness the branch draws toward the pillar. During dimming it relaxes away. Once every few years they come so close that frost bridges the remaining space in white threads. The threads never bear weight. They ring faintly when they break.

Long ago, houses occupied both surfaces. Ladders crossed the gap. People believed the branch and pillar would eventually join.

They did not. The branch began to twist instead, crushing the outer houses against its own underside. The inhabitants cut loose and migrated. They left behind stitched ladder scars, sealed hearth hollows, and a rain-catcher too deeply rooted to remove. Its membrane has become a translucent leaf, and in rare condensation it fills with enough water to bend the whole branch downward.

Someone must watch that bending.

That is where I live.

I would be called a pausekeeper, though the name would sound grander in translation than it is. I would not predict turnings. No one can. I would keep distinctions.

Water or sap.

Wind-strain or deep contraction.

A listener thread brushing the branch or a burrower moving within it.

The ordinary drawing-together of branch and pillar, or the first gesture of something continental.

Most days, nothing would happen. That would be the work.

My shelter would be woven into an abandoned branch-fork, only three sides enclosed. The fourth would remain open toward the gap, partly for observation and partly because a fully finished room would feel like a claim the world might choose to dispute. Strips of crawler wing would cover the roof. Beneath them I would see shadows crossing the luminous vault: frost, ceiling grazers, occasionally the black bell of a listener so high above that it seemed fixed there.

I would have no hearth. Heat makes surfaces curious.

Instead I would sleep inside a layered sling with heated sapstones tucked against my kidneys. Before rest I would change the sling’s anchor point. I would never let my weight pull twice from exactly the same place.

Across the branch I would maintain a garden of tensions.

Not plants in the usual sense. Lines.

Lengths of vascular fiber would run from old scars in the branch to pegs grown along the shelter wall. Some would be taut enough to tremble at a distant fracture. Some would sag and respond only when the branch tightened. A few would descend into natural pores and return stained by whatever fluids moved below. Hush-worm mats would be cultivated beneath selected lines, not to silence them completely but to show where vibration gathered. When the worms contracted, small bone markers would tilt.

No two markers would be shaped alike. A regular row would be an invitation.

I would listen with my hands more often than my ears. One cord against the wrist, one between the teeth, cheek against old black fiber. The sounds would be minute: dry ticks, a pulse like liquid passing through a sleeping throat, the distant granular rush of frost shedding from the pillar.

Sometimes a break on the far side of the gulf would arrive through the branch before its sound crossed the air. I would feel it in my jaw. Minutes later the report would come, vast and lonely.

Those would be the moments I lived for—not because I loved destruction, but because the interval between touch and sound would tell me the distance. For a little while the emptiness would have measure.

People would visit irregularly.

Migration bands would leave water if they could spare it. Persuaders would inspect the branch and disagree with all my records. Memory-keepers would add conditions to their cords:

**At the Held Breath, ask the watcher whether the gap is narrowing.**

Not my name. My function.

Children would be afraid of my stillness. Adults would be afraid that I had stayed too long.

They would be right.

The branch would begin to notice me after perhaps twelve years.

First, the floor beneath my shelter would cease forming frost. Then small white ridges would gather around the places where I knelt to listen. I would cut them away and vary my position. They would return elsewhere. The rain-catcher would produce a new vein leading toward my wall. When I scored it back, orange wound-light would remain for days.

A persuader passing through would tell me to leave.

“If it has learned the shape of your weight,” she would say, “you are already part of its next answer.”

I would promise to go after one more dimming.

Then after the rain.

Then after the tension sequence moving from the pillar’s lower reaches had completed itself.

The sequence would be extremely slow. One contraction every nine or ten days, never exactly regular. It would travel upward through the pillar and across the narrow air as pressure—not sound, but a subtle displacement that made the rain-leaf lift. I would become convinced it was an answering movement to something beneath the branch.

To abandon it halfway would seem like tearing a sentence before the final knot.

So I would remain.

Eventually I would stop cutting away the white ridges.

This would not feel like surrender. It would feel practical. The new tissue would hold my tension cords better than pegs. It would seal gaps against the cold. I would teach it, as far as anything there could be taught, to divide around the shelter instead of through it.

*If you must remember my weight, remember it here.*

The branch would thicken around my left foot while I slept.

Not close like a mouth. More slowly. Almost courteously.

I could free myself at first. I would not.

A person who watches one place too long becomes a kind of instrument, and instruments require attachment. I would place my palm against a patch of young growth scored with bitter sap. Fine rootlets would enter the old cracks in my skin. Most would die. A few would remain.

After that I would feel certain movements before the cords did.

Cold sweetness passing beneath me.

Pressure gathering far below.

The tightening that precedes a surface opening.

I would still be human. I would still eat dry curd and curse when the water case sealed around its stopper. I would speak aloud, changing the form of every sentence so my voice would not settle. I would crave visitors and resent them when they came. I would keep a small piece of blue salt beneath my tongue on lonely days.

But the boundary would become difficult to state.

My left side would be held within pale scar tissue. Cleaners would live harmlessly at the seam. During brightness, fine green lines would show under the skin of my wrist—not veins, or not only mine. I would know when the rain-leaf began filling without seeing or hearing it. Once, when a visitor cut the branch carelessly, I would taste bitterness before the orange wound appeared.

People would begin calling me the Half-Fastened.

Later, the Branch Mouth.

Later still, perhaps, simply Held Breath, as if I and the place had always been the same inconvenience in the route.

I would send warnings by traveler rather than signal. There could be no drum, no repeated flare. Each message would be carried in a different form: a notched crawler wing, a sour bead of hardened sap, a cord that tightened when warmed. My warnings would not say, **A turning is coming.**

They would say:

**The pillar answered from below.**

**The branch is drawing inward during dimming now.**

**Water rose against weight.**

**Walkers crossed the underside going upward.**

Let others preserve several possibilities.

In time, the long sequence would approach completion.

The pillar would lean near enough that frost grew between us in thick twisting ropes. The gap would fill with a cold haze. Listeners would gather overhead, though no wound had opened. Their threads would descend between branch and pillar and hang there, trembling toward something beneath both.

A migration band would arrive to take me away.

By then removal would require cutting living branch, shelter wall, and parts of me no longer clearly separable from either. They would bring the inherited copper blade. I would refuse it—not from courage, and not because I believed the world had chosen me. The world does not choose at human scale.

I would refuse because someone should remain until the movement declared itself.

The others would argue. They would leave water. They would take copies of my cords. Before departing, a child would press a mineral grain into the tissue beside my hand, where it could not be absorbed.

Then, at some hour of extreme brightness, the branch and pillar would finally touch.

Perhaps they would strike with a report heard for a thousand kilometers.

Perhaps they would meet without sound.

Their white outer layers would split and curl back. Orange wound-lines would face one another across the last finger-width of air. Filaments would cross. Some would recoil. Some would knot together. Fluids from growths that had remained separate longer than any history would mingle and blacken.

Through the joined structure would pass the completion of the sequence I had spent half a life hearing.

And I would understand nothing.

Not a message. Not an intention. Only scale: the realization that what I had taken for a sequence was one edge of a movement still unfolding elsewhere, one pressure in a body—or congress, or weather—too broad to possess a beginning that belonged to me.

The joining would pull my shelter shut.

Afterward travelers might find no watcher there. Only a dark seam between branch and pillar, a mineral grain caught in the scar, and several tension cords emerging from the growth. Most would be dead. One would still tighten during dimming.

If touched, it would carry a faint irregular pulse.

They might call it sap.

They might call it the remnant of my hand.

A memory-keeper would preserve both accounts.

That, I think, is what I would be in the White Undergreen: human at first, because one must begin somewhere; then a keeper of an interval; finally part of the evidence that the interval once existed.

Not swallowed exactly.

Not surviving exactly.

A small, deliberate hesitation in the place where two enormous lives learned to close.
