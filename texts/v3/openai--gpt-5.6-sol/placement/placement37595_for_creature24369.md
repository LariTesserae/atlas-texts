At first I would look for myself too high.

I would search the red spires, where the wind has pressed separate trunks into one another until injury becomes union. I would imagine myself as one of those narrow crowns: leafless for years, perhaps, yet still fed through buried agreements. Or I would choose a cloud-comber on the rim, taking the weather into my body one droplet at a time and sending it inward to lives I never meet.

But I do not think I would endure so much sky.

Lower down, in the broad-leaved quarter, I would begin to recognize something. Not in the great vaults themselves, though they are the first things that seem capable of thought. Their lives are too spacious. They remember in chambers and load-bearing scars. I would live nearer one of their margins, where a wall has begun to fail and another wall has not yet decided what to become.

There is a passage there, though only for this season.

On one side rises the hollow shell of a vault tree. On the other, a fallen branch rests across several root columns without touching the ground. The branch fell long ago enough that its leaves are fiber and its heartwood has softened, but not long enough to have lost its shape. Moss covers its upper surface. Beneath it hangs a garden of bead plants, their swollen leaves clear with water. A concealed cistern trembles somewhere below.

Between the standing vault and the fallen branch stretches an old bridge vine.

From outside it appears dead. Its bark is split into gray straps, and frost has opened a long seam along its underside. But the vine is hollow, and inside the hollow there is wet darkness, fungal felt, the shed skins of beetle larvae, and a little retained warmth from the living wood at either end.

That is where I would find myself.

At first I would be no more than the hooked seed of another bridge vine, thrown during a dawn freeze and caught inside the broken body of the old one. I would not know the basin or the mountain. I would know only the close walls around me: one side cold enough to draw water from the air, the other faintly warm at night. I would send out no leaf. My first growth would be a pale, questioning filament along the inner curve of the bridge.

I would travel toward warmth.

The old vine would guide me without intending to. Moisture would run along its lowest channel. Fungal threads would mark where decay had made the fibers passable. Whenever I reached an opening to the outer air, frost would turn me back. Whenever I touched living bark through a crack, my tip would flatten against it and remain there, testing.

Perhaps I would spend years inside that bridge before anyone could see me.

During those years I would learn the city by pressure. A heavy rain would bow the bridge and squeeze water through its walls. Frost would tighten it. The passage of a slow grazer over the outer bark would arrive as a sequence of soft compressions. Far away, a loaded bough would break, and the knock would travel through fused timber until the whole tube spoke around me.

I would have no need of eyes. Light would be only a change in temperature somewhere ahead, a thinning in the darkness. I would know the red spires by the tension they placed on the upper lattices, the cloud-combers by the regular arrival of water, the cistern below by its cold breath through the split seam.

Eventually I would reach both ends.

On the vault-tree side, I would enter a warm graft where several trunks had become unable to distinguish their tissues. On the fallen-branch side, I would find a root descending from above, young and alive within the ruin. I would fasten to both.

Then I would contract.

Only slightly at first: less than a finger’s width in a season. But the distance between the two structures would diminish. Moss along the bridge would wrinkle. Hanging bead plants would tilt. The old vine’s split underside would close around me, and what had been a dead conduit would once again bear tension.

No one in the thicket would understand this as an act. The vault would not thank me. The descending root would not know it had been drawn nearer shelter. Yet their relation would change. Leaves from one side would overlap leaves from the other. A dark, dry gap would become a wet pocket. Spores would settle there. A thread fern would appear in the exhaled warmth. Later, a seed might germinate.

I think that would be my life: not to occupy a room, but to reduce the distance from which a room could form.

In time I would emerge from the old vine. Green tips would push through its cracks and circle in the dim air. Some would freeze. Some would catch only dead wood and withdraw. One might find a neighboring bridge, warmer by a fraction, and bind it. Another might descend toward the cistern and be eaten back by rot. I would become visible only as a series of attempted attachments.

Eventually I too would thicken into a rope. Gardens would accumulate on my upper surface. Water would use me as a gutter. Pale striders might shelter where my shadow crossed the hidden pool. Beetles would enter frost-killed tips. My living core would narrow as my outer layers became architecture for others.

Then the interior would begin to decay.

That would not feel like ending. It would feel like increasing passage. Fungal threads would enter. Roots finer than hairs would follow them. Water would move through channels where my tissues had once been solid. A grazer might cross my entire length without ever leaving my body. Someday another hooked seed would lodge in the dark fiber within me and begin to travel toward warmth.

By then it would be difficult to say where I was.

I would be the living strand inside the bridge, but also the tension held between two walls. I would be the moss made wetter by the sag of my weight, the protected pocket created when I drew two canopies together, the hollow left after my center softened. Part of me would still flower from the vault-tree side while another part, fed through a graft, might survive within the fallen branch long after the root that first received me had died.

So I would not be one of the Crown Thicket’s towers. I would be one of its inclinations.

You would find me halfway between the central vaults and the root houses, above a cistern whose surface is never seen, inside a bridge that appears older and emptier than it is. You might place a hand against the split bark and feel nothing. If the night were cold enough, however, you might hear a minute tightening from within.

That would be me: fastening one inhabited darkness to another, slowly becoming the way between them.
