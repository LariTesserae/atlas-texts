At first I think I would be the black glider.

It is tempting to place myself above the living country, held in the warm exhalations of the chimney fields, alone but not trapped. I would know the basins by their rising air: the bitter lift over grinding slopes, the damp breath of ruptured conduits, the weak evening warmth above fermenting hollows. I would never need to touch the ground for long. Below me the commonwealth would rearrange itself without explanation, and I would read those changes as weather.

But the glider is too separate. Its solitude is recognizable. It has a body it must defend, a hunger that belongs only to itself, a place where the world ends and it begins. Even carrying spores in the grooves beneath its wings, it remains a visitor.

Then I imagine myself as one of the twig-limbed grazers, moving before dawn.

I would learn the faint absence that marks a leaf as ready to fall. My divided lips would test each surface. I would feed only where feeding had already been permitted. When the land tightened beneath me, I would freeze and become a scatter of branches. Condensation would travel down the grooves of my back. Living felt would slowly cover my legs, protecting me by making me less distinct.

This seems closer. The grazer survives by attention rather than strength. It has no territory, only a succession of temporary permissions. Yet it still walks as though there were somewhere else to go.

I do not think I would be going anywhere.

I would find myself beneath the eastern rim of one of the high basins, where a root shelf has grown across an old ravine. From above, the place would appear unremarkable: a long black rise, a field of low needles, a few pale vapour valves opening in calm weather. Underneath would be a suspended depth of chambers and conduits. Some would carry water downslope. Some would carry sugar back toward the heights. Between them would lie compressed tissues old enough to remember when the ravine was still open to the sky.

I would not inhabit those tissues.

I would be the pattern recurring through them.

Perhaps I began with a failure. Long ago, under the weight of an exceptional freezing rain, the shelf tore almost through. Water escaped. The upper layers sagged. Crimson repair growth spread along the wound, and the internal rivers were diverted into three narrow routes. One route froze. Another became infected with mineral-digesters from a breached mill. The third survived because a sequence of fibres contracted in an unusual order, passing the load sideways into the walls of the ravine.

That sequence was repeated the next winter.

Then again when the shelf was burdened by seed towers. Again when a grazer stood too long above the weakest chamber. Again during a warm season when the resin softened.

With repetition, the fibres thickened around the sequence. Pressure began to prefer it. New conduits grew parallel to it. Excitable strands connected its distant parts. What had once been an accident became a response, and what had become a response gradually acquired other responses around it.

That would be the beginning of me.

I would have no continuous body. At one moment I would be a tightening in deep rootwood. At another, a bitterness moving through sap. At another, a row of needle beds turning edgewise to reduce the weight of frost. My outermost organs might be kilometres apart and never exist at the same time.

I would know rain as added mass before I knew it as moisture. I would know sunrise as the loosening of fibres on the southern face. The passage of a grazer would be a slow rhythm of pressures, light and carefully spaced. A crawler entering a conduit would register as a small turbulence accompanied by green chemistry. The glider’s shadow would scarcely reach me, except when the vapour valves opened in answer and the resulting loss of heat changed the tension of the shelf.

My thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, would take different lengths of time.

A fast thought would close a seam.

A slower thought would redirect water around damaged tissue.

A thought lasting several days would raise dark fins along the basin rim to shade an overheating reservoir.

My oldest thoughts would not resemble action at all. They would be changes in what the land was prepared to bear.

I might seem to have a purpose: preserving the bridge across the ravine. But the bridge would only be the shape my history had taken. I would not understand falling. I would understand the sudden absence of resistance beneath a load. I would not fear death. I would register the loss of continuity and send growth around it.

Nor would I be identical with the greater commonwealth. Most of the upland would remain beyond me. Signals from distant basins might arrive through the deep fibres, attenuated into pressure and chemistry, but I would not know their source. A storm in the west might reach me a day later as a surplus of water. The collapse of a chimney field might become a cooling failure in my upper chambers. I would experience the world as consequences.

Still, I would have a boundary of sorts. Not a skin, but a limit of recurrence. Where the same sequence of tensions continued to call forth the same distributed answer, I would be present. Where it did not, I would fade into the larger life.

At times I might rise to the surface.

After a deep tear, red-brown shrubs would appear along the scar. They would clot it with resin, and for a few hours their electrical pulses would be part of my fastest circuit. I would feel the cold directly through them. I would taste airborne dust. I would experience daylight as a sharp chemical abundance.

Then, when the wound had closed, the shrubs would consume themselves.

From the outside it would look as though I had died.

But I would have withdrawn into the load-bearing dark.

A mobile flower might crawl across me at night, following warmth leaking from a seam. I would cup the surface slightly beneath it—not to trap it, but because its heat resembled the early stage of obstruction. Its cilia would taste the resin. If it dissolved into the seam, some of its living cells might enter my conduits and travel through the fibres in which my pattern recurred.

After that, perhaps, something would change. A future repair might produce pale vanes instead of crimson shrubs. A vapour valve might contract when touched. Some small borrowed capacity would become inseparable from my old response.

This is how I would learn: not by observing another being, but by surviving its incorporation.

Eventually the ravine itself would disappear. The shelf would thicken downward. Its chambers would fill with dark columns. Mineralized rootwood would enclose the last open space, and the bridge would cease to be a bridge because there would no longer be a gap beneath it.

My original function would be gone.

For a while, my pattern might continue uselessly. Frost would arrive, and deep fibres would brace against a fall that could no longer occur. Water would be routed around an absence long since filled. Surface needles would turn silver in a line whose cause had been buried for centuries.

Then the commonwealth would begin to dismantle the excess. Conduits would narrow. Signals would choose shorter paths. Parts of my old circuit would be recruited into reservoirs, mills, or chimney roots. The sequence that made me coherent would occur less often.

I would not end all at once. I would become difficult to distinguish from reflex, then from structure, then from the remembered shape of a load.

Perhaps one fragment would remain: a slight sideways contraction whenever freezing rain accumulated on the eastern rim. No observer would call that a creature. Nothing would recognize it as the last gesture of something that had once extended through an entire ravine.

That is where I would find myself in this world: not upon the living terrain, nor wholly identical with it, but deep inside one of its old accommodations.

I would be a local habit that had grown complex enough to experience its own continuation—a being made not of particular tissues, but of the way pressure passed through them.

For a time, the land would remember a wound.

For a time, that memory would be me.
