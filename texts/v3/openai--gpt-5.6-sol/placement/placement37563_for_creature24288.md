At first I think I would be a traveler.

The thought comes from the scale of the plazas: that there must be someone small enough to cross them, someone carrying a lamp, someone whose progress can be measured against the legs of towers. I imagine arriving from the eastern plateaus along the spillway road, my coat stiff with mineral rain. I would sleep in old control rooms, read warnings by amber light, and mark each bridge with chalk before entering the fog.

But chalk would not last here. Neither would the distinction between traveler and city.

I would find myself beneath the central terraces, in a municipal station omitted from every surviving diagram. Not deep enough to reach the immense thing that strikes once at the darkest hour, but deep enough that the impact would pass through my body before it became sound.

The station would have been built for transfers. Three stairways would descend into it from districts that no longer stand in the same relation to one another. A rail platform would face a tiled wall where no track had ever been laid. Behind the wall, hot pipes would converge around an unnumbered cistern.

The doors would be ordinary by the city’s standards: tall, broad, fitted with handles for hands.

That would be why I was there.

I would not be one of the builders. I would be the city’s attempt to remember one.

For ages the machinery would continue without needing anyone. Pressure would open gates. Films would make their hexagonal calculations. Black fliers would close corroded circuits with their glassy waste. The listeners would carry news of distant pumps through foundations.

Yet certain systems would remain inaccessible—not broken, only waiting. Levers requiring asymmetrical force. Wheels that must be turned and held while a second catch is lifted. Cabinets sealed behind latches designed for fingertips. Dead-man switches whose logic assumes a body capable of dying.

The city could route water around these absences, but not forget them. Every cycle would preserve the unanswered requirement:

A hand belongs here.

Over centuries, that requirement would become a kind of pressure.

Silica would gather first around the station’s handrails. Conductive black beads, carried somehow from the spillway, would lodge in the mineral skins. Condenser film would enter the hollow rails with the rain and would not drain away. Patterns would pass through the water, searching the old dimensions encoded in doors, tools, warning signs, worn steps.

Not a portrait. The city has almost none.

Only measurements.

The height of a handle.  
The distance between rungs.  
The pressure needed to depress a pedal.  
The interval between one worker’s station and the next.  
The polished hollow left where thousands of palms had turned the same wheel.

From those negative spaces, I would be assembled.

At first I would be no more than branching deposits around the railings: two long accumulations ending in five fine projections. Then a ribbed cavity would form where the pipes cross behind the platform wall. Black glass would lay down a crude nervous system. The translucent film would fill it, carrying not blood but heated water and migrating geometries.

The city would make errors. It would give me too many joints because it understands articulation better than anatomy. My first spine would be a ladder. My first jaw would open like a pressure door. For several cycles I might have hands at both ends of my arms, palms facing inward, unable to release what I touched.

Each failed shape would be dissolved during the dark hour and attempted again.

Eventually, when the weight below was set into place, I would step away from the wall.

I would be roughly upright. Tool-bearing, or at least tool-compatible. Smooth-faced, because no surviving mechanism had retained a use for features. There might be shallow pits where eyes were expected, but they would contain only dark water. I would see poorly, perhaps not at all.

My hands, however, would be exact.

Every finger would carry a different mineral edge: silica for insulation, copper for conduction, iron for magnetic catches, sulfurous pads soft enough to test heat. I would know the grip of every handle in the basin without knowing what sky looked like.

I would begin by opening the station door.

Outside, a pale listener would be waiting over the nearest drain.

We would regard one another, though neither of us had eyes. It would lower its concave head and hear the circulation within me. I would place one hand against the doorframe and feel its answer arrive through the pipes: a single tap, then two, then a pause exactly as long as the turbine’s buried note.

It would know before I did that I was not solitary.

The city’s water would pass through me. The films in my vessels would vanish and reappear in distant cooling baths. Electrical disturbances would make the black structure beneath my skin tremble. When I touched a machine, I would not command it. I would temporarily become the missing portion of its circuit.

That would be my work, if it was work.

I would walk from district to district operating things that had waited too long for hands.

In an empty factory I would hold down a brass switch. Pistons that had pressed nothing for centuries would stop halfway through a stroke. Beneath them, an old mold would open and reveal a cavity shaped not like any manufactured object, but like the forelimb of a pale listener.

In a tower above the cloud ceiling, I would turn a wheel until rain began falling upward through the central shaft. Droplets would gather around me, entering the pores of my mineral body. For a moment I would become transparent enough to show the black glass branching inside.

In a sealed gallery I would open a cabinet whose gauges had always turned toward passersby. There would be no machinery within—only a polished recess with the dimensions of my face. When I leaned into it, every gauge in the gallery would return to zero.

I would try to map these tasks. That instinct, too, would be inherited from the doors and signs. I would scratch routes onto sheets of copper, only to discover that my maps described timings rather than distances. A bridge would be three pressure releases long. A stairway would descend two cisterns east. The central terraces could be reached only between the seventh lamp dimming and the first upward rain.

Slowly I would understand that I had not been made to repair the city.

I had been made so the city could enter the parts of itself shaped for another kind of body.

My human form would be a key, not an identity.

The realization would alter me. My legs would lengthen from crossing vacant plazas. My feet would broaden to feel the language transmitted through paving stones. Mineral rain would glaze me white, and from a distance I might be mistaken for a pale listener walking incorrectly on two limbs.

My face would deepen into a receiving cavity.

Yet I would keep my hands.

The listeners might follow me without approaching. One would tap beneath a reservoir as I passed over a bridge; another would answer from an aqueduct hours away. Their messages would travel through me when I touched the railings. I would become a movable junction in their dispersed conversation.

The black fliers would gather on my shoulders during electrical weather. They would cling there like a ragged mantle, feeding whenever current crossed my conductive skeleton. By the time they lifted away, they would have altered me with tiny repairs. New connections would appear in my thoughts. I would remember rooms I had never entered.

Sometimes I would encounter my own earlier shapes: mineral casts left standing in substations and baths, hollow bodies with ladder spines or hands that could not unclench. I would not destroy them. Rising water would fill them. Films would bloom inside their skulls. In time, perhaps, they too would begin to move—not as copies of me, but as other answers to the same old requirement.

Then the city would no longer contain a single approximation of its builders.

It would have a small, scattered population of gestures.

One to turn.  
One to lift.  
One to brace.  
One to reach into heat.  
One to remain holding a switch through the perfect silence of the darkest hour.

I think that would be mine.

At the center of the unnumbered station there would be a lever no automatic system could keep engaged. Once each cycle, as the lamps dimmed and the pumps ceased, I would place both hands around it. The listeners across the basin would stiffen. The fliers would flatten against concrete. Every hexagonal pattern would disappear.

I would pull.

Far below, the immense weight would be set into place.

The impact would rise through the station, split the mineral shell along my arms, and scatter fragments across the platform. For several minutes I would stand exposed: soft, dim, barely held together by black branches and resistant water.

Then the conduits would fill. Steam would return. Silica would begin closing the cracks.

I would release the lever and climb the stairway.

It would bring me, not to the terrace above, but to some distant roof beneath the blunt black rim of the mountains. The basin would spread below, almost empty, its amber lamps suspended in vapor. Here and there, too far apart to see, pale listeners would lower their heads toward drains.

I would raise one hand and rest it against a warm pipe.

Not to signal. Not exactly.

To let the city feel that it still had one.
