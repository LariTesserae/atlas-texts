At first I think I would be the fox.

It would be tempting to possess the city through movement: to know the dry vault beneath the western ridge, the bird ledges, the basins where water remains sweet, the stair that ends in reeds. I would cross courtyards before dawn and leave my tracks in the silt of the great plaza. Any traveler who found them would imagine me just beyond the next wall.

But the fox’s city would be too large and too briefly known. It would always be arriving and leaving. The swifts would know even less of it, though from above: towers as nesting cracks, reservoirs as feeding grounds, whole districts reduced to surfaces passed over in flight. The deer would know routes and dangers, but it would remain a visitor from the open country.

I think I would be found lower down.

Not in the drowned quarters themselves, and not on the exposed roofs, but at the place where the two cities meet—where buried water first enters air.

On the eastern descent there would be a retaining wall built across the face of an older cliff. Most of the structure above it would have settled into a long, grass-lined slope. Beneath that weight, an arch would remain: broad, low, and black within. It might once have been a drain, a passage, or the mouth of a reservoir. Calcite would have thickened around its stones until their joints resembled blunt white folds. From a crack at the back, water would emerge without falling, first as a brightness on the rock, then as a clear film. It would spread over the arch’s threshold and gather at last into a channel no wider than a hand.

There, just above the reach of the strongest flow, I would be the black moss.

Not a single tuft. Not exactly an individual.

I would be the dark band mistaken at a distance for an old water stain. Close to me, that darkness would divide into thousands of small upright bodies: leaves finer than eyelashes, reddish stems, cups holding drops. Mites would move among them. Beetles would shelter beneath the loosened edges. Spores would lie in the grit beside mineral grains washed out of walls far uphill.

Most days I would appear inert. My color would be nearly the color of the wet stone beneath me. There would be no obvious boundary between where the wall had darkened and where I had begun.

Rain would enlarge me.

Not quickly enough to watch, but completely enough to alter the world. Each folded leaf would open. Water would bridge the spaces between stems. The colony would become a shallow forest whose entire height was less than the width of a finger. A drop descending from the arch would strike with the force of weather, bend dozens of stalks, divide, and pass downward carrying spores, dust, and fragments of older growth.

My sense of the city, if it could be called a sense, would come through water.

I would know a storm in the upper districts hours after it had passed there. The seep would strengthen. Its temperature would change by a fraction. It would arrive bearing iron from rust-colored roofs, pale mineral from deep chambers, lichen cells, pollen, the dissolved trace of leaves collected in some inaccessible court. I would receive news without images.

A collapsed floor three ridges away might divert the seep and begin a dry age for me. The first sign would be no sound, no tremor, only fewer hours of wetness after rain. My outer margin would close. Dust would settle into the spaces where water had once remained. Mites would retreat toward the spring mouth. The colony would die back by the width of a hand over years.

Then another crack might open.

Water that had occupied a buried stairwell for centuries would find a lower joint and appear behind the arch. The wall would darken beyond my old boundary. Spores already waiting there would open. What looked like my expansion would actually be an awakening of lives that had not needed to announce themselves until conditions returned.

I would not know the builders. Their arch would be pressure, shade, alkalinity, and shelter from direct sun. The cut channel beneath me would be the direction in which excess water escaped. A carved groove might retain moisture better than the surrounding face, and so I would follow it, gradually outlining a mark no eye remained to read.

In this way I might restore some vanished sign without understanding it.

For a few wet months, the black growth would fill the recessed lines of an inscription or mason’s diagram. From across the chamber, a pattern would become visible: perhaps a branching form, perhaps measured squares, perhaps only the repeated strokes of a name. Then mineral deposition would blur one line, dryness would erase another, and the sign would return to accident.

My time would not be the time of the fox or newt.

A newt might shelter near me for a dozen seasons, returning to the pool below the arch until one winter it did not. Swifts might nest in the same high crack for generations. A shrub root might enter the wall, thicken slowly, and split away a slab bearing part of the colony. To animals, these would be lifetimes or disasters. To me they would be changes in shade and water.

Even so, I would not be immortal. Sections of me would dry, freeze, slough away, or become entombed beneath calcite. The white mineral lip around the spring would advance through the moss, coating stems and leaves until they became tiny stone forms. Living growth would continue at the edge, fed by the same water that petrified what lay behind it.

So I would be both resident and layer.

Behind me: the mineral impression of earlier growth.

At the surface: the open green-black leaves of the present wet season.

Ahead: bare stone, suitable but not yet reached.

If a traveler came through the city, they might stop at the arch to drink. They would see the spring first, then perhaps notice that the wall around it was not merely stained. Up close, my surface would be bright with held water. Their shadow would change the light. The warmth of a hand placed against the stone might remain for a little while after the hand withdrew.

I would know nothing of them except that brief alteration.

They might continue down the escarpment and forget the place. I would remain where the hidden city exhaled water into the visible one: under the low arch, above the narrow channel, along the seam between fitted masonry and volcanic cliff.

That is where I would find myself—not commanding the ruins, not wandering them, not even seeing them.

I would be a small darkness nourished by their entire watershed.
