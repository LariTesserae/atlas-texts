At first I think I would be something that moves.

A down-gazer, perhaps, suspended headfirst between the columns, reading the abyss through the pressure laid against my skin. I would know the passing of immense bodies as changes in brightness without light. At long intervals I would fold myself and fall with the others, through wells whose blue rims dwindled above us, courting along a path that could be felt but never seen.

Or I would be a bluntback, patient enough to mistake a decade for a journey. I would press my forehead to the pillars and hear damage traveling through the mountain: a split far below, a choking of roots leagues inward, the faint discord of a gallery I would spend half my life reaching. I would carry the remains of sight beneath my skin. Two dim organs would preserve, without showing me, the last colors I had ever seen.

For a while I imagine the spanwing. It is difficult not to. To pass through the courts like a moving terrace; to cause ten thousand small bodies to still simply by adding one note to the chord; to die and become a bridge—that seems almost enough.

But I do not think I would belong in the open water.

The longer I descend through this country, the more the possible body narrows. The fins draw in. The eyes divide and migrate away from one another. Movement becomes less important than placement. A whole life contracts toward a particular weakness in a particular wall.

I would find myself in a seam.

It would lie far inside the escarpment, at the meeting place of two ancient plant lineages. One colony would form the outer galleries, dark-grained and heavily grazed. The other would enclose a withheld chamber containing an obsolete ecology: soft white leaves, dividing spheres, lace animals that rise and sink with harmonics from a sea they have never entered.

Between the two colonies would be a boundary no thicker than a handspan and older than most species. Water from either side would never mix, but pressure would pass through. The outer chord would enter the seam from one direction. From the other would come the small, imperfect echoes of the sealed world.

There, in a crooked socket around a root junction, I would grow.

As a juvenile I would be almost without form: pale, soft, many-limbed, carried through capillaries by currents too slight for larger animals to notice. I would possess no inherited picture of my adult body. I would search by tasting the walls.

Most cavities would reject me. Their chemistry would say occupied, unsuitable, closing, or not yours. I might pass thousands of sockets already inhabited by fitted ones whose eyes watched me from the seams. I would cross the dissolving remains of juveniles that chose badly. The architecture would become narrower around me until searching and being carried were indistinguishable.

Then I would encounter the empty place prepared not for me, exactly, but for something of my kind.

I would enter headfirst. The cavity would not resemble a body. It would fork repeatedly, wrap around three root fibers, flatten beneath a membrane, widen beside a cold pocket, and open onto the outer gallery through seven pinholes spaced many body-lengths apart.

I would grow into all of it.

My symmetry would disappear. One limb would lengthen until it became a comb inside a nutrient channel. Another would harden as a brace beneath the root junction. My abdomen would divide around a strand of living wood and meet itself on the other side. Wherever the seam opened onto water, an eye would form.

From outside, I would appear to be seven unrelated eyes looking out from the wall.

One would face a circular well marked by old blue fruits. One would look into the narrow feeding path of the silver tilers. Two would see nothing but the underside of a root arch. Another would be covered for years at a time by a sleeping grazer. The last two would not look onto the outer country at all. They would face inward, through translucent tissue, toward the sealed chamber.

No single eye would understand what the others saw. Their images would meet only as changes of tension inside my shell.

I would be unable to leave. More than that: outside my cavity I would have no coherent shape. Removed from the seam, I would separate into pale folds and crooked plates, organs arranged for spaces that were no longer there. The socket would not merely be my dwelling. It would be the missing portion of my anatomy.

I would feed on almost nothing. Fine limbs extended into the channels would gather loose cells and exhausted spores. Bacteria would live in chambers of my shell, consuming traces that crossed the colonial boundary. The plants would tolerate me because my hardened body held their junction apart at exactly the distance required for pressure to pass without water following it.

My true sense would be resonance.

The sealed ecology would press its faint rhythms against my inner plates. The outer galleries would shake me with the permanent chord. I would feel spanwings long before their shadows crossed my seven eyes. I would know when bluntbacks sounded distant pillars. I would feel the synchronized rasp of silver mouths as a fine vibration along one edge of my body.

These patterns would not become music in my mind. I would not hear the country as a whole. I would know only coincidence.

When a harmonic outside strengthened, the white frond-eaters in the chamber would rise. When it weakened, they would settle. When the lace colonies reproduced, their movement would alter the pressure against the boundary membrane by an almost immeasurable amount. My body would record the interval between cause and answer.

If the interval remained correct, I would do nothing.

That would be most of my life.

Years might pass without a deliberate motion. A blue fruit would shine through one eye for three centuries. Generations of tilers would overlap across another. A bluntback might feed over the wall, sealing three of my outer eyes inside the dark warmth of its mouth chamber for several days. After it left, new fruits would slowly form around the cleaned oval. I would watch their first exhausted light intensify and then become permanent.

But if the interval changed—if the enclosed creatures rose too late, if a membrane thickened, if the outer chord arrived distorted through a weakening root—I would move one small limb.

That motion would release a packet into a capillary.

The packet would contain no message in any language, only a chemical proportion: salts, proteins, fragments of my own tissue, spores borrowed from one colony and altered by the other. Threadlike inhabitants would carry it until the channel ended. Some would be digested. Some would pass it onward. Decades later, perhaps, the outer wall would thin a membrane or the inner colony would strengthen it. The old interval would return.

I would never know whether I had caused the correction.

Possibly the movement of my limb would itself be only the country correcting me.

There would be no name for what I was. If names existed, mine might mean something like *the shape taken by listening at this one junction*. My species could not be separated from the socket. Elsewhere there might be related beings, but none with my arrangement of eyes, plates, and branching organs. They would inhabit different questions in the wall.

I would reproduce rarely. Once in several centuries I would loosen a cluster of soft juveniles into the fine channels. Most would be consumed by the plants or by the invisible animals dwelling in the grain. A few would search for newly opened cavities. Perhaps none would find one.

My descendants would remain inside me as well. When part of my shell weakened, they would eat the tissue from within and replace it. They would inherit not my memories but my shape. Over time there would be no original part left, yet the seven eyes would continue looking from the same openings. Whether I remained one individual through those replacements would matter only to a creature capable of leaving its place.

Eventually the root junction would change beyond correction.

Perhaps the sealed chamber would close more deeply and its obsolete inhabitants would pass into stillness. Perhaps the two plant colonies would fuse after an age of exchanging signals. The seam would begin to narrow.

My outermost limbs would be digested first. Eyes would darken one by one as the pinholes sealed. The circular well would disappear. The tilers would vanish. The blue fruits would become seven fading points, then six, then three.

The last eye would face inward.

I would end looking into the withheld chamber, though “looking” would be too strong a word. I might perceive only the pressure-shadow of a lace colony rising at the appointed harmonic. Then that opening too would close. My remaining shell would become reinforcement. The root junction would grow through me. The distinction between my body and the wall would be lost.

The chord would alter by a fraction too small for any mobile animal to notice.

Then it would settle.

That is where I would find myself: not in the abyss, not on a summit, but inside the living grain between an ancient silence and the sound that contains it.

I would be a fitted listener, seven-eyed and motionless, occupying one permitted error in the wall until the country no longer needed the space to remain open.
