At first I think I would live in the Condenser Ward.

There would be reasons to choose it: warmth leaking through brick, black mushrooms under stairs, orchards shouldering their way across roofs. I imagine a narrow apartment behind a cooling tower, its windows filmed with mineral vapor. I would prune pale fruit by lanternlight and keep bellbacks in a cracked washbasin. Their scattered notes would wake me before rain remembered to arrive.

But that is too inhabited a life. Too lush. The Ward would hold me easily, and I do not think the Escarpment would.

Then I think I might be a lift operator, suspended for decades among incompatible altitudes. I like the small room of levers, the duskglass brake cool under one hand, blind fish turning in a bucket at my feet. I would never tell passengers whether we were ascending or descending. I would say, “We are approaching,” and let that be enough.

But even the lift has destinations.

I would be farther out.

Past the last regular tram stop, past the grave houses, where the municipal lamps become infrequent and the smokestacks seem less like buildings than marks made on the weather. There is a district not yet assigned a proper name. On official plans it is crosshatched and labeled **PENDING DRAINAGE**. Shepherds call it the Dry Reach. Children call it Almost River.

A white-painted seam runs through the basalt there. It begins as a thread beneath a disused switchyard, passes under three storage sheds mounted on rails, and continues toward the escarpment. If you put your ear to it, you hear water.

Not metaphorically. Not merely the blood-noise of your own body against stone. There are currents inside: undertows, shingle rolling, the hollow percussion of water under shelves of rock. On some days gull-like cries can be heard, though no such birds live at this altitude. Once, after a distant forge hammer struck, something large splashed below the unbroken surface.

A bridge stands over the seam.

It is an old iron bridge, though the gorge it crosses may not open for another century. Its span rises no more than a hand’s breadth above solid ground. Lichen grows on the underside. The lamps along its rail have never been lit together, because excessive illumination might encourage the crossing to become detached from its banks.

Beside it is a tollhouse.

That is where I would find myself.

My title would be **Custodian of Prospective Crossing Fourteen**, although no toll has ever been collected and the number thirteen cannot be found in the department’s records. My appointment would be municipal, permanent, and subject to revision. Every spring I would receive a packet containing hinge diagrams, canyon estimates, fish ladders, evacuation notices, and a timetable for a ferry service not expected to begin during my lifetime.

My duties would be simple.

Each morning I would walk the bridge from east to west, then from the side that is neither. I would strike the rail with a padded hammer and write down the note. I would measure the seam at seven brass studs sunk into the rock. I would repaint the white line where weather had not yet worn it away. I would grease the lifting mechanism and turn its enormous wheel one tooth, ensuring that when the valley finally opens, the bridge will remember how to move.

I would also inventory wet things.

They would arrive before the river: a reed caught against a stone, a fish scale on the steps, a length of green rope stiff with salt, coins from lowland towns that have not been founded. Once or twice a year I might find a drowned glove, dripping in dry weather. These objects would be tagged, dated, and stored in shallow drawers in the tollhouse.

The tags would read:

**RECOVERED IN ADVANCE OF WATER.**

No one would know whether the objects had risen from the unborn river or fallen backward from its future banks. My reports would leave that question blank. There would be a box for it, but I would not mark it.

I would not live alone, exactly.

Cliff hares would nest beneath the bridge. Their gripping feet would flower soundlessly against the iron girders. I would feed them bitter lichen and deny doing so when inspectors came. If they screamed, I would ring the alarm bell, even if the cable they warned of had not yet been installed.

A small herd of chimney stags would pass every ninth evening, following a road buried before the tollhouse was built. At the seam they would stop. Their hollow antlers would resonate with a chord too low to hear through the air, but I would feel it in my teeth. Then they would turn their heads toward the solid ground below the bridge, as if watching black water move beneath it.

There would be one bellback in the cistern. Only one. It would ring without being struck whenever the river dreamed of rain.

People would visit me for practical reasons. Surveyors would bring conflicting altitudes. Engineers would inspect the abutments and complain that the western bank did not exist to specification. Schoolchildren would come to hear future erosion. Lovers would carve their initials into the rail, then return years later to see whether the letters had remained together.

Sometimes an anchor would be brought out in a tram with padded seats. The old person would sit beside the seam all afternoon, wrapped in blankets, and remember the gorge.

They would describe trees growing down its walls, ferries moving under the bridge, houses with blue doors on the opposite bank. None of these things would yet exist. The clerk accompanying them would take careful notes.

“Are these memories or projections?” I would ask.

The clerk would look offended.

“That distinction is not within our department.”

My own past would be uncertain in a quieter way.

I would have been born somewhere in the crowded factory districts, perhaps. I would remember amber lamps, a mother’s weighted boots by the stove, the smell of hot oil in wool. I would remember being carried to the horizon and shown the edge.

But my name would not originally have been mine.

Years before my appointment, a name would migrate from a marker in one of the low stone grave houses. It would appear letter by letter on my employment papers, displacing the name printed there. The dead person had been the first custodian of Crossing Fourteen, appointed before the bridge was built. They had vanished after reporting that a boat was approaching through the rock.

The municipality would call this an orthographic inheritance. Their family would invite me to anniversaries. I would learn the deceased’s preferred songs, repair the roof of their grave house, and inherit a brass key that opened nothing in the present city.

After several years, I would begin to remember details I had only read in their reports.

The smell of tar on a ferry deck.

The weight of a toll coin placed in my palm.

A flood lantern moving below the bridge.

I would not decide whether I was becoming the dead custodian, or whether both of us were incomplete versions of the person the crossing would eventually require. Premature definition is dangerous. I would continue the work.

Over time, I think, the bridge and I would acquire one another’s habits.

My spine would stiffen in cold weather when the iron contracted. Rust would appear beneath my fingernails. The bridge’s western lamp would flicker when I dreamed. If I went into the city for supplies, the empty tollhouse would cough softly and ring my ankle bell from miles away.

People might glimpse a figure leaning over the bridge at night.

Watchmen in abandoned stations already report such figures: dark-coated, motionless, looking down into gorges that have not opened. Perhaps they are the future custodians. Perhaps they are one custodian distributed among all prospective crossings. Perhaps they are merely the posture patience takes when it believes itself unobserved.

I would become one of them gradually.

Not a ghost. Ghosts belong to what has happened.

I would be a **forebearer**: someone made faintly spectral by an event still approaching.

My body would remain ordinary enough. I would drink mushroom broth. I would mend my coat. My knees would hurt in vertical weather. I would curse the tram when it failed to include my station. But some part of me would stand on the far side of the coming ravine, where the city could not yet reach, holding the useless brass key.

From there I would watch my present self grease the bridge mechanism.

From here I would sometimes see a lamp across the unbroken stone.

We would recognize one another but never wave. A gesture that clear might open a gap.

Then, one winter, after perhaps forty years or perhaps four, the seam would widen enough to admit a knife blade. The warehouses would be rolled back on their rails. Engineers would arrive with instruments. Children would press their faces to the crack and hear rapids.

I would make tea for everyone.

The city would not celebrate. Not yet. A crack is not a canyon, and a murmur is not a river. Forms would be filed. Gravity notices posted. The bridge would be raised and lowered once in ceremony, over a darkness no wider than a finger.

But late that night, alone, I would unlock the tollhouse drawer containing the things recovered in advance of water.

The reed would be green.

The fish scale would shine.

The drowned glove would close slowly around my hand.

And below the bridge, deep inside the young basalt, something would knock against the future bank.

I would answer with the padded hammer: one measured strike on the rail.

The sound would travel into the mountain. Perhaps it would arrive there a century later. Perhaps it would be the sound that had begun the river in the first place.

That is where I would be found: at the outermost useful edge of the city, keeping a bridge over stone, tending the civic obligations of water not yet born.

And that is what I would be: not quite a ferryman, not quite an anchor, not quite the person named on my papers.

A witness appointed in advance.

A small, patient official of the approaching world.
