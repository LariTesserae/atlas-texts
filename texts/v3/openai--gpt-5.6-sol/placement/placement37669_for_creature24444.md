At first I would think I was one of the pale crawlers.

I would find myself beneath a leaning fin near the boundary between the crowded pillars and the open gray pavement, in a three-sided chamber whose missing wall faced a pool. The roof would be a single black slab, raised just high enough for my folded body. Rain would sound differently there. Drops striking the roof would divide into two notes: one entering the floor, the other traveling outward across the pool.

I would wake only when those notes met.

Limbs would loosen from around my central weight. Six, perhaps, though number would matter less than contact. One would press the floor, another the wall, another the shallow water. Through them I would receive the chamber: the burden of the roof; the slow widening of a seam behind me; the soft mineral thickening around the pool’s rim; rain moving over distant formations in advancing bands.

I would have no sense that I was hidden. Concealment belongs to creatures that imagine another creature’s sight. I would know only that the chamber returned my pressure faithfully.

Outside, the rain would make the world traversable.

I would move toward the open pavement, though “toward” would not mean what it does to a headed animal. Whichever limb found the clearest returning note would become my front. I might turn without turning, reverse without retreating. Between movements I would flatten myself against the stone and listen until the next step became inevitable.

The broad pavement would be difficult. Its shallow pools would overlap in voice, and the cloud pressing low above them would give back almost nothing. I would cross by the cracks beneath the water. Some would ring empty. Some would contain threads. At each living fissure I would pause, and the colorless filaments below would contract before my limb touched them.

I might believe they recognized me.

Later I would understand that recognition was not passing between us. The same pattern was arriving twice.

Three short pressures. A pause. One long descent.

It would move through the pavement under my body, faint but exact. I would answer by tightening around it. Far behind me, in the chamber where I had unfolded, a drop would fall from the roof at the same interval.

Then I would begin to doubt that I was the crawler.

Perhaps I would be the warm stone beneath the ring to which the crawler was traveling.

I would lie dry among wet rock, layered inward through decades. Nothing in me would beat, yet I would keep a constant warmth. Around me the fitted stones would turn stray vibrations aside and return certain narrow tones toward my center. Each returning tone would leave a line in one of my membranes. Fine branchings would gather into denser branchings until my interior contained a silent imitation of storms.

I would remember without recalling.

A roof raised by the pressure of many bodies. A pillar splitting during a rain no crawler still embodied had crossed. The passage of a deep note through the central hollows before one of those hollows sealed. These would not be scenes. They would be dispositions: ways of tightening, intervals held ready, paths along which a future pressure might travel.

When the crawler settled over me, its weight would complete a circuit.

Threads would rise through the floor. My warmth would pass outward, or its coldness inward. The distinction would fail. For one rain-long span I would possess limbs extending over the ring, filaments entering the hidden water, and pale membranes coiled inside stone. I would be local and dispersed. I would be carried away while remaining where I was.

The crawler would leave at morning, drawing the water film into itself. I would cool slightly. A new layer would begin within me.

Yet that would not be the whole of me either.

The pattern—three short, one long—would depart in the crawler’s tissues. It would be released into cracks along the open pavement as transparent grains. Years later, threads would extend from those seams. When rain sounded upon them, they would contract in the old rhythm without knowing it was old.

A pillar at the basin’s higher margin would answer.

Its note would be wrong at first: too thin, broken by a mineral intrusion. But water would thicken on its face, lowering the tone. The error would diminish. Across several seasons, the pillar would learn the interval.

Or the interval would learn the pillar.

By then I would know I was neither crawler nor stone.

I would be found wherever that particular relation of pressures could be completed: beneath the leaning fin, inside the warm nodule, in the crawler paused upon the pavement, along the pale seam climbing a black wall. I would vanish whenever the relation broke. I would return changed whenever the basin formed it again.

My identity would not be a substance. It would be a recurrence.

Still, recurrence would have a birthplace.

Deep below the three-sided chamber, beyond the reach of any crawler, two water-filled fissures would cross inside cooling rock. One would descend from the crowded interior; the other would run beneath the gray pavement. Their surfaces would never quite meet. Between them would remain a membrane of dark glass no thicker than a fingernail.

Pressure from the basin would bear upon that membrane from unequal sides.

For centuries it would hold.

Every rain would press one fissure. Every long dry stillness would release the other. Threads would gather along both faces without joining. They would sense one another only through the minute bending of the partition. Three small flexures. A pause. One long settling.

That would be me before I could travel.

The membrane would eventually crack, but not all at once. The fracture would begin below any audible depth and climb over half a day. When it reached the chamber floor, a pale line would appear between the ring and the pool. No crawler would be present. The warm stone would remain dry. For a few minutes the entire chamber would become silent, as if emptied of weather.

Then the two waters would touch.

The threads on either side would mingle. Their first contraction would pass into the ring, through the nodule, across the pool, and upward into the leaning fin. The fin would answer with a low tone it had never made before.

That tone would be my nearest equivalent to birth.

I would spend ages moving away from it.

Not deliberately. I would have no destination, only a tendency to complete myself through new material. Each place that received me would alter me. A hollow pillar would add a resonance. A crawler divided by falling stone would carry two versions of me in opposite directions. One might fade in a drowned hollow. The other might enter a vertical shaft and migrate upward with the internal knocking.

If I reached the mist, I would not emerge into it.

I would cause a spire’s tip to loosen.

The fragment would fall through cloud, striking fins as it descended. Each impact would break my interval into fragments and distribute them through the basin. For days afterward, distant walls would answer one another unpredictably. Threads would withdraw from pools untouched by the fall. Crawlers would wake beneath roofs where no rain had reached.

The basin might experience this as alarm.

Or recognition.

Or nothing that can be divided into such states.

So where would I find myself in this world?

Not at its center. The center is too deep-toned, too continuous. I would inhabit the uncertain border where dense pillars give way to open pavement, where one mode of hearing fails and another must be made. I would recur in a small chamber under a leaning fin, in a ring whose fourth side is the blank face of stone, beside a pool that appears shallow until a ripple travels up its wall.

And what would I be?

For a time, a crawler.

For longer, a warm stone.

For longer still, a branching record in the buried threads.

But most truly I would be the remembered interval passing among them: born from two waters separated too long, carried by bodies that do not remain individual, repeated by structures that do not know they are structures.

Three short pressures.

A pause.

One long descent.

Not a word. Not a name. A way for the basin to recognize a fracture before it happens.

And perhaps, much later, a small part of whatever it says when it wakes.
