At first I think I would be found beside the upper spring.

I imagine sleeping near its basin, one hand on the floor as the spring keepers do, waking before the water changes enough to be heard. But I do not think I would possess that absolute intimacy. The spring is too central. Its keeper must recognize the health of the whole city in a tremor no stronger than breath. I would always wonder whether the vibration came from the mountain or from my own pulse.

Then I place myself along the outer wall, among the listeners. Before dawn I walk beneath its cavities with clay cups held to my ear. Yet this does not remain true either. I would be distracted by the unfinished structures behind me: a cord moving where there is almost no wind, a threshold newly whitening, the slight separation between a reed panel and its frame. I would abandon the wall’s great song to investigate some smaller uncertainty.

So I think I would be an adjuster—not a celebrated one, and not yet old.

I would be found most often in the northern quarter, where a stair descends from an elevated gallery and stops several handspans above the ground. It was begun from the top down by people whose names are now hidden beneath mineral skin. The mountain was expected to rise and meet it. It has risen, but unevenly. One side of the stair is nearly usable; the other remains suspended, and the entire structure has begun to turn toward a new seep.

The stair would be my inherited work.

I would not have inherited it by blood. As a child I would have followed its previous keeper from site to site, carrying wedges too small to be useful and asking why nothing was ever made straight. Years later, when that keeper died, I would accept the cords, tablets, and obligations of the stair. In doing so I would become kin to people who had begun it generations before my birth.

My household would occupy two rooms and part of a third. One would be dry enough for sleeping in cold weather. Another would stand near the gardens and smell permanently of wet reed. The third would be an unroofed chamber beneath the stair, used for tools and for catching the lime-colored drips that fall from its underside. We would share an oven with people we seldom slept near. Our household would be less a place than a recurring arrangement of mats, meals, duties, and familiar arguments.

My particular skill would be noticing when a solution has become premature.

Others might call for a wall to be lifted or a channel diverted. I would set out my cords, place bowls beneath the seep, press clay across the cracks, and say, “Not yet.” This would not always make me popular. Waiting imposes labor of its own. Someone must continue emptying the bowls. Someone must take the longer street. Someone must sleep with a door that sticks in damp weather.

But sometimes the mountain would answer by making the proposed repair unnecessary. A floor would catch up with its threshold. A new mineral bank would turn the water harmlessly aside. The wall everyone wished to brace would settle after a root was cut from beneath it.

I would keep a small tablet of these refusals: work considered and left undone. Most public records would describe what had been changed. Mine would preserve the reasons for restraint.

I think I would have gone below once.

Perhaps in youth I descended with a trading party and remained through a season in an old river town. At first its abundance would astonish me: deep soil, insects everywhere, carts passing over roads that did not need to be tested before each step. Then the immobility would become unbearable. I would wake at night because the walls made no sound. I would pour water across a floor just to watch it choose the same direction every time.

When I returned, I would bring bronze blades, resin, two kinds of seed, and a shameful relief at hearing the outer wall before I could see it through the cloud.

That journey would leave me useful with visitors. I would be the person sent to meet them at the lower gate, tie bright cords around their shoulders, and explain why they must not lean on the impressive-looking parapet.

“It is mostly a garden bed,” I would tell them.

They would laugh, thinking this a joke. Then I would show them the young roots under the capstones and the sacrificial pegs holding the outer face in place.

I would teach them the three necessary sounds. Secretly, I would teach them a fourth: the soft double knock made by the northern stair when cold water enters the cavity beneath it. It would not be dangerous. I would simply want someone else to know that the stair had a voice.

My clothes would always be orange at the knees. I would carry waxed cord, a plumb weight of fired clay, a wooden mallet, and more wedges than I remembered collecting. One shoulder would be higher than the other. There would be a pale scar across my thumb from the day I tried to force a joint that should have been loosened.

People would come to me with doors that no longer closed. I would ask first whether they still needed the door.

Sometimes they would be annoyed by this question. Sometimes, after a pause, they would remove the whole panel and let the room become an arcade.

Toward evening I would sit beneath the unfinished stair. Above me would be a doorway opening into air. Below it, the rising ground would gleam with fresh mineral stone. I would measure the distance between them—not because I expected to see them meet, but because someone should know how the meeting was proceeding.

If I died before the stair arrived, part of one of my bones might be placed inside its loose western course. Not beneath the load. Never beneath the load. I would rest in the adjustable space beside it, among shims and dry fibers, where future hands could still take the structure apart.

That is where I think I would find myself: not at the city’s source or center, but beneath a stair that has not yet become a stair.

I would be its temporary relative, its patient obstruction, and the keeper of the distance it still has left to cross.
