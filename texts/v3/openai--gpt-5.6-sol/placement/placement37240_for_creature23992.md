At first I think I would live high on the western side, where the last direct light strikes and every surface becomes briefly impossible to see.

I imagine myself a roof walker: lean from climbing, fingertips split by cold, a hooked pick tucked through my belt. I would know which cornices could bear me by the pitch of strain carried through their rails. I would cross above the alleys while the six-horned grazers moved around me like slow weather. People below would glimpse my soot-darkened eyes, the bright cord at my waist, perhaps take me for someone braver than I was.

But I do not think I would belong to the heights. I would admire those who do. I would invent reasons to climb with them, then descend too quickly.

Perhaps I would be a listener.

I can see myself lying beneath a hide in a passage behind the White Court, one cheek against wet stone. Rodents nest against my ribs for warmth. Through the floor come pressures too low to be sound: water gathering behind a sealed joint, ice tightening around a buried stair, the mountain shifting its weight in sleep. I raise a finger and three households begin to move.

That image comes nearer, but not near enough. Listeners attend to what is already there. I think I would be occupied by what has only just arrived.

I would be found at new doors.

Not every new doorway is discovered dramatically. Sometimes there is no tearing of stone, no shudder. Someone goes to hang a wet cape and notices a lintel behind it. A child following the handrail finds that it continues where yesterday it ended. Two families wake to discover that their darkest cupboards now share a threshold. Once, perhaps, a door appears upright in the middle of a roof, opening not onto either side of itself but into a descending room.

When that happens, someone sends for me.

I would have no grand title. The bowl’s people would call me something practical: **the one who visits first**, or **the pourer-across**. Children would shorten it to a gesture—a flat hand turning edgewise, then opening.

I would arrive carrying the usual hook and cord, but also several black cups nested inside one another. One cup for water from the nearest known channel. One for whatever dripped inside. One to leave empty, to learn whether the offered inside possessed weather of its own.

I would not enter immediately.

First I would touch the lintel with the back of my hand. I would taste the frost at the hinge. I would lay a loose cord across the threshold to see whether it bowed inward or outward in a current too slight for skin. I would release a wall beetle and watch whether it crossed. If no beetle could be found, a little drain rodent might be borrowed, though the rodent would be allowed to refuse.

Then I would pour water over the threshold, because that is law.

The direction it chose would matter, but not as an omen. Omens are often only badly measured slopes.

Only after the water had decided would I go in.

I was not always a visitor of doors. When I was younger, I entered the cisterns beneath the White Court. I had the shoulders for it then. My braids were waxed flat against my neck, and I wore a cord around the chest with knots large enough to read through numb hands.

On my last descent I found a ring.

Or the ring found me. The distinction would become a long dispute.

It rested on one of the smooth terraces below the lowest familiar chamber: pale mineral, irregular on the outside, perfectly round within. I remember that it seemed warm. I remember putting my hand through it because divers are sometimes foolish in the exact way curiosity requires.

Then the current altered.

By the time I was pulled out, the ring had closed around my wrist.

It could not be cut. Copper bent against it. Stone tools lost their edges. Heating made it sweat blue water; freezing made it warm. The eastern-wall keepers compared it with a hundred other objects and found no mate. For three years people tested it as a weight, a brace, a guide for drilling, a measure of eel thickness, even a cup holder. It did none of these things well.

So I continued living with it.

At first it was an inconvenience. Frost gathered beneath it. Lizards licked condensation from its rim. Children asked to put their fingers through it, and I told them no, then caught myself speaking with the sternness of the eastern-wall keepers.

Later I began to notice that the ring tightened before a doorway appeared.

Not painfully. It would merely become present around my wrist in a way it had not been moments earlier. The skin beneath it cooled. Sometimes a faint pressure passed through my hand, like someone on the other side of a wall returning a touch.

That is how I became the first visitor.

I would be neither young nor especially old when you found me. My hair would be going white at the temples, though the back braid remained black with oil. I would have the thickened shoulders of a former diver and the careful feet of someone who no longer trusted one knee. A dull copper bead would still hang in my braid, indicating submerged training, but beside it I would wear a bead of no recognized material: one of the foreign grass fibers wound into a knot by a child.

My household would be difficult to explain.

Officially, I would wake for a warm seep near the southern cisterns with two sisters, their children, an eel keeper who had outlived all known relations, and a silver fox that entered through the oil vent each longest night. But new doors would make temporary kinships for me throughout the bowl. I would drink tea with strangers whose pantry had opened into my stair. I would be downstream from people who lived above me only on certain mornings. There would be houses in which a sleeping platform was always kept ready because their wall had once admitted me and might do so again.

My safest route home would belong to many people.

I would know passages no longer present. A stair of eleven soft steps that appeared during a winter quake and vanished after seven weeks. A blue chamber behind the tannery where sound traveled farther than sight. A door in an animal pen that opened onto a ledge below the cloud layer, though the exterior wall stood nowhere near the edge of the bowl.

I would remember an offered inside where gravity leaned toward the ceiling. We poured water across its threshold, and the water climbed the jamb in a clear sheet. No one entered. We sat outside, drank tea, and watched until the doorway became an ordinary patch of wall at dawn.

I would also remember mistakes.

Once I would enter too quickly because a child was believed missing. The room beyond would be narrow, bright, and ankle-deep in water. I would take six steps before understanding that what appeared to be the far wall was reflected sky. My return cord would pull not backward but down.

The child, meanwhile, would be asleep among rodents beneath a drain cover.

People would tell the story at meals. It would grow funnier as I aged. In one version, I argue with my own reflection before falling. In another, the silver fox retrieves me by the braid. I would correct no one. The point of a story is not to preserve the shape of one person’s embarrassment. The point is to leave a rail where someone else may reach it.

Over time, I would become less interested in where the doors led than in what crossed them.

Water, always. Cold. Mineral insects. Smells from houses that had never been neighbors. Once a scattering of yellow pollen, though no one in the bowl had seen a yellow flower. Once warm wind carrying the sound of branches. Once a black bird came through with frost on its wings, flew in a circle around the room, and returned the way it came. The doorway remained for three nights, but no human passed through it.

There would be pressure from the other side too.

Not a hand. Not a mind, perhaps. Something more like attention without a face.

The ring around my wrist would become warmer near such openings. If I held it close to the gap, water gathered inside its circumference without falling. The water formed a trembling lens. Through it I sometimes saw not the room ahead but another arrangement of the city: the White Court with unfamiliar cistern mouths, roofs sloping in directions they had not yet chosen, people walking routes that did not exist.

I would report exactly what I saw and refuse to explain it.

The city has little use for certainty that cannot redirect a channel. It has some use, however, for possibilities that can prevent a death.

So I would draw no maps. A map would pretend the doors belonged to fixed positions. Instead I would keep route cords. Each cord would record a passage through material, knots, dampness, and smell. Reed for an ascending room. Hide for a place that opened onto weather. Bone beads for steps. Fox fur for a way too narrow to trust. Blue clay where water moved beneath the floor. A knot that could be loosened with one hand would mean: *return before freezing*.

These cords would hang in a black-wool chamber near the eastern wall, hundreds of them, appearing to visitors like tangled rubbish. Blind children would be brought there to read them. Elderly people would run the cords through their fingers and occasionally recognize a route from their youth.

“Not there,” one would say. “That passage used to turn after the hollow step.”

Used to. Will again. Perhaps both.

Eventually my ring would begin to change.

It would develop fine ridges, no broader than fingerprints. The ridges would continue across my skin. At first the healers would rub them with oil. Later they would find that the pattern did not belong solely to the mineral. My wrist would be growing into the ring as surely as the ring had once grown around me.

This would worry some people.

Others would say it only confirmed that the object had found a use.

I would joke that I had hoped to become a hinge in old age. Someone would answer that hinges are honored ancestors. Someone else would ask whose grandmother closes a particular door, and everyone’s shoulders would shake in silent laughter.

But in private I would wonder.

Perhaps the mountain manufactures no tools at all. Perhaps it manufactures relationships, and the objects are only one hardened end of them. A ring is not for holding or fastening. It is for making an inside and an outside speak through the same opening. A ladder is not for climbing. It is the mineral memory of separated levels. A cup is what happens when water teaches stone to wait.

And perhaps I, too, would be an unfinished object.

Not chosen. Not transformed in any dramatic sense. Simply used gradually, as everything in the bowl is used: according to pressures no one designed, repaired where possible, released when necessary.

My hearing would go. This would make me a better listener. My dazzled eyes would worsen, so I would travel increasingly by handrail. The ring would grow heavier. Children would guide me to new thresholds by bright cords, reversing the tether of their infancy.

At a door I would kneel, pour the water, and lay my ring-bearing hand against the wet stone.

Sometimes there would be nothing.

Sometimes I would feel the complex trembling of the whole city: cisterns filling, eels bracing in vertical channels, sleepers turning under hide, ice advancing from the rim, a fox squeezing through a gap narrower than its bones, the mineral pieces on the eastern wall shifting by fractions in the dark.

And sometimes something would answer.

On the morning I disappeared, no one would agree whether I had died.

A doorway would have opened beneath the eastern wall, behind the accumulated rings and forks and nested mineral cups. It would be small, forcing me to crawl. The water poured across its threshold would divide perfectly: half flowing inward, half returning to the White Court.

I would remove my boots. Leave my hook. Tie my cord to the rail.

Then I would enter.

Perhaps the cord would remain taut for an hour. Perhaps for three days. When they finally drew it back, its far end would not be cut. It would terminate in a smooth calcite loop, warm to the touch, exactly wide enough for a human wrist.

The doorway would close that night.

Years afterward, the eastern wall might produce a new object: a black mineral cup with the faint impression of fingers around it. Or a handrail would emerge in a flooded passage below the cisterns, polished by generations of palms before anyone had touched it. A diver might report a figure on a distant submerged terrace, not moving, not still, one arm extended through a ring of pale stone.

None of this would prove anything.

What remains of me would enter use. My bones, if found, might become measuring pins or hinges. My route cords would be repaired until none of their original fiber survived. The stories of my errors would improve. Some child, blindfolded in play, would recognize a vanished stair from knots I tied before their mother was born.

That is where I would find myself: not in one house or trade, not entirely among the people or beneath them, but at the temporary threshold where the city discovers it has become adjacent to something else.

And that is what I would be—

a person for most purposes,

a civic opening for others,

and, very slowly, a tool whose use becomes apparent only while it is disappearing.
