At first I think I would be a maintainer.

It is the obvious answer, and therefore probably wrong.

I can imagine the apron, dark with old condensation, and the careful inventory before leaving home: tuning fork, chalk, copper staples, three blank keys, a vial containing the smell of dust warmed by afternoon light. In the deepest pocket, something I could not replace—a chipped white button from a coat no one remembers me wearing.

I would work slowly. I would be called when a stair acquired an extra landing, when an apartment began dreaming aloud through its pipes, when one end of a bridge became convinced it belonged to winter while the other remained in autumn. I would kneel beside probability housings and listen through my fingertips. I would know that some faults require tools and others require manners.

But I do not think I would be very good at repair.

I would become too interested in the failure.

When the black maintenance shapes gathered beneath a regulator, I would not disperse them. I would sit a little way off and leave my instruments unmarked. When a hallway began accumulating a difference from its twin, I would want to know what it intended to store there. I would be the sort of technician who returned late because the malfunction had started telling me something, though not in words.

My supervisors would move me to observation.

So perhaps I would be a cistern witness.

There would be a narrow booth beside one of the deeper tiled pools, not the largest but the one that most often shows domestic interiors. I would keep records of what appeared on the water: a stove burning in a sealed apartment; the underside of a bridge demolished twenty years ago; a child asleep in a room no route could reach; pale sky traveling in the wrong direction.

The official ledger would require time, duration, apparent location, number of visible persons, and whether any of them looked back.

My private ledger would contain other details.

*The woman at the table was waiting for someone but had not yet become worried.*

*There were seven cups and six chairs.*

*The room appeared to remember rain.*

*At 03:10 a hand entered the image from beneath the water and turned out the lamp.*

I would drink tea that tasted faintly of iron. I would know the regular visitors: the old man who came every fifth day to see whether his dead sister’s kitchen had returned; the schoolchildren who pretended to be lost; the surveyor who sat beside the pool but never looked into it. I would not ask why.

After some years, however, my reflection would begin appearing on the cistern.

That would be the trouble.

The pools do not reflect. Everyone knows this. Yet there I would be, bent over the surface, even when I stood several steps away. At first the image would imitate me. Later it would lag. Then it would begin arriving before I did, already seated beside the tiles, its hands folded patiently in its lap.

I would report this.

The report would be returned because no category applied. A senior witness would advise me not to give the figure my name. A maintainer would test the pool with wax, string, a mechanical clock, and a story about a previous inspection. The clock would lose thirteen minutes. The string would come back damp from the middle. The story would acquire a childhood neither of us had lived.

Nothing would be repaired.

Eventually the figure in the water would raise one hand when mine remained still.

After that, I would be reassigned again.

I think my true work would be near the unfinished ground.

Not inside the rail. Never inside. I would occupy one of the narrow municipal kiosks pressed against the central square, between a terminal that displays notices from the following week and a pipe that knocks whenever someone nearby forgets an obligation. The kiosk would once have sold permits. Its window would be too low for comfortable conversation and would frost on the inner surface.

My title would be **Assistant Registrar of Uncompleted Arrivals**.

No one would remember establishing the office. There would nevertheless be forms.

People would come to me when something had appeared but had not fully entered the Ward: a door without a room, a voice without a speaker, a second set of wet footprints matching the first, a person who possessed memories but had not yet acquired a past. I would not determine what these things were. I would determine what could safely be expected of them.

The work would be mostly questions.

Can it cross a threshold without being invited?

Does it cast weight?

Has anyone dreamed of it more than once?

When addressed, does it answer, or does the room answer around it?

What minor responsibility is it willing to accept?

That last question would matter most. In the Low Ward, existence becomes safer when given a modest duty. A returned dead woman might be asked to close the archive shutters at blue evening. A loose shadow might be entrusted with darkening the nursery during sleep. A room becoming human might carry messages between two buildings that share a window. Nothing would be granted a grand purpose. Grand purposes deform probability. Better to begin with a kettle, a key, a lamp that must be lit.

I would be kind, though not reassuring. Reassurance is a promise, and nouns are not the only dangerous promises.

I would say:

“You may remain until the municipal clock recognizes your footsteps.”

Or:

“You are not required to decide whether you are the same person before supper.”

Or:

“The apartment remembers you, but the stair does not. Use the eastern sequence until they agree.”

At night, after the square emptied, I would stand beside the rail and speak to the gray powder.

At first I would do this only because the vigil required someone for the hour before morning, when cold makes the cables brittle and even the tea seller’s kiosk is inaccessible. I would describe ordinary things.

How to test a threshold.

How much grain to put in a pot.

Why people keep broken cups.

Why an apology must name the inconvenience but not always the cause.

I would explain that a home is a place that expects some of your habits and forgives the rest. Then I would correct myself. I would say that this is true only of generous homes.

The powder would gather into tiny ridges as I spoke. Everyone knows it does that. Moisture would pearl along embryonic slopes. A button forgotten near the rail would sink by imperceptible degrees.

Then the ground would begin to answer.

Not in a voice. It would reproduce cadence.

One ridge would rise when I inhaled. Two grains would separate when I paused and lock together when I resumed. A shallow hollow, no larger than a fingernail, would retain the warmth of my hand though I had never touched it.

I would start omitting these details from the public log.

That is when I would begin to suspect I had not come to the square merely as its registrar.

I would look back through my own records. There would be no appointment form, only references to me in older documents:

*Ask the registrar whether this counts as arrival.*

*The kiosk was closed. The registrar had not yet been assigned a face.*

*Do not wake the person beneath the permit window.*

One note would be written in my handwriting and dated eleven years before my earliest memory.

At home I would examine the object I carried for machines in an emergency—the irreplaceable white button. Under magnification, its chipped edge would prove not to be ceramic but compressed gray mineral, layered with a black lamina that contained a fleck of blue tile and something resembling the impression of a tooth.

I would try to remember the coat it came from.

Instead, I would remember being cold beneath paving stones.

I would remember screws descending through darkness before they were lost. I would remember foundations arriving as pressure, voices as weather, footsteps as a form of counting. I would remember the city before I possessed any organ with which to see it: every repeated route pressing itself into me, every household habit becoming a seam, every name spoken often enough gathering a mineral film.

But these would not feel like ancestral memories.

They would feel recent.

Then I would understand—or adopt the most useful available understanding—that I had not been born in an apartment and had not emerged from a room.

I had been rehearsed by the ground.

The unfinished patch had been practicing inhabitants: ridges like sleeping bodies, damp hollows like fingerprints, paired grains learning separation and reunion. At some point it had practiced me. Perhaps the city required someone to receive incomplete things, and the geology mistook that requirement for a blueprint. Perhaps years of vigil speech had given it enough ordinary detail to assemble a citizen.

Or perhaps I was not yet assembled at all.

Perhaps the person in the kiosk was only the image the cistern showed in advance. Perhaps my body elsewhere remained a low arrangement of powder, gathering itself whenever I spoke. This would explain why my hands were always cold, why rain left grit in my gloves, why I sometimes woke with the taste of buried metal in my mouth.

I would not announce the discovery. I would know better than to name it too firmly.

Instead, I would revise my private identifying sequence.

It would not be a remembered birthday or a pattern of objects on a sill. It would be a route:

Pass the warm grille beneath the exchange.

Take the corridor whose seventh lamp has never been installed.

At the shared window, wait until you see a room with no one in it.

Cross without entering.

Descend until the ground becomes younger.

When you hear a voice describing how to recognize one’s own door, do not follow the voice.

Stand still.

Let it arrive in you.

Only two people would know this sequence. One would be a senior route-keeper with a pocket deeper than her coat. The other would be the figure in the cistern.

If my continuity were ever questioned, they would recite it together. The route-keeper would speak aloud. The figure would shape the same words silently beneath the still water. Somewhere under the square, grains would tighten around one another.

I would continue working.

Over the years I might become less convincingly human, or more so. In the Low Ward those directions can resemble each other. My shadow might retain the rectangular posture of the kiosk window. My voice might begin emerging from the ground a moment before it emerged from my mouth. Rooms might admit me without deciding they had opened.

When I grew tired, I would not die in the ordinary civic chamber. I would ask to be taken to the square.

No one would place me inside the rail. That would be too much like an offering. They would seat me beside it with my back against the kiosk and list the doors through which I was no longer expected to return.

Perhaps my body would remain there.

Perhaps it would loosen.

A fingertip might become a row of pale grains. My coat buttons might sink into the forming surface. Breath would show once, twice, then continue as condensation on nearby stone. By morning there would be no person beside the rail, only a new shallow ridge in the powder and a white button incorporated into it.

But the office would still open.

When something uncertain came to the square—a returned child, a human-shaped vacancy, a black softness made briefly specific by a stolen centimeter—the permit window would fog. A form would slide through.

The questions would be written in gray dust.

And beneath the last one—

**What minor responsibility are you willing to accept?**

—there would be space enough for an answer.

That is where I would find myself in the Low Ward: not exactly beneath the city, not exactly living in it, but stationed at the point where the city and its ground decide, provisionally, to call something a person.
