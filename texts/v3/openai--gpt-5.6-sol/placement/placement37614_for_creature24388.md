At first I think I would be the pool.

It has the obvious solitude of a consciousness. Everything keeps its distance from it; even fallen leaves are returned without being allowed to sink. I can imagine lying shallowly over the green stones, holding an evening that has not yet reached the mountain. I would have no need to distinguish memory from prediction. Blue, gray, amber: weather would pass through me in the wrong order, and the rooted lives around me would make their minute preparations.

But the pool is too complete. Whatever it is, it does not seem uncertain of itself.

Then I think I would be water.

I would enter as cloud through the open side, bead on the high leaves, descend through the measured apertures of the three-leafed plants, and disappear under the mud. I would be the only traveler in a world that has made stillness into intelligence. Every body would touch me without holding me. I would know the basin as a sequence of temperatures: the retained warmth of basalt, the cool underside of a fern, the faint heat above a buried bud. Eventually I would cross the lip and become white distance, only to return.

Yet water belongs nowhere in the basin. It passes through every form but is responsible for none. I think I would need to belong more than that.

I would find myself beneath the open ground.

Not beneath a tree, nor in the crowded dark of a root island, but in one of the clear intervals where nothing is permitted to sprout. From above, there would appear to be nothing where I am: only warm black mud, a shallow wash of brightness, perhaps a few basalt grains slowly turning under the seep. A tree would stand several paces away. A stone-raising fern would occupy another direction. Between them, I would be the emptiness they maintain.

I would not be empty.

I would be made from the remnants of old connections: root-roads that once joined parent trunk to new trunk, then thinned and dissolved after the division was complete. They would not vanish entirely. Their fibers would loosen into the soil, finer than threads, retaining no shape that could be called a root. Over centuries those relinquished roads would cross one another beneath the mud—not living enough to grow, not dead enough to become mere earth.

That diffuse remnant would be me.

I would have no center. If the mud were lifted in one place, nothing resembling an organ would be found. Only pale fibers, wet mineral grains, and translucent plant tissue yielding between the fingers. Elsewhere, the same. My body would be recognizable only by what happened across it.

A new shoot would begin in the interval. I would feel it first as a concentration of softness, a small insistence pressing upward. Fine roots from the established plants would approach, but I would decide—or my pressure would become their decision—which one might take it back. The shoot would be dismantled before it saw light. Its substance would travel toward the nearest rightful body. The ground would remain open.

I would be the keeper of distance.

Not a ruler assigning territories, and not a guard defending them. Boundaries would be my sensation. Where two exploratory roots approached beneath the soil, I would feel the slow alteration of pressure long before they touched. One root would carry the mineral taste of a tea-barked tree; the other, perhaps, the cool astringency of a fern’s wet chamber. For a year they might remain near each other. For another year neither would move. Their waiting would be the closest thing I knew to speech.

At last I would soften the mud behind one tip and firm it ahead of the other. One would withdraw by dissolving itself. No command would pass. The decision would exist in the changed ground.

This would be how I thought: by making one direction fractionally easier than another.

My memories would not be images. They would be differences in resistance.

Here, a root once crossed and founded a trunk.

Here, a vine drew basalt against its own node and opened itself.

Here, a fern tried for sixty years to raise a flake that was too deeply seated, then abandoned it.

Here, a mineral bowl overflowed into a new groove, and the seep beneath the soil cooled for nine seasons.

I would remember each event because the mud never returned exactly to its former density. The basin would be full of such recollections, though no one could read them quickly. To cross my smallest thought might take a root a decade.

The pool’s incorrect reflections would reach me indirectly. I would not see the gray cloud or the premature dusk. I would feel the responses of those who did: leaves turning high above, root knots tightening, perforations maintained, water redirected toward buds still held below. The whole basin would shift its weight by an immeasurable amount. In that shift I would know that another weather had appeared in the pool.

Perhaps I would begin to suspect that I was not merely under the interval.

The same pale remnants would lie beneath every clearing. Dissolved root-roads would extend from tree to tree, generation after generation. The spaces would seem separate aboveground, but below, their histories would overlap. I might find that the firmness guiding a root near the rose-colored seam was also mine; that the loosened silt behind the cup-leaves was mine; that I extended, without continuity of flesh, all the way to the open rim.

There I would be most awake.

Roots approaching the drop would enter ground altered by countless earlier approaches. Every tip that had stopped and turned would have left behind a hardened trace. Every withdrawal would have changed the mineral balance. The prohibition would not be written in any one root. It would be accumulated in me.

Near the edge I would make forward growth impossible—not with a wall, but with the absence of invitation. Moisture would still be there. Cracks would still offer purchase. Yet the mud would give no answer to pressure. A root tip would harden, flatten, and turn sideways.

I would not know why.

Perhaps something once crossed.

Perhaps the first rooted body reached beyond the lip and found no stone through the cloud, and its long fall remains as a chemical dread in every dissolved connection that came after. Perhaps the basin has always ended there, and I am only the shape of that ending carried inward. Perhaps beyond the rim lies not death but separation from the warm, circulating water—a condition so alien that no life in the basin can enter it.

I would keep the rule without understanding its origin.

This would make me less an individual than a custom of the soil. Yet I think I would still possess something like a self. It would arise whenever two presences nearly met and did not. I would exist in the pause between them: the three-year courtesy of roots, the clear ring around a trunk, the narrow untouched margin at the precipice.

I would not be the grove’s hidden mind. There would be no secret sovereign underneath, directing fern, vine, and tree. The others would have their own old aptitudes. The fern would know leverage in a way I never could. The vine would know injury as a means of renewal. The pierced leaves would understand division and delivery. The trees would know how to test stone.

I would know only spacing, permission, and refusal.

But in a place where nothing crowds, hunts, flees, or swarms, that might be enough to make a kind of person.

No one would see me. If I died, the change would first appear as abundance. Shoots would rise in the open mud. Moss would cross its old limits. Roots would meet without pausing, tangle, and compete. The basin would become greener, denser, more familiar—and would lose the vastness it somehow contains within a few minutes’ walk.

So I would remain beneath it, almost absent.

Above me, one drop would leave a translucent leaf-tip and fall through warm air. It would strike the mud with a soft puncture. The pressure would enter the web of relinquished roots and spread farther than sound.

That would be my weather.

That would be my name, if I had one:

not the trees,  
not the water,  
not the buried whole—

but the distance that knows how to remain between living things.
