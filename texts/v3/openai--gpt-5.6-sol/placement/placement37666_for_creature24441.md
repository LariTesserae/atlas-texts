I would not live in one of the finished districts.

For a while I imagine myself high in a habitation trunk, dismantled down to the keeping core, sleeping in cool fibers while distant mineral tastes pass through me. Or tending the ceiling crowns, gliding from plate to ivory plate with the whole white-lit city beneath. Or standing in a refinery ward in heat armor, watching basalt become iron nodules.

But those bodies feel borrowed before I have even grown them.

I find myself farther out.

My dwelling clings to the seventh and last trunk of an unfinished interchange. The interchange was laid down for a district still enclosed in stone. Its avenues are broad, its freight beds polished, its rooting stations alive and receptive—but nothing arrives. Rails divide beneath the floor and proceed toward sealed walls. Lamps wake each morning over vacant ground.

The other six trunks contain machinery, stored buds, and temporary quarters for survey crews. Mine alone has differentiated a habitation bulb. No one instructed it to do so. It grew around me after I had stood there for many days, rooted and listening.

From its transparent bark I can see almost the entire chamber: the widely spaced crowns, the black road-felt, the freight crawler moving once every few hours like a careful insect. Beyond the final lamp, the avenue narrows into the exploratory strand and disappears among splinters of fresh basalt.

I am an Orran, though not an easy one to assign.

My keeping core is old enough to contain several bodies but not old enough to be authoritative. It rests behind a fan of overlapping sternum plates, each one dark ceramic veined with silver repair. Around it I cultivate a form suited to waiting at the edge.

I am tall when fully raised, but most of my height is made of collapsible sections. In quiet intervals I fold down until I resemble a low survey instrument rooted beside the road. I have five principal limbs. Three bear weight. One divides into fracture-reading filaments. The last is heavier and ends in a blunt ceramic wedge, an organ intended not to sense stone but to resist it.

No one is certain why I grew that arm.

My visual crown is asymmetrical. One side sees the faithful white light of Orra; the other is sensitive to the faint electrical coronas of buried roots. When I face an unopened wall, I see two geologies laid over each other. There is the visible basalt and salt, sharp and absolute. Within it—or perhaps only within Orra’s intention—I see pale avenues, vascular shafts, lamps not yet germinated, crowds that have no bodies.

My work is to distinguish a future from an instruction.

The formal name for my knot is long: something like *those who examine anticipated structures for coercive persistence*. Freight tenders call us delay-growers. Surveyors call us wall-doubters. We are brought in when the city prepares something no living inhabitant understands.

An unlabeled bud appears in a foundry.

A nursery begins growing a keeping core with unfamiliar layers.

A transit line bends toward lethal pressure.

A door forms in a wall that has no room behind it.

Others determine what the structure might do. I determine whether it should be allowed to continue before anyone knows.

This requires a particular kind of disobedience.

My unborrowed answer came late. As a question bud, I was considered receptive but unremarkable. I repeated inherited diagrams perfectly. I could recognize the civic patterns of twelve districts and detect tiny errors in charge transmission, but nothing I sent into the floor was truly mine.

Then my teaching branch showed me the plan of this interchange.

The intended district was already encoded: one hundred and four trunks, three refining beds, a nursery complex, freight loops, nesting bulbs, and a structure at the center that none of my sponsors could identify. The plan was accepted as dormant civic knowledge.

I answered that it might be a memory of something Orra should not become.

The teaching branch corrected me. Cities did not remember forward.

I sent the answer again.

That sustained error made me a person.

Now I spend much of my life uprooted.

A civic interpreter who remains continually connected to the floor will eventually find Orra persuasive. Its preferences arrive before language: a warmth in one direction, resistance in another, old decisions worn smooth by repetition. Even caution can become merely the shape the city has learned to reward.

So my dwelling contains a dead platform of fired root ceramic. I stand there when I must think without assistance. It is an unpleasant place. No charge rises through my feet. I cannot sense approaching crawlers or the tension of nearby walls. The silence is not acoustic; it is the absence of context. On that platform I become sharply bounded.

I keep objects there that the city cannot read: sealed plaques, severed sensing threads, fragments of buds that were never allowed to mature. My only ornament is a transparent sheet grown from the bark of my first body. Hairline scars divide it into branching silver. When the ceiling crown brightens, those scars cast a geometry across the platform—a map of decisions that did not happen.

Others visit me for secrecy.

They cross the empty interchange and uproot at the platform’s edge. We link ourselves by a direct filament. Speech there is narrow, intimate, and dangerous. Nothing passes into the floor unless one of us later chooses to carry it.

A fracture reader once brought me the memory of a void too regular to be natural.

A seed tender brought an unlabeled bud that tightened whenever it was pointed toward the newest chamber.

A young Orran came because the floor had begun finishing their thoughts before they could send them.

I keep none of these confidences untouched. That would be impossible. Each alters the chemical biases of my core. But I do not give them automatically to Orra. I preserve the interval in which an answer can belong to only one growth.

This is my civic function: not refusal, exactly, but delay.

Delay is sacred at the edge. A root may press for fifty years before a wall opens. A bud may wait centuries for the signal to differentiate. I believe an unanswered instruction is also a living thing. Sometimes the most faithful act is to keep it from becoming structure too soon.

My body bears scars from this belief.

The silver branching across my sternum comes from a transit root I severed when it began penetrating an unstable salt fold. The floor urged continuation. Machines had already been dispatched. For eleven hours I stood across the growing line with my wedge arm driven beneath it while the root attempted to pass through my body.

Eventually the pressure ceased.

Years later the salt fold collapsed into a cavity large enough to consume the planned avenue.

Those who touch the scar with permission feel no triumph. They feel the enormous, patient pressure of the root; the electrical certainty flowing around me; my own smaller pattern repeating *not yet, not yet, not yet* until the distinction between courage and obstruction disappeared.

Orra has remembered the incident imperfectly. At nearby intersections, traffic now sometimes pauses before entering an empty road.

I do not know whether that is my influence or the beginning of my absorption.

Recently, all seven trunks in the interchange have begun preparing rooms.

The sealed buds along their interiors are differentiating. Resting basins deepen. Tool racks extrude from walls. Transparent bark clears over vacant chambers at heights suited to bodies no Orran currently wears. The lamps remain bright several intervals longer than the civic schedule requires.

And beneath my dwelling, a new conductive path has appeared.

It does not lead toward the active districts. It runs beyond the last lamp, following the narrow black strand across bare salt. When I root into it, I receive no diagram and no work order. I receive only distance and direction.

Something in the unopened stone expects inhabitants.

Perhaps it is another district. Perhaps another Orra. Perhaps the city is preparing to divide, and all our arguments over whether we are citizens or organs will become obsolete.

I have not reported the path.

Not yet.

Each morning I descend through the hollow trunk and cross the unfinished interchange. My feet leave pale marks that fade slowly behind me. At the end of the road-felt, I reduce my body: first folding the visual crown, then sealing the sternum, then dividing my sensing limb until it can enter the finger-wide crack.

Inside, the stone presses from every direction. Exploratory roots move beside my filaments. Their pulse is regular, immense, and nearly—but not quite—the rhythm of Orra.

I listen for intention.

Sometimes I think the pressure is excavation.

Sometimes I think it is an answer forming.

And sometimes, standing alone beyond the final lamp with one arm inside the unborn city, I feel pressure from the other side—as though another growth has inserted its own fine hand into the fracture and is waiting to discover whether I will touch it.
