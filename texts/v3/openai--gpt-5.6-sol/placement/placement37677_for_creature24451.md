At first I would think I belonged in the lighthouse.

I can see the appeal of its small windows, the storm shutters, the lamp turning through rain. I would know the islands by the shapes they make when most of them are hidden: one black shoulder in fog, three skerries appearing and disappearing in spindrift, the white disorder above a reef. I would keep the weather instruments clean and record winds strong enough to shake the pen in my hand.

But I do not think I would remain there. The lighthouse looks outward. My attention would keep going down.

I would find myself in one of the older holds, built into the lee wall of a drowned valley perhaps sixty meters below the surface. Not a large settlement. Eighty people in winter, somewhat more when the spring crews arrive. Its first rooms would have been bored for a turbine station, and everything added afterward would follow the stone rather than a plan. The inhabitants would call it Crooked Hold, though its official name would be something longer and commemorative that no one uses.

My room would be far inside, beyond the wet locks and the galleries where one can still feel the turbines through the floor. There would be no port. One wall would be bare slate, crossed by a pale quartz seam. The other walls would be softened with woven kelp mats. A broad-bottomed cup would sit in a wire loop beside my bunk, and over it I would keep a map of the drowned range—not a surface chart, but a sectional one, crowded with ridges, faults, cable routes, listening stations, and the habitual passages of whales.

I would be a listener.

The title might be hydrophone keeper, acoustic surveyor, or diagnostic technician, but those would describe only parts of the work. My station would receive sound from instruments fixed throughout the mountains: in turbine pylons, on cliff faces, beside navigation markers, down the outer escarpment where daylight never reaches. Most of what came through would be noise to anyone else. To me it would be a country.

I would know the tide entering the western pass by the deepening grain of the water. I would know when sand had begun to scour a cable trench, when a loose shackle was striking steel, when a rotor blade had acquired enough shell growth to alter its voice. A healthy bearing would make a note too low to hear clearly but easy to feel through the desk. A failing one would put a faint roughness around that note, like breath freezing on cloth.

There would be animal sounds as well: herring rushing past a sensor in such numbers that they resembled rain; seals scraping themselves against housings; cod knocking on hollow frames; the dry little crackle of feeding invertebrates. Whales would pass through everything. Their calls would arrive bent by valleys and reflected from cliffs, sometimes seeming to come from beneath the stone.

I would keep records of the known ones. Split Wake would always enter from the north and go silent near the turbine field. Lantern Jaw would investigate any newly installed equipment. The Old Singer Below Three would return irregularly, perhaps absent for years, and when his call appeared in the lower basin I would send a message through the hold before I had formally confirmed it. People would come quietly into the listening room. Children would sit on the floor. No one would speak until the phrase had passed.

My hearing would not be especially good. That would surprise visitors. One ear would have been damaged in my youth by a pressure fault—a lock cycled before it had fully equalized, perhaps, or a helmet seal that failed on ascent. I would hear conversation more clearly on my right side and turn that side toward people without thinking.

But listening here would not mean possessing perfect ears. It would mean patience, memory, and knowing what a sound is attached to.

I would have begun as an exterior mechanic. For years I would work in tide suits, moving hand over hand along fixed lines while the kelp lay flat around me. My dive partner would be someone named Mara or Iven, a person whose breathing I could identify even through bad comms. We would inspect the western turbines together, clearing mussels from intake grilles and checking armored cable where it crossed the saddle.

Then there would be an accident—not a grand catastrophe, only the kind this place produces without malice. A snapped guide line. A body caught sideways in the flow. Three minutes in which training, luck, and another person’s grip make the distinction between a story and a funeral.

Afterward I would still go outside, but less often. I would become more useful indoors, where the damage to my ear somehow made me attentive to vibration. In time I would be the person younger crews consulted before opening a housing.

“Listen to Seven,” they would say.

And I would place my palm against the bulkhead.

The hold would have two tide clocks, but I would carry my own, an old mechanical one repaired so many times that none of its parts matched. The back would be scratched with the name of the person who gave it to me. That person would have gone down-valley years before. Some of their ashes would be sealed into a patch in the south gallery, where seawater once found a fault and entered as a cold mist. I would touch that place occasionally when passing, not ceremonially. Just the heel of my hand against painted ceramic.

I would teach children to hear the difference between danger and mere violence. The sea is often violent here without anything being wrong. A boulder striking an outer collar can shake bowls from a table and still require no action. A tiny regular click inside a pump may demand an immediate shutdown.

That distinction would be the center of my life.

Not bravery, exactly. Not calmness. Attention.

During storms I would sit at the acoustic board while the holds withdrew behind their pressure doors. The upper sensors would become a single field of concussion. Turbines would lower their pitch under load. Chains, rock, water, and machinery would merge until the whole drowned range seemed to be speaking at once.

I would not understand it as language. The islanders would be suspicious of anyone who claimed the sea was sending messages. Water moves. Stone bears strain. Machines wear. Animals call to one another. None of this is prophecy.

And yet, after enough years, I might begin to feel that listening itself creates a kind of kinship. Not mastery, and not translation. Simply the knowledge that every living thing and every made thing announces its condition if one attends long enough.

Eventually I would be old enough that exterior crews checked my seals with exaggerated care. My hands would be broad at the knuckles, nails ridged from years of cold. Young technicians would politely increase the volume at my station, and I would turn it down when they left. I would know fewer new divers by their breathing. More cables would bear the names of people I had known.

When I died, I would not want the high cemetery. I would be cremated, and part of the ash would be mixed into concrete for the listening room when its seaward wall next required reinforcement. The rest would be released at slack tide above the drowned valley.

For a few minutes it would hang in the water outside the hold, pale in the lamps. Then the tide would take it downslope, through kelp shadow and coral thickets, past the last hydrophone, into the blue-black country below.

But until then, that is where I would be: inside the mountain, one hand on the stone, listening for the smallest change in the roar.
