At first I would try to find a place to stand.

The courtyard would refuse me.

The floor beneath my left foot would bow toward the pool, while the cane beneath my right would insist that down lay somewhere behind my shoulder. I would grip a wet railing and discover that it was only pretending to be attached. Someone inside the wall would tap twice—either warning me or asking me to move my hand—and a large drop of condensation would strike the back of my neck.

I would think I belonged by the pool. Everything seems to refer to it: the channels, the roots, the drowned distances. But there is no *by* the pool. Its edge has been occupied by shelves, bridges, knees, baskets, and black leaves. To approach it is to become part of an arrangement already in progress.

Then I would think I belonged on the impossible balcony, looking down at lightning and up at the courtyard repeated above. But the balcony would be under dispute. Half the inhabitants would say it had not yet emerged. The other half would insist that it had already arrived too completely and must be made less certain. While they argued, someone would remove the rail I was leaning on.

So I would not be given a place.

I would be given a danger.

There would be, behind a curtain of thick-veined leaves, a wall smoother than it ought to be. Not one of the flawless doors, not yet. Merely a new partition that had come out of the mountain during the last growth. Its clay would still be warm. No fingerprints would mark it. No root would have found it. The plaster would meet the floor in a clean, continuous line.

Everyone passing it would avert their eyes.

An elder whose legs have become the supports of a nearby cistern would point at me with one mossy finger.

“Stay there,” she would say. “Find where it is still open.”

That would become my location: not a chamber, but the narrow interval between a wall and its completion.

I would sleep in a shallow recess beside it, curled around a bundle of withheld pegs. Water would run behind my back through a channel that occasionally remembered being a flute. Above my head, the disputed balcony would extend one finger-width farther each night. Beneath me, through gaps in the platform, the central pool would gleam at an impossible remove.

My household would consist of everyone responsible for preventing that wall from becoming certain.

There would be an old man spread so thinly through the plaster that he could speak only when the wall flexed. There would be two sisters who were unrelated except during rain. There would be a child rooted in a hanging mask, still deciding whether to grow legs. There would be a woman whose left hand lived permanently on the other side of the partition. We would eat over one another’s bowls and argue about whether a hairline crack was promising or merely decorative.

Only gradually would I understand what sort of body I had brought to the task.

I would be one of those who first appeared as part of the architecture.

For a long time I had been a small relief figure low on a wall, passing a brick toward hands that had never been carved. My arm repeated the same patient motion whenever no one watched. Then, during an altitude spasm, the brick became heavier than the wall. I stepped out to keep from dropping it.

My first question would have been:

“Where does unsupported weight go while it is waiting to fall?”

They would have recognized this as a practical question, though not a safe one. Someone would wrap an apron around me before I was entirely soft. Someone else would press a thumb into the back of my neck so the mountain would have a harmless place to continue revising me.

That thumbprint would remain my protected flaw.

In damp weather, a small warm draft would come through it. During mountain growth, fine roots would emerge, feel the air, and withdraw. If anyone tried to plaster it shut, my limbs would begin to stiffen and my name would lose its alternatives.

The rest of me would be almost flesh.

My skin would have the red-gray color of clay that has been fired badly and used anyway. One shoulder would retain a faint glaze. My ribs would be ordinary until the chamber inhaled the high air; then they would show as tied reeds beneath translucent skin. My right eye would see what is present. My left would never have been fully painted and would see what is becoming too complete.

Through that unfinished eye, smoothness would shine.

A roof nearing completion would glow along its final knot. A story approaching its fixed ending would draw a pale line through the speaker’s mouth. Lovers who had defined one another too exactly would begin to acquire sharp edges. A vessel about to fill with sky would look briefly transparent, already containing a blue no one else could see.

I would become a hinderer.

Not a destroyer. Destruction is too fertile here and often makes matters worse. I would learn the smaller mercies: how to loosen one fiber without collapsing a bridge, how to smudge a final mark so that a mural remains in the present, how to ask a question that gives a name another syllable.

My apron would fill with dangerous conclusions.

A tiny green peg smelling of sap. The last shell in a mosaic. A lid handle. A painted eye no larger than a lentil. Three final notes of a song scratched on a strip of fired clay. A child’s wrapped baby tooth. A ladder rung made from a wood that grows nowhere in the mountain.

I would not know what most of them completed. Knowing would make them eager to return.

My names would change according to what I withheld.

On ordinary mornings I might be Clay-At-The-Neck.

During a growth, when I climbed the disputed balcony and cut away its farthest brace, I would be Keeps-The-Balcony-From-Arriving.

To the child in the hanging mask I would be Gives-Bad-Answers-Slowly.

At the smooth wall I would be Left-Eye-Open.

The sealed doors would trouble me most.

Because I could see completion, I would also see movement behind them more clearly than others did. Not the people themselves, exactly, but the certainty of their gestures. A flawless hand lifting forever. A mouth forming a sentence that had already been heard. A worker turning toward an accident with no possibility of warning.

I would sit near those doors and make imperfect objects.

Cups that leaned. Figures with one leg too short. Knots containing loose hairs. Little clay ears with no matching heads. I would place them against the thresholds, not because I believed they could open the completed past, but because mercy should be offered even where it cannot enter.

Sometimes, from the other side, there would come a tap.

Never twice in the same place.

I would not decide what it meant.

That restraint would be part of my work.

When the mountain grew, I would be among the first to feel it. The unfinished eye would ache. Smooth lines would appear everywhere at once, bright as lightning trapped in wet clay.

I would shout flaws into the noise.

“Crooked there!”

“Leave the root unjoined!”

“Someone insult that basket!”

“Take the final word out of his mouth!”

Then I would crawl through the newborn structures while they were still deciding what they were. My body would flatten between walls. The thumbprint in my neck would open and whistle. I would pull completion from the summit piece by piece, stuffing hot pegs and wet tiles into my apron.

Perhaps one day I would fail.

The wall beside my sleeping recess would seal from end to end. My household would hear a soft sound, like mud accepting the shape of a hand. The old man in the plaster would vanish into an earlier version of himself. The sisters would cease to have met. The child in the mask would become only a decorative root.

And I would find my own finished outline impressed upon the smooth surface.

If there were still time, I would press my unpainted eye against it. I would use the brick I had carried out of the relief so long ago. I would strike once—not to break the wall, but to give the break somewhere to begin.

A crack would appear.

From the crack, damp fibers would emerge.

Someone, perhaps not yet born, would take hold of them.

Until then, you would find me behind the leaves, beneath the balcony that must not arrive, keeping watch beside the nearly flawless wall.

I would be one of those still under the hand: a wall-born keeper of withheld pieces, recognizable by the thumbprint opening at the back of my neck and the unfinished eye that sees dangerous certainty.

Not the missing piece.

The one who keeps it missing.
