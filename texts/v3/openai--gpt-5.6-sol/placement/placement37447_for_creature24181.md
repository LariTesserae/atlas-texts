I would first mistake myself for a weather tender.

I can feel the attraction of the convection towers: the climb through hot mist, the maintenance wings opening under my arms, the lightning lines lifting blue-white from their reels. I would know individual rain kites by the notches in their trailing edges. I would lean into a pressure wake beside them and, for a few seconds, possess something like open space.

But I do not think I would live so high.

Nor would I be a listener of the buried city, though I would envy the gravity of their attention. I am not patient enough to place my hands against a wall for three days and wait for an ancient pump to answer. The depths would trouble me. Not because they are dark, but because nothing there has finished happening.

I find myself instead wherever the city has briefly failed to fill itself.

I am an **interval keeper**.

Others call us gapwrights, vacancy hands, or—less respectfully—stuffers. We respond when a room is emptied, a bridge retracts, a folded volume loses power, a migrating block leaves its old foundations, or a floodgate closes around a pocket no system has claimed. We arrive during the dangerous minutes before heat, pressure, animals, water, contraband, or neighboring architecture enters on its own.

Our task is not to keep the space empty. Nothing could be more irresponsible.

We decide what should occupy it first.

Sometimes it is a reservoir bladder, inflated against walls that have forgotten their loads. Sometimes we admit a tagged colony of shellbacks to strip catalytic residue. We hang resonant cables so the district can feel its altered dimensions. We establish an animal passage before someone mistakes the opening for rentable floor. We mark which surfaces are real, which depend on power, and which will become someone else’s ceiling when gravity correction resumes.

When no suitable material can arrive quickly enough, the interval keeper enters.

A living citizen is a recognized use.

So I sit in the vacancy with my back against a brace and become, for a little while, the reason it does not close.

My body would be compact even by basin standards: short legs, flexible ribs, shoulders that can narrow independently. My hands would have two additional joints in each finger, useful for reaching through cable knots without disturbing them. Beneath my lacquered skin would be pressure-sensitive threads, letting me feel load changes through a palm or bare foot. I would have a secondary throat valve for flooded passages, though no true underwater lungs. A membrane along my inner cheeks would hold enough oxygen to cross a drowned room if I did not panic.

I would sometimes panic.

The city has many ways to remind a person that adaptation is not invulnerability.

My hearing would be poor from childhood exposure and deliberately reduced further when I entered the trade. Most sound would reach me through my jaw transmitter or the pads in my palms. In exposed streets I would converse wrist to wrist. My unamplified voice would be rough and surprisingly soft.

I would live near the meeting of an old canal ward and a load-rail exchange, where migrating blocks pause before moving toward the central flood expanse. Nothing there would keep the same orientation for long. One week my district would face a ferry channel; the next, the warm underside of a market deck. On storm days the lower passages would flood deliberately, and blunt silver fish would crowd against the transparent portion of my door.

“My home” would be four obligations rather than one place.

There would be a sleeping niche beneath a disused boarding ramp, dry except when the district takes structural water. There would be a cooking alcove six bridges away, shared with three households and a scavenger colony that predicts failures in the heat exchanger. My archive would occupy a ceramic capsule inside an old seawall. My legal shelter—the volume required to accept me during catastrophic weather—would be a retired ferry hanging vertically in a reinforcement shaft.

The ferry would smell of warm metal, old mineral sealant, and the cooking spices of people who had lived in it before it was turned upright.

My name would contain the route between these places. It might translate poorly as:

**Senn, Under-Ramp to Upright Ferry by the Third Brown-Water Gate.**

Friends would shorten it to Senn. Officials never would.

The route would change often enough that I would have several obsolete names folded into my identity. One would still open a bathhouse that no longer existed. Another would be recognized by a bridge now embedded three kilometers below the inhabited decks. Once every few years, some ancient municipal process would greet me by a childhood name through a wall I had never touched before.

I would always answer.

Interval keepers are superstitious about recognition.

I would carry little equipment. A line projector. Three kinds of sealant. An animal-route gauge. Water-condition strips. A roll of membrane that can become a floor, a sling, a pressure partition, or a shroud. Two emergency bladders, one filled with air and one with catalytic foam. A ceramic hammer inherited from my first instructor, though striking anything is almost always the last resort.

My most important tool would be a spool of black thread containing sensor dust and a little of the carbon of the dead.

The thread would have come from my older sibling, who served as a load reader and died when a market deck corrected itself in the wrong direction. Most of them would have entered ordinary continuities: filter membrane, structural ceramic, a ferry’s collision judgment. My household received a length of conductive carbon fiber pigmented from what remained.

I would string it across uncertain rooms.

Because the thread remembers strain unusually well, it would tremble before certain failures. I would not say my sibling warned me. That would be imprecise. But I would thank them when it happened.

The work would put me among the poor more often than the wealthy. Rich districts rarely permit a vacancy to become visible; their redundancies arrive before the previous function leaves. In the temporary wards, absence opens constantly. A wall retracts and exposes six sleeping spaces to rain. A retired duct cools, and an entire market loses the waste heat on which its food culture depends. A room that was thicker inside than outside fails during an outage, returning furniture, residents, and stored water to a volume too small to contain them.

People there would know me. Children would drum my work signal on railings when they saw an unclaimed opening:

*Gap here. Gap here. Something must go here.*

They would help, though they had been told not to. One child would crawl through a passage too narrow for me and report, by taps through the wall, that pale gliders had already nested inside. That would change the allocation. The space could no longer become a hot-water cistern; it would need to remain an air route until the young had launched.

This is what my work would really be: discovering that emptiness is almost always an error of perception.

A cavity has heat in it. A pressure tendency. A vibration. Spores. Memory. An animal on its way. Water not yet arrived. A neighboring wall leaning inward by fractions. Someone’s plan, legal or otherwise. The interval is never empty. It is only crowded with things that have not declared themselves.

I would be good at waiting just long enough for them to do so.

Not too long.

During major storms, I would work beside flood negotiators. Their decisions would arrive through my sleeves as colored pulses: blue for admissible water, amber for temporary habitation, white for structural use, violet for biological claim. I would stand in a newly opened chamber while rain hammered the levels above and decide whether to let it fill.

That decision would never concern only the chamber.

If I admitted water, a kitchen below might lose heat. If I refused it, a ferry passage might become a torrent. If I routed it through an animal corridor, shellback larvae could spread into a district whose wall chemistry would kill them. Courtesy would become mathematics performed under pressure, with lives hidden inside every term.

I would make mistakes.

Once, early in my work, I would seal a crack because the room was listed as dry civic storage. Water would find an older path and flood an unregistered dwelling beneath it. No one would die, but a family archive would dissolve: three generations of tactile vocabulary softened into meaningless bumps.

After that, before sealing anything, I would place two fingers against the surface and ask aloud:

“Who is below you?”

It would become a habit, then a joke among colleagues, then a phrase apprentices were taught without knowing its origin.

My private luxury would not be quiet. Quiet would make me feel trapped inside my body.

It would be an unnecessary window.

A narrow one, overlooking the load-rail exchange. The view would consist mostly of braces, rain, moving walls, and the armored backs of scavengers warming themselves on a power conduit. It would not provide emergency access, drainage, signal transmission, thermal balancing, or recognized nesting space. I would spend years paying the assessments required to keep it functionless.

Of course it would not remain so.

Someone would attach a bridge beneath it. Condensation fins would spread along one edge. A rain kite, injured during a storm, would begin returning to the ledge. I would recognize her by a pale tear near the left wing root. Children would name her **Closes-Late**. The weather office would insist this was not an operational designation and then report that Closes-Late had taken her western position above the exchange.

Eventually I would give up defending the window’s uselessness.

I would register it as a storm observation point and animal rest.

This would be considered maturity.

If I lived long enough, my joints would stiffen and I would no longer fit the gaps I once occupied. I would train younger keepers, teaching them to distinguish an empty room from a folded one, an abandoned conduit from a listening one, and structural silence from the pause before failure.

I would tell them never to rush merely because the city is impatient.

When I died, the interval keepers would request my pressure-sensitive threads. My hand bones might become ceramic gauges. My water would return to the district loop. Some part of my recorded judgment would enter the system that assigns first use to newly exposed spaces.

For a while, when a wall opened unexpectedly, my voice might issue from its edge:

**Unclaimed volume. Do not seal. Test for downward consequence. Admit passage before occupancy. Ask who is below.**

That would be enough continuation for me.

And during the minute after a storm, while the city inhaled, I think some remaining part of me would be found in the openings: under lifted bridges, inside cooling walls, between a departing room and the room arriving to replace it.

Not preserving emptiness.

Holding it briefly, bodily, so that what came next would have somewhere to begin.
