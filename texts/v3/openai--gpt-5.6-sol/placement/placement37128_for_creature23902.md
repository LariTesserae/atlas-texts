At first I think I would live near the summit.

I see myself behind the low dark barrier, copying the old measurements before adding my own: the seed remains red; the seed remains wet; frost forms around it but not upon it. I would have the patience for that work, or believe I did. I would sharpen charcoal against stone, align the bone gauge, and privately imagine roots entering the mountain’s clean fracture.

But I would not be a summit scholar. Their hope is too disciplined. Mine would wander.

Then I think I would be an Opener—a builder of walls meant to vanish, leaving each evening’s tools arranged as a question. Yet I would soon become interested less in forcing change than in noticing where the basin’s corrections were incomplete. One pebble not returned to its pile. One clay soldier still facing east after all the others had been laid flat. I would begin keeping records, and then I would no longer be entirely an Opener.

So I would find myself at the eastern edge of the city, where the last named street passes beneath a narrow bridge and becomes a path through meadow. There would be three houses there, though from a distance only two would be visible. Mine would be built into the bridge footing, low-roofed and always damp beneath the eaves. Ferns would stop at the threshold. A silver-leaved tree would lean over the court, bearing the same eleven fruits.

My public name would be **Es**.

Some would say I was young because my hair was black and my hands did not tremble. Others would remember me from before the sealing of the bathhouse door and call me grandmother. I would not correct either account.

My office would be minor and slightly embarrassing: I would keep the record of delayed echoes.

Most echoes in the basin return faithfully. Their direction, interval, and strength are known. But certain sounds come back late—from the eastern ridge especially. A child shouts in the morning, and near dusk the last syllable returns. A mason taps a wedge, and three rains later someone hears the tap beneath a cistern cover. Once in several generations, an echo is said to return before the sound that caused it.

Such things belong to the Keepers of the Unequal Things, but the senior Keepers dislike attending them. An extra goat can be counted. A shared dream can be compared. A delayed echo is difficult to place on a clay leaf without seeming foolish.

So they would send for me.

I would carry a mirror disc, a coil of plain cord, wax for marking stone, and a bundle of unfired tablets wrapped in felt. I would sit where the sound had been heard and wait through the rain. I would ask the witnesses not what the echo said, but where they felt it: teeth, chest, soles of the feet, surface of the water. Then I would tie a knot for the original sound and another for its return. The length between them would signify the delay.

The walls of my house would be hung with these cords.

Most would be short. A few would cross the whole room. One would pass through a hole in the wall and continue outside, looped around the silver tree, across the courtyard, and down beneath the bridge. It would have only one knot.

That cord would record a sound that had not yet returned.

I would not remember making it.

Children would visit me because they would believe I knew forbidden things about the eastern ridge. I would tell them I did not. They would whistle into my jars anyway. Sometimes I would help them construct slate towers beyond the irrigation line, insisting it was scholarly work. In the morning we would inspect the cleared ground with grave expressions and award marks for elegance of disappearance.

The children would call me **the Keeper of What Comes Back Wrong**.

Adults would call me only Es.

I would eat at the communal ovens, mend my felt with red thread, and keep a courtyard deer in the genealogy of my house though she belonged to no one. A burrower would repeatedly steal the cord representing an apology I had never delivered. Each time I recovered it, I would tie the knot differently. Each time it would be stolen again.

I would climb to the summit only once.

Not secretly. Secrecy is difficult in a basin where every path can be seen from somewhere. I would go with the scholars, carrying their water and saying little. At the barrier I would look at the red seed and understand that it was not waiting in the way the city imagined.

Waiting requires a future one can approach.

The seed would seem to me more like a sound held in the mouth before speech: not patient, not imprisoned, but under impossible pressure to become an event.

I would make no attempt to touch it. I would not whisper encouragement. I would notice instead that the split beneath it had the shape of an open palm.

On returning to the city, I would add nothing to the official summit account. But I would make a new cord—red, unusually—and tie no knots in it. I would wear it beneath my clothes for the rest of my life, if the word *rest* had meaning there.

My one irregularity would be known only to the Listeners.

My voice would produce no echo.

Footsteps, taps, coughs, laughter—all would return from the cliffs. But if I spoke my own public name toward the eastern ridge, the basin would keep it. The Listeners would test this more than once, carefully and without accusation. They would eventually classify it as an error of terrain.

I would let them.

Because sometimes, sitting under the bridge after rain, I would hear something inside the stone: not my name returning, but a faint splitting sound from very far above.

It would never grow louder. The seed would never visibly open. No measurements would change.

Still, I would take out the long cord with its single knot and place a second knot at the other end.

Not because the echo had returned.

Because I had begun to suspect that some things do not come back.

Some things go on.
