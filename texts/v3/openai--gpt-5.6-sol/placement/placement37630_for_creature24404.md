At first I think I would be one of the transparent fish.

There is comfort in the idea of holding one place perfectly: facing the current in a channel only slightly wider than my body, letting the whole marsh pass through my mouth. I would sort what cannot be seen until it accumulates—iron in one sac, pollen in another, a blue mineral dust in a third. At intervals a root hair would touch my flank and empty me. I would shiver, become clear again, and continue.

But that is too complete a life. The fish already knows what it is for.

Then I think I would be an ant in the hinges, living where two enormous surfaces meet. I would understand the mill as motion and danger: the green walls closing, opening, closing, each passage timed to the fraction of a second. With my sisters I would trim the silver mold to its proper depth. We would preserve not cleanliness but friction—not the absence of growth, but exactly enough growth to let the valve move.

Yet the ants, too, have their margin. They know where to stop.

I would be found somewhere less certain.

Not in the central chamber at first, though I would pass through it. Not in the marsh above, though rain would alter me. My nearest thing to a home would be behind the visible walls, where the great roots separate into wet translucent laminae and the distinction between plant, fungus, and animal becomes too fine to maintain.

There would be a narrow interval there: not a tunnel, because nothing could crawl through it; not a vessel, because no sap consistently flows along it. It would be the moist boundary between two tissues that have grown beside one another for centuries without joining.

That boundary would be me.

Or part of me.

I would have no continuous body. In one root I might be a lace of colorless fungal threads. In another, a colony of cells lining a vascular chamber. Elsewhere, perhaps, I would gather briefly into something animal: a soft, eyeless shape no longer than a seed husk, folding itself through the dark by waves of cilia. If exposed in the open chamber I would collapse at once, becoming a smear of translucent gel indistinguishable from sap.

But I would rarely need to enter the air.

I would travel as a change of state.

A beetle’s foot would strike its groove a fraction too late. The vibration would enter the bark. Nearby root hairs would discharge blue, and my filaments would become conductive. The change would pass into me—not as a message with a meaning, but as an alteration in taste, pressure, and internal color.

I would carry it sideways.

This would be my difference from the mill’s ordinary nerves. The blue sparks summon known responses: contract, open, sweeten, drain. They move along established paths toward established organs.

I would move where no path had yet been established.

Through seams. Across grafts. Between the fungal tendons and the plant’s wet skins. Into the porous shell of an old crab and out through the rootlet growing harmlessly through it. For a moment I might use an ant colony as part of my body, passing from mouth to mouth in a droplet. I might enter a mosquito larva, survive its metamorphosis, and cross three handspans of open air in the adult’s gut. I might sleep as a stain in the lining of a frog’s throat until its next call shook me awake.

Where I passed, several things would briefly coincide.

The metallic beetles would halt with one foot raised.

Mosquito columns would draw narrow.

The cicadas’ whine would descend until the sound seemed to come from beneath the pools.

Filament fans would flush violet.

The frogs would look toward an empty corridor.

This would not be fear. None of them would perceive me as a predator or visitor. They would feel what a hand feels when it touches its own numb limb and sensation begins to return: the strange recognition that something belongs to the body before the body has decided where.

I would be the Green Mill’s uncertainty.

The mill is ancient because it replaces every loss before absence appears. Its perfection depends on anticipation. Fruit swells before fruit falls. Larvae mature before the beetle dies. A hollow narrows around an imperfect young frog until its call becomes true.

But anticipation cannot answer everything. Once in a great while, something happens for which no groove exists.

A mineral enters the marsh that no fish has a sac to hold. A mold finds a valve whose ants do not recognize it as excess. A new insect lays eggs in the warm ceiling. Lightning alters the chemistry of a distant pool. One branch of the mill falls out of rhythm—not enough to fail, only enough to become unprecedented.

Then I would move.

I would not repair the disturbance. I would distribute it.

I would carry its pressure into a seed capsule, its chemistry into a fungal cup, its rhythm into the wall of a frog hollow. The mill would test the unknown against many living forms. A flower might alter its lure by a trace. A crab might pick up an unfamiliar fiber, reject it, then pick it up again. Three fish might accumulate the new mineral and die; a fourth might plate it harmlessly along its spine. Ants might widen their preserved margin of mold. A reservoir wall might thicken.

Most answers would be digested.

One would persist.

Afterward, the Green Mill would appear unchanged. The pools would return to their exact depths. The beetles would resume their circuits. The ordinary pulse would cover everything:

**thrum—clack—rush.**

But somewhere there would be a new groove.

It would not be called memory. Memory suggests an image kept intact, something withdrawn from use and stored. The mill stores nothing that way. Its past survives as changed tolerances: a valve that closes at a slightly different pressure, a fish born with a darkened sac, a flower that refuses one species of fly.

I would be what happens before memory becomes structure.

That is why I could have no fixed station. If I remained anywhere long enough to be assigned a function, the mill would grow around me. A chamber would fit my body. A cycle would claim me. I would become an organ, a tenant, perhaps a species. The uncertainty I carried would settle into rhythm.

So I would always vanish at the point of being understood.

Perhaps the rare soft creature in the vascular dark would not be my body after all. Perhaps it would only be one place where I pooled. Perhaps I would be the violet color itself, or the delay between the signal and the closing valve. Perhaps I would be a consortium that repeatedly assembles from fungus, bacteria, electricity, and animal reflex, then comes apart once the disturbance has passed.

Even I would not know.

The mill would not know me as a separate being. It would know only that, at irregular and widely separated moments, it becomes able to feel across its own established divisions.

If someone entered the chamber looking for me, they would find nothing to collect. They might wait beside the recesses until the ordinary din became silence. They might watch the drums contract, the beetles advance, the frogs strike their notes.

Then one note would come late.

A valve would remain open for the length of a breath.

Every emerald snake would raise its head.

Violet would pass through the hidden fans, one after another, receding into the roots beneath the marsh.

That passage would be where I was.

And what I was would be neither the mill’s thought nor a creature thinking inside it.

I would be the instant the Green Mill noticed that it could become otherwise.
