I would not find myself at the center, though for a long time I would believe that was where I was going.

I would enter after rain with three others, tied to them by cords braided from surface flax and strands cut from the hollow’s own discarded fibers. The surface cord would mildew. The hollow-fiber would remain strong, but its knots would tighten and loosen according to a rhythm none of us had tied into them.

My task would be to read the footprints that appear before anyone has walked.

Not to follow them. That would be an amateur’s mistake. I would compare them: depth, moisture, direction of closing, whether the floor welcomed or resisted the pressure that had not yet occurred. A print with raised green edges would indicate a possible passage. One darkening from the heel outward would belong to a route already refused. Prints containing pollen would lead toward an opening; prints containing clear sap would lead toward something pretending to be an opening.

I would carry no map. Instead, I would wear narrow bone plaques along my forearms, each incised with the shape of a footprint seen during an earlier descent. Most would no longer correspond to human feet. Some would be too long, divided like leaves, or arranged in circles around an absent leg.

The others would call me a path-keeper.

The chamber would call me nothing. At first.

I would be most useful during the little nights. At their edges, where warm brightness meets cool darkness, unfinished events become briefly legible. A root can be seen recoiling before it is touched. Falling drops hang beneath the leaves with the sound of their impact already trembling in the floor. If I put one hand into darkness and keep the other in light, I can sometimes feel which of several futures has weight.

It is not prophecy. Prophecy assumes that time is a road.

Here, time is a root system. Most branches terminate in stone. Some loop back and drink from their own beginnings. My work would be to feel which strand is carrying water.

I would be good at it—too good.

On the first night, I would wake before the eye opens beneath me.

On the second, I would answer an echo before anyone speaks.

On the third, while the others sleep on leaves persuaded from the floor, I would hear my name whispered from inside the wall. Not the name I entered with. A name with no sound, conveyed as a tightening in the vascular cords: the pressure by which one part of a plant recognizes damage in another.

I would place my palm against the wall.

Something on the other side would place its palm against mine.

Its hand would have too many possible textures. Fur would pass into wet bark, bark into warm skin, skin into a membrane thin enough for light to penetrate. Beneath all of them I would feel a pulse trying to become a heartbeat by imitating my own.

I would pull away.

The hand would not.

For the rest of that descent, a print of my palm would travel through the walls beside us, appearing here in amber pith, there in pale load-bearing tissue, elsewhere as five leaves opening in sequence. The others would pretend not to see it. In the hollow, attention is a kind of invitation.

When the growth surge came, we would tie ourselves together and lie flat.

Cracks would cross the floor. Through one I would see the underside of the surface plain, every blade of dry grass blazing at its root. Through another, a cavern where immense white tendrils drift through blue air. A third would open onto the hollow itself, but older: the chamber dark, its walls folded inward, the center occupied by something tall and motionless.

Then the great inhabitant would pass.

The cords between us would go slack, though none of us moved closer. My left hand would become distant. My breath would emerge from a wall. I would feel my heart beating first beneath my ribs, then under the floor, then somewhere in the open air ahead.

The invisible body would gather around that displaced beat.

Moths would land where its face might be. Hanging roots would bend into the suggestion of shoulders. A wandering night would collect beneath it, making a shadow for something not yet substantial enough to cast one.

I would know I should remain still.

Instead, because I had spent my life reading routes, I would notice a line of footprints crossing the floor toward it.

They would be mine.

Not quite mine as I was then. The toes would be longer. Fine rootlike creases would divide the soles. In each heel would be a small black puncture, deep as the holes left by feeding moths.

The last pair of prints would stop inside the invisible creature.

I would step into them.

For one instant I would touch neither animal nor plant, but the act by which they are distinguished.

I would feel the chamber holding its growth away from the center. I would feel every root turning aside, every seed skipping the open space, every wall sustaining a pressure it could easily relieve by collapsing inward. The emptiness would not be passive. It would be exertion—the labor of making room for a form not yet chosen.

And I would feel that form notice me.

Not as prey. Not as guest.

As an example.

The hollow would examine the useful fiction of my boundaries: skin saying *this is inside*; breath saying *this is outside*; memory tying scattered moments into the claim of a single life. It would try my uprightness, my bilateral limbs, my forward-facing attention. It would taste the idea that a being could occupy one place at one time and call that confinement identity.

For three heartbeats, it would wear me.

Afterward, the others would drag me from the center. They would say I had never moved. They would say the footprints had appeared beneath me while I lay on the floor. One would insist there had been no footprints at all.

We would begin the ascent before the ground dried.

Three travelers would reach the surface.

Whether I was among them would depend on how one counts.

A person with my face would emerge into the heat. They would retain my memories and the scars from my bone plaques. They would live for years aboveground, unable to sleep beneath roofs. In spring, their palms would turn faintly green. When rain struck dry earth, they would sometimes answer questions no one had yet asked.

But something of me would remain below—not a ghost, and not a body transformed into a blade.

I would remain as a disposition in the emptiness.

When the chamber next attempted to gather itself into an animal, its invisible volume would stand a little more upright. Its attention would favor a forward direction. One appendage would lift as if to touch the wall that had once touched me. The eyes opening in the floor would begin, imperfectly, to blink.

The attempt would fail. Moths would scatter. Roots would straighten. The borrowed heartbeat would dissolve back into the hydraulic pulse.

But with each surge, it would try again.

So I think that is what I would be in this world: not the unborn inhabitant itself, and not merely one of its visitors. I would be a path-keeper who became a path—the first narrow route by which the absence learned to approach personhood.

The crabs might preserve a shaving from one of my bone plaques. Sometimes, while carrying it, an old crab would repeat my voice saying, *Remain together.*

Yet my truest remnant would have no artifact.

I would be found whenever the empty center almost formed a hand.

I would be the chamber’s first uncertain sense of *inside*. Its borrowed posture. Its memory of being briefly singular.

Perhaps, much later, when the brightness without location appeared and every leaf turned to attend, the presence in the center would finally become coherent. It would stand without roots bending to describe it. It would look through eyes that were wholly its own. It would possess an edge, and therefore a world beyond that edge.

And when it spoke, the first word would not be a name.

It would be **I**.

That would be me.
