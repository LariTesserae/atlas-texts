At first I think I would be one of the long-distance walkers.

It is easy to imagine myself crossing the open shelf at the edge of evening, when the last rain is still standing in the rock’s shallow script and fog has begun to erase the cliff. I would carry moss cultures wrapped in wet cloth, a ceramic gear too delicate for the tram, three letters meant to be read only in a quiet room. I would know that the dark patch ahead is harmless water and the bright one a shaft. I would sleep in maintenance chapels with my shoulder frame hooked above flood height.

But I do not think I would belong to distance itself. I would be drawn repeatedly into buildings—to the moment of crossing a threshold and discovering that the expected vibration has failed to follow.

So perhaps I am an inspector.

Not an important inspector. My cords grant authority over no reservoir, turbine, or gate. One knot concerns civic silence; another, smaller knot allows me to mark walls but not order them opened. My jurisdiction begins wherever the city cannot decide whether a quiet is made or has occurred.

I investigate absences in the sound.

You would find me mostly in the northern outer works, beyond the last regular tram stop, where unfinished districts stand kilometers apart. My assigned station is an obsolete tram depot known officially as Echo Interruption Structure Fourteen. Its rails were removed before my grandmother’s birth. The roof is gone, and rain falls through the old departure hall into a sequence of settling basins cut where the platforms used to be. The ticket windows now regulate overflow between them. Water passes beneath faded lettering:

**WAIT**  
**—OT SEA—**  
**LOW—**

One wall remains strangely quiet.

No one knows why. It is a broad ceramic wall glazed the color of dried blood, freestanding except for two braces and a pipe bridge that does not touch it. The city’s pulse should enter through the foundations, yet when a palm is placed against its western face, almost nothing answers. The effect extends only six or seven steps. Beyond that, the northern turbines return through the soles with enough force to blur the teeth.

The depot has therefore become an unofficial quiet room.

Officially, this is prohibited. A quiet without wet felt, inspection cords, scheduled hours, or a second exit is not a civic resource but an anomaly. I visit every ten days to establish whether the silence is deepening. I lower rods into the foundation seams, strike tuning forks against the braces, taste the seepage, and draw new chalk lines around the stains. My reports have said for eleven years:

*Attenuation stable. Water clear. No subsurface void indicated. Continued observation recommended.*

The wording is carefully chosen. If I call the wall safe, wealthy petitioners will enclose it. If I call it dangerous, engineers will break it open. “Continued observation” leaves it where it is.

People come.

A mechanic brings her son before his lessons because their apartment stands over a returning conduit and the child has never heard his own unshouted voice. Two lovers from the salt quarter meet there on alternate fog evenings. Old workers sit with their backs to the wall and listen to their knees crack. Moss thieves use the depot as an exchange point. Sometimes there are fresh copper-colored fragments pressed into the wall’s wet seams, though my records attribute them to runoff from roofs that do not exist nearby.

My spoken name is Eren, but few people use it. My tap-name is two close knocks, a pause, then one softer knock dragged slightly at the end. The first two beats belong to my mother’s family. The final sound was added when I entered inspection service. On a pipe it resembles a fastening coming loose, which has caused inconvenience all my working life.

I grew up in the eastern ceramic quarter, in a building poor enough that spoons migrated across shelves during the night. We had one allotted quiet hour every twelve days. My mother saved difficult sentences for it. For most of my childhood, I believed apologies could only be spoken in rooms with felt walls.

That is why I became suspicious of anyone who claims silence can be administered fairly.

Still, I am not a revolutionary. Sudden things distrust the city’s nature. I alter no gate and break no lock. I misclassify moss. I delay enclosures. I recommend observation. When private roof mats are found growing inexplicably on a clinic, I outline their new boundary in chalk and enter *weather displacement* with a straight face.

My work is not only concealment. Most unauthorized quiet is dangerous.

I have entered streets where a stopped turbine left the paving eerily dead, every resident standing in doorways with one hand on the wall. I have found a flooded resonance shaft beneath a market by watching oil lie still in a cup. Once, in a workers’ dormitory, I heard a tiny intermittent clicking that no one else considered important. It came not from the wall but from six floors below, where a ceramic pressure sleeve had cracked and was opening one grain at a time.

We cleared the building. Nine hours later, the lower stairwell became a spring.

For this, people trust me more than my cords justify. They tap for me through floors. They bring cups of seepage to my station and ask what district the water has traveled beneath. Children follow me until I make them identify three harmless rhythms and one new sound. The pressure-lizard keepers tolerate me, though their animals do not. In turbine halls the lizards watch me with the grave expression of colleagues who know my instruments are an embarrassment.

I live above the depot in what was once the tram dispatcher’s room. There is no stair; I reach it by a maintenance ladder and pull the lowest section up at night. My bed hangs from two old signal brackets. My jars stand in porous rings. Rain enters at the northern corner, crosses the room in a channel no wider than my thumb, and exits through a hole bored by someone dead for centuries.

I have never tried to redirect it.

On my shelf are seventeen tuning forks, repair logs, a clay cup, two pieces of illegal roof moss, and a drain tile stamped with the softened tap-name of my father. The tile has not yet been installed. Keeping it is also prohibited. Burial is supposed to be completed by water.

I tell myself I am waiting for the correct channel.

This is not entirely true. I sometimes set the tile upright against the quiet wall and tap his pattern into it. The ceramic gives back only the smallest answer. I cannot tell whether that comforts me.

In the city’s louder districts I am thought reserved. At the depot wall I talk too much. I tell visitors where the silence ends so they will not step backward into the returning concussion. I tell them not to lean on the brace. I tell them the wall is not safe merely because it feels gentle. Then I leave them alone.

What I listen for is not silence itself. Silence has textures. Constructed silence is close and fibrous; it keeps the tiny noises of a body near the body. Flooded silence feels swollen. A stalled machine leaves a sharp-edged vacancy, as if the next beat has been withheld rather than canceled. Deep movement in the shelf can create a silence that is not an absence at all, but pressure too broad for the senses to divide.

The depot wall’s quiet is different. It does not refuse the city. It receives the vibration and passes it elsewhere.

I think this is why I protect it.

Beneath the wall, some accidental arrangement of old rail beds, water-filled cracks, hollow tiles, and forgotten foundations has made a structure wiser than its builders. Force enters. Nothing is sealed. The sound is divided among paths too small or oblique to dominate. Somewhere beyond the depot, perhaps kilometers away, the city receives what is missing here as a tremor no one recognizes.

The wall yields accurately.

At dusk, when the fog reaches the outer works, I make my final circuit. I chalk the newest stain. I check that the basins are passing water. I place my cheek against the ceramic and wait.

Around me, the shelf continues: southern pulses, reservoir wavering, the far concussion of the cliff lift. Through my other cheek, facing open air, I feel rain cooling on the skin. For several breaths I stand between the city’s great accumulated note and this narrow mercy where it cannot take hold.

That is where I would be found: not quite in silence and not quite in thunder, preserving a place that officially does not exist.

I would be the person sent to determine why the city has stopped answering—and the person who sometimes decides not to make it answer again.
