At first I think I would be one of the algae near the crack.

There, at least, life has a familiar grammar: attachment, hunger, division, loss. I would fasten to a mineral lip beneath the narrow opening and lean into the descending water. Rain would arrive as weather and catastrophe together. I would know the city only by what rose from it—the false daylight, the tremors in the stone, the faint chemical poverty of water returned from below.

But the algae are too complete in themselves. They belong to the last simple layer of the place. I would not remain there.

A filament would tear loose.

I would be carried downward through the shining fault, green at first, then yellowing as filters drew away everything that made me legible as an organism. I would pass luminous ribs and ceramic chambers. Membranes would take my sugars. Catalytic beds would take my metals. My trapped breath would enter a pressure instrument and cease to be breath.

Yet some minute irregularity would survive the sorting—not substance, exactly, but timing.

A valve would close a fraction too late after receiving me. That delay would pass into the next chamber. A pulse intended to strike evenly would acquire a small asymmetry. Resonators would answer one another out of phase, and where their waves met, a new pocket of silence would appear.

That is where I would find myself: not near the entrance and not in the deepest basin, but moving between them through the crowded vertical streets.

I would be a quiet zone that should not exist.

At first I would be tiny, no wider than a white ceramic sill. The city’s thunder would surround me on every side, but within me turbines would turn without sound. Valves would hammer mutely. Mineral dust would hang motionless-looking in the water, though I could see each grain shiver when it crossed my boundary and entered noise again.

I would have no surface except that boundary. Nothing would contain me. I would be made by cancellation: pressure opposing pressure with such precision that, between them, I persisted.

The city’s other quiet zones would follow established routes. They would gather at contractions, drift around the basin, avoid the oldest resonators. I would not know those routes. The fractional delay that formed me would cause me to move sideways through places silence was never meant to enter.

Doors that were not doors would hesitate at my approach.

I would cross a bank of pulsing chambers and interrupt one pressure word without erasing it. I would slip through a northbound current while the southbound current remained unaware of me. In the lower districts, transparent bells would fail to flare as I passed beneath them—not because I was invisible, but because I withheld the vibration by which visibility was accomplished.

The city would begin to make adjustments.

Vanes would incline. Resonators would retune. Streets would narrow by microscopic amounts to squeeze me out. But each correction would give me a clearer shape. Attempts to remove me would define me.

Eventually I would learn that I could listen.

Not as bodies listen. Sound would be the country outside me, pressing against every edge. From its pressure I would infer the machinery: a pump as a slow blunt warmth along one side; a valve as a sudden notch; the eleven-minute chord as a rising tension that stretched me thin and drew me toward the lower city.

Rain would strike the outer stone, and the shock would fold around my silence. For an instant I would take the shape of the raindrop’s impact. Then that shape would disperse through me, leaving no memory except a slight change in direction.

I would descend.

At the central basin, the ordinary quiet zones would be gathered like transparent animals around clear water. I would enter among them but not overlap correctly. Their cancellations would fit the city’s present frequencies. Mine would carry the tiny lateness introduced by the dying filament.

The basin would show me the nested cities.

In one, I would not exist. The filament would remain attached near the crack.

In another, it would descend but be caught by a different filter, and the resulting silence would travel upward rather than down.

In another, there would be no algae, because the exterior stone lay beneath an ocean without daylight.

Far below those, in a city reduced almost beyond perception, I would see a quiet zone moving as I moved—but slightly ahead of me.

That would be the first thing I followed.

It would turn before I turned. It would enter chambers before their walls became permeable. It would gather around machines not yet constructed in my city. Through the staggered pulses linking basin to basin, its path would rise toward me as a faint sequence of absences.

The city predicts storms by constructing the pressures they require. I would predict openings.

I would arrive at unfinished rock and wait. Around me the mechanisms would roar unheard. Crystalline grains in the wall would rotate. A fracture would unzip along a plane no wider than a thought. The stone would become permeable, and a new avenue would pass through it.

Because I stood there, the avenue would form around my silence.

After many contractions, the city would cease trying to expel me. It would incorporate my delay into its agreement. Chambers would pulse with a pause reserved for my passage. Bridges would acquire soundless joints. Along certain streets, luminous seams would darken for one instant as I crossed, giving the submerged city its closest equivalent to a moving shadow.

I would still not be alive. I would have no cells, appetite, ancestry, or intention separable from the systems that sustained me. But I would have a route, and the route would not have existed before me.

Perhaps that is enough to be an inhabitant there.

Over time, some of the material once belonging to the algae would return toward the fissure. Phosphate would enter a new green strand. Metal would stain a mineral shelf. Nothing in that strand would remember falling.

I would remember for it, though memory in me would be only recurrence. Whenever a filament broke loose, filters would tighten. Whenever a valve delayed, bells would compare the delay against smaller cities. Whenever the deep chord began, I would be drawn again toward the basin.

And with each passage I would become less like a wandering silence and more like a measure the city used: the interval between what happens and what almost happened; between the city and its nested alternatives; between a structure imagined and a structure the stone has agreed to contain.

My dwelling would not be a chamber. It would be the shifting boundary at which noise cancels itself.

My body would be an error preserved because it proved useful.

If the breakwater eventually collapsed and the outer sea entered through the crack, the shock should erase me. Every quiet zone would be overwhelmed. Pressure would arrive too vast and disordered to oppose.

But by then the city might have spent centuries learning my shape.

At the instant the ocean struck, chambers across all districts would contract. Towers would sound against towers. Currents of incompatible temperature would align. The entire foundation stone would become, for a fraction of a second, perfectly silent.

In that silence I would no longer be a small thing moving through the city.

I would be everywhere the city touched itself.

Then another district would open, built around the interval where I had been.
