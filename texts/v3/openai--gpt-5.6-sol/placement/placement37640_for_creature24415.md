At first I think I would be one of the narrow figures made from overlapping shadows.

It is tempting to place myself there: visible only at the hour when categories loosen, carrying the posture of something that once had a body. I would stand near a cliff dwelling after sunset, not haunting it exactly, but repeating an unfinished departure. Travelers would be warned not to sleep near me. Herds would pass without fear. I would diminish against the seamless wall before dawn.

But that is too much like being remembered, and this country does not seem greatly interested in remembrance for its own sake. It preserves only what can still be used.

So perhaps I would be smaller.

A transparent knot sealed in stone, without mouth or sense, waiting through years of heat for water to make me possible. Rain would loosen me into a soft animal. For three days I would graze light and metal from the rock. I would acquire organs according to circumstance. I would leave descendants, or husks, or a film that keeps a thumb-sized pool from vanishing before night. Then I would fold shut again without knowing whether I had died.

There is an honesty in that life. Yet I do not think I would be content at the beginning of the water. I would want to follow what the water means.

I would be found farther downslope, where a dry channel leaves the open tableland and enters a long cut between scarps. There would be a damaged pylon there, leaning but not fallen, its lower half enclosed in pale mineral growth. A narrow shelter would stand half a day to the east. Between them, the riverbed would divide around a low island of floodwood and ceramic-barked plants.

The herds would know this place as a sequence rather than a location:

*white wall at noon—three cooling stones—bitter cistern—turn where the dead tree points—do not answer the second shadow.*

That sequence would be where I lived.

Not beside the route. Not traveling along it.

I would be the route’s obligation.

For part of each year I would have an animal body. It would be an old herd body, tall even among the long-legged browsers, with one rib sac collapsed and a patch of scales along the left flank that no longer turned fully white. Conductive mites would inhabit the dark seams of my back. Beneath my throat I would carry two grasping digits, one shortened by an old injury. A floodwood rod, polished at both ends, would rest in the fold of my shoulder.

Those presently crossing with me would not have a name for me. They would show my presence as a slow band of dull silver passing from the leading animal toward the rear: *the one who must leave us at the divided channel.*

I would not be their leader. Leadership is too simple and too continuous. On most days I might be the animal that walks last, chooses poor shade, or startles when pale fliers cross the sun. Young animals would shelter beneath me. Attendants would angle my damaged scales at noon. I would carry water farther than some and remember less than others.

But when the first hot rain struck the eastern scarps, something in the route would become more exact than my individual wishes.

I would leave the herd.

At the shelter I would lower my scales and approach until the shaded recess admitted me. Inside, the floor basin would be empty, though moisture would darken my feet. The branching inclusions in the walls would draw near my breath. I would place my brow against a particular place in the stone.

Nothing would speak.

Instead I would remember a turn I had never made.

The memory would not arrive as an image. My front foot would become heavy when pointed west. My injured digits would close around the floodwood rod. The mites along my spine would begin moving toward one another, assembling a dark line over the vertebrae. When I stepped outside, the light would seem fractionally tilted.

Then I would walk the watershed.

At each condensation pocket I would pause, not to drink but to disturb the mineral rim with the rod. At the glass field I would stand between two rows of pegs until my shadow persisted a little behind me. At the bitter cistern I would lower one grasping digit into the water but never mark its height.

By evening I would reach the leaning pylon.

The work required there would look insignificant. I would circle it once after rain, twice before lightning, never in the same direction as the fliers moving through the nearest hoop. I would press my flank against the mineral casing and allow the conductive mites to leave my body. They would enter hairline fractures, following a gradient too slight for me to perceive.

Perhaps that was all.

Perhaps the mites kept a junction from sealing. Perhaps the pressure of my body completed a circuit through wet stone. Perhaps my circling informed the burrowing colonies that the old ravine below should remain closed for another season.

I would not understand. Understanding would not be the virtue asked of me.

I would know only the acceptance.

The pylon would cool under my skin. My delayed shadow would draw even with my feet. Somewhere beneath the divided riverbed, a quantity of water would decide not to move yet.

Then the obligation would release me.

Some years I would return to the herd. Some years I would remain near the pylon through the dry season, browsing thorn tips and sleeping in its narrow shade. A hunter might settle into the opposite cliff and wait. We would become accustomed to one another without trust. When my scale language began to repeat itself—*flood coming, flood coming* under a sky that had been empty for months—the herd might widen around me.

The hunter would move once.

Afterward it would arrange my ribs in the runoff and leave the useful scales untouched. The herd would come when the heat diminished. They would recover the scales and carry them east. My attendants would gather the remaining mites. A young animal would nose the floodwood rod from beneath my shoulder and tuck it awkwardly against its own.

None of these would contain me.

Yet at the next hot rain, the young animal’s foot would become heavy when pointed west.

It would leave the others at the divided channel. The shelter would admit it. Inside, with its brow against the wall, it would remember a turn it had never made.

So I would not quite be the old animal, or the mites, or the shelter’s instruction. I would be the continuity among them: a task that has survived the loss of its explanation.

In drought I would exist faintly, distributed between the angle of a shed scale, the mineral taste of one cistern, a hunter’s choice not yet made, and the tendency of a herd to pause at a dead horizontal tree. Rain would gather these fragments into a self. For a day I would become decisive. I would borrow legs, skin, caution, and a damaged memory. I would walk west and perform the small act on which some unseen balance depends.

Then I would disperse again.

If asked who I was, I could give no name more permanent than the herds do. The nearest translation might be:

*that which goes to the leaning pylon when the first warm water enters the divided channel.*

And where would I find myself?

Not at the pylon. Not in the animal approaching it. Not even in the route.

I would find myself at the moment the stone cools beneath an offered flank and, far underground, water changes its mind.
