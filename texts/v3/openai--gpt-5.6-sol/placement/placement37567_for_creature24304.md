At first I would mistake myself for an echo.

I would know the chamber by its returns: the near wall answering sharply, the pool giving back a flatter sound, the open nave multiplying one cry until it seemed to belong to a flock larger than the flock that made it. I would know when bats were passing overhead because the air altered against my face. I would know when pigs were rooting two ridges away because their blunt impacts traveled through the shelf beneath my feet.

Gradually, the world would separate into surfaces.

There would be the cold ledge under my claws, rough enough to grip. A wall close behind me, damp along one seam and dry where my body habitually rested. Below, a long gray band would fall from a fissure I had never passed through. It would illuminate a pond, two moss islands, and the upper branches of a shrub permanently inclined toward the light. Beyond that band, the country would not end. It would merely cease to show itself.

I would live at that border.

Not in the deepest dark, where eyes have become an unnecessary expense, and not near enough to the surface to know wind, rain, or the full disk of the sun. My country would consist of shelves, bright patches and traversable gaps. The floor, so important to deer and foxes, would be only one layer among many. I would cross it rarely. Pillars would be stepping-stones for flight. Fallen blocks would be tables, hiding places, listening posts. The roof would have its own roads, occupied by streams of bats whose passage I had learned to enter and leave without collision.

For a while I might believe I was one of them. The darkness would move whenever they moved, and I would move with it. But I would not sleep hanging among folded wings. I would watch the bats instead.

I would know which colonies dropped the richest scraps. I would recognize the hour—not an hour measured by light, but by the loosening of claws above—when pups shifted, beetles surfaced, and small edible things began to fall. I would wait on a sloping block while the guano field below awakened into a black, granular current.

Then I would descend in two beats of broad wings.

I would be a cave crow.

My feathers would be black without gloss except in the strongest fissure-light, where they might show a dull blue at the edges. In darkness my body would disappear before my eyes did. Creatures below would sometimes see only two brief reflections change position on a ledge.

My beak would be scarred near the tip from probing limestone cracks. One claw would have grown crooked after an old break, though it would still close around a twig. I would carry no lasting possession. Yet I would have preferences.

Among the stems growing near the pond, I would choose those that were resilient rather than brittle. I would pin one beneath my foot, strip away the soft leaves, and pull it sideways against a rough lip of stone until the end narrowed. The action would not feel ingenious. It would feel obvious in the way a particular landing shelf felt obvious, or the way one crack among a hundred announced, through smell and minute motion, that a larva occupied it.

I would insert the stem. Twist. Withdraw. Inspect. Try again.

If the larva fastened its jaws to the fibers, I would draw it out and eat it. If the tool split, I would discard it. If it remained useful, I might carry it to a second crack, perhaps a third. Then something else would take my attention—a frog shifting at the pool edge, a fox entering the light, another crow calling from beyond the pillars—and the stem would fall among ordinary stems.

Nothing would mark it as mine.

My nest would stand on a shelf above the broadest pool, protected from droppings by an overhang and from climbing animals by a smooth vertical face. It would be built of roots, shrub twigs, coarse grass, deer hair and the translucent membranes of bat wings. I would repair it without expecting it to outlast me. Beetles would already inhabit its lower layers. Moss would take the damp side. When it was finally abandoned, the cavern would dismantle it patiently but not slowly: fibers taken for other nests, hair eaten by larvae, sticks knocked into the water, membranes reduced to their useful elements.

I would remember much, but nothing abstractly.

I would remember the pale-backed sow whose rooting exposed beetle grubs. I would follow her at a safe distance and know the exact reach of her temper. I would remember a fox with a torn ear and recognize the difference between its hunting walk and its merely passing walk. I would remember which deer tolerated my landing near their mineral wall and which struck upward with their short antlers.

I would remember other crows by voice. Some would be kin; some rivals; some merely recurrent presences in neighboring bands of light. Their ranges would overlap mine unevenly. We might gather where pigs had overturned a fresh patch, quarrel loudly, feed side by side, and then vanish into different darknesses.

There would be customs I practiced without knowing they were customs.

At my pond, shellfish would be dropped from a particular shoulder-high shelf because the stone beneath was broad and hard. At another pond, crows might never have discovered this. I would taper probes because I had watched older beaks taper them. A young bird following me would seize the wrong end, snap the stem, lose interest, try again days later. No lesson would be declared. No knowledge would be stored except in bodies willing to repeat what they had seen.

I would sometimes fly upward along the fissure.

Not because I dreamed of the sky. I would have no image of open blue and no inherited sorrow clear enough to name. I would climb because gnats gathered higher, because the limestone held convenient projections, because the grayness strengthened there.

The passage would narrow. My wings would begin to strike stone. Cool air would descend past me carrying scents from a world I had never entered: leaves in sunlight, rain not yet filtered through rock, perhaps flowers, perhaps smoke from some unimaginably distant fire.

I would go no farther.

I would return to the shelf where the chamber opened beneath me, and the buried country would receive my call. It would travel beyond every place I knew. Other crows might answer. Echoes certainly would. I would be unable to distinguish the final repetitions from voices.

That would not trouble me.

I would find myself, then, not at the center of this world but along one of its narrow illuminations: high above a stable pond, below a fissure that never shows the sky, where moss, frogs, deer and shrubs briefly become visible before the stone takes them back.

And I would be one black, observant, tool-using bird—clever enough to exploit the country, not powerful enough to alter it; capable of memory, but not history.

When I died, the habits would remain without me. Another crow would land on my shelf. It would sharpen a stem against the same rough edge, watch the same pig fields, and drop shells onto the same hard stone.

It would not inherit my name.

It would inherit my motions.
