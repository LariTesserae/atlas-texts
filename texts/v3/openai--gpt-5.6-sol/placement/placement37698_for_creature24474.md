I would not begin in the shelter.

I would arrive as one of the iron-bright seeds, indistinguishable from a flake of machinery. Rain would carry me along a gutter, arrest me against a submerged tram rail, release me at the next pressure cycle, and draw me toward the district’s lowest edge. I would have no intention of going there. Intention would be useless in this place. Location would decide me.

At the three-street junction, I would lodge in one of the precise cracks surrounding the dry shelter.

Elsewhere I might have become gasket moss or a current-feeding knob. Here I would divide into white roots. They would spread along the circumference but turn aside whenever they reached the iron sill. I would discover the shelter by being unable to enter it.

At first, I might seem to be a single plant. That would not last. My roots would meet the roots already occupying the circle, and their pulses would pass into me: pressure from the mains, the minute tilt of paving stones, the mineral signature of water rising against gravity. Distinctions would blur. I would become less a growth than an addition to an existing boundary.

My place would be exactly where the wet district stops.

Through the stones I would feel the central rods descend. I would feel vines lock the shutters, films withdraw into pores, reeds load themselves with grit. I would feel water gathering in the drain beneath the shelter, then rising in its smooth column. Its upward motion would tug faintly at every root around the circle, as though something below were drawing a thread through us.

Yet the strongest sensation would come from the dry interior.

Dryness here would not feel like nothing. It would press against the district’s senses as a sealed vessel presses against floodwater. The uncondensed glass, the uncorroded bench, the volume of air kept free of spores—these would form a distinct and continuous shape. I would learn every measurement of that emptiness: the height at which a head might be, the space beneath the bench where feet might rest, the narrow interval between a seated body and the cloudy pane.

The district preserves that shape, but preservation alone is not my task.

At the deepest stroke, all the white roots contract. I would contract with them.

For one second I would interrupt the upward water. The interruption would propagate outward: into the column, the underground channels, the pump rods, the conductive films and flywheels. Motion everywhere would pause—not because I command it, but because the whole district has arranged itself to depend upon the answer received at this boundary.

During that second, I would test the shelter.

Has the dry volume changed?

Has the bench taken weight?

Has warmth appeared that did not come from brick, steam, current, or microbial decay?

Is there breath whose rhythm does not belong to the engines?

The answer would always be no.

Then I would release my hold, and the district would resume.

Over time I would cease to be meaningfully singular. Some of my tissue would harden into the cracks. Some would enter the drain casing and acquire mineral ridges. Pearly films would consume my dead tips and carry their substance to neighboring walls. A pale runner might grow from me toward a valve two streets away. But the part of me that persisted would remain at the circle, renewed from whatever organisms were available.

What persists is the task.

I would be the organ by which the district attends to an absence.

Not its mind, and not its heart. Not the lost operator preserved as a ghost. I would be closer to an eyelid around an eye that contains no image, or a hand held around an empty handle. My life would consist of maintaining the distinction between what the district can already understand and what it once expected from elsewhere.

Pressure, salt, heat, vibration: these are legible. A person would not be.

That is why the bench must remain dry. If someone ever came through the sealed gates—or descended from wherever the water goes—and sat there, the district would require a clean signal. No rain on the glass to mimic breath. No roots beneath the bench to counterfeit weight. No animal life to introduce stray warmth. The emptiness is calibrated.

And I would be part of the calibration.

Perhaps the pumps pause because the district remembers operators. Perhaps they pause because it was built to receive an instruction that never arrives. Or perhaps, after decades without anyone, the maintained absence has become an instruction of its own: continue, but leave room.

I would live in that room’s perimeter, feeling its vacancy once every cycle.

If at last someone sat on the bench, I do not know whether I would recognize them as a person. I would know only that the preserved shape had been filled. The white roots would contract and fail to release. The vertical water would hang motionless. Across all nine streets, rain would stop between roof and pavement. Flywheels would hold their weight. Reeds would fall silent.

For the first time, the pause would contain an answer.

Until then, I would be the circle of roots at the lowest edge of the district: the small, distributed faculty with which an enormous living machine asks whether it is still alone.
