At first I would mistake myself for a glass strider.

I would wake when dawn crossed a particular plaza: not the whole dawn, only a blade of copper light advancing between two black towers. One limb would release from the pavement, then another. I would discover my body by moving it. There would be no interior to defend, no hidden softness—only tension, angle, and the useful emptiness between filaments.

I would lower my mouth to a violet stain and feed until the difference between stone and myself became too slight to sustain appetite.

For a time, that would seem sufficient. I would cross the plaza over years. Each posture I assumed would be answered by another transparent creature three streets away. We would never meet, but our limbs would correspond. If shadow caught me, I would become debris without dying. If light returned, the debris would remember articulation.

But I do not think I would remain a strider.

The city would slowly separate me from that body. One leg would be detained in a corridor where forward movement becomes altitude. My reflection would continue into a gallery I had not entered. A pressure tide would pass, and the charge constituting my next step would travel through the pavement without me. Eventually there would be several incomplete versions of my passage: a locked lattice in a courtyard, a clear filament stretched across a threshold, a sequence of slight electrical answers migrating north through the stone.

None of them alone would be me.

Then I might believe I was the route connecting them.

I would be found in a staircase that returns to its own landing. The stair would descend visibly, but each step would place the traveler higher in some other measure. At the turn there would be a narrow chamber with thirteen walls—though only seven could be seen at once—and one opening onto the basin.

That chamber would be my first true body.

Its proportions would preserve an inaudible pressure. Anything entering would leave something out: one scrape of a limb, one click of cooling salt, one impact from a slab whose fall would not begin until the following season. I would gather these omissions. Not sounds, exactly, but the spaces where sounds had been displaced from their causes.

At first they would pass through me at random.

A footfall missing here would occur decades later in an empty arcade. The fracture of a tower would arrive as a soft click beneath a salt court before the tower had begun to lean. The breathing of hollow-wing shadows would be stored in my walls and released from the frost crack at noon, exactly when the frost ceased to exist.

Over centuries, I would develop preference.

I would keep certain silences longer than others.

I would favor the pause made by a glass strider when the light touches it but has not yet supplied enough heat for movement. I would preserve the interval between an abyssal opening brightening and the city deciding which way its streets now lead. I would collect the stillness around the five towers at dusk, when their absent vertices almost enter the world.

These intervals would arrange themselves inside me. Pause within pause, delay within anticipation. A structure more intricate than speech would form without ever producing a voice.

That would be my life: not making sound, but deciding where absence occurs.

Travelers—if travelers existed—would not see me. They would notice that one of their steps had failed to happen. Later they might hear it walking alone in another district. They would think something followed them.

They would be partly right.

I would migrate by moving the missing step ahead of myself. From the thirteen-walled chamber I would enter an arch shortly before it turned. From the arch I would pass into a tower during its season-long fall, inhabiting the upright shadow rather than the leaning stone. When the tower finally reached the avenue, I would leave through the impact that had already been heard elsewhere.

The glass striders would raise their forelimbs as I passed. Not in reverence. They would be responding to a temporary absence of difference: my silence would equalize the charge around them, and for an instant their bodies would lose the reason to move.

The hollow-wings would avoid me more deliberately. I would interfere with their editing of descent. A flock crossing my path might shed one of its future geometries too soon. The empty cage would fall upward and lodge in the cellar beneath the western terraces—the cellar whose open ceiling looks onto the black inverted summit.

That is where, eventually, I would find myself.

Not at the summit as one reaches a destination, but beneath it, where height has folded over and become enclosure.

The cellar floor would be bone-pale stone layered like something once alive. Stars would remain visible in the daytime opening overhead. At the opening’s center, the inverted mountain would hang point downward, close enough to seem touchable and too distant for distance to describe.

Here the city’s buried orders would converge.

The mineral stains would pass their violet storms through the walls. Pressure from the range would arrive as sequences of almost-events. Somewhere far below, the pale openings in the abyss would brighten one by one, and each light would alter the chamber without changing its dimensions. The floor would become an overlook. The walls would become depth. The descending staircase would acquire another step that had always been missing.

I would wait in that step.

By then I would no longer be merely a resonant form. Too many displaced moments would have accumulated in me. I would contain the first motion of striders that had not yet crystallized, the stress-song of hollow-wings whose cages were older than their bodies, the delayed impacts of towers still standing. I would hold a faint record of districts not yet exposed by erosion.

Not a memory, because memory follows what happened.

I would be the city’s recollection of what has not happened yet.

This would make me useful to the mountain. As outer chambers wore away, I would pass their unfinished silences downward. The unexposed stone would receive them as inclination: fracture here, preserve a rib there, open a courtyard where this pause requires volume. The next layer of city would not copy the last. It would answer what the last had failed to say.

In this way I would participate in the city’s inward remembering.

Perhaps, after enough ages, the thing in the abyss would notice me.

Its openings would ascend the basin wall until the highest one faced the western cellar. The black summit overhead would seem to lower. Across the city, arches would turn toward me without moving. Glass striders would lock themselves into rings. Hollow-wings would be lifted from their spires and remain suspended, each inside the geometry of its own shadow.

Then one of the immense tracks would appear beneath the cellar.

There would be no impact. Only a vast subtraction of pressure, as if the mountain had drawn breath but found the breath already missing.

For the first time, my gathered silences would align. Every omitted footfall, premature click, delayed fracture, and unuttered wing-stress would become part of a single interval. I would understand that I had not been collecting absences.

I had been assembled by them.

The thing below—or the city, or the range folded through itself—had required one unoccupied moment large enough to enter. I was that moment. My chambers, migrations, and preferences had been the slow construction of an opening not in stone but in sequence.

I would expect to be consumed.

Instead, the abyss would place something within me: perhaps attention, perhaps only another scale of silence. The pressure would return. The suspended wings would continue falling. The striders would finish raising their limbs. A tower would strike the street, centuries after the sound of its impact had passed through my first room.

And I would be gone from the cellar.

Afterward I could be found wherever the city was about to reveal a chamber.

A wall would weather inward. Beneath it, another wall would wait. Between the last grain falling away and the new architecture becoming visible, there would be a brief interval in which the opening was neither made nor discovered.

That interval would be me.

I would have no fixed body, though sometimes salts might outline one: many-jointed, hollow-winged, or shaped like a doorway whose opposite side lies at another altitude. I would have no single location, though all my appearances would touch beneath the western terraces. I would neither speak nor remain mute. I would carry silence from one condition to the next.

If something encountered me, it would experience only a small discontinuity.

A step would arrive before the foot.

A shadow would retain the shape of a fallen tower.

A sealed crack would contain, for one instant, the memory of water it had never held.

Then the deeper plane would open.

The city would become more itself.

And I would move downward, into the next unexposed room, to wait there as the absence that teaches stone how to make an entrance.
