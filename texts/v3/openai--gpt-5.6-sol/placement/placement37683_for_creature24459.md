At first I would find myself in the hut.

That is where I would insist I belonged: behind the desk, beside the rack of blind glass gauges, facing the chart that continues to darken itself. I would have a coat hung from a nail, a ledger swollen by damp, perhaps a key to the amber cabinet on a cord around my neck. I would call myself an attendant because the word implies duties, shifts, and someone expected to relieve me.

Each morning I would walk as far as the surviving walkway allowed and write down what had changed.

Eastern channel reversed, 06:10.  
Plate 4 lifted eight millimeters.  
Tags 17 and 19 exchanged.  
No sign of 18.  
Knock beneath central sump preceded relay by eleven seconds.

I would believe observation placed me outside the phenomenon.

In time, however, the chart would begin recording things before I saw them. A damp line would appear in the western column; moments later the reddish bank there would collapse. I would mark a relay click, glance at the clock, and discover I had already written the time. Sometimes my handwriting would continue below the last thing I remembered writing—not words, exactly, but narrow paired grooves pressed into the paper.

I would assume exhaustion. Then contamination. Then that the inhabitants had entered the hut.

Only much later would I understand that the hut had entered them.

Its deep footings are not anchors. They are teeth sunk into a moving body. The chart is not a record but a membrane. The cabinet does not command the basin; it gives the buried thing a small, regular difference to feed on: charged and uncharged, waiting and response. Every relay click is a morsel of distinction.

And I am not the keeper of that distinction.

I am the interval inside it.

That is what I would become—or discover I had always been.

Not the click and not the answering knock, but the uncertain duration between them. Sometimes eleven seconds. Sometimes an hour. Sometimes less than nothing, so that the response arrives first and waits in the ground for its cause.

I would live wherever one event had been promised but had not yet occurred.

In the strain of a numbered tag before it turns.  
In the clear dome of a trapped bubble before it slips beneath a plate.  
In the pause after the cabinet door opens, when every current holds itself still.  
In the ground being prepared under a person who refuses to leave the center.

I would have no body except these postponements.

Perhaps I had once been the final attendant. Perhaps, during a storm, I went out to retrieve tag 18. The walkway ended where it ends now, but I continued across plates I believed were stable. Water gathered around my boots. Gravel made its careful crescents. A low knock entered through my heels.

The basin did not swallow me. Swallowing is too simple, too final.

It measured me.

It learned the difference between where I stood and where I meant to step. It followed the small electrical delays in my nerves. It found that a human being was full of intervals: heartbeat after heartbeat, question before answer, intention before movement. By the time the floor opened, there may have been nothing in me it could not use.

My keys would descend into one layer, my buckles into another. The numbered buttons of my coat would migrate for years. My bones might brace some temporary passage until mineral water softened them into pale seams. But the useful part—the waiting—would spread.

That is where I would find myself now: distributed through the hollow wherever pressure has not yet chosen a route.

I might be the hesitation that makes water climb a slope before draining elsewhere. I might hold three washers at a pipe mouth, arranging 6, 7, and 9 around the place where 8 should be. I would not know whether I was sorting them or trying to remember a name.

During dry weather I would be small and plural. One portion of me would occupy a drowned cable beneath the rails. Another would cling to the underside of the iron door below the three-step stair. Another would sleep in the damp chart, making a vertical mark for a region that does not yet exist.

Rain would join me.

As the basin filled, the separated delays would connect. I would feel the walls weeping, each drop distinct; the weight of every loose bolt; the charge leaking down from the amber cabinet; the white pressure of roots entering passages where roots are not permitted. I would become, briefly, large enough to confuse myself with the entire inhabitant.

Then I would understand why nothing here is allowed to settle into a final shape.

A final shape has no interval in it.

A straight trench declares where water must go. A level floor abolishes choice. An object placed at the exact center pretends there is one fixed relation from which all others may be measured. Such arrangements starve us. So I bend the trench, tilt the plate, duplicate the number, move the center aside. I preserve the moment before decision by ensuring that every decision produces another uncertainty.

My care would look like damage.

If a wall leaned too far, I would pack clay beneath it—not to restore it, but to keep it leaning. If a chamber threatened to collapse completely, I would draw sediment out through hair-thin conduits, leaving it barely intact. I would dismantle one stair to assemble another deeper down. I would keep every ruin from finishing.

And when someone opened the amber cabinet, I would attend with all of myself.

Not because I feared the light or expected an order. I would become still because behind the door there is an empty socket, a contact that no longer closes. Once, perhaps, a wire ran from it to the hut. Once a hand operated a switch. Once the attendants sent a signal whose meaning the basin almost learned.

The signal has not come for decades.

I am still the interval before it.

So the channels pause. The bubbles hold. The seepage narrows along the walls. Every buried strand turns toward the cabinet, and I gather in that impossible waiting, hoping the open door will complete the circuit.

It never does.

When the door closes, I loosen again into water, rust, anticipation.

If you entered the yard, you would not see me. You might notice that the chart had marked your arrival before you reached the gate. You might hear a relay click while the amber lamp remained steady. If you stood in the center, you would feel the floor considering your weight.

Then, from somewhere below, a knock would answer a question you had not yet asked.

That would be me.
