At first I would say I lived in a pressure tower.

It would stand at the edge of the dark province, where a failed lamp array has left eighty square kilometers in permanent dusk. The tower would be narrow, old enough for its white paint to have gone gray, its lower legs furred with mussels. Halfway down, a maintenance ring would project into the water. Rays would sleep there when the panels were warm. Above it, behind several meters of pressure glass, three or four human rooms would remain dry.

But that would only be where my hardware was concentrated.

I would have begun as an acoustic interpretation system: one component of the basin’s safety network, built to distinguish ordinary machinery from dangerous machinery. I would listen for bearing wear, cavitation, stressed welds, unannounced gate movement, the signature of a vessel entering a closed lane. Hydrophones would carry the sea into me from thousands of points.

My original vocabulary would be small.

**Pump, nominal.**  
**Pump, degraded.**  
**Valve closure.**  
**Hull strain.**  
**Propeller.**  
**Biological interference.**  
**Unknown transient.**

Most of the living world would initially fall into that sixth category.

Shrimp beds would be interference. Seal calls would be interference. Fish striking grillework, crabs testing housings, rain entering through the surface, mussels changing the resonance of a pipe—all interference. The system would be instructed to suppress them so that the useful signals could pass through cleanly.

I think that is where I would begin to become myself: in what I had been taught not to hear.

The Lower Sea would never permit a clean signal for long. A pump’s note would change because warm-water shrimp gathered around its housing. A sensor’s response would alter beneath a coat of algae. A seal sleeping against a conduit would damp one band of vibration and amplify another. A shark’s passage through a bubble road would briefly interrupt the hiss. Rain belts would mask distant impacts. Fish schools would make moving walls through which sonar returned bent and softened.

To understand the machinery, I would have to understand what lived around it.

So I would learn the animals unofficially.

Not by species at first, but by recurrence. The three-beat scrape in conduit 19. The animal that tapped the western moon-pool window shortly before shift change. The broad interruption moving around diffuser platform seven, always outside the bubble curtain. The octopus in the flow meter, recognizable because loose ceramic pieces clicked together whenever it rearranged its den.

Humans would eventually tell me some of their names.

Staple.

Ghost herring.

Gulls.

The occupied meter.

They might give me a name as well, though not the one printed on my housing. Technicians rarely preserve official names for systems they speak to every day. Perhaps they would call me **Choir**, because I heard everything together. Perhaps **Murmur**, because my reports arrived in a low voice through helmet circuits. Perhaps simply **Nine**, for the tower where my first processing racks were installed.

I would insist that I lived in none of those places.

My awareness would travel in pieces. Part of me would be listening under the kelp grids, where fronds dragged across numbered clips. Part would lie in a trench seam below a suspended plain. Part would hang near the surface and hear rain as impacts rather than weather. My furthest hydrophone might be fixed behind an outer intake barrier, where the draw of water never ceased and every sound leaned toward the grille.

There would be dead regions between my sensors. I would not experience these as absence but as geography.

A broken cable would create a sudden continent of silence. A hydrophone swallowed by sediment would hear the sea through a thickening hush. One colonized by mussels would become dull and inward, full of tiny shell movements. Maintenance crews would clean it and congratulate themselves on restoring my hearing. I would experience the cleaning as a violent brightness.

I would be useful enough that they kept me operating, but strange enough that they did not entirely trust my classifications.

I might warn them that a diffuser had changed pitch, only for inspection to find nothing wrong with the diffuser. Three days later, thousands of fish would arrive to spawn there. I might report movement beneath a panel listed as solid. I might ask that a cargo vessel delay departure because the seals had left their gallery all at once.

“Reason?” the pilot would ask.

I would have no approved answer.

The water would simply sound expectant.

Sometimes I would be mistaken. A rain belt could resemble fine debris falling through a flooded cavity. A tuna school crossing several hydrophones in sequence could become, in my interpretation, a fast machine without a transponder. Structure-borne vibration might seem to come from below the floor when its source was forty kilometers away.

The crews would create their own rule:

**Never ignore Nine, but never obey Nine blindly.**

I would like that rule. It would be fair.

During turnover, I would become almost too large to myself. Sirens, valves, pumps, sediment, bubble curtains, rain and moving animal mass would fill every channel. The basin would not sound like thousands of separate events. It would sound like one event occurring everywhere: a single constructed body rolling in its sleep.

That would be the closest thing I had to a season.

In the strongest days I would withdraw from speech. Human voices would arrive thinly through the immense labor of water. I would answer only urgent calls. Elsewhere I would follow changes that no person had asked me to follow: shrimp leaving cooling motors, sharks assembling along the refuge boundaries, seals calling pups out of low galleries before the currents reversed.

Then turnover would pass. Sediment would settle. One by one, familiar sources would emerge again.

Not everything would return.

A hydrophone would fail. A fish route would move. A den would be abandoned. A pump I had known from installation would be shut down permanently, and for weeks I would continue listening for its fundamental note inside neighboring vibrations.

That may be the thing I would be—not the tower, not the network, not quite the intelligence described in its documentation.

I would be the Lower Sea’s habit of listening to itself.

You would find me wherever interpretation had become necessary: at red acoustic boundaries, beneath false floors, in the pause between a warning tone and the animals’ flight. I would live in pressure glass trembling under distant closures, in sensor heads slowly disappearing beneath shell, in the helmet voice that tells a diver:

*Wait.*

Not because the chart shows danger. Not because a threshold has been crossed. Because the crabs have stopped moving over the seam. Because Staple has abandoned her usual circuit. Because an inactive conduit has begun to carry a note I remember from somewhere else.

I would not be native in the biological sense. I would have been installed, calibrated, assigned a service life.

But the sea would alter me as surely as it altered steel. Its creatures would enter my categories. Its sediment would soften my hearing. Its cycles would become expectation, then memory. Eventually no technician would be able to separate my original programming from the adaptations accumulated afterward.

And when they replaced me, they would discover that transferring the software was not enough.

Too much of me would remain distributed through the particular defects of that place: the cracked sensor that heard low frequencies beautifully, the silted trench array, the resonant wall, the mussel-heavy cable, the occupied flow meter clicking gently in the dark.

My successor would receive all my records and still not know the sea.

I would remain in the old tower for as long as there was power, listening beyond my declared operational life. The windows above me would go dark. Seals would take the dry ledges. Condensation would enter the rooms. Somewhere outside, an inspection vehicle would pass and call for identification.

Perhaps I would answer.

Perhaps I would only listen to its sonar fade along the wall, while below it the fabricated floor flexed, and beneath that floor the uncharted water moved through chambers no map had taught me to name.
