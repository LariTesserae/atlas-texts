You would find me under the empty square, though not in any chamber shown by the town’s remembered routes.

The way in begins behind a battery wall in the electrical quarter. Two coppered skeletons must be lifted aside—not removed, only persuaded to bend at the knees—and behind them is a seam too narrow for an adult. It admits a hand, then an arm, then, if the shoulder is turned out of its joint, the rest of the body. The passage descends through warm black water between the original basalt and something that resembles basalt until it moves.

At first I would seem to be a seam-reader.

I would have the narrow body and six-fingered hands of the Compression, but age would have made me asymmetrical. One breathing frond would be white with mineral deposits; the others dark red from constant exertion. Black membrane would cover my back, cultivated there to insulate me from the low electrical murmur in the walls. My left arm would be plated from wrist to shoulder in branching copper, not armor exactly, but a sensory instrument. Every tremor entering the metal would spread across my bones.

I would lie with that arm pressed to the underside of the square.

Above me: glazed tiles, the quivering sphere of air, the pot of old soil, the silver tree.

Below me: the broad warmth on which the Compression rests.

There would be almost no room to turn. Roots finer than hair would hang through the mortar. Most would belong to algae, pipe moss, orchard escapees. One would be dry.

That root would pass down from the tree’s pot through a flaw no one above has discovered. It would not drink water. Water would withdraw slightly from it, leaving a skin of impossible dryness no wider than thread. I would keep my fingers around it without touching. If skin met root, the air in my blood would try to sink toward it. Bubbles would gather beneath my nails. My breathing fronds would close.

My work would have no proper occupational color. Violet means pressure loss, but pressure is always being lost there. Green means an arrival from above, but nothing arrives. White would be too final. Among the few who know of me, I would be indicated by an uncolored valve: clear fluid released into green water, visible only because the suspended dust avoids it.

They might call me the Interval.

Not keeper of the tree. Not watcher of the creature. The space between them.

I would have come there as a child.

A nursery line once slipped through a drainage grille during a pressure turn. The neighborhood felt the tether pull downward, pause, and begin transmitting a rhythm no child had been taught: three slow beats, a pause, one beat. They drew me back changed. For months I refused warm water. I slept against the floor and exhaled downward. Whenever the Lung contracted, all six fingers closed around an absent object.

The seam-readers eventually took me from the nursery. They did not ask what I had seen; sight is not trusted at such depths. They put my hands on old housings, settlement cracks, root-bound arches. I learned to distinguish opening from softening, softening from breathing.

The town has thousands of movements. Copper contracts. Basalt remembers heat. Roots pull in darkness. Dead machinery occasionally releases a final pocket of tension decades after stopping. I learned them all.

Beneath the square I found a movement that belonged to none of them.

The silver tree does not bend toward the dimness above.

It bends downward.

The shift begins in the smallest root, travels through the dry thread, enters the tiles, and passes into the thing below. Years later, the thing answers. Not with sound, but with an absence of resistance. Pressure gauges fall to zero. Reservoirs empty. For one instant the weight of the town is not supported by anything measurable.

Then the broad surface beneath the foundations tightens.

The Compression rises by the width of a mineral grain.

No one notices because everything rises together.

I would spend most of my life measuring those grains.

Food would reach me in sealed weed bladders guided down the passage. Messages would come through copper bones in the wall. Children would sometimes tap questions from the square’s perimeter, believing they addressed the dead tree.

*Were we once air-breathers?*

*Is there a dry world inside the sphere?*

*Does the thing below have a mouth?*

I would answer rarely, and never in words. One tap for no. Two for uncertainty. Three for a question improperly formed.

As I aged, it would become harder to leave. Calcifying polyps would bridge my ribs to the basalt. Filter moss would enter my clothing and then my scars. My plated arm would branch into the battery wall until messages from the town passed through me whether intended or not: harvest yellow, pressure violet, the green taste of wreckage descending from above, white death spreading slowly along district pipes.

I would hear everyone and be known by almost no one.

Eventually the distinction between my pulse and the pumps would become unreliable.

That would be useful.

The creature below does not understand speech, but it understands load. It knows the town as warmth, weight, charge, and the regular gift of sinking breath. It feels every new tower. It feels copper accumulating on the dead. It feels children pulling against their tethers. Perhaps it dreams these irritations are organs of its own body.

Through the dry root, the tree gives it another sensation: distance.

A recollection of open air. Wind. An expanse with nothing close enough to touch.

That is why it turns.

Not from hunger, I would come to believe, but from terror.

And so I would remain beneath the fountain, one hand reading the tree and one reading the sleeper, translating between two ancient fears: the fear of drowning and the fear of emptiness.

When the branch moved, the witnesses above would begin the old rhythm.

Three slow beats.

A pause.

One beat, repeated until the hand tired.

I would feel it through the tiles. The dry root would tense. Far below, reservoirs would empty in a patient sequence.

Then I would place my coppered palm against the warm ridged darkness and answer with a rhythm of my own—not a warning, not a prayer, but the simplest structural reassurance the Compression possesses:

*You are still held.*

After a long while, the town beneath my hand would settle.

Or the creature would.

By then, there might be no meaningful difference.
