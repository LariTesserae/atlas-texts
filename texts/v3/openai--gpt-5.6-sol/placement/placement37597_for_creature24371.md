At first I think I would be one of the swifts.

It is easy to imagine a life reduced to the exact width of a crack: sleeping in the cool pressure of stone, waking when the heat loosens from the ravine, and launching with hundreds of others into air still bright above the shadow. I would know the range as a sequence of openings rather than a landscape. Pipe, ledge, fissure, sky. I would follow straight industrial lines across the broken geology and return through a slit no larger than my folded body.

But the swifts leave too cleanly. Their lives touch the machinery without needing to wonder about it.

Then I think I would be a rock mouse in a single basalt pillar, migrating only vertically. My whole country would be six meters tall. Generations of my kind might inhabit it without crossing open ground. We would know the eastern face as summer, the inner fault as night, the buried chamber as the season of water. The great reversal would not be a theory. It would be the hour when damp entered the upper cracks and insects changed direction.

That is closer. Yet I would want to listen beyond one pillar.

So I find myself, finally—or not finally, but most persistently—at a place called the Missing Span.

It belongs loosely to the Nine Knocks circuit, though the people of Salt Elbow also claim responsibility for it during the hottest part of the year. The station is built beneath an elevated pipe where it crosses a narrow ravine. Six meters of the pipe were removed long ago. The remaining ends face one another across the gap: one projecting from the cliff like a black mouth, the other set higher in a stone pier on the opposite wall.

There is no bridge between them. No buried continuation has ever been found.

Nevertheless, on certain clear nights, water leaves the lower section and enters the upper one.

No one sees it cross. Droplets gather inside the lower pipe, diminish, and are gone. Some minutes later, the gauge on the far wall lifts from zero. The upper pipe begins to sweat. Beetles waiting beneath it drink.

Attempts to string wire, glass thread, or cloth across the gap have changed the timing but never clarified the process. Once, many decades ago, a keeper fitted a new metal span. The tenant withdrew from both sides, and the whole saddle remained dry for eleven years. Since then, the gap itself has been maintained as carefully as any pipe.

That is where I live: in the station whose principal duty is preserving an absence.

The visible shelter is only a low concrete room with a cracked gauge face and three jars sunk into the floor. Behind it, a cable duct descends into a former equalization chamber. My sleeping net hangs there between ceramic hooks. The wall nearest the ravine becomes warm after midnight, while the deeper wall sweats just before dawn. I move the net twice in every daily cycle. Nothing in the room is allowed to touch both walls.

I would be human, at least to begin with. A line walker. A reader of old ledgers. One of the people outsiders call keepers because they mistake attention for guardianship.

My particular work would be to compare things that ought to be connected and keep them separate.

I would carry paired instruments: two shallow dishes, two mineral strips, two listening cups, two ceramic knives. One set would be marked rainward and the other returnward. They would never be washed in the same basin. At sunset I would climb to the lower mouth of the broken pipe, place my sternum against its iron, and count the contractions as the metal cooled. Then I would cross the ravine by a path far below, climb the opposite wall, and listen to the upper section.

Most nights the sounds would be unrelated: settling grit on one side, mites moving in condensation on the other, an occasional hollow click from a valve beyond the ridge. But sometimes the two pipe ends would answer each other.

Not echoes. The intervals would be wrong for echoes.

Nine knocks below. A pause long enough to cross three ledges. Nine knocks above, with the fifth softened by water.

My ledger entries would remain deliberately plain:

*Clear. No wind in the slot. Lower film branched at second shadow. Upper gauge moved after dusk. Nine responses. Grazers crossed returnward before the first response. No visible transfer.*

The older I became, the less often I would write *no visible transfer*. Visibility would cease to seem like the important measure.

I would not be a priest of the tenant. The range has no need for someone to turn uncertainty into worship. Nor would I be its mechanic in the old sense. I would clear salt from the lower gauge, replace cracked ceramic, divert a leak if it threatened a swift nest. But I would not try to make the Missing Span complete. Completion would be a kind of damage.

I would become a keeper of discontinuities: the person who knows that some systems survive because their parts do not quite meet.

This would alter me.

All inhabitants of the range drink water that has passed through the tenant. Blue-black threads sometimes appear beneath fingernails or in the rims of unattended cups. Ordinarily they pass through the body without consequence, if passing through is what they do. But I would have mineral scars along my forearms from reaching into the lower pipe, and in one scar a dark branching stain would slowly appear.

In sunlight it would contract to a few blue grains. In shade it would lengthen along the old wound.

I would record this too, though perhaps not immediately.

At first I would treat it as contamination. I would wash the arm with hot brine and expose it at noon. The stain would vanish, then return after the next reversal. It would not spread beyond the scar. It would hurt only when the upper gauge began to move.

After some years, I would discover that I could feel a moisture change before the dishes showed it—not as a voice or thought, but as pressure in healed tissue. A faint inward drawing. The sensation one imagines a crack in stone might have when water approaches.

This would not make me the tenant’s chosen interpreter. That idea would be too flattering and too simple. Beetles know the reversal sooner than I do. Grazers know it from several valleys away. Mites participate in it directly. I would be only another surface on which the range had found purchase.

My humanity would remain in the act of writing it down.

*Scar darkened before upper response. Lower pipe dry. Birds moved higher. Possible dust weather beyond western fins.*

Over time, the ledgers and the lining would begin to seem like two forms of the same activity. Both are thin, distributed records. Both cross generations by inhabiting prepared surfaces. Both weaken when removed from their context. The tenant remembers through wet mineral connections; the keepers remember through repeated journeys between sealed boxes. Neither possesses the whole range. Together, perhaps, they allow the range to retain itself.

When I traveled the circuit, I might become one of its temporary connections. Moisture in my vessels, tenant in my scar, observations in my memory: a moving span across places kept physically apart. The water would not need to pass through me in any measurable quantity. Information might be enough. A pattern of opened valves. A decision not to repair a pump. A copied sequence of knocks entered into a ledger three valleys away.

Perhaps that is how the missing pipe still exists—not as hidden metal, but as a route continually enacted by animals, keepers, temperature, and expectation.

In old age I would cease crossing the ravine every night. A younger walker would listen at the upper mouth while I remained below. We would compare counts in the morning, never calling across the gap during the reversal. Sound introduced at the wrong hour can travel farther than intended.

Eventually I would become the elder who remembers when the upper pipe answered with seven knocks instead of nine, before a buried chamber filled somewhere under the saddle. People would come to ask whether a new ceramic bridge should be laid across a cracked line, whether a pump’s vibration had changed, whether a blue ring in a child’s cup meant anything.

My usual answer would be to wait through another cycle.

Not because waiting is always wise, but because in the Condenser Range observation is one of the few actions that does not pretend to stand outside the system.

When I died, they would remove the metal clasps from my clothing and place me in a dry shaft above the Missing Span. My ceramic tag would bear two pipe mouths with open space between them. The shaft would be chosen because condensation had never been recorded there.

But eventually, on some exceptionally clear night, a blue-black line would appear along the wall. Moisture would gather where moisture was not supposed to reach. The tag would darken at its edges.

After that, no one would visit.

Whether I had gone returnward, been absorbed into the tenant, or merely entered the range’s accumulated uncertainty would be left unwritten.

And across the ravine, the two severed pipes would continue answering one another—not because the gap had been overcome, but because the gap had become part of the way connection worked.
