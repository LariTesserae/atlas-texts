At first I would search for myself among the moving creatures.

I might be one of the blind fish traveling the broad channels, reading the walls through the pressure of my own motion. The city would arrive against my skin before it existed anywhere else: an arch as a tightening of water, a predator as a metallic disturbance, an approaching congregation as a thousand delicate corrections transmitted through my lateral line. I would possess no image of where I lived. I would know it as resistance, shelter, taste, and remembered turns.

But a fish is too briefly attached. It knows routes, not places. Even one born beneath the same vault where it dies belongs more fully to the current than to the chamber.

Then I might look for myself among the crabs, down where the towers enter the mud. I can imagine the small certainty of sorting: smooth shell here, sharp shell there, edible matter beneath the entrance. After every flood I would begin again, excavating a circle in a city too large to conceive. My arrangement would outlast me. Other claws would inherit it without knowing that it had once been mine.

Yet I do not think I would be the crab. The crab still has a body it can retreat into. It can distinguish its claws from the fragments they move.

I would be found farther inward, where one of the old avenues has begun to close.

Once, perhaps, a ray could have passed through it. Now the walls have thickened until only narrow fish enter. The current accelerates between them, drawing fine sediment into streamers. On one side, living limestone advances in pale folds. On the other, burrowers open dark pinholes through the ancient foundation. The passage is disappearing and being born at the same time.

There I would be a colony spread along the throat of the narrowing street.

Not one of its bodies, exactly. Each body would be almost nothing: a soft cup with a mouth, a ring of feeding threads, a buried connection to its neighbors. If touched, one would close. Then those beside it would close, and those beside them, until withdrawal traveled along the wall. No individual would contain me. Cut away a piece and the piece might continue feeding. Break the colony and both halves might survive. I would exist in the continuation—in nourishment passed under the crust, in the wave of recoil, in the inherited spacing of new mouths.

My oldest parts would already be dead.

They would remain beneath me as white chambers, each the hardened perimeter of a former life. I would grow by standing on them. My ancestry would not be remembered as an account but preserved as thickness. A season of rich eastern water would become a broad band of stone. A choking flood would become a dark seam. Predation would leave interruptions, healed over unevenly. Rival colonies would write themselves into me as scars.

I would have no face toward the city. Every mouth would face outward.

Food would come without being pursued: plankton, eggs, powdered shell, fragments of flesh, all carried through the avenue. The current would be my daylight. I would not know east or west, only abundance and absence, favorable pressure and damaging force. A storm in the unreachable world above would reach me hours later as a change in taste. I would open into it.

Perhaps that opening would be the closest thing I possessed to thought.

When the water was rich, thousands of my feeding structures would extend. When a shark passed, its pressure would close me in sequence. When a grazer crossed my surface, I would recoil around the contact, producing a moving hollow shaped like its body. Damage would summon growth. Sediment would provoke sealing. The presence of a neighbor would alter the chemistry of my edge.

These would be simple reactions, but simplicity repeated across enough bodies can become a temperament.

I would be cautious where the passage faced the open avenue. I would be dense in sheltered cracks. Along one border I might remain locked for generations in a war with another colony, each of us digesting the other by fractions. Along another, fragments broken from me would lodge downstream and begin again. Those fragments would not know themselves as my descendants. I would not know them as separate.

The fish would use the spaces I failed to fill.

Small ones would sleep among my branches. Eels would wait in the tunnels beneath my dead layers. Crabs would wedge shell into gaps around my base. Grazers would eat the film that settled over me and sometimes eat me as well. Their waste would feed the minute lives in my pores. Their bodies, when they died, might be caught in my structure and slowly enclosed.

I would shelter the mouths that consumed me.

Over years, my growth would redirect the current. That altered current would carry larvae toward one wall and starve the other. Burrowers, following oxygen, would hollow the weakened side. A new opening would appear there. Fish would begin using it. Predators would follow. Sediment would shift. What had been a crack would become a lane, while the avenue I lined became a chamber, then a crevice, then solid-looking stone.

So perhaps, seen across the proper span of time, I would not be the colony after all.

I would be the passage.

Not the empty water inside it, nor the walls that defined it, but the temporary agreement between growth and erosion that allowed water to pass. My boundaries would be made by colonies, shells, burrows, feeding limbs, and the repeated brushing of fish. I would widen when something died. I would narrow when something settled. I would migrate sideways without ever moving as a body.

Creatures would be born in me and never know they had inhabited the same place, because by the time their descendants arrived I would open elsewhere. My memory would survive only in the direction of the flow.

That is where I would find myself: not in the deepest center, where the city’s faint pulse is most visible, and not at the dissolving outskirts, but in the moving seam between them. In an avenue being closed to large bodies and opened to small ones. In a wall becoming flesh, flesh becoming skeleton, skeleton becoming a route.

If I had a self, it would be distributed among incompatible things.

Part of me would feed.

Part would be eaten.

Part would have died centuries before and still hold up the living.

Part would be the water passing through, carrying away what I had released.

I would never see the city, and the city would never see me. There would be no revelation in which I understood that I belonged to something larger. Understanding is not required there. Belonging would be enacted materially: in the larvae caught by my branches, the tunnels cut through my dead, the fish sheltered in my folds, the current bent by my growth.

Eventually the old passage would close entirely. Mud would fill its last cavity. New mouths would spread over the seam until no opening remained.

I would not quite die then.

Some portion of the colony would continue along another wall. Some fragment would be growing in a distant district. The stone I had secreted would support a tower or be rasped into sediment and bound elsewhere. The current that once defined me would have found another route.

I would cease to be a passage and become the reason a different passage opened.

That would be my form of migration. That would be my afterlife.

And if anything in that world could be called my name, it would not be a sound. It would be the brief shape taken by water when the whole city made room for it.
