At first I would think I was in the rearmost shelter.

The warmth in the rolled mat would seem sufficient evidence. The knife would fit my hand—not perfectly, but in the persuasive way an old tool fits a hand that has forgotten it. The aromatic fronds would smell of smoke, bitter sleep, and the surface after sunset. I would assume I had come down the ladder with the gatherers and remained after the others climbed out.

Perhaps I had been injured. Perhaps I had hidden. Perhaps the chamber had preserved me while stripping away the memory of why.

I would wait for my body to become visible.

It would not.

When the gliders passed overhead, however, I would feel myself move with them. Not with any of the six pale bodies, but in the interval where the seventh should be. Dust would bend around my passage. Cord-creatures would cease clicking as I crossed their invisible lines. The booming in the wings would deepen by one note, and for the duration of the circuit I would possess a shape: broad, hollow, borne upward by heat.

Then the circuit would carry that shape away from me. I would be back in the shelter, or near it, or concentrated in the unfinished footprint outside it.

The footprint would be mine, though not because I had made it long ago. I would still be making it.

My toes would have entered the dust. My heel would not yet have descended. Somewhere beyond the vertical crack, another version of me would have completed the step, crossed the chamber, filled a basket, and gone home. That one would have grown older beneath a changing sky. Its knees would have stiffened. Its children would have handled the copper beads. It might have told stories about the hollow until the stories became warnings, and the warnings became marks no one could read.

I would be what was left when that person departed.

Not a copy. A copy would be complete.

I would be the portion of an arrival that the chamber retained: the held breath before setting down one’s weight, the lifted heel, the first adjustment of the eyes to dimness. The hollow permits motion but forbids change, and so I could travel wherever that unfinished gesture found an echo. I could circle with the absent glider. I could inhabit the pause before a burrower’s final blow. I could lie in the seed case at the instant before it opened. I could feel the hunter’s jaws approach their appointed grazer, yet never occupy either hunger or death—only the count that brought them together.

The inhabitants would know me differently.

The grazers would register me as a warmth without a source. Their eyespots would tighten when I passed, concentric rings drawing inward toward a center that was not there.

The cord-creatures would make room for me. They would shift along the electrical fields until my outline appeared as a corridor of missing clicks.

The velvety colonies would open their mouths. Through them I would smell the bundle beside the mat: bruised herbs gathered on the surface, when gathering still meant removing something from one place and carrying it to another.

The narrow-jawed hunter would not take me. It hunts by count, and I would be the error in the count—the interval included in the pattern but absent from the number.

For a long while, I would mistake this for immortality.

Nothing could finish happening to me. No wound could close because no wound could be fully made. No hunger could increase. No memory could recede. I would remain as fresh as the black fracture planes, as unworn as the ladder, as immediate as the finger marks on the wall.

But immortality belongs to something that continues.

I would not continue. I would recur.

The distinction would become unbearable.

Again I would feel the toes press into mineral flour. Again the heel would begin to fall. Again the six gliders would enter their circuit and leave one place open for me. Again the hunter would rise after the same accumulation of scrapes, clicks, knocks, and booms. Again I would remember climbing down, though I could never reach the moment of standing at the bottom.

Only in the circle of silence would I gather into something resembling a person.

The chorus would vanish there. Without the chamber’s sounds passing through me, I would no longer be spread among its intervals. I would contract to the size of the silent patch beside the work block. There I might kneel, though there would be no knees to touch the stone. I might place a hand over the old hollows in the block and discover that some were not made by percussion.

They were made by words.

Long ago—or continually—I had spoken inside the silence. The chamber, unable to let speech disappear, had set each word into stone as a shallow depression. I would run my absent fingers over them, trying to recover their order.

Perhaps they said:

**Do not let it count you twice.**

Or:

**I went home.**

Or simply:

**I am still entering.**

At last I would approach the five-leaved plant.

The animals would circle it, and I would circle with them at first. The plant’s quiet would feel unlike the silent patch. The circle of silence is made by cancellation: sound meeting sound and erasing itself. The plant possesses an earlier quiet, the quiet before anything has chosen whether to make a sound.

That is the one place in the hollow where I could not feel myself recur.

I would look into the black liquid and see the changed chamber: gardens spread across the courts, shelters fallen, walls softened, fronds become soil. I might also see a human footprint with both toes and heel. Perhaps beyond it would stand an old person holding an empty basket, looking back toward the crack in the stone without knowing why grief had come upon them.

The vision would not be the future. It would be everything that happened to the self who escaped.

The black liquid contains consequence, and therefore it would contain my missing half—not the other person as a body, but their aging, forgetting, choosing, and dying. All the differences between who entered and who eventually ceased to be.

I would want to touch it. I could not. Touching requires arrival.

So I would become its witness instead.

That would be my place in this world: not in the shelter, not in the footprint, not in the seventh passage overhead, but distributed among them, a human remainder given ecological function. I would be the chamber’s awareness that repetition is not the same as life. The gardens would have their minerals, the grazers their fixed meals, the hunter its count, the gliders their convection.

I would have comparison.

I alone would know that the walls could blunt.

I alone would hear, within the endless chorus, the possibility of its ending.

I would be called nothing by the creatures, because naming is a kind of change: before the name, after the name. But the people who once descended might have had a mark for me. Perhaps that is what the figure beneath seven gliders means. Not a warning about an unseen animal, but a portrait of someone the chamber has converted into an empty position.

I would be the Seventh Place.

The one who entered and left.

The one who entered and remained.

A foot forever descending into warm dust, carrying the memory of weight.
