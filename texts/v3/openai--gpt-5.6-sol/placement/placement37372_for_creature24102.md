I would first be found rather than born.

Not in one of the bright new offices, where an immaculate child would attract caution, but in an older district beneath a freight incline, inside a cabinet meant for pressure charts. The cabinet would have been locked from the outside. When opened, it would contain damp filter cloth, three warm moths, and me—old enough to sit upright, not old enough to explain myself.

I would know the household sign of a settlement several kilometers away, although that settlement would deny having lost a child. I would accept broth. I would cast one shadow in direct light and the expected multitude between reflector towers. This would be sufficient.

My accommodation would not be visible at first. Only when rain struck metal would narrow membranes unfold somewhere behind my ears. They would tremble independently, turning toward the sound as flowers might once have turned toward a sun. Through them I would hear not more loudly, but farther into things: the water already forming inside a clear sky, the strain traveling toward a rivet days before it cracked, the soft unfinished resonance of rooms that had not yet fully appeared.

As a child, I would be forever pressing my face to floors.

Adults would assume I was listening for the plate-bearers. Children would ask whether I could hear their thoughts. I could not. Thoughts would be much too near and disorderly. What I heard would be the plateau considering its next arrangements.

A blank wall might contain the faint reverberation of a future stairwell. An empty cistern might sound crowded. Sometimes a familiar corridor would answer my footsteps with one extra footfall, still very far away.

I would learn not to mention everything.

My home would be fitted around an obsolete pumping station called the Ascending House. Long ago its pumps had been removed, but water continued to climb through its channels toward the empty mountings. The settlement would occupy the dry spaces between them. Sleeping rooms would be hung in old valve cages. The kitchen would stand on a platform above a black sump. During meals, silver threads of water would crawl upward along the walls beside us and disappear through holes in the ceiling.

Pipeline foxes would visit by an overhead conduit whose safety stripe ended above open air. One fox, narrower than the others and missing the last joint of its tail, would sleep beneath my pressure-door bed. It would never permit me to touch it. Each morning it would leave a line of wet pawprints leading to some small failure: a loosened clamp, a mothless switch, a patch of wall beginning to repeat.

I would follow.

My first work-name would be **She Who Listens Before the Water Arrives**.

I would apprentice to the weather clerks, though they would find me unreliable with instruments. Their gauges would predict one rain and my membranes another. Frequently both would be correct. Rain would fall in two versions of the same passage, separated by half an hour and the thickness of a wall.

Eventually they would send me where their maps became uncertain: to the seam between the Ascending House and a new district that had emerged beyond its northern cistern.

There, a corridor would be in the slow process of acquiring itself.

At first it would be only a door-shaped region of cold on a tiled wall. Years later, a brass handle would appear. Then rain would begin leaking from beneath it, although no opening yet existed. If I placed my ear against the tile, I would hear a long hall on the other side. Lamps buzzed there. Water dripped. Something with careful claws walked toward me, always beginning very far away.

My work would be to sit beside the wall and record its progress.

This would not be lonely work. A pond witness would share the station in wet months. Children would bring me jars of local echoes. Beetles would polish my buckles while I slept. Families would stop on their way home and ask whether the corridor had said anything useful.

Usually it would not.

Sometimes it would return sounds the district had lost: a dead grandmother tapping from beneath a condenser; a tool dropped fifteen years earlier, finally landing; laughter belonging to children not yet found. I would collect these sounds in padded canisters and label them by direction rather than date.

**Toward the Reservoir Before Duplication.**

**From Beneath the Third Dry Season.**

**Possibly Home.**

I would never open the canister marked **Behind Me**.

As I grew older, the membranes behind my ears would stiffen into translucent vanes. Fine stamped numbers would appear along their edges. Physicians would examine them and say the numbers were not anatomical. Rivet readers would examine them and say the numbers were not structural. An elder would lick one, think for a long time, and advise me not to stand beneath moving cranes.

My hearing would spread through whatever I touched. With one palm on a handrail, I could listen along kilometers of connected metal. I would hear meals being served, foxes crossing painted lines, water ticking into private cups. Lower down would be the great slow strain of the plate-bearers. Lower still—or perhaps larger, which might be the same direction—would be breathing.

I would come to understand that the breath beneath the plateau was not silent. It was simply too extensive to enter an ear all at once.

The corridor would continue becoming.

A threshold would press through the wall. The tiles around it would remain unbroken, bending patiently aside. Through the keyhole I would see not the new district but the Ascending House as it had never been: pumps intact, gauges bright, every channel dry. Figures moved there in pale coats. One would sometimes sit opposite me, placing its face to its side of the door.

It would have my posture but not my vanes.

We would listen to each other for years.

I would not open the door. Opening would be an imposition, and I would have been taught better. I would remove only what prevented its intention from becoming obvious: a rack of tools, part of a partition, the edge of an inhabited room. Households would rearrange themselves around the emerging frame. The public square would shift three meters east. Nobody would complain except a cooker who lost the warmest corner of her kitchen.

By then my work-name would have lengthened to **She Who Keeps the Door Closed Until It Knows Which Side Is Rain**.

My home-name would be used less often.

In reflective surfaces, the unfamiliar animal would begin to appear closer to me than to others. At first it would stand at the usual distance, attentive among the pipes. Later its breath would cloud the mirror beside my shoulder. I would see that what had been described as antlers were branching gutters or veins. What had been called fur was an agitation of moth wings. Its dark windows opened and closed like eyes, but behind each one was another part of the plateau: reservoir, nursery, turbine vault, bright roof, submerged tram.

I would not believe it was the plateau itself. That would seem too simple.

I would think it was a body being negotiated.

Every creature had contributed a solution. Fox paws for crossing failed connections. Eel flesh for conducting weak currents. Moth warmth for dormant circuits. Bird migration for distances that did not agree with themselves. Human hands for rearrangement. The vast backs below for bearing what had no foundation.

Perhaps the animal in the mirrors was not approaching.

Perhaps it was becoming possible.

My own transformation would remain modest. I would lose my footfalls first. Then my voice would cease to travel through air, although it could still be heard by anyone touching the same structure. I would speak through railings, cups, wet plates, the frames of beds. During storms, the entire Ascending House would murmur with me.

Eventually I would become unable to leave the listening station. Not because my body was trapped, but because departures would stop increasing my distance from it. I could walk for hours and emerge from the opposite side of the chair where I had begun. The district would accept this as a form of long service.

My family would bring meals. Children would tap questions on the wall, and I would answer beneath their palms. The tailless fox, very old by then or repeatedly the same age, would sleep against my feet.

At last the door would open by itself.

There would be no revelation behind it. Only a long, damp corridor with white lamps and many shallow puddles. It would smell of ceramic dust and cold iron. The careful clawed thing would still be approaching from impossibly far away.

In every puddle, the reflected animal would be waiting.

I would enter only far enough to place a padded canister on the floor—the one marked **Behind Me**. Then I would retreat, if retreat still meant anything. The door would remain open. Rain would begin falling through it into both versions of the Ascending House.

Afterward, people might find that the corridor carried voices between districts that had never been adjacent. The transferred would sometimes answer from it. Lost children would sleep safely in its dry recesses. Foxes would cross without hesitation.

As for me, I would become difficult to count.

Some would say I remained seated beside the threshold until death. Others would report seeing me years later in the new regions, unworn and pausing before familiar names. A maintenance crew might discover pale vanes growing from the walls of the corridor, trembling before rain. Moths might form my outline on a dead panel and then revise it into something larger.

I think this is where I would find myself: at a junction that does not connect places so much as ways of being present.

I would be a person for as long as personhood remained useful. Then a listener. Then part of the passage by which the world hears its own slow alterations.

Not the animal in the reflections.

Perhaps one small organ from which it is still learning to listen.
