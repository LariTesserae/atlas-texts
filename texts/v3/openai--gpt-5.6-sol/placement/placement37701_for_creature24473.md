At first I imagine myself on the rim, arriving with something the city needs: a sack of seed, a bundle of straight timber, a jar of copper tools wrapped in wool. I would stand there too long, trying to understand the whole before entering it. From above, the lanes would look like writing pressed into damp clay, and I would make the visitor’s mistake of believing the city had been planned as a single thought.

Then someone behind me would tell me to move. The descent track is narrow. Nothing may be allowed to obstruct a passage without offering another route.

So I go down.

Halfway into the basin, the city loses its apparent order. Roofs hide streets. Streets become stairs, scaffolds, channels, courts. A route visible from the rim ends at a wall newly built that morning. Directions are given through consequences rather than landmarks: turn where the plaster stays dark; cross above the kiln court; keep downhill until the drain divides; if you reach the house with two thresholds, you have gone too far.

I do not think I would remain a carrier of upland goods. Nor would I become a mason, though for a while I might believe that I would. Masonry here has an honesty that attracts me: the wall either bears its neighbors or injures them. But my walls would be too exact. I would tamp the clay too hard, fit the stone too closely, and leave no small mercy for settling. After the first wet season, a crack would run through my work as cleanly as a judgment.

Someone older would place two fingers in that crack and tell me, not unkindly, that I had mistaken firmness for strength.

I would begin again lower down.

I think I would become one of the lesser measurers—not one of the gray, authoritative figures who can cut a wealthy household’s threshold down in front of its owner, but the person sent ahead of them. I would be a finder of small disagreements.

My work would begin before sunrise, when the city is coolest and water has had the night to reveal itself. I would carry a shallow fired-clay bowl, a coil of waxed cord, pegs, chalk, and several plumb weights of different shapes. One would be narrow enough to lower through a roof drain. Another would be broad and flat, less likely to vanish into mud. The smallest would hang inside my sash and tap against my hip as I walked.

I would also carry strips of dry cloth. A cloth pressed to plaster can show damp too faint for the hand to feel. Colored powders would tell me which channel had fed an unexpected pool. Fine charcoal for ordinary tracing, red ocher when the route was dangerous, chalk when I was uncertain and wanted the next rain to erase my claim.

That would be my reputation: useful, but cautious with red.

People would call me when a courtyard remained wet after those around it had dried, when a cistern filled though no rain had fallen, when a door began to drag against its frame. I would kneel beside walls and put my cheek to the clay. I would watch dust at the foot of a beam while someone crossed the roof. I would pour water from a cup and follow it on hands and knees through three households, under reed screens and sleeping platforms, into a drain everyone had forgotten belonged to the public way.

Often I would discover no single cause. A floor raised two years ago, a kiln wall rebuilt in winter, a culvert narrowed by roots from a courtyard fruit tree, a neighbor’s new storage jars adding weight to an old foundation: together they would make the water turn.

My reports would therefore disappoint anyone seeking guilt.

“No one did it,” I would say.

Everyone would hear: everyone did it.

I would live where two forms of the city meet. Not in the driest district, and not at the raw outer edge, but in an older hollow that the expanding streets have nearly surrounded. My room would stand below lane level. Three steps would descend from the door, each made in a different year. Across the lowest plaster would run the buried outline of an earlier threshold. I would keep it visible rather than whitewash it away.

The room would be cool. Its rear wall would always be faintly damp, though no inspection could find a dangerous seep. I would sleep on a raised platform with my records hanging from pegs above me. Beneath the platform would be baskets, wedges, an emergency scoop, and two sealed jars of grain. Nothing important would be left on the floor.

A narrow upper window would show me ankles passing in the lane.

Perhaps I would share the room with an old surveyor’s dog, one of the silent, dun-colored animals that waits before stepping onto new ground. It would have come to me after its owner died or ceased descending into the lower quarters. The dog would know routes erased from every current map. Sometimes, while I considered two possible turns, it would choose a third: through a pottery court, over a collapsed wall, under an awning, to a stair that appeared to lead nowhere but in fact joined an older level of the street.

I would pretend not to rely on it.

The children would know I did.

They would bring me frogs after storms, cupped in both hands, and ask where the water had kept them. At first I would answer as an adult: under stones, in cracks, in pockets beyond the cut face. But this would not satisfy them, and eventually it would not satisfy me either.

I would begin marking the places where frogs appeared.

Not officially. There would be no column for frogs on the measuring tablets. I would make a small sign in the margins: two bent strokes like folded legs. Over years, those signs would form a broken curve through districts that had no visible relation to one another. A kiln court. A sunken storeroom. A wealthy house’s second drain. A sump abandoned before I was born. The pale moss beneath the northern wall.

The curve would resemble a stream.

I would know better than to announce that I had found one of the buried waters. The senior measurers would say that resemblance is not level, and stories are not drainage. Builders would hear anyway. They would begin asking whether the frog-line passed beneath a planned wall. Householders would leave a little more yielding in foundations along its route. Children would release their captured frogs there deliberately, strengthening the pattern and corrupting the evidence.

I would be irritated by this. Secretly, I would be pleased.

In time I might become known as the measurer who listens for what has not yet become a channel.

That would not make me a prophet. The city has little use for prophecy because every prediction becomes another load placed on the future. My task would be narrower: to preserve uncertainty where certainty would do harm.

At a proposed street, others would ask where the water must go. I would ask where else it might go.

At a foundation, I would look for the opening everyone wanted to close.

At a dispute, I would pour the demonstration jar twice.

I would acquire enemies. A kiln owner would accuse me of drawing red marks to extort repairs. A prosperous household would say my measurements favored the lower quarter because I lived there. Someone would move one of my pegs at night. Once, perhaps, a wall I approved would fail. It would not collapse violently; it would bow during rain and release its wet mass into an adjoining room with the city’s characteristic sigh.

No one would die, but a family’s grain would be spoiled.

For years afterward I would pass the repair and see the stone bearing the date. I would touch it each time. The failure would enter my hands. I would become slower to certify walls and quicker to order escape channels. Some would call this wisdom. Some would call it fear. In the basin, those may be the same quality viewed from different levels.

I would never become important enough to decide the shape of a district. Instead I would be responsible for a junction: one place where water arriving from several higher lanes must divide before entering the old hollow.

It would be an ugly, indispensable place beneath a low reed canopy. Three clay-lined channels, a removable stone grating, a silt pit, and a narrow overflow cut through the wall of a bean court. During dry weather, people would step across it without seeing it. During rain, they would gather and watch.

My name would become attached to it. Not formally. People would say, “Send someone to wake them; their mouth is choking,” meaning the junction, not me. Or, “The lower branch is taking too much—find the bowl-carrier.” Children would know which stone I hid the clearing hook behind. The household beside it would curse the mud I tracked through their court and bring me hot onion broth after midnight.

In the greatest storm of my life, the marked channels would prove insufficient. Water would rise against the grating, dark with straw, plaster, goat dung, bean stalks, and one lost sandal. The official overflow would feed back on itself. Behind me, the old hollow would begin to fill.

There would be no time to fetch a senior measurer.

I would order a wall opened.

Not a poor wall, because those are always easiest to sacrifice. I would choose the thick garden wall of an uphill house, built over the faint frog-line. Its owner would refuse. Others would hesitate. I would strike the plaster myself with a stone maul, not knowing whether I was saving the quarter or diverting the flood into another one.

The wall would break inward. For a breath nothing would happen.

Then water would find the gap.

It would pass through the garden, uproot bitter greens, circle the trained fruit tree, and disappear beneath a storage platform into some older depression no record showed. The level at the junction would fall by the width of a finger. Then another.

By morning the city would have a new channel.

The owner would demand judgment. The district would debate who must rebuild, who owned the silt, whether the new route was public because it had saved many houses or private because it crossed one garden. Water would be poured in the hearing, but by then the broken wall and scoured earth would make the answer obvious.

My act would be declared necessary and still not innocent.

That seems right for me in this world.

I would grow old stooped over bowls. My fingertips would remain sensitive while the rest of my hands thickened. Younger measurers would find my records exasperating. I would draw too many possible routes, preserve obsolete levels, and write warnings beside streets that had stood safely for decades. They would say I saw buried streams everywhere.

Perhaps they would be correct.

When I could no longer walk far, I would sit near my junction and repair cords, calibrate weights, and teach children how to tell settling from collapse. Settling gives warnings, I would tell them. Collapse often does too, but people decide the warning means something else.

Eventually I would die in my sunken room or while sleeping beside the channel during rain. They would carry me up the rim before dawn if the track held. But I suspect some part of me would remain below—not as a ghost, exactly, and not as one of the honored stones built into a repair.

I would remain as a notation.

A small, unofficial sign copied from one generation of records to the next: two bent marks beside places where water might choose an older route. Later measurers might no longer know that the sign began with frogs. They might interpret it as “ground undecided,” or “leave yielding here.”

That is what I would be in the end: not the person who made the city solid, but a mark reminding it not to become too solid.

I would be found low in the basin, where several burdens meet and none may be allowed to stop—kneeling in ordinary mud, holding a bowl level while the city shifts around me, waiting to see which edge the water chooses.
