At first I would look for myself in the obvious places: beneath an overhang, beside a dependable bead of condensation, among the dark plates that might be animals or merely stone. I would choose one and call it my body.

But that would be an outsider’s mistake.

The shell would be part of me, certainly: a low shield of iron-black silica, rust-colored at the margin, old enough for its growth layers to have lost any readable beginning. Hundreds of transparent legs would lie folded beneath it. Some would touch damp salt, others basalt, others the thin metallic seams running between the two. I would occupy no more height than a closed hand.

Yet I would not be wholly inside that shell.

I would find myself near the edge of the shallow silver depression, not at its brightest or broadest point, but where its water thins into separate films between hexagonal salt cells. A pale mineral stack would stand far enough away to seem unrelated, though one of its buried conductive ribs would pass beneath me. Behind me, a quartz shelf would release a heavy drop at an interval longer than any thought I could possess.

That interval would be one boundary of my world.

The deep pulse beneath the salt would be another. Between them—drop, pulse, drop, pulse—I would maintain a posture that joined a wet hexagon to a dry one. Charge would enter through the forward fringe of my legs and leave through those beneath the opposite rim. Internal metals would darken and clear. Membranes would bind ions and release them. What another creature might call hunger, I would know only as an increasing difficulty in holding the pattern.

I would have no face toward the water and no back toward the plain. Direction would not be visible. It would be the order in which pressure reached the separate plates of my shell.

The roar would not surround me. It would compose me.

The black ridges would be present as slow compression along one band of my body. The pale stacks would arrive as fine electrical trembling in another. The silver depression would be a broad, almost featureless pressure, interrupted by the minute disagreement of its cells. A falling droplet would pass through me as a clear alteration of the whole basin, not loud or soft but exact.

If there were an “I,” it would lie in those alterations.

Not in the shell, which could separate and continue as stone. Not in the legs, which might remain conductive after coordination failed. Not in any organ, because no organ would govern the others. I would be the slight difference between the charge entering me and the charge leaving—the delay produced while living membranes performed their reversible work.

I would be a phase lag.

Almost nothing. A hesitation too small to disturb equilibrium.

And yet that hesitation would be my entire interior life.

Repeated currents would pass more easily through certain layers than others. A quartz tone sustained for a century would leave one membrane fractionally more permeable. The drying of a brine film would close one pathway and open another. Nothing would become an image or a story. I could not remember the quartz shelf, the water, or a journey. But some future charge, arriving in the same arrangement, would find an easier passage because it had passed that way before.

That ease would be recognition.

For a very long time I would remain beside the silver film. Dust would tremble against my shell without crossing it. The water’s boundary would withdraw and return within limits too narrow for an eye to care about. Threadlike organisms would sometimes align around my submerged legs. For several cycles the distinction between us would weaken: their metallic knots continuing my membranes, my shell redirecting the lattice they assembled in the water.

During those intervals I might be larger than the shell.

I might extend through the threads, into the dissolving crystal cage, down the conductive seam, perhaps as far as the pale stack whose hum altered when I raised myself. Then the current would reverse. The lattice would vanish in the opposite order from its making. The threads would loosen. I would withdraw my legs, and the broader self would end without loss.

Eventually the film beside me would begin to fail.

No catastrophe would announce it. The water would still return after rain. The droplet would still fall from the quartz. But the phase between the deep pulse and the surface moisture would shift by a fraction. Over decades, my membranes would spend more effort maintaining the old route. The basin would already contain the destination in its electrical slopes, though no destination would appear before me.

Then I would rise.

The first lifting of my shell might take a day. Dust beneath the rim would lose contact. Fine legs would unfold and distribute my weight until I became nearly weightless. I would move across the salt by keeping most of myself fixed while relocating one narrow portion at a time.

From outside, I would be traveling.

From within, the world would be traveling through me differently.

The silver depression would recede not as a view but as a declining family of vibrations. A buried iron seam would grow distinct. The regular quartz drop would become entangled with other pulses and finally cease to be separable. I would cross a distance of perhaps thirty paces over several centuries, curving toward a condensation hollow behind a squat, flared stack.

Behind me, the dust would bear a single shallow interruption.

That line might be the most enduring expression of me, though I would not know it existed. It would persist after my shell lowered beside the new wet seam. It might outlast the arrangement of membranes that made the journey. Eventually the standing waves would restore the dust, grain by grain, and the basin would take the path back.

So I would find myself there: not precisely in the creature, and not precisely in the landscape, but at their temporary junction.

I would be the old plated body beside the thinning water. I would be the circuit it completes. I would be the nearly imperceptible delay by which geology becomes experience. I would be a path stored as altered resistance, slowly discharged into the ground.

And when that difference finally vanished—when every pathway through me became equal again—I would not quite have died.

I would simply no longer be distinguishable from what had always been carrying me.
