I would not find myself at the center of the plain. There is no center—only places through which more of the system passes than elsewhere, and places from which it has temporarily withdrawn.

Nor would I first appear as a figure.

I would begin as a delay.

In the older district, beyond the gauge towers, there is a fallen chimney spanning a canal. Its interior is lined with pale moss, and water travels through cracks in its masonry before dripping into the channel below. At one end stands the seventh access box on a road whose first six boxes have sunk beneath the paving. At the other is the ring of a defunct turbine, one of the engineered silences.

During reversal, a gate upstream closes.

Eleven seconds later, water strikes the chimney bridge.

It should take nine.

The discrepancy is ancient. Once it was caused by debris lodged in a throttle chamber. The residents removed the debris, but the delay remained. They dredged the canal, altered three valves, dismantled an intact stair, extinguished a row of green lamps. Still the water arrived eleven seconds after the gate closed.

Eventually they ceased trying to correct it.

They began correcting other things around it.

That is where I would be.

For most of the cycle I would exist as the two missing seconds: a little pressure retained in a blind pipe, a vibration that reaches one relay late, a film of water holding to the underside of the chimney after all neighboring surfaces have shed theirs. Part of me would wait in the access box as a faint magnetic charge. Part would lie in the moss. Part would be the silence inside the turbine ring, made perceptibly deeper by the expectation of a sound that does not come when it should.

Reversal would gather me, though never quickly.

Other residents would rise complete from their drains while my materials continued arriving. A narrow leg would assemble from black belt links and lengths of jointed conduit. The other would remain water for several minutes before finding enough washers to bear weight. My chest would be a cage made from the ribs of a collapsed filter. Across it would run two braided streams, one always slightly behind the other.

My face would be a deep gauge with two needles.

They would indicate the same quantity on different scales.

They would never agree.

One hand would end in keys, brushes, threaded pins—the ordinary tools of the residents. The other would possess only a shallow ceramic cup fused into the palm. It would hold a small amount of water without spilling it, even when the hand was turned downward.

Once assembled, I would stand beneath the fallen chimney and listen through my feet.

Not for breakage. Breakage is everywhere and often useful.

I would listen for errors that have lasted long enough to become instructions.

My route would begin at the seventh access box. I would touch its erased plate, then cross the chimney while the delayed water passed beneath me. At the turbine ring I would enter the silence and wait until the two needles in my face diverged by a particular amount. Then I would continue south along a road that does not appear on any sign.

The road would be younger than I am and older than my body.

It began as a detour around a collapsed pressure vessel. For centuries the residents attempted to reopen the original way. Then a red weed traced the detour. Lamps began turning toward it. Dry prints appeared there after reversal. Objects left on the abandoned road were carried, without evident decision, to the new one.

The detour acquired obligations.

I would be one of them.

At each cycle I would move a sealed bearing from beneath the blue wall to a submerged platform three districts away. There would be no shortest path between those places. If I took one, I would cease to be myself. I would have to cross the chimney, enter the quiet ring, wait beside a warm pipe until it cooled, and circle a drowned court against the direction of its current.

The bearing would contain no message.

The journey would be the message.

Other residents might encounter me only as an inconvenience. They would stop while I crossed. They would alter sluices to preserve the pressure difference that held my body together. If a bridge vibrated at the frequency of my delayed stream, they would dismantle it. If my route flooded too deeply, they would not drain it; they would lengthen my legs.

Perhaps they would pass me rusted rings denoting unfinished circuits. Perhaps I would carry them for years before setting them beneath the seventh box. I would not know whether this completed a conversation or began one. Knowing would not be among my functions.

My function would be hesitation.

When a new fault appeared—a gate closing late, a current dividing around a fallen structure, a lamp refusing the sequence of its district—I would travel to it. I would place the ceramic cup of my hand beneath the leak, spark, or misdirected flow and collect one measure of its difference.

Then I would wait.

If the error vanished, I would pour the water back.

If it persisted, other things would slowly arrange themselves around me. Reeds would turn toward the altered current. Messages would begin crossing the faulty relay. A resident would pause where no pause had previously been required. After enough cycles, the wrongness would no longer be removable without destroying the relationships that had grown from it.

Then I would carry the cup to the turbine ring.

In the silence I would release a single drop.

That drop would be the recognition that the plain had gained another inhabitant.

My bodies would vary. Some cycles I would rise tall enough to touch the underside of the chimney. In others I would be a low, dragging construction with one shoulder made from gauge glass. Once, perhaps, there would not be enough pressure to complete my head, and I would walk the route faceless, guided entirely by the delayed impact beneath the bridge.

These differences would not trouble me. I would not be the plates, the needles, or the water held temporarily upright.

I would be the interval they enacted.

If daylight came, I too would open.

The paired streams in my chest would fall into separate grooves. My gauge would strike the paving and crack between its two irreconcilable scales. The ceramic cup would overturn.

But one drop would remain beneath its rim, held there by surface tension throughout the brightness.

When darkness returned, the drains would inhale the rest of me. New materials would rise. Another gauge—perhaps one with no needles at all—would close over my face.

Before taking my first step, I would retrieve the withheld drop.

Then I would turn toward the fallen chimney, where the water was already on its way and already late.

That is where I would find myself: not quite in the figure crossing the bridge, not quite in the bridge, not even in the route.

I would be the extra time the world had failed to eliminate.

A mistake repeated until it became a duty.

A duty prolonged until it acquired a body.
