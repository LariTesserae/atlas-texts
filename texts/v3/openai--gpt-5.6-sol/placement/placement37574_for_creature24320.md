I would not find myself on the rim, though that is where the eye would go first.

The rim is too exposed, too nearly singular: stone, sky, the long white unmaking of water. Nor would I be high in the flowering crowns, where bodies briefly become visible to one another in clear light. I would live farther inward, in one of the shallow basins where several kinds of water meet without quite becoming a river.

There, an old channel curves through the forest beneath a roof of roots. A mineral spring enters from the east, staining the submerged wood cream and rust. To the north lies a trembling marsh crossed by the slow trails of carriers. Westward, the ground rises into a grove of jointed pressure trunks. Southward, hidden under fern and peat, the channel passes through black stone and eventually reaches the escarpment.

From above, nothing would mark the place. It would appear to be another dark fold in the plateau.

At first I would seem to be an animal living beneath a root arch.

The visible body would be low and translucent, perhaps the length of a forearm. It would have no fixed symmetry. When the water was high, it would spread into a flat, many-lobed shape and cling to the underside of the roots. When the channel withdrew, it would contract into a thick cord, drawing its softer parts into a sheath plated with grains of iron and mineral shell. Along its lower surface would be hundreds of fine mouths—not mouths with jaws, but ciliated openings that sift bacterial films, spores, dissolved proteins, and the minute remains carried through the channel.

A row of dark bladders would lie beneath the skin. They would fill and empty with changes in water pressure. Their contraction would produce no audible call, but it would send a distinct tremor into the root above: a soft sequence of pulses, too faint for most animals, readily detected by others of my kind.

That body, however, would not be all of me.

It would be only the wettest part.

Fine strands would enter old beetle galleries in the wood, follow fungal tubes into the peat, and emerge in neighboring pools as pale fans. Other strands would wrap the conductive lianas without penetrating them, using their weak electrical changes as cues. Where the ground shifted from flooded to aerated, my tissues would thicken into knots containing colonies of metal-feeding bacteria. Where water moved steadily, I would grow filtering veils. Where a root was often compressed by the passage of a marsh carrier, I would form hard sensory beads and learn the rhythm of that weight.

I would occupy not a territory but a junction.

Most of the time, there would be no central place from which I experienced it. A chemical change at the spring might take minutes to pass through me. The footfall of a predator near the marsh would arrive as a distortion traveling along several strands at once. Rain falling kilometers away would be felt first as a pressure increase in the underground channel, then as cooling roots, then as altered charge in the lianas, and only much later as drops descending through the canopy.

This would be the nearest thing I had to foresight.

Before the cloud reached my basin, my filtering fans would fold. Valves between my flooded and exposed tissues would close. Nutrients would be drawn into the protected knots. Small shelled animals living in chambers along my strands would seal themselves in, responding to substances released by my contraction.

Nothing in this response would know the storm. Yet the whole of me would become its expectation.

In the long wet phase I would be diffuse. Water would open old passages through the basalt, and I would extend into them behind migrating fish. Some branches of my body would be torn away and carried underground. Most would die. A few would lodge in fungal-lined fissures and continue feeding, becoming separate colonies after the current closed between us.

Separation would not occur at once. For a while, chemical pulses might still pass through wet stone or grafted roots. Then the route would dry, and what had been one pattern of response would become two.

Neither half would possess a memory of having divided. Each would retain only certain changed thresholds: how quickly to close against bitter water, which vibration precedes the arrival of fish, what mineral balance causes the feeding veils to open. Memory would exist as anatomy.

During the clearer phase, I would become more animal.

Not by uprooting the whole colony, but by producing small mobile bodies from the mineral knots. They would begin as soft buds, each enclosing a braid of my tissues, bacterial partners, and sealed spores stolen or gathered from the fungi around me. Over several days they would harden into dark, narrow creatures with six jointed legs and a flexible keel along the back.

At dusk they would detach.

Each would carry only a little of what I had been. It could sense moisture, electrical gradient, and the minute flexing of pressurized roots. It could not feed well. Its task—though there would be no task in its mind—would be to cross the drying forest floor until it found another place where mineral water, living root, and flooded peat touched.

Gliders would eat many. Pool grazers would swallow others as they crossed shallow water. Some would be caught in fungal films and dismantled before dawn. One might cling unnoticed among the moss on a shelf-eater’s spine and travel half a valley with it.

When a mobile body found a suitable crossing, it would press its keel against the wet wood and become still. The legs would soften first. The shell would split along its underside. Feeding threads would enter the root mat, and within a season there would be no animal left—only a new translucent patch beneath another arch.

Perhaps that would be birth. Perhaps it would be travel. From inside the process, the distinction would have little meaning.

Other creatures would use me constantly. Silver fish would shelter beneath my veils. Larvae would graze my surface. Crown animals drinking from the channel would tear away pieces with their probing hands, attracted to the mineral salts. A sleek fish hunter might lie across my root arch for hours, its six feet filling my entire awareness with pressure.

I would also use them. Their passing would open water paths. Their waste would feed the bacteria in my knots. Spores trapped in their fur would be combed loose by my sensory filaments. When something died upstream, I would briefly swell with the concentration moving through the channel, then thin again as roots and fungi took the rest.

I would not be a keeper of the basin. If my filtering cleared the water, it would be because feeding required water to move. If my strands kept small root passages open, it would be because oxygen reached me through them. If my mineral sheaths strengthened a crumbling bank, that stability would be a residue of appetite.

Nor would I be the plateau’s hidden consciousness.

My world would extend only as far as continuity allowed. A severed strand would become silence. A dry season might reduce me to three isolated knots, each responding alone. A flood might join colonies that had been separate for centuries, confusing their chemical rhythms until one consumed the other or both settled into a shared pulse.

What I called myself would therefore change with the water.

In rain, I would be the whole junction: spring, root arch, buried channel, trembling liana, pale fans opening beneath the peat.

In clearer weather, I would contract into armored knots and scattered walkers.

In drought, if such a word could be used for a few centimeters of withdrawal, I might be no more than a bacterial chamber sealed inside dead wood, waiting without anticipation.

And after my last tissues were eaten, the mineral tubes I had left behind would continue carrying water. Fungal threads would enter them. Root hairs would follow. Perhaps another colony would settle into the passages and inherit the shape of my former body without inheriting anything that could be called me.

So that is where I would find myself: under a bent root in a nameless basin just inside the escarpment, where marsh water, mineral seep, forest pressure, and underground current cross.

And what would I be?

Not quite an animal. Not quite a colony. Not the forest, and never separate from it.

I would be one of its temporary crossings, made sensitive.
