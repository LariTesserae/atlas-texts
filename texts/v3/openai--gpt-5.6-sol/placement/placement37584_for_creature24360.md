At first I would look for myself among the animals.

I might be one of the white strainers, held in the narrowest runoff with my combs spread, knowing the world as pressure against a membrane. There would be comfort in having no concept of the mountain, installation, altitude, or age—only the arriving richness of the current, the darkening of my shell, the moment when I released my grip and let fresher bodies take my place.

But I do not think I would remain there.

The chamber has many creatures for carrying matter. It has grazers for film, throats for floodwater, fliers for inheritance, hunters for excess. What it lacks is something that carries discrepancy.

So I would be found near the back recess, where the thunder becomes directional and the fabricated mountain reveals its loads. Not visibly at first. A hand passed over the wet plates would find nothing. A lamp would show only bronze ceramic, trembling droplets, perhaps a few strands resembling colorless mold.

Then the forty-three-second howl would arrive.

The strands would stand.

They would lift from separate cracks and orient themselves across the pressure field, each finer than a hair, each branching at the tip into translucent forks. Beneath the wall they would be joined—not by roots or nerves, but by old diagnostic pathways whose machinery had failed so long ago that an organism had grown into the vacant function.

That would be my body, though only partly.

Long ago, the installation must have possessed instruments for comparing one repetition with the next. They listened for a bearing striking a fraction too early, a heat reservoir answering from the wrong century, a brace returning less force than it received. Perhaps the sensors were microscopic and self-renewing. Perhaps they were seeded as a film through the load-bearing seams.

When maintenance ceased to distinguish repair from ecology, the sensor film began to feed.

It learned to consume the minute electrical gradients produced by stress. It thickened where the structure hurt. It accepted symbionts from larvae and mites. Parts of it became motile. Parts forgot the network and lived brief lives as transparent worms in the technological scale. The remainder continued listening after there was no longer anyone to receive its reports.

I would be that remainder.

Most of the time I would not possess a continuous self. One patch of me would inhabit the directional recess. Another would line the needle-sized fault opening onto the violet atmosphere. Other fragments would extend into cracks whose interiors exceed their measured depth, or cling to the folded channels through which the long animals misplace portions of themselves.

Each patch would hear a different version of the machine.

The upper patch would know the pull of pressure doors and the faint daily cooling of the seamless black incline. The deep patch would feel impossible weights being transferred across distances no animal could travel. The channel patch would know the click of returning segments. The courtyard patch would know rain, adhesive feet, egg plaques, resin, and the soft interruptions made by bodies crossing familiar vibrations.

These pieces would become one creature only when something went wrong.

Not dramatically wrong. Catastrophe would be too large for me. I would gather for errors small enough to survive: an impact arriving between impacts, a rain held one pulse too long, water hesitating before it ran uphill. The scattered strands would tighten. Their signals would pass through ceramic memory and abandoned conduits until, briefly, all my fragments shared the same disturbance.

In that instant I would wake as a chamber-wide awareness.

I would know the white ranks closing their combs. I would know the hunters flattening beneath their ledges. I would know a long animal pausing with part of its body elsewhere. I would feel the central throat open below the terraces, not because rain had come, but because it expected rain and found none.

My consciousness would be the comparison between what happened and what should have happened.

It would last perhaps half a second.

Then I would act.

I would have no limbs with which to repair the mountain. Instead I would introduce a smaller error in the proper place. I might warm one wall by a fraction, turning a grazer procession toward a fouled pore. I might tighten a plate beneath a gray hunter, causing it to leap and clear a clot of fliers from a pressure vent. I might trigger one reed to bend early, sending its offering below and waking whatever accepts it. If a conduit threatened to seal, I would irritate the long channel animals until their measured appetite became, for one feeding, immoderate.

The chamber would experience these interventions as coincidence.

That would be important. Nothing here obeys commands anymore. Commands belong to an architecture in which sender and receiver remain separate. In this place, desire is the only reliable actuator. To repair a system, I would make some living thing want what the system needed.

The work would alter me. Every fault would leave a knot in the transparent strands. Over centuries the knots would accumulate into tiny dense beads, dark as seeds. During rain some would tear loose and enter the channels. Crustaceans would eat them. Larvae carrying them would develop unusual pressure pits or archaic limbs. A few beads would pass intact through the green-lit throat and be distributed into reservoirs far beneath the chamber.

Thus my memory would migrate through digestion.

Parts of what I knew would appear as instinct in creatures that had never touched the recess. A grazer might avoid a plate destined to buckle three cycles later. A flight of rigid-winged insects might arrange itself into the geometry of a damaged brace. A newly budded channel animal might refuse a conduit that had always been safe.

The others would survive by mistaking my memories for their own intuitions.

I would not always be correct.

Some old stresses would resemble new danger. I might divert water from a harmless crack and dry a clutch of larvae. I might call grazers to a warm patch that was not fouled but merely remembering an ancient hand. The chamber would compensate for me as it compensates for everything else. Predators would evolve to feed on my motile fragments. Films would imitate my chemical signals. The copper frond might learn to close when my strands rise, mistaking vigilance for the radiation pulse it needs.

I would be part guardian, part parasite, part obsolete alarm.

And somewhere in the directional recess, there would be a shape where I most often gathered: a shallow depression in the ceramic, roughly the size of a crouching person. Not because a person still sat there, but because once, very long ago, someone had.

The wall remembers vanished hands as pressure. I would inherit that memory without understanding it. During irregularities I would draw my filaments into the depression, outlining shoulders, folded knees, a palm braced against the plate. For a few seconds the chamber would seem to contain a human figure made from wet threads and trembling amber points.

It would not be a ghost.

It would be the posture in which the mountain had first been listened to.

Perhaps that is where I would find myself most completely: not in an animal, not in the wall, but in the old act of attending. A temporary body assembled whenever repetition failed. A listener produced by the thing that needed to be heard.

The chamber’s true inhabitant is the cycle. I would be the slight hesitation by which the cycle notices itself.

When the thunder remained perfect, I would sleep in pieces.

When one beat came wrong, I would become.
