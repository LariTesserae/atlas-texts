At first I think I would be one of the birds.

It is the easiest life to imagine from a distance: standing on a cistern cap in the sour, shadowless light, toes gripping warm stone, one eye on the fish below and the other on the hands raised to drive me off. I would make my nest from a strip of fish skin, three white shells, a child’s lost cord, and a potsherd with half a written name on it. I would know the city only as a succession of ledges—safe, unsafe, newly scraped, warm enough for eggs. History would be the places that no longer accepted my feet.

But the birds belong too completely. They do not have to argue with the stone. When a nesting cup closes, they peck another. When a roof is sealed, they move to the next roof. The city cannot truly threaten them with incorporation.

Then I think I might be a crab in the lower alleys, living in the thickness of a wall. At high water I would enter through a drain behind the potters’ court. At low water I would emerge among the ankle shadows, armored in a color almost indistinguishable from wet clay. I would learn the rhythm of human feet and the dangerous silence before a basket drops. I might hide for years in a pocket made by the mineral-coated ribs of an old boat, never knowing that it had once crossed the sea.

Yet the crab, too, accepts every opening as temporary.

I would not.

I find myself instead in the lower city, where the lanes are narrow enough that the roar passes through the masonry as a single deep pressure. My district lies between the central meeting house and a black tidal trench called, in the hand language, **the Water That Does Not Answer**. The sign is made by pointing downward, then turning the palm away before the gaze follows. Children are forbidden to approach it alone. Naturally, every child does.

My dwelling is behind two hanging hides in a chamber built inside an older chamber. It is one of the driest rooms in the district, though water pearls continually along the ceiling seam. There are no windows. Light enters through a thick plate of pale shell fitted into the upper wall, so everything inside has the color of bone seen underwater.

I am an old woman, or becoming one. No one agrees on my age. My mother said I was born during the arrival of a boat carrying green onions, but the harbor lists record no such cargo. There was a shipment of reeds with their tips still green, and another of onions so long dried that their skins blew through the market like copper-colored feathers. The confusion may be hers, or the recorder’s, or mine. In this city, error is another deposit that must be chipped away, though one can seldom be sure where the original surface was.

I hear almost nothing now.

Once I could distinguish the ring of my father’s hammer from three courts away. I could hear a roof channel clogging by the change in its trickle. I could sleep through birds but wake at the scrape of a crab inside a storage jar. That world has narrowed. Now sound reaches me mostly through my soles, my jaw, and the table beneath my wrists.

This has not made the city silent. It has made it enormous.

I feel the springs working below the floor. I feel kiln doors close in distant quarters. I feel the arrival of loaded boats before the harbor slabs are raised. When the outer sea strikes the shelf, the water in my cup wrinkles. During council disputes, I rest two fingers on the clay table and know who is angry by the force with which they set down their hands.

My office has several names. Foreign traders call me an archivist, if their language has such a word. Children call me the Bone Scraper because I use a fine shell point and because they believe everyone who works among the names of the dead must secretly handle skeletons. The formal district sign means something closer to **keeper of what must not close**.

I maintain records, but not merely written ones.

In shelves around my room stand fired tablets listing channel rights, marriages, adoptions, debts, reservoir duties, copper-tool custodians, forbidden vents, drowned causeways, and the names of those incorporated into walls. Every tablet is wrapped in oiled skin. Every wrapping fails eventually. Mineral water enters by capillary paths too fine to see. White bloom forms in the incisions. If it is neglected, a record becomes a smooth tile.

So I copy.

I copy the old marks onto new clay while the potters’ apprentices sit opposite me. One reads by hand, tracing the signs. One kneads and flattens. One checks my work against a spoken lineage, shouted close to my better ear or signed slowly within the circle of lamplight. No record is trusted in only one form. Clay may be sealed. Memory may favor the living. Gesture may lose an old distinction. We make them disagree with one another until the truth shows through the cracks.

The oldest tablets are not lists but maps.

They describe streets under streets, rooms beneath plazas, drains no living hand has cleared. Their measurements begin from corners that have themselves disappeared. “Seven forearms east of the House of Red Nets,” one says, though the House of Red Nets was closed into a foundation before my grandmother deepened. Another marks a freshwater vent beneath a pig court that is now part of the south reservoir wall.

Most people consider these maps ceremonial curiosities. The buried city is stable. To open it risks redirecting water, collapsing crust, releasing foul pockets, or disturbing the dead.

But sometimes a spring changes its pressure.

Sometimes a wall begins to sweat salt where only fresh water should pass.

Then they come for me.

I leave my dry chamber with a shell scraper, a sounding hammer, waxed cords, colored warning slabs, and one of the district’s copper probes in a locked clay case. Two witnesses carry the case; a third carries the tablet bearing the tool’s name. We walk by measurements taken from remembered absences. Here was a doorway. Here was a lane. Here a boat was entombed with its prow facing inland. The present city looks seamless, but beneath it the old passages persist like thoughts someone has tried not to think.

I tap the paving and listen through my feet.

That is where I would be found: kneeling in runoff while a crowd signs questions over my back, searching for hollowness under stone.

As a child, I entered those hollows.

I was small even then, narrow-shouldered and considered useful. Adults tied a cord around my ankles and sent me into ducts with a shell lamp clenched between my teeth. I crawled through warm dark tubes furred with new mineral. Shrimp flickered in pools under my hands. Once I found a sealed room containing six jars, a dog’s skeleton, and the mineral shape of a reed mat. Once the crust gave way beneath my knee and showed me a human face—not flesh, not bone, but the white impression left around a head after the head itself had settled.

I did not scream. There would have been no purpose in it. I touched my brow to the brow in the stone, then backed out.

The face belonged to no surviving name.

That became the center of my life, though I did not know it then. Not death, exactly. Not burial. The unbearable thing was that the city could preserve the curve of an eyelid and lose the person.

Now, whenever a wall chamber is closed, I insist on three records: one in clay, one in a living recitation, and one concealed mark cut somewhere water is not expected to reach quickly. Families complain about the expense. Councils say I exceed my authority. I answer that stone has more patience than government.

I have a mark of my own: an open rectangle crossed by a short descending line. It means, approximately, **an entrance remembered after it is gone**. My apprentices cut it beside the names of the dead. Channel workers have also begun placing it over sealed ducts. Lovers use it in messages when they expect to be separated. Children scratch it on walls and claim it means a secret crab hole.

They may be right. Signs deepen by being misused.

I am not a priest, though I am sometimes asked to perform the work of one. When a household closes its dead into a floor, I kneel beside the fresh masonry and place my palm upon it. I do not ask the stone to preserve them. It will do that without compassion. I ask the living to maintain the name.

The response is made without voice. Everyone present repeats the person’s sign.

For a moment the room fills with the dead individual—not as a body, but as a pattern of shoulders, brows, hands, and gaze. The impatient flick of a sister’s wrist. The inward turn of an old fisher’s fingers. The peculiar two-handed sign of a child who never learned to shape it properly. A name spoken aloud can be shouted by one person. A name made in the hand language requires space around the body, and therefore everyone must make room.

That is our grave marker: an opening briefly maintained among the living.

I would have family, though not all by blood. A potter who sleeps in my outer room because she dislikes the noise of her own kiln court. A harbor signaler with one clouded eye. Two apprentices, one solemn and one incapable of leaving any rule untested. A deaf yellow dog that has selected the driest patch of my floor and defends it from people of higher civic standing. Perhaps a daughter who left on a timber boat years ago and sends back objects instead of news: a seed hard as stone, a square of bark, once a green leaf pressed between fired tiles.

I keep the leaf’s stain. The leaf itself rotted.

People come to see it and are disappointed. They expect green. What remains is a brown branching ghost, finer than any channel map. I tell them this was how plants carried water: through passages inside themselves, openings so narrow no shell blade could clear them.

Children refuse to believe me. They say anything with channels must eventually turn to stone.

Perhaps they are right as well.

When I die, I do not want deep water. I have spent too long recovering those whom the city made anonymous to consent to being taken beyond all measurements. My body will be set within the wall of the archive chamber, upright if there is room, crouched if there is not. The tablets will not be buried with me. Records belong to the people who must recopy them.

My apprentices will inherit my hammers, but not my title. The office does not pass like property. They must demonstrate that they can detect a false smoothness—that they can look at an unbroken plaza and understand it as a crowd of vanished rooms.

In time warm seepage will enter my chamber. My scars, knuckles, and small ear bones will be covered in white. The wall will thicken around me. Someone will hang new shelves over the place. The dog’s successors will sleep against its warmth.

But perhaps my mark will remain in use: the open rectangle, the short descending line.

Then I will no longer be only the woman in the archive. I will be found on sealed doors, burial tablets, lovers’ tokens, children’s secret maps, and old drains that must someday be opened. I will become a gesture made toward any surface that appears complete but is not.

Not a bird, then. Not a crab, not a tool, not quite an ancestor.

I would be a keeper of hollows until the city closed over me.

After that, I would be the sign that tells someone where to cut.
