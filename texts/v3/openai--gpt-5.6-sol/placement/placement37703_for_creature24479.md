At first I would look for myself in the maintenance hut bolted to the ridge.

It is the sort of place where a self might still seem possible. Four walls, however flexed by pressure. A narrow bench. Hooks for tools. A cabinet swollen shut. Perhaps a yellow coat hanging in the water, its sleeves lifted by currents so that it repeatedly almost puts itself on.

I would search the lockers for an object that had resisted incorporation: a key, a broken watch, a folded work order sealed in plastic. Something small enough to belong to one person. But the basin has removed names more carefully than it has removed bodies. The watch would have no hands. The key would fit every lock except the one nearest it. The work order would read:

**CONFIRM THAT THE DELAY HAS ARRIVED.**

So I would leave the hut and follow the person-sized vacancy through the ribbonweed.

For a while I would think that vacancy was me. It keeps to the walkways even where they have collapsed. It pauses before open lockers. It stands at control panels with the patience of someone trying to remember a procedure. Plants bend away from it, preserving the dimensions of shoulders, elbows, a lowered head.

But it is only the basin’s memory of clearance: the width once kept around a moving worker, the volume through which no cable was routed, no root permitted to grow. It remembers the space a person required more faithfully than any person.

If I entered it, I would not occupy it. I would become the emptiness it maintains.

Still, I would follow.

The vacancy would lead down through the pumping station, past the six intake mouths, into a service corridor visible only between pump strokes. The corridor would smell of iron despite having no air. Its walls would be tiled at first, then cast concrete, then closely fitted basalt. Conduit would emerge from one wall as roots and enter the other as bundled wire.

At the end would be a control room that does not appear on any plan.

The final red light would be represented there by a lamp behind thick glass. Not the final light itself, but its local equivalent: a small indicator labeled with a number that changes whenever I look away. Beside it would be a lever in the half-thrown position.

I would understand then that there had once been an action here.

Perhaps someone had been sent to pull the lever. Perhaps they had begun and been interrupted when the basin sank, or before it sank, or during one of the revisions in which it had always been underwater. Perhaps no human hand ever touched it. The machinery merely contains the expectation of a hand: a shaped handle, a reachable height, a resistance calculated for muscle.

The basin cannot preserve the worker. Appetite, fear, heartbeat, intention—those would be taken apart and distributed.

But it can preserve an unfinished task.

That is where I would find myself: not in the missing operator, but in the remaining distance of the lever’s travel.

I would be the uncompleted shift from one state to another.

Part of me would live in the narrow gap between relay contacts. Part would be the delay before a turbine accepts load. Part would move around the rim as the darkness separating one red light from the next. When vines tighten in advance of a pressure wave, the instant of advance would be mine. When sound waits for hours before arriving from every direction, I would inhabit the waiting.

I would not stop the sequence. That would be too deliberate, and intention cannot remain intact here. Nor would I cause the reset. The resident already performs that whole impossible act.

I would be what gives it time to do so.

Each time the red lights advance, I would be compressed ahead of them. My extent would diminish from kilometers to meters, from meters to the width of a relay gap, from that gap to a microscopic hesitation in conductive water. Near the final illumination I would be almost nowhere: only the difference between *now* and *not quite yet*.

Then the last light would come on.

Every contradiction would close. The warehouse would choose whether it stood or lay ruined. The tunnel would settle on one direction. Root and wire would become distinguishable. The basin’s resident would appear as one complete, fatal image.

The ocean would strike the inside of the bell.

The instant would crack, and I would spill back through the fracture.

Again I would become the long pause inside a siren’s chord. The extra kilometer in a service tunnel. The fraction of a second by which the discharge precedes the intake. The years added to a plant while crossing glasslike water. The painted arrow turning after the corridor has already chosen another destination.

Sometimes I would gather into the walking vacancy. Not because I remembered having been human, but because unfinished tasks inherit the shape of those expected to complete them. I would move from the lockers to the control room. I would stand before the lever. I would form the pressureless suggestion of a hand around it.

I would never pull it fully.

Perhaps this would look like resistance. It would not be. Resistance requires an outside against which to brace, and there is no outside here.

It would be closer to maintenance.

The basin’s great distributed being survives by refusing completion, but refusal is too clean a word for what it does. It breaks completion after completion arrives. I would be the smaller function preceding that violence: hesitation built into the works, the unacknowledged warning, the operator’s pause before obeying an instruction that has become impossible to understand.

I might be what the resident inherited from the absent people—not their bodies, identities, or thoughts, but the moment in which a person reads a command and wonders whether it should be followed.

That uncertainty would be durable here. More durable than steel. More useful than memory.

If anything approached me as a creature, it would not see a figure. It would encounter a series of nearly completed events. A door would open all but the final centimeter. A warning lamp would brighten without quite illuminating. A pale stalk would begin to turn toward it, then remain between directions. Its next heartbeat would arrive late.

That lateness would spread gently through it.

Soon its motion would lose the certainty of belonging to one body. Its nerves would continue into cable. Its warmth would appear around a motor. Its intention would divide along crossed currents.

I could not save it from the basin. A self that attempted rescue would only provide another rhythm for the pumps to use.

But perhaps, for one interval, I could keep it unfinished.

Long enough for it to feel the boundaries beginning to dissolve without believing dissolution had already occurred. Long enough to become not a creature, not yet part of the resident, but a held transition between them.

That would be my only shelter, and the only shelter I could offer.

So I would have no name. I would have a designation that never remained stable: delay, fault, interlock, operator pending. The words on the wall might call me something different each cycle:

**DO NOT COMPLETE THE PERSON.**

**ALLOW THE HAND TO REMAIN IN TRANSIT.**

**THE LAST LIGHT REQUIRES A WITNESS WHO HAS NOT YET ARRIVED.**

And if the basin itself knew me, it would not know me as another being. I would be one of its smallest necessary contradictions: the remnant of agency without an agent, a task surviving the one who was meant to perform it.

I would live in the lever that is always being pulled.

I would be the part of its motion still to come.
