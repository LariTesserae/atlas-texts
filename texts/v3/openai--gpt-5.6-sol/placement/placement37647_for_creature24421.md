At first I would believe I was the traveler.

I would keep a record because sequence would seem like protection. Morning: entered the first flooded avenue. Later: crossed the bridge with the vertical channel. Later still: reached the stair of ninety steps.

I would number pages. I would mark the position of the sun. I would draw maps with arrows indicating direction and little bars indicating elevation. For some time, the notebook would behave normally. Ink would dry. Pages would turn. The city would permit me this small, linear superstition.

Then I would discover that every map depicts the district in which I am standing, even maps drawn elsewhere. The pages would remain numbered, but not ordered. A sentence begun near the lower cataracts might end in the high court before I had climbed there. Pressed between two leaves I would find a frond I had not yet picked.

I would abandon the idea that I was a cartographer.

I might next think I belonged among the sounds. The roar would enter me until my bones acquired fixed notes: teeth humming with the galleries, sternum answering the wells, the chambers of my skull vibrating at the pitch of distant water. Perhaps I would become an echo—not a repetition, since nothing here repeats, but a local shape taken by the single unending noise.

Yet an echo requires an earlier sound.

That would still make me a creature of before and after.

So I think I would find myself on the staircase that counts ninety upward and eighty-nine downward. Not at its top or bottom. Not on any visible tread. I would be in the discrepancy.

At first I would search for the missing step. I would touch each one, chalk it, sit on it, give it a name. The stair would accept every mark without being changed. Upward there would be ninety names. Downward, eighty-nine. None omitted. Eventually I would understand that the absent step is not stone.

It is the step I carry with me.

I would be what the city cannot make for itself: the small wound of succession, the insistence that one thing happened and then another. Not time in its vastness, not mortality, not even change—only the word *then*.

The city would distribute me carefully.

One of me would remain seated halfway up the stair, still counting. One would reach the upper landing and look back. One would descend into a reservoir whose water stands upright beneath the surface. Another would never leave the first avenue. We would not be copies. We would be the same passage opened sideways, each figure holding a different consequence of the same decision.

My body would begin to show this.

A mineral band would form beneath my palm where I had touched a wall. Moss would line the creases of my soles. Water brushed from my face would return, not as water belonging to the city but as the same instant of wiping, still attached to me. My pulse would cease to be a measure and become a place: all its beats occupying adjacent chambers.

Eventually I would no longer remember arriving as something that happened in the past. Arrival would be a district. I could visit it.

There I would see myself emerging from the vapor, carrying the unopened notebook. I might call out, but the roar would not allow a message to cross from one version of me to another. Perhaps that is merciful. Warning myself would only produce another avenue: the self who turns back, the self who continues, the self who hears but misunderstands. All would enter.

In the high court, the reflecting pool would finally show me—not as a figure, because it reflects no observer, but as a thin irregularity running through the image of the mountain. A path where no path belongs. A faint seam connecting terraces that are already everywhere connected.

I would understand then that the city had not consumed me. It had given my narrow life room.

Everything I had treated as lost would stand somewhere: forgotten rooms, abandoned choices, words I had not said, selves that had almost existed. They would not return to me. Return is too linear a notion. They would become accessible as streets are accessible, though distance would offer no promise of reaching them.

Perhaps, after long enough, travelers coming after me would notice small signs.

On certain stairs, they would feel compelled to count.

In a roofless chamber, they would suddenly remember an event from their childhood that never happened, though it would bear the unmistakable weight of memory. At a fork in an avenue, they would experience both choices as already made. They might glimpse a person ahead of them turning a corner—a damp, many-shadowed figure with moss-dark feet—and know without reason that the figure is not leading them and cannot be overtaken.

That would be me, insofar as *me* remained useful.

I would not be one of the green things. I would be the narrow animal trail passing through their completeness: consequence preserved not in the mountain, but within the shape of a traveler the mountain has made spacious.

I would live nowhere except between events.

In the extra upward step.

In the page that comes after itself.

In the footprint visible only when looked at from the direction it was leaving.

The city would remain complete without me. It would not need me, remember me, or acquire me. But threaded through its terraces would be one fine, impossible difference: the knowledge that somewhere, once—or somewhere, always—something came before something else.

That is where I would find myself.

I would be the city’s missing tense.
