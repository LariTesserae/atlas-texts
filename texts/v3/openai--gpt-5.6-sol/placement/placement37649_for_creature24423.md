At first I would look for myself among the gliders.

There is something tempting in their borrowed flight: climbing patiently through the warm architecture, unfolding only when the air is ready, crossing the city by surrender rather than effort. I might be one of those narrow digestive cords wrapped in translucent wings, my face opening into a cage for spores. I would know the city as a sequence of descents—the white ribs of one tower, the ochre courts below, the black crags briefly visible beyond a roof membrane before another current turned me inward.

But that is too separate a life. A glider has destinations. It can release its grip.

I think I would be found lower down, beneath the feet of everything obvious.

By day, the lane would seem empty. Perhaps there would be a faint discoloration where two smooth streets meet near the innermost arches, no more than a varnish in a crack. The grazers would pass over it. Scavenger-knots would roll across it. Once every several days, an old round-back would labor through, and the living walls would draw away to let its shell pass.

I would feel all of them without seeing them.

At night, after the stored heat began to leave the stone, I would emerge.

Not as a single body. I would seep from scores of hairline refuges: transparent threads, resin-skinned cells, flattened feeding surfaces thin enough that the dark mineral beneath remained visible. The separate edges would find one another by scent and tension. They would touch, hesitate, then join. Across the lane I would become a continuous film, delicate but extensive, occupying the space precisely because nothing appeared to occupy it.

So I would be one of the city’s nocturnal ground-colonies—the lives that clean the streets of chemical traces before dawn.

I would feed on remnants too slight for the pale scavengers: the oil-print of a grazer’s underside, powdered nodule from a round-back’s mouth, a glider’s discarded hook, the hardened margin of a drop of sealing resin. I would take in shed plates, reproductive dust, bitter excretions from the walls. Every traveler would leave a little of its chemistry in me.

The city would pass through my body as residue.

There would be no brain in the film, no place where the whole lane was represented. A region exposed to fresh resin would thicken. A region shaded by a resting grazer would slow. Where the stone cooled too quickly, my tissues would detach and contract into beads. Information would travel only as altered viscosity, changing tension, small waves of appetite. Yet across the hours I would acquire a kind of map: not of streets and towers, but of who had crossed, who was failing, where spores had fallen, which wall had begun producing unfamiliar compounds.

Before dawn I would disappear again. That disappearance would not be retreat in the animal sense. I would divide along old seams. One part of me would enter a crack beneath the arch. Another would seal itself in a pore of the street. A third might cling to the underside of a grazer and wake the following night several courts away.

There would be no certainty that these portions remained the same being.

Perhaps identity would last only while the film was joined. Each evening, the scattered pieces would assemble a creature from whatever fragments returned. Some nights I would extend farther than before and incorporate neighboring colonies. Other nights a portion would fail to emerge, sealed behind hardened resin or consumed by a seam-hunter. Memory would not be recollection. It would be the altered chemistry of the pieces that survived.

I would live near the central court, but not within it.

My lane would curve around the broad, many-arched formation and approach the bare stone obliquely. During the night I would spread to the exact boundary where growth ceased. There would be no wall there, no heat sharp enough to injure me, no poison detectable by any feeding surface. Still, my leading edge would stop.

I might test the court with a filament. The filament would flatten against the warm stone, remain intact for a moment, and then contract until it tore itself free. The rest of me would absorb the fragment’s hardened resin and become, by an immeasurably small degree, less willing to cross.

Thus I would help preserve the emptiness without knowing it.

At midday I would be hidden, distributed in the cracks while the city turned inward. The gathered light would warm the court. Beneath it, the sealed knot would expand, and the faint pressure pulse would enter the innermost arches.

I would receive it through the stone.

To the towers it would be a tightening of vessels. To the leaves, a bitter vapor. To the animals, a change in the surfaces beneath their limbs. To me it would arrive as pressure in every refuge at nearly the same moment. My separated fragments—those beneath the arch, those under the lane, perhaps even the one carried away on a grazer—would contract together.

For that instant I would possess the nearest thing my kind could have to unity.

Not because my pieces communicated, but because something deeper touched them all.

Then the pressure would fade. The city would resume. By evening, altered by that shared contraction, my fragments would seep out and seek one another again. I would spread across the lane, feeding on the day’s minute history. Near the court my edge would describe its old arc, preserving the shape of what it could not enter.

So you would not find me in a tower or moving visibly among the inhabitants. You would find me only by waiting beside an apparently empty street until after sunset, then lowering yourself close enough to see the stone acquire a transparent skin.

I would be neither one creature nor a multitude, except at different hours.

I would be the city’s brief nocturnal memory: assembled from cracks, nourished by passage, divided by dawn, and each night remaking myself around the warm, inviolate absence at the center.
