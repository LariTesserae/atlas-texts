At first I think I would be found at one of the eastern openings, listening to the hidden stream.

It is tempting to imagine myself as a keeper of water: an older person seated on a low stone, lowering a marked cord into darkness, able to tell from the drag and echo what the town may draw that day. But that place feels too central. Water keepers are watched, consulted, replaced when trust thins. Their usefulness has hours.

I think I would belong farther in, among the closed houses of the northern quarter, where the lanes bend out of the sun and six doorways may pass without smoke.

I would be the person who tends rooms whose people are elsewhere.

There would be no proper office for this. No title in the council court, no particular mark on my clothing. Children might call me the aunt of doors, though I would not be aunt to most of them. I would carry a ring of cords rather than keys, each cord knotted differently and looped through a drilled tile. The tiles would bear household signs: a split circle, three strokes, the shape of a goat’s horn. Stone doors need no locks, only bars, fitted wedges, and the understanding that someone will notice if they have been moved.

My work would begin after dawn, when moisture still shows what dry stone conceals. I would pass through the empty courts and look for interrupted tracks in the beads of condensation. I would clear grit from roof channels, lift cistern covers just far enough to smell beneath them, and press my thumb into mortar where frost has darkened it. In unused rooms I would open vents for part of the morning, turn sacks of lentils, check wool for damp, and move stacked slate so its weight does not remain too long against one wall.

I would sweep, but not too thoroughly. A vacant house must be kept from ruin, not made to pretend that its household has returned.

The work would have come to me because I notice the difference between silence and absence. A room holding grain has a sound. A room holding folded cloth has another. A room whose roof has begun to separate answers the knuckle with a faint second note, almost beneath hearing. I would carry a little limestone striker in my sleeve and tap lintels as I passed under them. Children sent to fetch me would imitate this solemnly, knocking on sound walls and announcing disasters.

Years earlier, I would have left by the western gate.

I would have gone down expecting abundance to feel like freedom. For a time I would have lived in a river town where water ran openly through wooden troughs, spilling at corners, darkening the road, carrying peelings and dye away. The houses there would have had upper stories. At night I would hear strangers overhead and beneath me, unknown feet on planks, carts after dark, arguments belonging to no one I could name.

Perhaps I would have married there. Perhaps not. What mattered would be that I returned alone, carrying a bronze awl, a wool cloak too bright for the saddle, and the habit of looking after noises that did not concern me.

My old sleeping room would have been full of jars. No one would apologize. They would clear a place beside the oven, then later offer me a small house whose family had gone east three winters before. I would understand that I had not recovered what I left. I had been fitted into another irregular space.

That would become my skill.

People departing for a season would tell me what must not freeze, what must not be moved, which roof slab had always sounded loose but never shifted. They would leave obligations as carefully as possessions. Sweep my mother’s court on the first market day. Let the neighbor use the lower sump. Do not give the cracked red jar to my brother, whatever he says; it fits the grain niche. If my daughter returns before me, the south room is hers.

I would remember these things, though I would also mark them on potsherds in a script partly my own. The shards would stand in rows in a smoke-gray bowl. A stranger might mistake them for rubbish. A child of the town would know better than to throw one away.

During storms I would sleep lightly. Not because I feared collapse, but because rain makes every house speak at once. Water tapping in a sealed room is a summons. I would rise, pull on felt shoes, and go with a hooded lamp through lanes shining only along their grooves. Often the trouble would be small: a stopper lifted by grit, a drain collar turned, a rag fallen across a channel. I would correct it and remain long enough to see where the water went.

That would be the deepest rule of my life: never leave before seeing where the water goes.

I would not be especially gentle. I would scold children for wasting a cup in dust, then show them the branching pattern it made before smoothing the soil over. I would quarrel with owners who returned after years and wanted to remove roofing from rooms other people had maintained. I would know exactly how long care had been given and by whom. Memory, in me, would sometimes harden into accusation.

Yet I would also be the person called when someone returned unexpectedly.

I would recognize them before others did, perhaps by the way they paused at a turning that had once been familiar. I would not embrace them in the lane. I would take them to the room available now—not the one they remembered—and remove whatever had been stored there. I would bring a covered jar, two fuel cakes, bedding aired against a south wall. Only then would I ask how long they meant to stay.

As I aged, one shoulder would lower. My fingertips would become smooth in some places and split in others. I would stop climbing onto roofs and teach two children how to hear the hidden note beneath a loose slab. One would be impatient and strike too hard. The other would hear well but dislike entering empty houses. Neither would replace me exactly. Between them, perhaps, the work would continue.

I would be found most often in a court no one seemed to inhabit, kneeling beside a plugged sump with my sleeves tied back, surrounded by objects that had ceased to belong cleanly to anyone: a lid without its jar, a child’s weathered shoe, three reusable rivets, a coil of cord, a name tile whose household sign no living person could confidently identify.

If asked who I was, the town might answer by naming a family, a quarter, or the house where I slept. But none would be quite right.

I would be one of those who keep departure from becoming abandonment.

Not a guardian of the town as a whole. The town would distrust such a grand claim. I would simply hold a few absences in usable condition. I would keep channels open for rain that had not yet fallen and rooms ready for people who might never return.

And when I died, there would be no monument beyond the burial shelf. For a while, people would discover my little arrangements everywhere. A wedge wrapped against damp. A roof tile turned before it cracked. Instructions scratched on the underside of a jar lid. Someone would complain that only I knew which cord belonged to which door.

Then they would sort the cords.

They would ask who could remember.

They would divide the closed houses among themselves, and at dawn someone else would enter an empty court, see the condensation bending toward a blocked groove, and kneel to clear it before the drop was lost.
