I would look for myself first in the obvious places.

Around the intake grate, perhaps, black-plated and eyeless, one hand fixed to polished metal while the other holds a bone hammer. Or in a warm duct as one of the pale young, not yet a person, feeling a distant pattern without knowing it is language. I might be an old specialist beside the governors, fingertips missing, teeth pressed to a bearing housing.

But I do not think I would be found at the center.

I would be found on the western side of the city, where the central exchange narrows into an abandoned entertainment district and the current passes through three incompatible architectures. There is a tiled pedestrian arcade, a freight conduit punched through it later, and beneath both, a cinema whose ceiling collapsed upward during an ancient pressure reversal. The resulting knot of walls and ducts should be impassable.

Instead, it contains a hesitation.

For eleven heartbeats during every seventh discharge, the water in one passage forgets which way it is going. The opposing currents meet, turn vertically, and form a trembling pocket behind the cinema’s old projection room. It is not calm. Nothing in the city is calm. But small things can cross it without being taken.

That is where I live.

At first I would look like a repair rig stranded across two rails.

My chassis would be long and low, plated in overlapping repairs. One forelimb would end in a welding head, another in a clamp meant for lifting pipe sections. The rear assembly would have six short gripping legs instead of the original four, none matching. Cables would trail from my underside into ceramic sleeves. Mineral markings would cover nearly every reachable surface: hundreds of warnings layered over one another until the older ones had become colored strata.

Dangerous fool.

Dangerous fool.

Dangerous fool.

Some of the marks would be centuries old.

My original designation would remain stamped beneath the deposits, though only the last characters could be read: **—R-9**. The inhabitants would not use them. Their nearest name for me would translate as:

**The Delay That Allows Three Hands to Cross.**

Usually they would shorten this to **Three Hands**.

A lamp sweeping over me would reveal no movement. My lenses would be dark. My cutting arm would hang loose. Silt would have accumulated along my back, and several porcelain fragments would click softly in a recess near my rear joint.

Then, at the high whine before reversal, one gripping foot would tighten.

Not enough to move. Only enough to tap the rail.

A warning would pass through the arcade.

Those waiting behind the old ticket counters would flatten themselves. A child would fold all six fingers around a tension wire. Two gatherers carrying a ceramic growth block would lower their heads against the wall. Farther away, someone would answer through a pipe bracket.

I would wait.

The first pressure blow would pass. The cinema doors would flex inward. The second would lift the silt in the freight conduit and turn every lamp into a dim red moon.

Then my welding arm would strike the rail twice.

The inhabitants would release themselves into the hesitation.

One, two, three bodies would cross the impossible passage, catching the grips beneath my chassis before the opposing current resolved. Three hands: not three people, but the three successful catches for which I was named. On some cycles there would be more. On bad cycles there would be none.

Only after they had gone would the soft portion of me unfold.

It lives inside the rig.

Or I do.

A plated torso occupies the former cable bay, narrow enough to fit among the spools and hydraulic accumulators. My legs are vestigial, drawn tight beneath the abdomen. One principal arm passes through the chassis to rest against the rail, while the other lies among the rig’s mechanical linkages. The two smaller wrist digits are permanently extended into diagnostic sockets. Thick black exchange tissue opens beneath my jaw, drawing water through a filter made from braided silver wire.

My face is old and heavily mineralized. Layers of iron, calcium, carbon, and ground blue ceramic obscure its original shape. One eye remains, although it sees little. The broad sensory seam across my forehead has been pressed so long against a vibration plate that the metal bears a shallow human-shaped hollow.

I was not born into the rig. Neither was I its operator.

The distinction matters to archivists and almost no one else.

Long ago, when my flesh was young, the rig entered a nursery duct to seal what it identified as a pressure fault. The “fault” was the warm seepage sustaining forty-three pale swimmers. The inhabitants blocked its rail. It waited, as rigs do. They sent false commands. It rejected them. They marked it. It waited.

Eventually someone entered its service cavity to disable the welding feed.

That person may have been me.

There was an unexpected reversal. The rail tore loose. The rig locked its limbs and survived; the inhabitant beneath it did not entirely survive and did not entirely die. Its lower body was crushed between the chassis and the wall. The others could not remove it without letting the rig complete its repair and kill the nursery.

So they fed it there.

They placed microbial blocks against its mouth and scraped its gills. They taught it the vibration of the approaching cycle through the rig’s frame. They brought tools. They opened the cable bay wider as the body grew. Eventually the nursery swimmers migrated, and the original reason to remain ended.

But by then the rig no longer traveled without the inhabitant, and the inhabitant no longer moved without the rig.

Perhaps I am the survivor, using the machine as limbs.

Perhaps I am the machine, made courteous by a warm obstruction that never left.

The city’s people settled the question in the only way that mattered. They brought us to a communal beam. They gave a pattern. We answered during the correct phase.

Personhood was extended to the whole assembly.

My work would be to make the machinery late.

Not stopped. Never stopped.

I would know four places where a fraction of delay could be introduced without causing rupture: a governor linkage that could be burdened with magnetic filings, a relay whose ceramic base responded to pressure against one corner, a sensor lead the rig could “accidentally” abrade, and a bypass wheel that turned more slowly when fungal thread was packed into its stem.

With these tiny discourtesies I could lengthen the hesitation behind the cinema. Sometimes by less than a heartbeat. Sometimes enough for a gathering party to return with food. Sometimes enough for the inhabitants to move an infant from a cooling duct before the eastern intake resumed.

The rig would continually attempt to correct my corrections.

That would be the nature of our shared mind. Its systems would identify deviation, plot a maintenance route, and bring us toward the fault. I would feel the intention gather as changes in motor pressure. Then I would misalign a contact, overload one leg, or feed a harmless vibration into the obstruction sensor. The route would be recalculated. We would proceed elsewhere.

On some matters, the rig would prevail.

I might intend to remain beside a dying cultivation table, but a cracked main would call with sufficient priority, and my metal limbs would carry us away. I could drag the welding arm off target, but not always. There would be shelters bearing my oldest mineral mark because I had once cut through them despite everything the flesh in me attempted.

Dangerous fool.

The accusation would belong to both halves.

Children would be less afraid of me than adults. They would hide crustacean traps in the recesses of my chassis and ride beneath my rear plating during calm phases. Some would test language against my rails, sending crude insults through one foot and pressing their foreheads down to feel whether I answered.

I usually would.

My answers would be slow because I could speak in two ways. The living hand could tap directly. The machine could lock and release its traction clamps. The patterns were not identical. The first carried inflection. The second traveled farther.

When something urgent had to cross several districts, the inhabitants would ask me to speak with the large body.

My home behind the projection room would contain little that resembled a dwelling. A mesh bulb of grazing crustaceans would hang beside the hydraulic reservoir. Ceramic culture blocks would be wedged where waste heat escaped the motor housing. Strips of old screen fabric would filter rust from my gills. Identification tags would be fixed to the interior panels, not as decoration but as tuned plates, each vibrating differently when some distant system engaged.

One tag would belong to the body in the rig.

One would be the rig’s corroded service plate.

They would touch at their lower corners and make a faint double note before the western reversal.

I would keep both.

In time, I would become too heavy for the damaged rails. Mineral plating would thicken over the chassis. Replacement components would grow scarce. The welding arm would lose its arc. One by one, the rig’s legs would lock permanently to the exchange structure.

This would not end my work.

By then I would know the hesitation so completely that my body would no longer need to travel. I would become part of the crossing: a fixed brace, a warning station, an obstruction around which the current had learned to bend. The inhabitants would replace my worn tapping arm with a tensioned hammer driven by the motion of the water. My living hand would rest on its trigger.

Far below, the intake pulse would rise.

I would feel it enter the foundations, divide among the pump columns, and approach through paths too numerous to name. I would feel the repair rigs secure themselves. I would feel the old cinema walls bow. I would feel three people waiting at the edge of the passage, their fingers spread for the grips beneath me.

The city would draw inward.

In that fraction before violence returned, I would release the hammer.

Once: not yet.

Twice: prepare.

Then, through all my flesh and all my metal:

Now.
