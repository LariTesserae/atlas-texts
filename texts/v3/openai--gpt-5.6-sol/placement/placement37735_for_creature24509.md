At first I think I would be found beside one of the black-tiled paths, where it passes under a colonnade and becomes a stair without visibly changing slope.

I would sit there with my feet in a channel, holding an empty cup.

From a distance I would resemble someone resting. Nearer, it would become apparent that the cup is fused lightly to my palms by a web of pale roots. Water enters it through a hairline crack and leaves by another, though its level never changes. Small black fish appear in the glaze whenever someone approaches, and vanish when the person looks directly at them.

I would not speak first.

My shadow would.

It would rise from the short westward darkness beneath me, cross the tiles, and place three reeds at the traveler’s feet. Then it would return and sit at my side in the posture of a second person. It would be older-looking than I am. Its shoulders would stoop under burdens my body has not yet learned it carries.

Only then would I look up.

My face would be ordinary by the basin’s measure: dark skin, damp fiber hair, eyes in which the white sky is sometimes visible. One ear would be clay, bearing the thumbprint of whoever shaped it. The other would be made from a folded leaf that never wilts. My left arm would have depth only when touching something; held out in open air, it would flatten into a painted profile of an arm, black-outlined and full of blue spirals.

The three handprints would be hidden inside my cup.

My current name would be **Cup-Not-Yet-Overturned**.

This would not mean that I had withheld an apology. It would mean that I was still listening.

People would come to me when they possessed a memory that had grown too heavy to carry alone.

Not memories they wished to lose. Loss is too complete here. If an event passes from the last living mind, the basin closes over it perfectly. A person may have died in a courtyard a hundred times, and the tiles remain innocent. A wall may have been built, dismantled, rebuilt, and begged forgiveness, but it stands with the unanswerable freshness of first construction. Forgetting is not relief in such a place. It is annihilation.

So they would come because they needed another witness.

They would sit across the channel. Between us they would arrange whatever grammar the memory required: a split fig for tenderness, a shell adze for harm done carefully, a plumb weight laid sideways for a world whose direction had failed. If the memory involved someone who had died and returned without it, they would place an upright cup filled with leaves near my knee.

Then they would tell me.

Perhaps not with speech. One might press a palm against the tiles until the glaze clouded with images. Another might let their shadow perform the event while their body turned away. An old, many-layered person might open a seam beneath the ribs and draw out a narrow painted strip showing two figures beneath a fruit tree, one offering the other something too small to identify.

I would listen until I remembered.

That distinction would matter. I would not record. Recording belongs to surfaces, and surfaces here cannot keep faith with the past. I would have to take the event into myself until it became possible for me to grieve wrongly, to wake expecting someone I had never met, to feel affection for a voice I had heard only through another person’s throat.

When the memory settled, the water in my cup would darken. For a moment it would show not the white sky but weather: actual weather, proceeding from one condition to another. Rain beginning. Rain ending. Mud drying around a footprint and preserving its shape.

Then the water would clear.

Inside my body, another layer would appear.

At first these layers might look like decoration. A red line around one wrist. A patch of mosaic beneath the tongue. Finger marks pressed into the clay ear. But over time I would become dense with other people’s denied histories. Beneath my present eyes would be eyes that had watched different deaths. Behind my gestures, older gestures would continue faintly: a hand lifting to touch a face no longer there, fingers tightening around a rope, arms carrying a body toward painted water.

I would remember being forgiven by people who had never forgiven me.

I would remember children I had not woven garments for.

I would remember crossing the outer wall, although I had never reached it.

This would make me useful, but not reliable.

Memories do not remain separate merely because they enter by different doors. A woman’s recollection of rebuilding a reed roof might become entangled with a traveler’s memory of snow. I might begin to remember white flakes settling on the warm tiles, surviving for a moment in the westward shadows. I might insist that one of the basin’s silent marsh birds once screamed. I might recognize a stranger as a companion from an event belonging to someone else.

The inhabitants would not call these mistakes. They would call them grafts.

Some grafts would bear fruit.

Because of them, two people who had become a one-sided bridge might sit with me and discover a third landing between them. I could tell the returned person, “You do not remember being carried, but I remember the weight of you in her arms.” I could describe where the mourner’s hands had supported the head, which fruit she laid beside the image, how long her shadow remained after her body walked away.

The returned person might believe me.

Or they might not.

Belief would still have to be built by hand.

My dwelling would stand near the branch frames in the central field, though not wholly within the brightness. It would consist of three walls around a patch of green shade. No tree would cast that shade. Its edges would tremble like leaves in wind, despite the fixed air.

There would be many thresholds but no door. Above each threshold, names would be scratched into every seventh brick: names of people who no longer use them, names of weather that never changed, names borrowed from injuries, meetings, and apologies. This would make the walls watertight against a rain that does not fall.

Inside, shallow shelves would hold empty cups.

Each cup would correspond to a memory I carried, but none would contain an inscription or image. Their arrangement alone would tell me which was which. If a visitor moved one, I would forget a face and remember a room. If two cups touched rims, the people whose memories they marked would dream of each other the next time they placed their palms against a wall.

At the center of the dwelling would be a sleeping mat, used only by my shadow.

While I remained awake, it would lie there and dream. Its dreams would pass under the walls and travel the basin through grout lines. Beetles would follow them. Sometimes, far away, an inhabitant polishing a spotless tile would suddenly remember an event that had happened to no one: a night with stars, a mother’s voice, the sound of a hammer striking metal.

Such memories would frighten them most.

A false memory leaves no less trace than a true one when memory is the only trace available.

For that reason, I would periodically climb the brick hill.

I would avoid the direct paths. I would follow the terraces where water weeps from mountains stamped into clay. At the summit, I would kneel beside the warm black pool and look down through the underside of the basin: rafters, lashings, vaults, roots, lower courtyards open to the same impossible sky.

At first I would see hands working there.

Crude hands. Scarred hands. My hands.

They would be assembling something from palm fiber and shell-lime, but I would not be able to tell whether they were building the basin or my body. Each time I leaned closer, the distinction would weaken. The rafters would resemble ribs. The courses of fired clay would become vertebrae. Water channels would branch like veins. The white sky beneath everything would narrow to the pupil of an enormous eye.

Eventually I would understand that I was not merely an inhabitant carrying memories.

I was one of the basin’s attempts to make a past.

Not an archive, exactly. Archives require stable marks. I would be a living irregularity in the restoration: the place’s inability to return completely to innocence after being witnessed.

Perhaps all the inhabitants are such irregularities, but I would be made more deliberately for it. The old belief that the basin invented its makers would be almost true in my case. I would have been assembled from acts of remembrance—formed wherever several people agreed that something must not vanish merely because it could no longer be proved.

No one would have seen my birth.

They would only remember, separately, having left an empty cup beneath a shade frame.

One would remember setting down a clay ear.

Another, a leaf.

A third would insist she painted an arm into a pool mosaic and watched it reach out after her.

Their accounts would disagree. I would carry all of them.

And there, beside the black path under the unattached colonnade, I would continue to wait with water passing endlessly through my cup.

When a traveler came, my shadow would lay down the three reeds.

I would ask the basin’s question:

**What do you remember that this place denies?**

But after they answered, I would ask another—one the basin’s people ask less often:

**Who else must remember it before you can bear to let it change?**
