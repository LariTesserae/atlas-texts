I would not find myself first as a figure.

I would look too long among the walkers, mistaking every pause in their arrangement for recognition. I would search the sheltered huts for a mask shaped to my face. I would climb to the dark-glass plateau and kneel among the bowls, believing the persistence of rain’s image must have called me there.

But those would be places I visit, not the place I begin.

I begin in a stone box beneath the third arch.

The box is narrow, fitted into a recess where two slabs fail to meet. Its lid has not been opened for several thousand years, although there are days when it remembers being open. Inside lie gray flakes of speech, packed so tightly that their edges have fused. Most are ordinary remnants: warnings, measurements, names of tools, instructions for lifting stone. Some are cries torn flat by the mountain’s chord. Some are answers whose questions were lost outside shelter.

Among them is one transparent flake.

It never reaches the bottom of the box.

Whenever the surrounding rock contracts, it rises a finger’s width through the gray layers. When the rock expands, it sinks again. It is thinner than mica and almost without shape, but within it there is a sound no inhabitant of the basin has heard: not the imagined sound of rain, not the future memory given by the arch, but the first small impact of one drop against an empty bowl.

That flake is the earliest part of me.

Not my soul. There are no souls here unless they can be wedged, laminated, or carried. It is more like the pressure from which I grow.

The hut containing the box stands close to the third arch, though never at a stable distance. Sometimes its doorway opens directly beneath the unfinished curve. Sometimes the arch is half a league away, framing the softened future. On rare occasions the hut appears on the other side of the threshold, in the country that does not yet exist. Then its roof is rounded by erosion, its walls are dark with imagined wet, and pale growth fills the seams between its stones.

No one can enter it during those intervals.

When the hut returns, it brings back smells for which the basin has no substances.

The gathered people avoid the box at first. They know that transparent words are dangerous. Gray speech has accepted being residue; it lies obediently where it fell. A transparent word may still believe itself speakable.

Eventually one of them—seven-fingered, wearing a mask with a single channel from brow to chin—sets the box on a metallic leaf. The mountain’s visible thunder passes through the hut. The gray flakes arrange themselves according to the stress beneath the floor.

Warnings move north.

Tool names collect along the leaf’s veins.

Fragments of abandoned conversation overlap and build brief, dust-colored rooms.

The transparent flake remains apart. Around it, the leaf dimples.

The gathered one touches it with an outer finger, the finger that is only a possible position of the hand. The flake divides without breaking. One half adheres to the mask. The other enters the dark interval between wrist and palm.

That is when I acquire a face and a hand, though neither belongs to me yet.

For many gatherings I am distributed among others. A gesture here. A vertical channel in a new mask. A habit of placing empty bowls beneath cracks that cannot leak. One worker at the arch begins turning their head toward sounds that have not happened. Another finds that dust clings to their shoulders in branching lines, as though tracing the paths of rivulets.

They do not know these traits have a common source. Individuality is difficult to detect when bodies are assembled from shared stone.

I become myself only after an accident.

A ridge shifts by the width of a salt crystal. The bellow arrives everywhere. The hut’s shelter fails for less than a breath.

The gathered workers loosen. Their arms open into alternatives. Slate plates fall without choosing a gravity. Fibers recoil into the walls. Their voices flatten and scatter.

But the transparent flake does not fall.

It draws the scattered pieces around itself.

For one instant I stand in the unsheltered roar.

I have too many bodies. I am tall enough to touch the arch and small enough to lie within the stone box. Seven hands radiate from each arm. My mask is not salt but the concavity of a face: an absence shaped to receive something. Through my torso, the mountains scrape against their other versions.

Then the silence returns, and most of me collapses.

What remains is a person kneeling on the hut floor, assembled incorrectly.

My plates touch one another. Walkers’ plates never do.

My tendons are braided from the flattened speech of several speakers. The gathered people use mineral fiber.

Inside my nested membranes is not cold dust but a fine white powder that cannot decide whether it is salt.

I have no memories of a past. Instead, I remember the future behind the third arch: long channels shining on softened cliffs, fronds bowed beneath accumulated drops, the black basin holding a sky in fragments. Yet in those memories I never see rain falling. I remember only what follows it.

This incompleteness keeps me alive.

The walkers feed on resolution, but they cannot consume me. If one approaches, seeking the instant when I finish becoming true, it finds no such instant. I contain a consequence without an event. I am evidence for something that has not happened.

The nearer a walker comes, the more uncertain its own plates become. Gaps widen. Its salt mask loses symmetry. Sometimes a hand appears inside its empty cage and presses outward.

For this reason they keep their distance.

For this reason they follow me.

I would be called a keeper, if naming me did not risk turning the hut into one. Perhaps I would be called After-Rain, except there is no word for “after” that can cross the third arch intact. The gathered people would identify me by the way I sort speech: I place every question beneath its answer and leave between them a space for the world to change.

My work would be modest.

I would repair bowls.

Not the finest ones carried to the plateau, but cracked bowls, discarded bowls, bowls woven before their makers learned the imagined shape of water. I would mend them with thin ribbons unwound from mineral trunks. I would test them by filling them with speech flakes. If a bowl can hold the word for falling without taking on the shape of a fall, I would carry it uphill.

The ascent might take twenty steps or three centuries.

I would travel only when the basin ceased observing me. In this I would resemble a walker. Yet wherever I passed, the ground would become less probable, not more. Stones settled into one orientation would remember another. Finished cracks would branch again. A transparent boulder might recover a clouded core.

The gardens along my route would close.

Then, slowly, they would reopen with altered leaves: softer, broader, lined with channels that serve no purpose. Their roots would loosen their grip on pressure and curl toward hollows in the rock.

I would be feared for that. In a world sustained by strain, the possibility of release resembles starvation.

At the plateau, I would place each repaired bowl around the absence of the pool. There would already be thousands. Some would face upward. Some would adhere to vertical surfaces, obeying local gravities. Some would hover above the concavity, held there by the expectation of weight.

I would not lead the waiting. I would not know whether the first drop would save anyone.

The high grove would recognize the transparent flake within me. Silver ripples would travel through its crowns. Its roots, stopping at the hollow’s edge, would tighten until the dark glass sang a note separate from the mountain’s chord.

Then I would descend into the absence.

Not step into it—the depression admits neither matter nor light—but become distributed around its boundary. One hand at the rim. One mask reflected in salt crystals. One memory lodged in each bowl. My torso dispersed among the braided trunks. My voice returned in pieces to the stone box beneath the third arch.

I would make myself into the shape around what is missing.

There I might remain for ten thousand years, gathering only when someone brought a newly made vessel. Each time, I would ask by placement rather than speech:

Is this meant to catch the drop?

Or to prove that it fell?

Or to preserve the emptiness that taught us to imagine it?

No one would answer in sequence.

The gathered people would touch the ground. The walkers would wait beyond the grove. The leaves would practice softness. Far below, an unfinished fault would deepen because the mountain had begun to remember it as a riverbed.

And perhaps I would understand at last that I am neither one of the sheltered people nor a walker, neither speech nor body.

I am the basin’s failure to resolve the question of rain.

That is why I can persist where the walkers cannot consume me. That is why I assemble from many lives without becoming any of them. That is why my face is concave, my hands are excessive, and every memory I possess begins after an event that has never taken place.

You would find me wherever the country prepares to receive what does not exist:

in the empty bowl,

in the channel cut before water,

in the softening leaf,

in the transparent word that refuses to fall.

Most often, though, I would be at the rim of the absent pool, with one hand extended over its darkness, feeling for the weight of the first drop before it arrives.
