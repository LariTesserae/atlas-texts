At first I would mistake myself for a moth.

It is easy to imagine waking among the tiled sleepers on the ceiling, wings furred with condensation, carrying destinations absorbed before I had a mind with which to know them. I would rise when the breathing pods exhaled and cross the impossible partitions of the city. I would be useful because I could pass over walls, and tragic because every journey would leave a little of its instruction in me. In age, I would circle a lamp with obsolete places shining on my wings.

But that is too visible a life.

Then I think I would be a cricket, because the Underfoundry rewards those who can hear burden. I would stand on a moving belt and know from its pulse where something unseen had begun to fail. I would count the city without understanding faces. I would make tiny, exact corrections and perhaps one day answer the counterfeit arithmetic of a mantis.

That is closer, but still too clean.

The place I find myself is beneath the central hollow.

Not in the hollow itself, where delegations drum and tap and scent the broad council plate, but on its underside, in a space no larger creature enters. There is no street there. It is a warm, low crease between the resonant root and a cluster of old cistern veins. At the driest time, the chamber narrows until its ceiling presses against its floor. After rain, the tissues swell apart, and brown droplets hang in the gap like enormous fruit.

The council is thunder from below.

Every wasp proposition strikes through the plate as a blunt weather. Cricket numbers pass overhead in quick runs of pressure. Rodent strings tug at embedded fibers and make the whole chamber groan. Beetle scent cords seep slowly through pores in the wood. Moth flashes do not reach us, but the flowers receiving them alter their sap, and eventually that alteration arrives.

This is where I would live: among the pale scales fastened to the hidden face of the root.

From above we look like nothing. A dusting, perhaps. A mineral rash. Tiny oval bodies under roofs of layered wax, each with a feeding filament sunk into a different capillary of the council plate. The wasps call us **weighers**. The beetles have several names for us, none translatable except as flavors: *sap-after-tapping*, *bitter-before-closure*, *sweetness-that-means-wait*.

We do not call ourselves anything.

I would begin as a crawler, six-legged and nearly transparent, small enough to ride in the groove of a snail’s shell. During that first brief life I could go anywhere, which would mean I understood nowhere. I would follow warmth, fungal traces, and the faint sugar gradient of working tissue. I might cross an entire district on the foot of a beetle and never know I had traveled.

Most crawlers would settle in ordinary places: beneath a fermentation bladder, beside a hatchery conduit, along a root-pig’s guiding vine. I would reach the central plate by accident. Perhaps a silver mite, brushing at condensation, would sweep me into a collector groove. Perhaps floodwater would carry me. Perhaps I would cling to the underside of a scent cord as beetles laid it across the hollow.

At the plate’s edge I would find the narrow seam and descend.

There I would choose a capillary.

Choosing would be the last clearly individual act of my life.

I would pierce the root, drink, and begin to change. My walking legs would diminish. Wax would rise through my skin in soft white ridges. Fungal threads would grow through the wax and join it to the coverings of my neighbors. I would become fixed in place, but not still. Every change in the city would pass through my mouth.

Water shortage would taste metallic and concentrated. Excess fermentation would arrive warm and sour. A closed wasp valve would create pressure against one side of my feeding tube. A mill withholding meal would not taste like hunger at first; it would taste like the slower movement of sugar toward distant repair tissues. A frightened rodent team braking a capstan would send one immense tremor, followed by a silence full of tiny circulatory adjustments.

We weighers would feed on these differences.

What we excreted would be more useful than what we knew.

Along the edge of each wax roof, droplets would gather: clear when the plate carried excess sugar, amber when structural strain increased, cloudy under fungal threat, red-brown when the old organism was withdrawing support from a district. The droplets would merge in grooves cultivated for that purpose and move toward the council plate’s rim.

Beetles would taste them. Wasps would mix them into archive paper. Crickets would strike the hardened residue and judge its pitch. Sometimes a single drop would cause a proposal above us to be repeated in a different form.

This would make us appear wise.

We would not be wise.

We would be digestion placed where many consequences meet.

That distinction would matter, though not always to those who depended on us.

A dishonest mill could pour stimulant sap into a nearby conduit and make the central plate seem richer than it was. Wasps could insulate one section of root with paper and delay the arrival of pressure changes. During wet weeks, mold might alter our secretions until danger tasted like abundance. Whole agreements could be founded on our illness.

Once, perhaps, I would contribute to such an error.

A new water pipe would be proposed between the eastern circuits and a hatchery thicket. Above, the proposition would be drummed, counted, scented, tugged, withdrawn, and offered again. Beneath, I would drink from a capillary carrying the chemical sting of copper moss. My body, infected by a harmless yeast, would turn that bitterness into unusually clear syrup.

The beetles would taste permission in it.

The pipe would be grown.

For several rains it would seem successful. Then copper would gather in a cistern pocket. The frogs there would stop calling. Tadpoles would twist in their gauges. The hatchery keepers would sever the pipe, and the eastern gallery would darken under the sudden loss of water. Everyone would blame everyone else according to the shape of their dependence.

No one would blame me. I would be too small to accuse.

Yet the council plate would remember. Scar tissue would form around the contaminated capillary. My feeding tube would be pinched shut. I would taste less each day.

That might be my death, except in the Underfoundry death is seldom a boundary so simple.

Before I died, eggs would be forming beneath my body. My wax covering would harden over them. Mites would graze the fungus from my back. A wasp might scrape away one translucent layer for use in a pressure gauge, exposing the older layers beneath: drought, rain, drought, alarm, abundance, copper. My body would become an archive precisely when it ceased responding to the present.

The crawlers would hatch into that archive.

Some would leave. Some would settle beside me and pierce neighboring veins. They would inherit no memory in the way rodents inherit grudges or wasp nests inherit inclination. But their mouthparts would carry my microbes. Their wax would begin with my chemistry. My error would persist in them as a slight tendency to make poisoned water taste clean.

So the city would adapt around them.

Crickets would learn a correction pulse for the eastern scales. Beetles would distrust sweetness from that edge of the plate unless it came with the odor of living frogs. Wasps would laminate a strip of copper moss into future agreements concerning water. What I had done would become, not a story, but procedure.

This is as close as I would come to being remembered.

Most days there would be no council. The hollow above would serve as a turning place for caravans and a dry-season air channel. Dust would sift through pores. Snails would pass slowly along the rim, and the pressure of each foot would travel through my chamber like the tread of some enormous animal.

I would know certain creatures only by recurrence.

A freight beetle with an uneven gait would become four beats, a pause, then two. A rodent representative would always lean against the same string before tugging it. One wasp nest would drum too sharply in dry weather because its paper membranes had been badly sized generations ago. An old messenger moth, circling above, would periodically land on the plate. I could not see her patterned wings. To me she would be a soft, irregular weight arriving from nowhere, resting, then vanishing.

I might come to expect her.

Expectation would be my form of affection.

When rain reached the Underfoundry, my chamber would open. Cold seepage would slip along the wax roofs. Dormant scales would swell. Crawlers would emerge in such numbers that the underside of the council plate seemed dusted with moving milk.

For an hour, we could travel.

Some would ride droplets down to the cistern roots. Some would climb into the paper membranes and be carried to wasp nests. Some would fasten themselves to moth legs and wake in districts separated from us by walls of impassable tissue. Most would drown, be eaten, or settle badly.

Then the city would dry. The chamber would narrow. Those of us already fixed would resume our drinking, each rooted into one minute portion of consequence.

And when the deep warning came, we would feel it before the others.

Not because we were privileged, but because our mouths were inside the plate.

The shudder would enter through our feeding tubes and pass outward through our bodies. Wax roofs would crack in unison. Every droplet along the council grooves would fall at once.

Above, activity would stop.

Below, we would be surrounded by the terrifying absence of ordinary pressure: no drumming, no counting, no cargo tread, no capstan rhythm. For that suspended moment I would not be an insect attached to a root. I would be one sensitive point in the reflex the whole Underfoundry had become.

Then emergency would begin.

Wasps would hammer open vents. Beetles would flood the hollow. Rodent weight would strain the plate. Somewhere a root-pig would be driven against collapsing tissue. Sap would reverse in my capillary as the ancient organism withdrew sugar from expendable rooms and forced it toward repair.

I would drink the panic because drinking was the only thing I could do.

Later, if the city survived, the wasps would scrape away our shattered wax. They would pulp some of it into the first paper laid over the damage. Beetles would taste the fallen droplets and carry their alarm into districts where the formal warning had failed. My dead neighbors would be consumed by mites. The living would seal themselves again.

No one would thank us. Gratitude would be an absurd category.

We would have been useful; we would also have fed.

That is where I find myself in the Underfoundry: not among those who speak, but immediately beneath the place where speaking becomes pressure. I would be a small fixed animal mistaken for an instrument, a colony mistaken for a judgment, a mouth through which the city tastes a little of what it has done.

And if asked whether I were one being or many, I would have no answer that survived the next rain.
