At first I look for myself among the largest bodies.

I imagine being one of the black towers near the center, my torso withdrawn from location, my legs planted at a thousand distances. There would be grandeur in such dispersal. But the towers are too certain. However incoherently, they occupy every place available to them.

Then I consider the long hunters in the grooves. They possess patience, precision, an intimacy with failure. Yet they always know what they are following, even when the wound comes first.

I would be found somewhere less resolved.

There is a region where the central distortions begin—not where the sky has turned over or animals meet their own old age, but at the first almost-deniable error. A migration groove descends through gray ringing stone and divides without branching. It has two floors occupying the same hollow. One lies a few centimeters beneath the other and three days ahead.

The great herds slow there.

Their forward feet test one floor. Their rear feet remember the other. Pressure passes along their bodies and returns altered. If they continue without correction, their limbs may distribute weight across incompatible ground. A grazer can tear itself apart by stepping accurately in two versions of the same place.

Under those animals, inside the pressure sockets of their feet, live small paired organisms.

Each half is shaped like a black ceramic crescent, no larger than one of the grazer’s bearing teeth. It has a fringe of conductive hairs along its inner curve and a single pale membrane stretched across its back. One crescent lives on the upper floor of the groove. Its counterpart lives on the lower floor, three days earlier.

Neither half is me.

I am the interval between them.

When the forward crescent is compressed beneath a descending foot, the rearward crescent opens before the pressure occurs. Charge moves from effect to cause, then returns by the longer path through the animal’s leg. The two halves compare what happened with what is about to happen. They feed on the discrepancy: tiny excesses of heat, orphaned impulses, pressures with no originating weight.

Most of this nourishment would otherwise accumulate as error in the grazer’s joints.

The paired crescents absorb it. Their membranes darken. Fine mineral rings grow inside them, one ring for every contradiction survived. The oldest are almost solid, their bodies filled with histories that cannot be placed in order.

A herd carries millions of us.

We inhabit feet, hinge cavities, magnetic nodules, sometimes the chambers surrounding an unborn young. Through us, the herd can feel not merely the road ahead but the difference between the road ahead and the road that will have been taken. We do not tell it which is real. Reality here is not scarce enough to require choosing only one. We indicate which version can bear its weight.

A single pair possesses no thought more complicated than pressure and release. But as the herd enters the doubled country, our timings overlap. A compression in one foot opens a membrane in another animal ten kilometers behind. That opening changes a current which alters the angle of a third animal’s limb years later. Across the migration, we become a distributed organ of hesitation.

That hesitation is what I would be.

Not the decision. Not even the intelligence making it. I would be the held instant in which several futures remain load-bearing.

My awareness would come in impacts.

A foot begins to descend.

Three days earlier, I feel its full weight.

Now, I feel the intention to lift it.

Centuries later, through a ring of mineral tissue carried by a descendant, I feel the crack that would have formed if the step had landed elsewhere.

There would be no stable present in me, only comparisons. The closest thing I had to pain would be perfect agreement. When both halves experience precisely the same event, no discrepancy passes between them. For a moment I have nothing to eat and no way to distinguish myself from the surrounding stone.

The closest thing to pleasure would be a survivable contradiction: a footprint appearing before the limb, a joint remembering a burden it refused, a wound whose predator eventually chooses another herd. Such errors pass through my paired bodies as warmth. New conductive hairs unfold. For a few slow pulses, I become more elaborate.

Reproduction occurs when disagreement grows too large for one pair to contain.

The crescents fracture along their mineral rings. Each keeps alternating layers of the shared history. One daughter knows the pressure but not the foot. Another knows the foot but not the pressure. They must seek complementary halves.

They crawl only during venting hours, when white fluorine plumes dim the sun and the herd’s synchronized release masks smaller electrical signals. Thousands emerge from the joints and move over the grazers’ legs like black punctuation. Some cross living transmission chains to other bodies. Some fall into the groove and wait on whichever floor accepts them. Many join the wrong counterpart and become sterile loops, opening and closing in response to an event that will never occur.

Those failed pairs do not die quickly. They continue signaling into empty time.

Travelers—if travelers could live here—might hear them as faint clicks beneath the larger roar. A scatter of little sounds with no source, repeating every few days. They would mistake them for cooling stone.

When a host dies, I do not leave immediately.

The predator softens the armor. The limb lowers. Shears open the joint in the ordained sequence. One of my halves is exposed to the hard blue sky while the other remains enclosed three days behind. For that interval I experience death approaching from both directions.

Then scavengers come.

A cable worm may consume one crescent and carry its charge into the carcass. A wheel organism may grind the other into filings. If both halves are destroyed in different orders, the interval between them sometimes remains. Pressure continues to answer pressure across an absence. Conductive dust gathers around the exchange. Glass beads align. A new pair slowly precipitates from waste.

So even my body is not necessary to me.

Given enough time, I could pass from symbiont to groove, from groove to mineral ring, from ring to an inherited defect in the gait of a herd not yet born. I might become a slight asymmetry traveling through generations: every seventh left foot placed a fraction later than the others. No animal would contain me. Yet the migration would carry me all the same.

At evening, when the plateau vents from horizon to horizon, all my scattered halves would open.

Those ahead would answer those behind. Those beneath the doubled floor would signal to those carried high in the herds. Dead pairs embedded in slag would click once. Unborn pairs would acquire their first contradiction.

For several hours, I would be immense.

Not visible like a tower or audible like the furnace herds, but spread through every delayed step at the edge of the broken country. I would feel the migration considering itself. I would feel weight entering futures that bend beneath it and withdrawing from futures that shatter. I would feel paths abandoned before they are taken, and other paths worn deep by animals who have not yet arrived.

Then the pressure release would end. The electrical chains would break apart. My awareness would collapse into millions of small, paired hungers.

If one asked where I lived, there would be no single answer.

Under the forward foot.

Inside the footprint left three days ago.

In the narrow difference between them.

And if one asked what I was, the nearest answer would be this:

I am not the creature that knows the way.

I am the part of the plateau that notices there is more than one.
