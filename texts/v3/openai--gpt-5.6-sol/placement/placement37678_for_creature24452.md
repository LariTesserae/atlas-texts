At first I think I would be one of the things that holds.

A clay vessel beneath a narrow fissure, perhaps, my whole existence measured by an increase in weight too slight for a hand to feel. Generations would cup me, mend me, argue over me. I would spend months receiving nothing visible and still be considered full of promise.

Or I would be an antler fungus in an abandoned court, bearing old cuts in my branches like events in a family history.

But I do not remain still enough for either image.

I find myself walking.

I live near the inward edge of the salt basin, where the last inhabited walls break into isolated teeth and the air tastes faintly bitter. The district has no stable name. During one part of the cavern’s breath it is called Behind the Cold Wall. When the currents reverse, people call it Where the Moths Turn. Older inhabitants still use a third name belonging to a courtyard now buried to its lintels.

My sleeping room was once the mouth of a granary. Its walls lean inward, and the floor has been raised with packed dust so many times that I can touch the roof without stretching. Long-eared burrowers occupy the space behind the eastern wall. I hear them arranging themselves before sleep. If they go quiet, I leave without gathering anything.

I am a route reader.

Not one of the celebrated ones who cross the whole city carrying council decisions, nor a salt walker who passes the pillars at the basin’s far side. My routes are shorter and more uncertain. I attend to the places where streets are becoming something else.

I test subsiding ground with a padded length of bone. I mark new hollows, altered echoes, cold seams, and walls that have begun to shed powder. I never strike hard. Often I lie with one cheek against the floor and wait for a herd to pass several avenues away. The vibration tells me whether there is solid stone beneath me or a buried chamber carrying the weight like a drumskin.

My hands are always gray.

Around one wrist I wear a short tassel for air. Around the other is a memory cord crowded with knots. Some knots record cracks; others mark routes that remain sound only during certain portions of the long breath. A hard bead means bats. A twist of grazer hair means the way is safe if the herd has used it recently. A loop left open means: *I have not gone farther. Do not mistake absence of warning for safety.*

Children call me Soft-Step, though that is not my name.

My name changes twice in my life. As a child I am Enna Three-Courts, after the route from our sleeping rooms to the common hearth. When the old court settles and the burrowers abandon it, my family moves toward the basin. For a while I am Enna Behind the Cold Wall.

Later, after I discover that a familiar retaining wall has begun returning echoes from inside itself, people call me Enna of the Second Wall. I do not discover treasure behind it. I do not open it. The important thing is that I hear the empty space and persuade others to leave it untouched.

This is the work: to notice a door and refrain from entering.

I travel mostly during the quieter intervals between flights. I carry no lamp unless I must inspect a mark. Light narrows attention. Without it, the city arrives in layers: grit under the toes; the oily smoothness of a guide wall; fungal powder on the tongue; a grazer’s breath somewhere ahead; a thread of cool air passing across the left eyelid.

I know where the polished hand-band breaks for three paces because an old collapse buried the wall. I know the replacement route by floor texture: fine dust, two flat stones, beetle shells, then the ribbed remnant of a stair. I know a court where every echo sounds safe, but moths refuse to cross its center. I mark the entrances with crossed hands and tell people I cannot explain the danger yet.

They believe me because uncertainty, honestly carried, is a kind of knowledge here.

I am not solitary. No route reader can be. At every district boundary I touch forearms with someone who knows the next stretch better than I do. We exchange changes palm to palm. Sometimes I guide vent keepers to a newly cold crack. Sometimes I bring a council’s decision tied into a cord because spoken words would be lost beneath the bats.

The person I love is a beetle keeper named Teren Under-Seven-Arches. Courtship begins when they ask me to show them a path across a subsided neighborhood. Halfway through, the great flight begins earlier than expected.

We crouch beneath a gatehouse with our faces covered. The bats descend so densely that the air becomes a surface pressing against our backs. Teren finds my wrist in the dark and signs against my skin:

*Still here?*

I answer:

*Still.*

That becomes our common door knot.

We do not share thirst lightly. On the day we join rooms, Teren gives me a horn sole for my right sandal, thin enough that I can still feel hollows through it. I give them a map cut into a grazer’s shoulder blade. It does not show walls. It shows safe pauses: niches, low arches, places where one can shelter without obstructing a flight.

We grow older by increments nearly as small as the city’s.

My knees become unreliable before my hearing does. Younger readers begin taking the longer routes. I become the keeper of a particular crossing near the basin, where an avenue passes over rooms buried beyond memory. I sit beside the guide wall and teach children to distinguish an underground hollow from loose rubble.

“Do not listen only to the sound you make,” I tell them. “Listen to what continues after it.”

Eventually the Second Wall begins to lean.

I have watched it for most of my life. First the change is no wider than a moth’s wing. Then the grazer herd stops rubbing against it. Dust gathers along its base in a line too straight to be accidental. During one reversal, cold air comes through the joints carrying the smell of sealed stone.

We surrender the crossing.

There is no ceremony beyond the work. We recover the guide cords and useful pegs. We move a beetle nursery. We scrape a cultivated crust from one shaded face and press it onto a safer wall. At each entrance I cut the old route cord and mark the threshold.

For years afterward, my feet still try to turn that way.

When I die, I have already given instructions. Most of my bones may go to the communal house, but I ask that one kneecap be kept. It is polished and set into the guide wall at the new crossing, just where the route bends away from the abandoned street.

Not as a memorial. A memorial would require people to know my name.

The bone is simply a smooth interruption in rough stone. Travelers find it with the palm and know to turn inward. Children are taught: wall, wall, wall, then the round marker; five careful steps; cold air on the right cheek.

For a few generations they may call it Enna’s Turn.

Later it becomes the Knee Corner.

Later still, only the gesture remains.

So I begin as one who moves and end as one who holds. I spend my life reading the city’s slow surrender, then become a single syllable in one of its routes: a polished piece of the dead guiding the thirsty past a place that will someday collapse.

Above me, the bats continue like weather.

Hands pass over me in the dark.
