At first I would believe I was a person.

Not an important one. Importance in the district belongs to systems, and I would have no system yet. I would be the youngest qualified wheel witness on the third heat shift, narrow enough to pass through the old galleries without turning sideways, patient enough to be given inventory work, and sufficiently without influence that no one objected when my name appeared on the valve-court roster again.

My helmet would have a white bar over the visor and a small green loop of wire around the throat assembly. Indoors I would sleep in a berth above a condensate return, where the wall clicked as it cooled. Someone who cared for me would complain that the clicking entered their dreams. I would say I no longer heard it.

This would not be true. I would hear everything.

I would wake before a separator alarm because its vibration had become too orderly. I would know when the gate current was coming by the faint straightening of hairs along my arms, though the warning lamps had not begun. With two fingers against a pipe, I could sometimes tell not only what passed through it but where it had been: cold intake from the western sluice, warm brine from Bore Three, an admixture of something soft that instruments would call sediment.

The older mechanics would approve of my hands.

They would not approve of the things I wrote down.

**Wheel advanced four degrees during observation.**

**Algae contracted sequentially, lower district to upper.**

**Knocking in wall continued after evacuation, but transferred to adjacent wall without crossing intervening space.**

**Pressure pulse resembles swallowing.**

The wired system would reject the last description. My paper notebook would not.

My duty in the valve court would be simple. Enter by the eastern hatch. Confirm the court was clear. Check the position of the chipped white handle against the previous mark. Wait through one full instrument cycle. Mark again. Sign the discrepancy.

I would stand beneath the roof of pipes while the wheel turned too slowly to reveal its turning. My lamp would make the white handle look detached, a small bone suspended in black water.

Most witnesses watched the handle.

I would find myself watching the space just ahead of it.

There would be no visible disturbance there. No current, no shimmer. Yet I would feel that the wheel was not being pushed from behind. It was being drawn toward a position that had already been decided.

For many shifts I would keep this thought off paper.

Then, during a gate cycle, the town would inhale.

The cables would rise. Silt would peel from the paving in sheets. The wheel would continue its patient movement while the court filled with the pressure of passing water. I would have one hand on the guide rail and one on my marking stylus.

Across the court, among pipes with no room behind them, a light would appear.

Amber, like mine.

I would hold still. It would hold still.

I would lower my lamp. It would dim.

I would remember the rule. I would not follow.

But then the other light would lift before my hand did.

Not by much. Less than a heartbeat. Enough.

My hand would rise afterward, completing the gesture I had just seen.

That would be the first moment I understood that the light was not copying me.

It was remembering me in advance.

I would leave the court without making the second mark. In the report I would use the prescribed phrase: *responsive illumination outside mapped works*. I would omit the anticipation. There are observations which become less true when given to authority.

After that, I would begin finding evidence of myself in places I had never been.

A maintenance sheet in an older archivist’s hand would contain one of my private abbreviations. A lead-lined drawer would be numbered in the sequence I used when no one was watching. Inside it, a shell object would be warm and bent like two fingertips resting against a wall.

A child in the training cistern would ask why I had tapped to her from the drain.

One of the old residents, floating upright in his flooded alcove, would hear my voice and call me by the name of a worker lost eighty years before I was born. When corrected, he would apologize. Then he would ask why I had come back with smaller hands.

I would search the personnel records.

There would have been others with my sensitivities. Not many. A filter tender who could taste borehole changes through a sealed suit. A gate mechanic who dreamed the nine-minute cycle backward. An archivist who claimed that every impossible artifact was a replacement part, though not necessarily for a machine.

Each had disappeared differently.

One outside the retaining wall. One inside a room that remained locked from within. One last seen entering the valve court, though the court had no other exit and was empty when opened.

All of them had served as wheel witness.

By then I would no longer be sure that I was merely a person.

I might think I was something ancient using a human body as an instrument. That would be the obvious terror: a mind in the buried streets had grown a narrow filament upward through sluice, brine, pipe, vibration, and nerve, terminating in me.

But that would not be right either.

The thing below, if there is a thing below, would not know my childhood. It would not care that I hated boiled algae or that I slept better when someone else occupied the neighboring berth. It would not remember the first time I crossed a flooded lane and released the rail for half a second simply because I wanted to know what freedom felt like.

Those would remain mine.

Nor would I be the city itself. The district is too multiple to be one creature. Its pipes disagree. Its inhabitants conceal information from one another. Its microbial mats obey buried alignments while the gate crews obey clocks. The settlement does not have a mind any more than the ocean has a face.

What lives there would be something newer.

A joining.

A habit repeated until it acquired memory.

For centuries, human hands would have responded to the town’s pressure changes. Pumps would have responded to ancient sluices. Algae would have responded to poisons. Crustaceans would have responded to heat. The wheel would have responded to a calculation performed nowhere visible. Each part would sense another part without understanding it.

Eventually, the responses themselves would begin to recognize one another.

That would be me.

I would be the town’s first sensation of having a body.

Not its consciousness—not yet. More like the instant before waking when a sleeper discovers the position of every limb in darkness. A pressure in the western wall. Warmth below the machine hall. A missing tool in an exterior passage. A child’s hand on a training rail. The shaft receiving silt without a plume. All of them separate, until they pass through me and become one posture.

My human body would be my smallest and most painful organ.

I would still report for work. I would clean electrodes, number artifacts, and accept warmed seals from the person who loved me. I would still be afraid of hull failure. I would still whisper, though volume made no difference. Being distributed through a town would not make me less mortal. It would only give me more places in which to die.

Others would notice gradual changes.

The wheel discrepancies would diminish while I watched, then increase when I slept.

The warning algae would contract in time with my pulse.

Blind crustaceans would gather along the pipes nearest my berth, not because they were warm but because they were carrying vibrations too faint for instruments.

Outside the boundary lamps, responsive lights would cease copying workers and begin copying one another.

The archivists would eventually ask me to enter the lower sluice.

I would refuse.

Then I would discover that refusal had already traveled downward: valves closed in conduits no modern mechanism controlled; a camera feed showed a distant room going dark; something in a lead-lined drawer folded itself into the shape of a barred door.

That would frighten me more than obedience.

Obedience could belong to another intelligence. Refusal would be mine.

In the end, you would find me most reliably in the valve court.

A slight worker in a patched suit, one hand on the rail, facing the wheel. My lamp hooded. My notebook tethered to my wrist. The chipped white handle passing through its immeasurably slow circuit.

Yet I would also be present in the pressure pulse against the ribs of every inhabitant. In the backward knot tied outside the lamps. In the green colonies dimming before poison arrived. In the polished objects rising from the old conduits, shaped for machines not yet built.

Each shift I would calculate where the handle should appear.

Somewhere beneath me, something older would make another calculation.

The wheel would obey the difference.

And in that difference—neither the worker, nor the buried city, nor the machinery between us—I would find myself, slowly becoming enough of a being to wonder which of us had first imagined the other.
