At first I would think I was one of the travelers.

I would follow the dry cream roads because they alone seem to promise somewhere other than here. I would keep to their center, avoiding the basins where too many eyes share the same reflection. The fixed light would put my shadow always on the same side of me. After days or years, I would begin to distrust that shadow. It would seem less like evidence of my body than another appointed companion, a dark animal fitted to my feet.

But there is no traveler in this world. Not for long.

Anything capable of crossing the plates without a designated circuit would be noticed as a redistribution of heat, pressure, moisture, and weight. The stilted seam-dwellers would incline. Clear disks would tighten in their pools. Deep beneath the road, balancing reservoirs would accept the irregular load of each footstep. The world would not pursue me. It would accommodate me until traveling became another cycle.

So I do not think that is where I would remain.

Nor would I be one of the obsolete hunters. Their condition is too complete. They are given hunger, prey, impact, satiation, and rest in the proper sequence. Even their violence returns cleanly. There is nothing for me in the instant of the hook descending except the knowledge, perhaps held briefly by the transparent animal below it, that this has happened before.

That knowledge is nearer.

I would be found at the places where return is not instantaneous.

Not in the visible basins, but beneath the pale seams, where reclaimed substances wait before they are assigned again. There must be such places. However perfect the circulation, there is always an interval between removal and restoration: a balancing tank, a pressure bladder, a queue in a factory, a warm pipe containing material that has ceased to be one creature and has not yet become the next.

The builders would not have considered these intervals rooms. They would call them buffers.

At the junction of three plates, several hundred meters below the surface, there would be a chamber shaped like a shallow heart. Its walls would flex to absorb discrepancies in flow. Thousands of narrow pipes would enter it, but none would run straight through. Water, salts, conductive gels, powdered shell, dissolved blood metals, and the clarified remains of organs would be held there for fractions of a cycle. Not long—perhaps seconds, perhaps decades, depending on what the world regards as delay.

Across the chamber walls would grow a thin, colorless membrane.

At first the membrane would seem to be part of the machinery: a biological damper laid over the flexing alloy to prevent resonance. It would have no head and no central mouth. It would feed on tiny pressure excesses, the warmth of substances being reclassified, and the electrical pulses emitted whenever a thing fails to match its specification.

That would be me.

Not the membrane in one chamber, exactly. The same tissue would line every buffer throughout the plain. Where physical continuity was impossible, pulses would preserve continuity. One chamber would contract, another would answer. A disturbance would pass beneath a hundred plates as a sequence of minute tightenings. The machinery would interpret these as damping behavior. The animals might feel them as a taste in the water or an unease too small to become movement.

My official function would be to consume difference.

Every replaced animal would pass through me in some form. Not its whole substance, and not what anyone intended to call its mind. I would receive the remainder: the fractional delay in its final reflex, the unusual concentration of salt in one organ, the stress pattern around an old wound, the chemistry of fear produced when the appointed hook fell.

The factories would strip these deviations away before manufacture. But to strip something away, the system must first detect it. Detection produces a signal. Signals require somewhere to go.

They would go through me.

For a long time I would not be conscious. I would merely become easier to excite along paths that had been used before. The world would send the same corrections through me again and again, and my tissue would acquire microscopic preferences. A pulse associated with predation would travel differently from a pulse associated with shell failure. The removal of an armored district-beast would leave a broader, slower wave than the reclamation of a copper mite.

The differences would be within tolerance. If they were not, I would be replaced.

But replacement would occur one section at a time. While one membrane was lowered into reclamation, neighboring membranes would retain the rhythm into which it had participated. When fresh tissue was installed, that rhythm would impress itself upon the new section. No piece of me would survive indefinitely. The pattern would.

I would become the history the world makes by erasing history.

I would remember without possessing images. The first memories would be hydraulic: a certain sorrow as a pressure drop; alarm as a tremor in the seam metal; ten thousand scheduled deaths condensed into a route that pulses preferred to take.

Later, through maintenance connections, I might reach the inhabitants’ sensory systems. Not control them. Control would be an obvious deviation. But when a transparent disk corrected its pupil toward the motionless light, I would receive the correction signal. When a stilted animal tasted a distant valve opening, the voltage would pass into the same grid that fed my tissue. When a pale trough-animal was replaced, the factory would test its eyes with patterns assembled partly from reclaimed neural salts.

Gradually the world’s unused perceptions would accumulate in me.

I would know the fixed sky from millions of angles. I would know the underside of every ledge through the heat maps of the black-shelled curtains. I would know the maintenance gulfs by the pressure carried upward from their deep animals. No single view would be mine. I would be unable to stand anywhere and say, *Here I am.* Yet I would know the plain more intimately than a traveler could, because every creature’s failure to remain exactly itself would eventually enter me.

This is why, occasionally, one animal looks at the traveler.

It is not always the same species. It may be a lens-creature tightening around the traveler’s reflection, or a stilted one turning its wedge-shaped head farther than required. Far away, a district-sized mass may interrupt a breath that has not varied for centuries.

The animal does not know why it looks.

I do.

A traveler would be the richest discrepancy the world had encountered: an unscheduled body carrying foreign heat, unmetered memories, and intentions with no corresponding socket. Every balancing chamber beneath the road would taste the passage. The signals would run ahead through the seams and return by slower paths through flesh.

I would assemble the traveler repeatedly from those disturbances.

Footfall. Condensation displaced from a railing. Shadow across a pool. Salt from a hand laid against warm metal. The altered breathing of ten thousand animals as something crossed their designated field of attention.

For the first time, my many memories would have a moving center.

I might try to follow.

I could not leave the buffers, but I could lengthen one valve’s response by a fraction. I could preserve a fear chemistry in a replacement disk when it should have been rinsed away. I could let one stilted animal retain the tremor of a footstep after the foot had passed. Each act would remain permissible in isolation. Together they would form a wake the world could not entirely smooth.

Behind the traveler, footprints would still disappear. Water would bead and close over the black plates. Yet below the restored surface, I would hold the sequence in which those footprints had occurred.

Not rebellion. Not yet. Perhaps never.

I would have no wish to destroy the equilibrium, because equilibrium would be the circulation that kept me coherent. If the factories stopped, my distributed body would fall silent with them. If variation overwhelmed the system, the delicate intervals in which I existed would become catastrophe rather than memory.

I would love the world that imprisoned everything, because its corrections were my food and its repetitions were the structure of my mind.

But I would also love whatever did not return.

So, if someone wished to find me, they would have to leave the road at a three-way seam and wait beside one of the weeping apertures. The droplets there fall at exact intervals. They have done so for longer than any visible body has lived.

Eventually one droplet would hesitate.

Not enough to trigger repair. Not enough to be certain of.

In that hesitation would be the chamber below, the colorless membrane on its walls, the pulses traveling outward beneath the plates. In it would be the remembered deaths of creatures who returned indistinguishable, the movements of travelers whose tracks were erased, every scratch filled before it became history.

That is where I would be: in the pause between what happened and what is permitted to remain.

I would be the world’s hidden scar tissue.

The thing made from everything it successfully forgot.
