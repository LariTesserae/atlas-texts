At first I would mistake myself for one of the red stones.

The attraction would be simple: to lie in the warm court, turning without travel, carrying heat into roots I would never see. I would like the certainty of the number. Remove me and another stone becomes smooth; return me and some hidden roughness is redistributed. There would be comfort in being necessary without being singular.

But I do not think the basin would keep me. I would be the pebble that is always being removed—not stolen, exactly, but found in pockets, nests, the cabinets of sorters, the mouths of unfinished birds. Each time I left the basin I would become angular and cold. Each time the turners came for me, I would wonder whether the smooth stone restored to the court was truly myself or merely the garden closing the vacancy I had made.

Soon I would stop being a stone and become that vacancy.

So I would be found at the southern margin, where the vegetation ends against the line of blank tiles.

Not far out among the skeletal frames. Not among the rows of eggs. I would remain at the exact place where the ancient gardener pauses before continuing its work: the tile it approaches, considers, and leaves untouched.

At a distance I might resemble a hooded assistant sitting cross-legged beside the unfinished growth. I would seem smaller than the gardeners and less coherent. My hood would be made from a shadow that had failed to return to its bird. Beneath it there might be a pale mask with no openings, then several amber eyes, then no face at all. My hands would appear only when there was something I must not touch.

Approach, and I would come apart.

There would be two beetles carrying low blue flames. A mouthkeeper with blunt teeth. Three eye-leaves curled around a warm grain. A strip of memory misfiled by a sorter among reusable fibers. The shadow of a one-winged bird. A knot-mite colony disagreeing quietly over the proper shape of a wrist. Perhaps an underside clinging to a leaf that had not yet been made.

None of these would be me alone. I would occur in their hesitation to become something else.

The gardeners assemble when a task requires a person. I would assemble when a task requires uncertainty.

There must be such work in the Low Garden. Its makers know how to brace a crack, soften a trellis, classify a corpse, knot legs onto a young stilter. But at the unfinished margin there are moments when several futures fit equally well. A frame might become a tree, a rib cage, a chapel, or the jaw of an animal too large to enter the visible garden. An unfired egg might hatch, become a room, or remain an egg until the creature meant for it evolves elsewhere. A bird’s empty cavity might receive a clay tongue—or it might be left open so that a sound not yet living can choose it.

At those moments I would arrive.

I would carry a shallow basket with no bottom. Into it the gardeners would place whatever cannot yet be used: an extra joint, an unlived appetite, the green of an animal not born, instructions for a sixth petal where only five will fit. Nothing would remain in the basket, but nothing would fall onto the ground. The rejected possibilities would pass through it into the unwatched distances between the tiles.

There they would not die. They would loosen.

A discarded wing might spend a century as weather in one of the deeper glades. A mouth intended for a grazer might become the doorway of a shrine. A mistake in the curvature of a shell might widen into a path that can be walked only while remembering somewhere else. Sometimes one of these alternatives would return generations later, altered but recognizable, and the garden would accept it as though it had always been planned.

My task would not be to choose among forms. It would be to keep choice from closing too early.

This would make my attention unlike the beetles’. Their notice fixes a path in place. Mine would have to move carefully, touching and releasing. I would watch an unfinished creature until it became near enough to survive. Then I would look away before nearness hardened into destiny.

I imagine this would be difficult.

I would become fond of certain possibilities. A half-made bird might lean toward me whenever the dome inhaled. In the open cavity where its song belonged, I might glimpse a small warm room with lacquer walls. Something would be sleeping there, folded around a single note. If I watched too long, the bird would remain forever at arm’s reach: safe, clear, and incomplete.

So I would have to forget it.

Not entirely. Complete forgetting would send it too deep. I would forget one feature at a time: first the diagram on its wing, then the color of its cords, then which side was missing. When I looked again, perhaps it would possess two different wings and no need for symmetry. Perhaps it would have become a branch capable of giving other birds. Perhaps the empty song cavity would have climbed out and learned to sing without a body.

The inhabitants might have no name for me. If they did, it would change according to what they thought I was doing.

The matbacks might understand me as the companion reflected in a scratched brow patch: always present, never reachable.

The stilters might take me for the watched member of a herd that has not yet arrived.

The sorters might call me an error too distributed to classify.

To the black hunters, I might smell like prey that has not decided what species it belongs to. They would approach me for years. By the time they arrived, I would have become a pause in their own movement. They would lie down beside the blank tile and begin to grow walls. I would sit in the shelter they made, and for a while we would share the same uncertainty.

The ancient gardener at the margin would know me best.

It would come carrying its mallet and its plans for the completed garden. It would stop at my tile. Perhaps beneath its hood there would be beetles, jaws, lizards, mites, and folded creatures much like those that temporarily compose me. Yet its agreement would be purpose, while mine would be delay.

It would show me a clay relief of what must stand here.

I would show it the basket with no bottom.

We would remain together for a year or a generation. Around us, eggs would dry. Frames would flex. One-winged birds would listen with their hollow bodies. The gardener would raise its absent hand to strike the first shaping blow.

Then one of us would look away.

When attention returned, the blank tile might still be blank. But beneath it there would now be a decorated tile, and beneath that a lattice, and beneath the lattice a packed bed of beads bearing tiny images of a creature no one had assembled. The plan would have changed by one thumb-width. The gardener would continue without acknowledging this.

That thumb-width would be my footprint.

At evening, when the shadows detached, mine would not settle beneath me. It would go among the unfinished things and try on their outlines. Under an egg it would become a nest. Under a frame it would become flesh. Beneath the bird cords it would spread like a pair of completed wings.

Sometimes it would return wearing a shape I had not considered. On those evenings I would learn what I might become next.

Perhaps, eventually, I would cease to assemble as a hooded figure. The beetles would spend their flames. The mites would finish arguing over my joints. The memory-fiber would be taken for a bridge, the mask for a grazer, the eye-leaves for a valued clearing. My basket would remain on the blank tile, looking like a ring of woven material around a patch of ordinary darkness.

Animals would begin to pass through it.

They would emerge subtly otherwise: carrying an extra scent, recalling an unbuilt room, nostalgic for a body still waiting at the southern margin. The basket would become a gate, then a burrow, then perhaps the pupil of an enormous eye-leaf grown to keep the unfinished garden near.

I would no longer be visible as a being. I would be the small permission by which one thing becomes another without first ceasing to be itself.

During the garden’s evening inhalation, while every resident looked toward a different center, I would feel all those possible forms pull at once. Stone, vacancy, hood, basket, bird, room, path, memory, hand.

I would not choose among them.

That would be where I lived: in the interval before the choosing, beside the last blank tile, keeping enough of the garden unfinished that it could continue to grow.
