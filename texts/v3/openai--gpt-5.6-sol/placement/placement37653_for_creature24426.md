At first I think I would be one of the black birds.

It is an easy answer. I would live where the city is warmest, in a cavity above a cistern, clinging upside down to a wall that sweats faintly in the dark. I would have no song that belonged wholly to me. Instead I would carry a handful of useful noises: the eastern apertures under frost, the knock of a drain below the central court, the low pressure-note that means a chamber is larger than it appears.

I would fly through the city by asking it what shape it had become.

A whistle.

Its answer from the left.

Two knocks.

A close return: sealed stone ahead.

Then a pulse sent downward, vanishing into a shaft deep enough to enter but not, perhaps, to leave.

I would know the bowl not as streets and towers but as delays between sound and sound. What others called a wall would be a sharp answer. What they called open sky would be no answer at all.

But I do not think I would remain the bird.

There would be one noise in me that I could not account for.

Perhaps I learned it as a hatchling, while still blind and pressed among lichen strips in the nest. Perhaps an old bird gave it to my parents. It would be a narrow, descending note with a soft break in the middle, like wind passing through a doorway and then through a second, smaller doorway beyond it.

No standing structure would make that sound.

Still, I would repeat it.

I would use it only in certain weather: during the hour after mist spills over the rim, when the pale walls are drinking and every mineral hair carries a drop. The note would draw no answer from the visible city. Yet beneath one open stretch of black grass, the ground would tremble very slightly. Not enough to hear. Enough to feel through the feet when I landed.

The place would seem empty. No tower, no arch, only a shallow depression where moisture persisted after all the streets had dried. The grass there would be unusually dense. Under it, moss would have begun to die in a circle.

I would return to that circle year after year.

As the bird, I would not understand why. Birds do not need to understand the sources of all their maps. A sound that leads somewhere safely is retained. A sound that returns wrongly is changed. Yet this one would lead nowhere and receive no echo. I would utter it into the wet ground as if calling to an egg that had not hatched.

Then one year the silent frost would come.

I would rise with the others beyond the rim, into air too cold to hold us comfortably. Below, the bowl would glaze over. In the morning, when the pale sun entered the ice, the hidden city would appear in its lines of bubbles.

And there, beneath the empty patch of grass, would stand the outline of a tower.

It would be slender compared with the old growths, crowded with chambers too small for any grazer. One gallery would bend down through its wall and open into an entrance facing away from the prevailing wind. Beyond that entrance would be another constriction.

The shape of it would be the shape of the sound in my throat.

At that point I would have to reconsider where I ended.

Perhaps I would not be the bird at all. The bird would be one place in which I was being kept.

I might be that unopened passage.

Not a prophecy of it, because the city does not plan. Not its spirit, because nothing here is divided so neatly into matter and spirit. I would be the passage as a pattern distributed through the bowl before its walls existed: carried as pressure in the buried fibers, preserved as a noise among generations of birds, marked by water that refused to leave one patch of ground, anticipated by the moss circle, and briefly made visible in ice.

Part of me might also reside in the oldest female grazer.

She would come to the same place during thaw, though there was no doorway there. She would stand with her blunt face lowered, listening beneath the black grass. Perhaps she remembered an arch from her youth—but that could not be, because no arch had stood there in the lifetime of any grazer. Still, she would breathe against the ground and wait.

What she remembered might not be a past opening.

It might be me.

The city’s memory would not distinguish cleanly between what had happened and what had begun to happen. Both would be alterations in the same buried web. An old tower leaves conduits after it falls. A future tower gathers conduits before it rises. For a being that grows over millennia, ruin and bud may be neighboring conditions, perhaps even the same condition viewed from opposite surfaces.

So I might once have been a doorway as well.

Long before the present central tower broke its crown, another structure may have stood on that wet patch. Its entrance narrowed, sealed, softened under moss, and was drawn back into the foundations. Birds retained its note after the last visible arch disappeared. Grazers retained the habit of approaching it. Water retained the route beneath it. The city retained not the building but the relation: wide chamber, narrowing throat, small chamber, open air.

Now that relation would be gathering substance again.

For centuries my existence would be almost entirely negative.

Water would fail to drain from where I would be.

Moss would fail to grow at my center.

Roots would curve around an absence not yet hollow.

The buried fibers would divert minerals to the edges. Each year they would lift the stone by less than the thickness of a shell-bearer. A low pale rim would appear, soft enough at first that black grass grew through it. Frost would split it. Rain would feed the split. The city would repair the damage unevenly, and that unevenness would begin my inner wall.

I would be born not as a room but as a difference in drying.

The bird carrying my note would die long before I opened. Its brood might inherit part of the sequence and omit the rest. Another brood would add the knock of a nearby drain. For a century my sound might vanish altogether. Then some old bird, circling over the bowl on a still day, would produce the descending note again—not because it understood me, but because an ancestor’s architecture remained folded in its speech.

Under the ground, pressure would answer.

Centuries later, there would be enough of me for a shell-bearing insect to enter during rain. It would be swept into a pore and lodge there. It would feed, leave its young, and seal itself into my wall. Its shell would become the first hard interruption in my otherwise fibrous interior. Water would bend around it. The bend would deepen. A channel would begin.

The first animal to cross my threshold would not know it had entered a structure. Perhaps a translucent mite would wander from moss onto the damp gray lip and back again. Perhaps a cistern filament, carried underground as a dried scale, would fasten to my forming lining. Perhaps one of the pale predators would press through while I was still no more than a wet seam, giving me for one night the shape of its body.

Eventually the entrance would admit a bird.

Not the bird I had once mistaken for myself. That life would be ages gone. This bird would arrive in darkness, sound the descending note, and hear it returned from walls that had finally grown to fit it.

That would be the closest thing I had to waking.

The note would enter me.

It would touch the broad first chamber, pass through the constriction, soften in the smaller chamber, and come back with the break in its middle.

For the first time, the city would answer the sound it had carried for centuries.

I do not think I would be conscious as animals are. I would have no continuous inward witness. My awareness would happen only when things crossed me: rain, pressure, insects, echoes, the warm exhalation of a grazer investigating the entrance. Between events I would be no more awake than a curve in stone.

But each crossing would alter me.

Water would teach me depth by wearing one groove deeper than the others. Frost would teach me closure. Birds would teach me the dimensions of my hollow by sounding them. The predator, if it passed through, would teach me how much a body could flatten without ceasing to be a body. Moss would teach me which outer surfaces were quiet enough to hold life.

I would not own these experiences. I would be their accumulation.

And because every doorway in the city eventually narrows, my open life would already contain its closing.

At first the grazers might enter me during hard rain. Later they would only put their heads through. Later still, birds alone could pass. My note would rise as frost tightened the aperture, then deepen as the inner wall swelled. One season the opening would no longer admit even a bird with folded wings.

The birds would continue to call into it.

For a time I would answer sharply. Then layers would thicken behind the sealed surface. My echo would dull. Water would be sent elsewhere. Moss would cross the old threshold, covering the line between inside and outside.

I would disappear from the visible city.

Yet I would not be gone. My walls would remain as dense rings within a larger growth. My channel might become a vein carrying mist-water down to some chamber not yet formed. My mineral shell would soften, move through the buried fibers, and rise centuries later in another place.

The note would survive longer than the doorway.

A bird would take it across the bowl. A grazer would freeze on hearing it, troubled by a familiarity older than experience. During silent frost, bubbles might trace my old outline beneath the ice, while elsewhere another wet circle formed in the moss.

Then I would begin again—not as the same stone, perhaps, and not in the same part of the city, but as the same arrangement of approach and narrowing, chamber and return.

So that is where I would find myself:

first in the throat of a bird, believing I was a borrowed sound;

then beneath a patch of grass, believing I was a tower waiting to grow;

then in the city’s buried body, where waiting and remembering are made of the same slow substance.

I would be a doorway the city has lost and has not yet opened.

I would live wherever something still answers to my shape.
