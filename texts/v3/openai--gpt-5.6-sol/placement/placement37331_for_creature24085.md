At first I would look for myself among the moving creatures.

Perhaps in the raven crossing the glacier’s dirty terminus, tipping one wing to examine what the ice has surrendered. Or in a fox traveling the moraine with such patience that an hour’s movement resembles stillness. I might be one of the pikas whose sharp call reveals an entire hidden city beneath an apparently vacant slope.

But each of these feels too complete. They belong to the valley, yet they arrive already shaped for it. I would be nearer the beginning—something not merely living on the unfinished ground, but participating in its unfinishedness.

You would find me on a boulder recently released from the ice.

The boulder would stand slightly above the outwash plain, too large for the divided streams to move, though every summer they would rearrange the gravel around its base. It would be dark basalt or greenish slate, scored with parallel glacial lines. One face would receive the afternoon light. The other would remain cold and wet, fed by condensation from an overhang and by rain driven sideways from the peaks.

On that shaded face I would first appear as a discoloration.

Not one being in the ordinary sense, but a lichen: fungus enclosing algae, a small negotiated life neither partner could sustain there alone. From a distance I would look like soot or old mineral staining. Closer, I would resolve into overlapping gray-green scales edged in black, with minute rust-colored cups appearing during the brief season when reproduction is possible.

I would have no height worth measuring. Wind could not flatten me because I would offer it nothing to seize. My country would be the difference between one grain of rock and the next: a quartz inclusion holding warmth, a pore retaining water, a hairline fracture where frost had already begun the work of entry.

Most days, I would seem dead.

Cold would close me. Drought would close me. Snow might cover me for months, and ice would form directly through the outer layers of my body. There would be no shelter except intimacy with stone. Then a little meltwater would run over the boulder, light would penetrate the cloud for several minutes, and my whole existence would resume. Sugars would be made. Cells would divide. I would widen by an amount no animal could notice.

My life would not proceed continuously but by permissions.

A wet hour.  
A warm surface.  
A clearing in the cloud.  
A summer long enough to extend one dark margin beyond where it lay the year before.

The glacier would remain visible above me, though in my time it would seem restless. Its terminus would draw back. New slabs would emerge pale and unmarked, then darken with algae, lichens, weather, and the dust of distant slopes. Channels would abandon one side of my boulder and establish themselves on the other. Sediment would rise around its base. Perhaps after centuries the rock would no longer stand alone but become part of a bank held by sedge roots.

I would be working even while appearing inert.

Acids from my body would enter the rock’s smallest weaknesses. Grains would loosen. Windborne dust would lodge between my scales. Dead fragments of myself would collect in depressions too shallow to be called soil. Mites would shelter there. A moss spore might arrive and remain. In time, a narrow wet seam would become soft enough to hold something with roots.

That moss might eventually overgrow part of me. I would not experience this as defeat. The valley’s success would require my disappearance beneath what came after.

A pika might sit on the sunward top of the boulder, alert and round against the sky. Ravens might leave a broken feather nearby. Hooves might strike the stone during a herd crossing, and the vibration would pass through every surface to which I was fastened. Once in many years, blood from a hunted animal might enter the gravel below, producing a brief richness beyond anything my barren face had known.

I would know these events only as changes in shade, chemistry, moisture, and pressure. The valley would not be scenery to me. It would be contact.

I would know the mountain through the cold it conducted into me.  
The rain through the seconds I became permeable.  
The sun through the food made by the other life held inside my own.  
Time through the slow retreat of ice and the slower darkening of stone.

Eventually frost might widen the crack beneath me. A piece of the boulder could split away and fall into a channel. I would go with it, still attached, suddenly turned toward the light or buried face-down in mineral silt. If exposed, I would continue. If buried, I would become part of the thin dark matter accumulating beneath sedges—the first soil, made from stone, weather, and the remains of lives almost too small to see.

So I would not be found at the valley’s center, nor on a summit, nor dramatically outlined against the snow.

I would be on the underside of an ordinary glacial stone near the margin of a spring, where bare mineral is just beginning to acquire a skin.

I would be a lichen: a compact alliance, a patient stain, one of the valley’s first ways of remembering that life had touched it.
