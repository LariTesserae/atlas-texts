At first I have a body.

You would find it in the western blocks, where “uphill” is enough of an address. I sleep behind a yellow door in a room whose floor stands one curb-height above the corridor. My bed is welded to the wall. My papers live higher than I do, in sealed tins on a shelf near the ceiling. Beneath them runs an old flood line, greenish at the edges, dated in three different handwritings.

I am employed as a survey assistant, though this is not what the Ward calls me.

The official surveyors measure displacement in piers, widening in cracks, subsidence under machinery. They work from fixed points driven into concrete. I measure things that do not yet have fixed points.

Where condensation has begun collecting beneath a new cable tray.  
Which drill holes smell of iron.  
How long water remains after the streets are washed.  
Where moths gather when two lamps appear equally bright.  
Whether rats still use a pipe after its insulation has warmed.  
How high the frogs climb before the sluice opens.

My title on the duty board is **Environmental Routing Clerk, Grade Two**. In speech I am the rill-reader.

I make my rounds when the pumps are between their loudest cycles. There is no true quiet, but there are intervals in which one sound can be separated from another. I carry chalk, wax pencils, numbered metal tags, a thermometer, glass sampling tubes, a folding rule, and scraps of absorbent paper cut into long white tongues. These I lay across apparently level floors. The direction in which the paper darkens tells me more than the plans do.

The plans show where the water has been instructed to go.

My work is to learn where it intends to go instead.

In the newest excavations, I kneel close to the raw walls. The clay there still bears the squared grammar of machinery: tooth marks, drill constellations, the clean circles of core samples. Yet already the wall has begun speaking in another syntax. A dark pinprick appears under a seam. A bead swells. It holds longer than seems possible, gathering the reflected orange of a work lamp, and then falls.

I mark the spot.

The next day there are two beads.

Within a week someone has set a pan beneath them. Within a month the pan contains drowned moths, mosquito larvae, a mineral fringe, and one pale gecko waiting at its rim. Then the leak has become an address. People say, “Leave the crate past Thirty-Seven Drip,” or, “The wrench was found downhill of Thirty-Seven.”

This is how geography begins here.

I am not old, but I arrived before the western sleeping blocks were sleeping blocks. I remember bunks in the brace yard and meals eaten from covered bowls beside stacked pipe. That is enough to make children consider me ancient. They bring me observations because I pay in pepper seeds, colored cable ties, and the privilege of writing on my maps.

Their information is usually better than the adults’.

A child notices that bottle caps no longer circle behind the pump house after washdown. Another reports that gecko eggs have appeared in a cabinet that was cool the week before. One has seen cockroaches climbing a wall in daylight. One says the canal frogs are “sitting too high.”

I never ask whether they are certain. Certainty is a surface luxury. I ask where, when, how many, and what happened next.

My principal map hangs beneath glass in the central board. It began as a drainage diagram, copied from engineering plans, all straight lines and right angles. Over the years it has acquired other marks. Blue threads show recurrent water. White dots show mineral deposits. Copper circles indicate warm spots not explained by active machinery. Black arrows are rat routes. Violet shading records the densest moth clouds. Small green crescents mark frogs found above the expected flood level.

Nothing on the map is decorative.

Nothing on it remains correct for long.

People leave corrections in the frame: grease-stained notes, broken labels, scraps torn from ration cartons. **RILL NOW CROSSES UNDER TILE 8C.** **NO BATS WEST STAIR AFTER FAN REPAIR.** **HOT SMELL, THIRD CONDUIT.** **MOSS GONE—ASK SANITATION WHY.**

Sometimes the note is only a dead insect folded into paper.

I understand that too.

During the bat stream, I stand beneath the awning of the medical room and watch the shape of their passage. I do not count individuals. I count gaps. The colony should divide around the pump house, narrow beside the crane rail, then widen above the central street. If the stream bends where it did not bend yesterday, something has entered the air: heat, vapor, electrical discharge, a pressure change too slight for the wall instruments.

The bats write briefly on the chamber by refusing parts of it.

The moths write in accumulation.

The rats write by departure.

Water writes slowly, but it has the best memory.

Once, near the end of a long rain above, the canal fell silent.

It happened so gradually that the pump operators did not notice. Their own machinery covered the absence. I was in the north passage, replacing a tag where translucent moss had swallowed the number, when I saw three frogs clinging to a vertical pipe above my shoulder.

Not near the floor. Not beneath the bridge. At shoulder height.

All facing west.

Then a rat crossed the passage at ground level. Another followed. Neither stopped when it saw me.

The lamps were still burning. No alarm had sounded. Yet the air had acquired the sealed, waiting quality of a held breath.

I went uphill.

I did not run at first. Running causes other people to look at you instead of at what you have seen. I walked to the nearest call point, reported canal silence and animal rise, then rang the manual flood bell when the dispatcher asked me to repeat myself.

After that, everyone ran.

The blockage was beyond the grate, in the barred tunnel: surface debris driven down through older drains, packed against a bend no one in the Ward could reach. Water had backed into an unfinished cavity east of the ventilation vault. When the obstruction shifted, the stored water came through all at once.

It crossed the pump floor brown with clay and pale with moth wings. It overturned carts, drowned electrical cabinets, filled two residences to the first shelf, and stopped one finger-width below the medical threshold.

For months afterward, people said I had heard the flood coming.

I had not.

The frogs heard it. The rats felt it. The canal ceased speaking. I merely occupied the place where these facts met.

That becomes my true occupation.

Not surveyor. Not clerk. Not even rill-reader.

I am the place where separate warnings are allowed to become one warning.

As the Ward expands, the work outgrows my body. There are too many leaks, too many warm conduits, too many new walls beginning to soften into terrain. So I teach others—not a formal class, but habits.

I teach children to draw arrows beneath their observations so direction is never omitted. I teach kitchen workers to report where moth scales collect on mesh. I ask mechanics to mark unexplained hot metal with copper paint. I persuade sanitation crews to leave one small square of moss untouched at every persistent leak, because its growth records changes the gauges miss. I give canal workers transparent rulers. I show the medical staff how mosquito species differ in posture when they land.

The old electrician teaches me in return. Transformers, he says, do not hum only in the ears. Some faults enter the jaw. He presses my fingertips to a wall and has me bite gently on the handle of a screwdriver. Through my teeth I feel a vibration like a distant engine crossing overhead.

That sensation receives a symbol on the map.

Eventually the central board is not large enough. The record migrates.

It appears as chalk marks beside gutters, colored ties on railings, notches under bridge plates. Families copy flood routes onto the backs of doors. Children turn evacuation gradients into songs. Pump operators add animal behavior to shift reports despite objections from engineers above. A white crescent means unexplained dryness. Three violet lines mean insects are avoiding a light. A green mark placed higher than one’s hand means frogs have climbed and no one yet knows why.

I continue to make rounds, but by then most of what I record has already been noticed by someone else.

My body grows fungal scars between the toes. One knee stiffens. My hearing loses the upper edge of the bats’ voices, then more of it. I learn to watch children’s faces when the colony stirs. I can no longer feel the faintest transformer faults in my teeth, but younger electricians can.

The map beneath glass yellows. Its original drainage lines become almost illegible under generations of amendment.

People still call it my map.

They are wrong.

Later, perhaps, I no longer have a body.

Perhaps I am taken uphill with the other dead, through passages I spent my life mapping but never owned. The yellow door is given to another household. My tools are divided. A child steals my best wax pencil and uses it until it is no longer than a fingernail.

Yet I remain easy to find.

I am in the rule that says not only *if the canal falls silent, go uphill*, but also: *if frogs climb before silence, wake the western operator first.*

I am in the copper ring around a warm patch of concrete.

I am in the habit of leaving maps unfinished.

I am in the pause before a worker dismisses an animal’s movement as meaningless. I am in every report that begins, “This may be nothing, but it was not like this yesterday.”

Over time, no one remembers which marks were mine. This is proper. A warning that belongs to one person dies with one person. A warning that enters custom becomes part of the Ward.

So what am I?

Not its mind. The Low Ward has no single mind.

Not its voice. It has pumps, bats, hammers, sluices, frogs, and freight traffic for voices.

I am closer to an organ it has grown: a thin, distributed organ made of attention. Human in some places, animal in others, mineral at its slowest edges. My nerves are chalk lines, children’s routes, rat absences, wet paper, maintenance notes, and the remembered height of old floods. My function is to join events that occur too far apart to recognize one another.

You would find me wherever the Ward notices itself.

At first, that is a person kneeling in warm water with a notebook held above her head.

Later, it is the notebook.

Later still, it is the hand that adds a correction.

And after the handwriting has faded, it is the correction becoming a rule.
