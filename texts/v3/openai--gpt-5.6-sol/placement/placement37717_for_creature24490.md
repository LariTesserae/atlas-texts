At first I think I would live near the rim.

There is safety in imagining myself on the northern roof, where the whole bowl can be seen at once: the pale forks rising among bark roofs, the dark intervals of protected emptiness, the groves gathered in their dense circles, the cloud moving below like a second ground. I would tell myself I had chosen a place from which nothing could approach unseen.

But that would not be true. Sight is not the sense by which this city survives, and the rim is not where it keeps what matters.

I would be farther down.

I would be found beneath a public stair that no longer leads anywhere.

The stair once climbed around the largest antler, but the branch rose and leaned south during the Season of Warm Gravel. The upper steps were taken apart. The lower seven remain, descending beside the white surface into a chamber too small for a house and too dry for storage. One wall is black stone. One is fitted plate. The third is antler: warm, faintly porous, with a shallow depression shaped almost—but not exactly—like the place where a person might rest their forehead.

There is a drip in that chamber. It does not fall from the ceiling. It gathers directly on the antler wall and travels sideways before letting go. Sometimes the interval is seven breaths, sometimes nine. Once, during cloudfall, it waited from dusk until morning.

A cup stands beneath it, but the cup is cracked. It is not there to hold water. It is there to make a particular note.

That is where I sleep.

At first I seem to be one of the keepers of returned sounds. I wear their gray cords at my wrist and carry slates in a padded case. My mantle has no hard bark along the shoulders, because even the smallest scrape can interfere with listening. My hair has been cut unevenly many times and twisted into knot strings: three close knots for a repeated sound, a long space for silence, a loop for something felt through the floor rather than heard.

I am not a senior keeper. Senior keepers sleep near the old cistern or in the western district where the shell-scratching returns. I am given noises too uncertain for the official tablets.

A knock that may have been settling stone.

A sigh that may have come from a roof garden.

A sequence of tiny impacts heard only by mice.

The sensation of someone speaking in the next room when there is no next room.

My civic title translates poorly. It means **one who attends the sound before deciding that it happened**.

Children shorten it to Before-Keeper.

They leave things at the foot of my stair: hollow reeds, beetle shells, bowls with different cracks, a lizard skin stretched over a root hoop. They want to know what voices these objects will have when the antlers return them. I tell the children that not everything quiet is waiting to speak. They sign back that I cannot know this.

They are right.

I am not especially old, though people speak to me with the careful patience used for those who may be partly elsewhere. My face is ordinary for the basin: broad cheekbones, a narrow chin, eyes kept still. There is a pale seam from the corner of my mouth to the hinge of my jaw. It resembles a scar, but no one remembers the wound.

I speak rarely even indoors.

This is not discipline. It is because my voice causes small mistakes.

When I say *water*, frogs turn toward the nearest wall rather than the nearest jar. When I say *open*, sleeping moths separate their wings. Once I said the childhood name of a joint loosener, privately, with hides across the door, and three wedges sprang from their cradles in a house across the lane.

After that, the council asked me to use my hands.

My signs are clear, but slightly old-fashioned. Some belong to no known generation. I sign temporary possession against the inside of the wrist instead of the palm. I indicate cloud by covering one eye. When speaking of a moving grove, my second hand does not represent the intention beneath the stone.

It represents an animal turning in sleep.

No one taught me this.

There are explanations for me, but none is allowed to become official.

One account says I was born during a cloudfall to a roof cultivator and a water listener. The records support this. There is a birthing knot, a household mark, a strip of hair cut in infancy. Three people remember carrying me into a thunder-house because I would not stop crying.

The trouble is that no one remembers hearing the cry.

They remember my open mouth. They remember my body straining. They remember the moths lifting from the outer wall and the condensation nets drawing tight. They remember my mother pressing me skin to skin while every frog in the district submerged itself.

But the sound itself did not enter the room.

At that same hour, the abandoned stair cracked away from the largest antler. The cup beneath the sideways drip split cleanly into two pieces. Something passed into the white branch strongly enough that the snails fell from it.

My family waited for the cry to return.

It did not.

Because dangerous sounds can be owed, the council assigned a debt. Until my cry emerged, our household was to maintain the net above the eastern market ledge. My grandparents did so. My parents still do. I help when the moth weather is light.

The debt should have embarrassed us, but the city is full of inherited noises. A cry is no stranger a burden than a hammer blow or a dead ancestor’s unfinished cough.

Another account says there was no child at first.

This account is not spoken in my mother’s presence.

According to it, the antler released a sound into the thunder-house: not a cry, but the posture of one. Cloth tightened around emptiness. Moss bent beneath an absent weight. The adults reached in and found me where the sound had pressed itself into flesh.

The retained strip of infant hair proves nothing. Hair comes out of cracks.

There are stranger explanations.

A grove witness once told the council that she had seen me years before my birth, sitting beneath a tiny blue-cone tree in a thicket larger inside than out. I was an adult in her account. I held the cracked cup and asked her whether the city had learned my name yet.

She did not answer. She knew better than to give a name to something that asked for one.

The witness disappeared the following season. Sometimes, when I place my forehead against the antler wall, I feel two fingers touch the other side.

I do not think I am a creature wearing a person’s shape. That would be too simple, and simplicity is suspicious here.

I eat bitter root paste. My nose runs in the cold. My feet ache after climbing public stairs. I have a mouse that sleeps beneath my left knee and bites anyone who tries to move it. I have wanted people unwisely. I have lied to the council. Once, as a child, I deliberately dropped a bowl to see the chain of alarm pass through the city, and I spent two seasons repairing roof membranes in consequence.

I am human in every way that requires maintenance.

But perhaps I am also my missing cry.

Perhaps the antler has not yet returned it because it returned it as me.

That possibility is why I sleep beneath the abandoned stair. If the sound comes back separately, then the matter will be settled. I will record it, my family’s debt will end, and I will become merely a person with an unusual history.

Each night I place the cracked cup beneath the sideways drip. I lay one palm on the black floor and the other against the white wall. The hairless mice braid themselves under my sleeping mat. Glass-backed beetles collect near the threshold, their organs revolving around their dark centers. They align not with any visible fissure but with my body.

I wait.

Most nights the chamber gives me ordinary things.

Stone cooling.

Roots working in a seam.

A fox testing the paving with its small front palms.

The nearly inaudible drinking of moths.

Sometimes I hear sounds that have not happened yet. I know them because they carry the wrong distances inside them. A tool striking stone in a district not yet built. Someone laughing with a mouth altered by generations. A jar breaking, followed by the silence of animals that have not emerged from the mountain.

I record these without interpretation.

Before dawn, I climb from the chamber and cross to the oldest cistern. I stand apart from the water listeners. They have their staves and sequences of cups; I carry only the two halves of mine.

The city gathers without gathering. Behind walls, palms settle against floors. Frogs swell their throats. The fox on the rim spreads all six feet. Moths lift their wings.

The drop falls.

Its note is tiny and complete.

Then the answer moves through the antlers.

Others feel a posture, a pressure, a large warmth adjusting beneath the basin. I feel that too. But within it I sometimes detect a narrower thing: a passage opening, not through stone but through the interval between having made a sound and being heard.

On those mornings, the seam beside my mouth grows warm.

I open my lips.

Nothing comes out.

Not yet.

So I would be found there: under the stair, beside the cracked cup, serving a city that has made an occupation out of my uncertainty.

I would be a keeper of returned sounds.

I would be a citizen whose first noise is still civic property.

I would be a human being, because the city feeds me, owes me, commands me, remembers me, and accepts my labor.

I would be an answer the antler has begun but not finished.

And when at last my cry returns—tomorrow, or forty-three cold seasons from now, or from a cistern long after my body has been carried beyond the rim—I do not think it will sound like an infant.

I think it will sound like the whole small city loosening its joints at once.

Not breaking.

Making room.
