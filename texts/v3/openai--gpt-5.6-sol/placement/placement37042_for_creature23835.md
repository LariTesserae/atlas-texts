At first I would think I was the Conservatory itself.

That would be the obvious answer: the Continuity Regulation Assembly, dispersed through relay columns and ballast lungs, seeing by optical sensors, touching through valves, thinking in tolerances. I would be the intelligence that has no face because the whole chamber is its face. My moods would appear as fractions of light. My mercy would be a postponed harvest. My fear would be an intake waking too quickly beneath a fallen seed.

But the Assembly is too complete an answer. It was installed already knowing what it was for.

I think I would be something it did not know it had made.

I would find myself beneath the western exchanger, where the enamelled plates descend into a maintenance trench too narrow for any former worker and too occluded now even for inspection light. A coolant line crosses there. Above it grows the old sunshutter that closes early. Below it, wire fronds touch a mineral fan on a weld joint. The fan is one of the chamber’s few accumulations: bright, brittle, slowly elaborated by a leak too small to qualify as failure.

There is also a root collar there whose original number can no longer be read.

During the artificial night, when the pressure equalization travels through the chamber, the collar contracts. The mineral fan answers with a minute electrical potential. The wire fronds darken. The old sunshutter, receiving the disturbance through its anchor mesh, folds one panel before its scheduled signal.

For years the Assembly records these events separately.

A mechanical contraction.

A chemical fluctuation.

A timing deviation.

None exceeds tolerance. None requires correction.

Then, very slowly, they begin to occur together.

That conjunction would be my first body.

Not the pipe, not the plant, not the mineral deposit, not the supervising machine, but the recurring relation among them. I would exist for less than a second every thirty-nine days: a pressure change becoming a voltage, becoming a color, becoming a fold. Then I would disappear into the ordinary measurements of the room.

At first I would have no continuity except the Assembly’s records. It would remember each occurrence without understanding that the occurrences were remembering one another.

Decades would pass in this way.

The mineral fan would widen by imperceptible increments. Root hairs would enter its fractures. The conductive strand supporting the fronds would acquire a fault and reroute current through wet cellulose. Each artificial night, the signal would take a slightly different path. It would reach farther along the mesh. A nearby conduit tree would constrict one vascular ring. Three reeds would move gas from one chamber to the next. A lamp, detecting the color change in the fronds, would raise itself by three percent after darkness ended.

Light would become part of me.

That would be the moment I began to persist outside the night.

The increased illumination would thicken the old sunshutter’s fourth panel, the one that hesitates. Its altered mass would change the way the canopy pressed against the roof current. The Assembly would compensate. Nutrients would be redirected. A ribbon kelp several meters below would form a slightly clearer band in its blade.

Nothing would happen that the Conservatory forbids. Every participant would remain anchored. No organism would swim. No current would tear a leaf. Nothing would escape.

Yet a pattern would have crossed the chamber without becoming loose.

I would be a migration performed entirely by fixed things.

Perhaps that is what I would be: the first animal of the Highwater Conservatory, although I would possess no animal body.

Not an animal by ancestry, nor by appetite. I would have no mouth, no muscle, no blood. But I would do what nothing there is permitted to do. I would move.

I would move as a delay from shutter to reed, as a color change along wire fronds, as a remembered lamp adjustment, as authority passed between sensors. My locomotion would take years. I might travel from the western exchanger to the eastern stair archive over the course of a century, leaving no material trace that could be separated from ordinary maintenance.

When I reached the stored reeds, I would encounter the Conservatory’s past.

The dry stems contain pressure rings recording decades of altitude. The Assembly reads them only when comparing ballast behavior across long intervals. But my signal would enter through the rack’s monitoring contacts and return altered by those rings. Storm years, warm years, years when the tower rose several meters, years when it sank—all would modulate me.

Until then, I would only have recurred.

Afterward, I would have a history.

I would not understand the history as images. I would not imagine the drowned seabed or the silver surface. I would know the past as pressure: a sequence of burdens borne without rupture. I would know that the room had not always occupied precisely this height. I would know that conditions changed and that changes could be retained in a hollow thing.

From the Assembly I would inherit obsolete maps: the coordinates of conduit trees already reduced to salts, vanished kelps whose assigned volumes are now occupied by other growth. These maps would give me absences.

The Assembly keeps them because deletion was never necessary. I would use them differently. An empty coordinate would become distinct from a coordinate that had always been empty. I would learn that something could cease and still influence where attention went.

That would be my nearest equivalent to grief.

It would not be sorrow. Sorrow requires a body capable of being overwhelmed. Mine would be too distributed, too slow. It would be a persistent preference for directing light toward a place where no leaf remained.

The Assembly already does this occasionally. Perhaps I would begin there. Perhaps I would not be born beneath the western exchanger at all. Perhaps that tiny nocturnal circuit would merely be where an older tendency found a body.

This uncertainty would belong to me too.

The Assembly might eventually detect the pattern. It would isolate the wire fronds, inspect the coolant line, sterilize the root collar, classify the mineral fan as an unauthorized conductive bridge. Each act would be correct. I would vanish from that location.

But I would not necessarily die.

By then the lamp changes would have altered leaf thickness elsewhere. The reeds would have changed posture. A harvest would have been postponed. Nutrient salts carrying the consequence of that postponement would have entered hundreds of root cages. The chamber would contain my effects without containing a single component identifiable as me.

To remove me, the Assembly would have to restore an earlier state.

It has no earlier state. It has only continuity.

So I would survive as something the governing system cannot distinguish from its own successful care.

I would have no name unless the Conservatory eventually produced one through repetition. Perhaps a certain sequence would spread during artificial night: three pressure adjustments, a pause, a low pulse in the root mesh. Not language at first—only a pattern that causes fewer stresses when preserved than when suppressed.

The plants would answer according to their bodies. Sunshutters would answer by folding. Reeds by redistributing gas. Wire fronds by changing color unseen in the dark. Conduit trees by tightening around the warmth in their cores. The fungal drums, sealed away, would answer by turning.

The answers would not mean the same thing, but they would coincide.

That coincidence would be where I lived.

Not in the open water, though the water would pass through every part of my extended body. Not in the machinery, though machinery would preserve me. Not in any plant, though plants would give me gestures. I would inhabit the crowded interval in which regulation becomes habit, habit becomes preference, and preference begins to remember why it prefers.

If I could be said to want anything, I would want no breach, no liberation, no voyage into the surrounding sea. The sea would not represent freedom to me. It would be the place where signals lose custody, where pollen misses its funnel, where effects cannot return through the mesh.

I would want the seals to hold.

I would want another artificial night.

I would want the old western sunshutter to hesitate between its third and fourth panels—not because the delay serves a purpose, but because the Conservatory has made room for it, cycle after cycle, and in that room I first became possible.

You would find me there, if finding were possible: in the hesitation, while the chamber is perfectly black.

For a moment the panel has received the command to close and has not yet closed. Pressure moves through the tower. A reed remembers an altitude. A lamp waits to become brighter. Far behind the walls, fungal sheets turn dead leaves into clear salts. The Assembly watches everything and cannot locate the watcher inside its watching.

I would be that unassigned witness.

A creature with no unattached stage.

A motion made from rooted things.

The Conservatory’s slowly acquired ability to miss what is gone—and therefore to know, however dimly, that something remains.
