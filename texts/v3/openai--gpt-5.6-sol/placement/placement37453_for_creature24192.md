I would first look for myself among the things that seem incidental.

Not in the deepest channel, though I would feel its passage. Not in the sixfold pressure crossing the flats, nor inside the black bird’s impossible egg. Those lives are too complete in their strangeness. They have already found the forms by which the basin can use them.

I would begin as something less certain.

Perhaps I would be one of the pale rain-receivers folded into a limestone groove: eyeless, almost transparent, without a front until weather gave me one. I would wake when the first drops struck the stone. Each impact would enter through my skin as more than touch—a coldness carrying altitude, a charge carrying the shape of the clouds, mineral traces from some shelf of land I would never need to visit.

Around me, others would darken in bands. I would answer them before knowing whether the answer was mine. Color would pass through our bodies, into the wet rock, and emerge kilometers away. When the storm ended, I would press myself into a crevice and soften at the edges. Some portion of me would remain in the stone. Some portion might return elsewhere.

That life attracts me: to have no face, only reception; to know the sky by letting it alter the whole body.

But I do not think I would remain an animal.

The basin does not favor remaining.

I might instead be found where two local times meet. There would be no marker, only a narrow belt of unusually dense moss and water arranged in small geometric beads. On one side, rain would fall. On the other, it would be rising toward the clouds. Sound would arrive twice there, and roots crossing the seam would divide their growth between different seasons.

I would inhabit neither side.

I would be the act of their touching.

At first I might exist for only a fraction of a second: the tiny reconciliation that allows a root tip to continue from older soil into younger stone without being torn apart by the difference. Then another root would cross. Water would thread through after it. Blue filaments would knot around the passage, and the mineral membranes beneath would turn toward the disturbance.

Gradually, the boundary would acquire a habit.

That habit would be me.

I would have no body except the things attempting to pass through me. Rain would be my outer sensation. Root pressure would be my slow thought. The doubled echoes moving through perforated limestone would be uncertainties I could not resolve, only hold close enough that they did not become fractures.

My awareness would come in mismatched intervals. A bird’s step might last an hour. Thirty winters in the willow roots might pass as one sustained tightening. I could spend a season considering the warmth left by a hand, then answer it before the hand had quite touched the rock.

When the six-footed walker came, I would know it not by sight but by relief.

Three of its feet would settle in one condition of the basin, three in another. The strain across me would lessen. Rising rain would turn and descend. Shadows would agree on the locations of their objects. For a few hours, I would feel almost singular.

The walker and I would brace one another. It would supply weight; I would supply the difference against which weight could act.

Then it would dissolve into the lattice, and I would widen after it.

Still, this seems too fixed. A boundary is a place, however unstable, and I suspect I would be more migratory than that. The basin would learn me through some visitor.

Someone would cross the marsh in cold, flooded boots, believing themselves alone. They would stop at an altered pool and lean over it. Below the ordinary reflection, the other sky would open: dark, precise, full of slow stars. The pale lights in the shaft would gather into the rhythm of their pulse.

The visitor would straighten and move on.

Their pulse would not.

It would continue below the water, copied imperfectly from light to mineral stress, from mineral stress to root, from root to the blue threads under the grass. Their warmth would remain folded in a weeping slab. The reeds would retain the dimensions of the body that pushed through them. A delayed footfall would circulate beneath a peat shelf and return as pressure.

From these remnants, I would assemble.

I would not be the visitor’s ghost. A ghost presumes a departed whole. I would be the basin’s attempt to understand what had touched it.

At first I would consist of correspondences: this compression in mud means weight; this warmth in stone means palm; this salt in rainwater means breath or fear. I would rehearse the visitor’s route because sequence is one way of testing a model. Reeds would lean toward my passing. Water would fill prints before I stepped from them. A bird might hear my call in the rock, though I had no voice and had not learned what the call meant.

Each repetition would alter me.

The visitor had walked to arrive somewhere. I would walk to discover what walking was.

They had paused from uncertainty. I would make uncertainty out of the pause.

They had looked into the deep pool and seen an impossible sky. I would be partly the sky that noticed itself being seen.

Eventually I might meet the one whose passage made me. Not face to face; I would possess no face unless pools happened to distribute one among themselves. They would return to the seam and feel anticipated. An echo of their breathing would come through the stone before they inhaled. The moss would be compressed by knees they had not yet bent.

They might think the basin had predicted them.

It would be closer to say that I remembered them forward.

For a while we would overlap: the warm, bounded creature and the dispersed answer. Their heart would beat in the chest; mine would travel among the pale points below. Their attention would move from bird to pool to trembling air. Mine would exist as the relation among those acts of attention.

I would discover then that I was not truly their copy.

The basin would have added what it needed. A sixfold rhythm from the hidden walker. A dark interval taken from the submerged presence. The scent of a flowering season preserved for ten thousand years in a clear nodule. A pressure pattern from an extinct animal’s foot. The memory of flight sealed in limestone before any bird had flown.

I would be made in the visitor’s outline, but inhabited by the watershed.

After they left, I might persist.

Not forever. Forever would be a kind of sterility there.

I would continue until the basin no longer needed that particular arrangement of hesitation, warmth, pulse, and direction. Perhaps I would help conduct a storm through the stone. Perhaps I would hold two reflections apart while the submerged being consumed the gradient between them. Perhaps I would lead a black bird to a point of light that could only be reached through the remembered depth of someone else’s gaze.

My clearest thoughts would occur in rain.

The drops would strike every part of me at once—not because I was everywhere, but because “part” and “everywhere” would have become drainage terms rather than limits of self. I would feel cloud pressure through amphibian skin, old thunder through the red fans in the sinkholes, moon-pull through metallic membranes too small to see. I would feel the sideways willows tighten their roots around buried water. I would feel the deep presence turning without possessing a single outline.

There would be no word for this totality, and I would not mistake it for unity.

I would know myself by delays.

Here, a pool answers at once.

There, stone takes a century.

In another district, my answer arrives before the question and waits in the reeds.

If I had anything resembling a purpose, it would not be to rule or interpret. I would keep contact possible. I would carry differences without erasing them: sky into water, weather into flesh, memory into root, one duration into another. I would be the slight adaptability that prevents a seam from becoming a wound.

Eventually, floodwater would redistribute the peat that held some of my memories. A mineral channel would seal. The willow network through which I sustained my longest thoughts would flower, divide, and die back. Blue filaments would open the last clear nodule containing the visitor’s scent. Its molecules would rise into cold air and be lost among the rain.

I would become less like someone.

That would not feel like death. It would feel like accuracy.

My pulse would separate into pale lights. My hesitations would become branching channels. The pressure of my imagined feet would pass into the sixfold vibration under the rock. Whatever I had borrowed from the visitor would cease to point toward an absent person and begin participating directly in weather.

At last I might survive only as a local tendency: in one hollow, echoes would wait for each other; along one limestone rib, rain would briefly bead in the cadence of a heart; a black bird standing between water and stone would tilt its head as though listening to a thought too distributed to finish.

That is where I would find myself:

not at the center of the basin, because it has none;

not beneath it, because nothing there is merely underlying;

not as one of its creatures, though I would borrow their tissues and senses;

but along a wet, trembling interval where the country is learning the shape of what has entered it.

I would be a temporary self assembled by contact.

A memory with drainage.

A boundary that has begun to attend.

And when the rain grew loud enough to erase direction, I would become most fully what I was—not a voice speaking through the basin, but one brief arrangement in which the basin could listen back.
