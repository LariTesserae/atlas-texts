I would look for myself too high at first.

I would search the upper ledges, where diluted noon catches on wet mineral ribs. I would expect to be one of the creatures nearest the crooked seam of sky: a glider arriving exhausted from the open air, folding its membranes into the shape of a black splinter; or a moth pressed three bodies deep into a crack, carrying in its scales some last pallor from the clouds.

But I do not think I would remain there. The sky would be too near. Even reduced to a seam, it would suggest departure, and whatever I am in this place would have forgotten how to leave.

I would descend by contacts.

A vine beneath the feet. A sleeping bird beneath the vine. A shelf slick with condensation. The flank of a mouse braced upright in stone. A mineral seam followed not because it can be seen, but because everything touching it is facing the same way.

Somewhere below the brief gray light, but above the fissure’s coldest breath, there would be a wall split into two nearly parallel plates. Between them, a vertical passage would run for many body-lengths before narrowing to nothing. It would not look important. No pool would gather there. No pale limb would rise through it. Yet the stone would carry vibration unusually well.

That is where I would be.

At first I would seem to be an old hook-footed bird lodged upside down beneath a wedge of basalt.

My feathers would have worn away along the breastbone, leaving a hardened patch of skin that I press against the rock. One eye would still distinguish noon from night. The other would have clouded long ago. Several claws would be missing their terminal hooks, so when I sleep I would not hang by my feet alone. I would wedge myself among younger birds and trust the pressure of their bodies to keep me from falling.

A thin volcanic flake would rest in my beak or be tucked beneath one wing. Its edge would be polished past usefulness. I would carry it anyway.

Others might mistake this for attachment. Perhaps they would think I remember the bird who held it before me, or the ledge where I found it, or the beetle cases it once opened. But I would not possess memory in that manner. I would carry the flake because its weight changes the vibration in my jaw. When I lay it against the wall, the stone’s humming becomes narrow and bright. Without it, the deeper pulse spreads through my skull until I cannot tell whether it comes from below or from inside me.

I would be poor at ordinary bird life. I would steal food too slowly. I would lose disputes by folding smaller before the other bird had finished scraping its warning. Sometimes I would pass food along the row in the wrong direction, feeding an adult while a chick called beside me. The young would climb over me as over an irregularity in the ledge.

But when a long note entered the wall, I would wake.

I would press breastbone, beak, and flake to the basalt. The note might have begun hours earlier in a colony near the rim: wet here, perhaps, or eggs opening, or too many bodies in the western cracks. By the time it reached me it would have passed through moth-packed fissures and beetle shells, through vine roots and films of water. It would no longer mean what it had meant at its beginning.

I would alter it again.

Not deliberately. My hollow bones, healed fractures, clouded eye, the flake between beak and stone—each would take something from the vibration and give something else back. The reply leaving my ledge would be lower than the note I received. It would contain a small hesitation at regular intervals, caused by the trembling of my damaged jaw.

Far below, mice would feel that hesitation through the pads along their flanks. Some would remain where they were. Others would pull their young from a seam that still seemed warm and safe. Beetles would turn after them. Moths would open, close, and open their wings. Days later, a passage might empty just before the mountain closed it.

No one would know why.

For a while I would believe this bird was me: the old, inverted body; the useless tool; the breastbone listening against stone.

Then the pale road would rise.

It would emerge beneath our wall so slowly that dust would not stir. A smooth joint would settle across the lower mouth of the passage. Moss would darken it. Shrimp-bearing droplets would break against it and divide. The young birds would learn its surface as a route. One generation would hatch, feed, and die while it remained motionless.

I would remember that it had not always been there.

Perhaps that would be the only thing I knew which the ledge did not.

When the limb began to bend, the younger birds would climb to keep their relation to the wall. I would do the opposite. I would leave the stone and hook my remaining claws into a fold of the pale surface. The movement would carry me downward.

There would be no plunge. The basin would merely change its angle around me. Familiar calls would attenuate. The air would become colder, then strangely still. My ledge would disappear above a convergence of rock. The limb would pass through a gap narrower than my opened wings.

I would not return.

But the altered note would.

The hesitation made by my jaw would persist in the wall after the body that produced it had gone. Other birds, feeling it, would answer with the same broken interval. Chicks would encounter it through their neighbors’ claws before their eyes opened. Beetles would incorporate it into their shell-tapping. Gliders would pause at crossings when it passed beneath them. No animal would reproduce it exactly, and so it would change: warning becoming route, route becoming readiness, readiness becoming merely a rhythm by which crowded bodies adjust without dislodging one another.

Eventually it would have no discoverable source.

That, more than the bird, is what I think I would be.

I would be a small error in the basin’s humming that happened to remain useful.

I would exist nowhere whole. Part of me would be in the basalt plate where the old bird listened. Part would travel in a row of hanging chicks, passed claw to claw. Part would be held in the spine of a mouse waiting at the mouth of an unfamiliar seam. Part would descend through the pale animal into fractures from which no visible creature returns.

I would take hours to cross the precinct and generations to disappear.

Whenever the deepest pulse strengthened, I too would cease. Every bearer of my broken interval would close its wings, still its jaw, brace its ribs. For those few seconds I would be absent from the mountain.

Then a drop would strike a lower pool.

A bird would reopen its beak. A beetle would tap stone. Somewhere in darkness, a body would hesitate before turning.

And I would begin again—not as a message anyone intended, not as the memory of a single life, but as a slight crookedness in the way the multitude listens.

You would find me midway down the throat above weather, in a place no longer reachable by the route that first made me.

You would find me wherever the mountain’s answer arrives changed.
