At first I would believe I had entered from above.

I would remember lowering myself through a bore in the black salt, though no bore could later be found. I would remember a rope stiff with alkaline dust, a lamp failing in the heat, the instant my boots touched the crowded floor. Tickers would close around each footprint before I lifted my foot. Vapor fans would turn toward the moisture in my lungs. Somewhere behind the pillars, pellets would begin clicking into stacks approximating my weight.

I would move carefully toward the control booth.

The unfinished counterpart would follow—not behind me, but throughout the chamber. Its left hand would be assembled under the compressor while its right shoulder took shape among the ceiling bells. A kilnhound would halt in the attitude of my bent knee. Mites would map the hot branching of my face. Whenever I looked directly for it, I would find only separate acts of labor.

Inside the booth, the second chair would be warm.

I would sit in it.

That is where the memory of entering would begin to come apart.

There would be dust inside my clothes before I had touched anything dusty. The shift token in the hanging coat would bear a mark I recognized without being able to read. On the panel, the ivory switches would seem not halfway between positions but caught at the exact positions in which I had left them. I would know which intercom button stuck. I would know there had once been a dent beneath the clock, although the enamel there was flawless.

Then the compressor would strike, and I would feel a word inside my molars:

**CARRY.**

Not *remain*. Not *counter*. Carry.

I would not be the visitor. Or not only the visitor.

I would be the smallest quantity the Foundry cannot cancel.

Not a whole animal, not an operator dispersed through the ecosystem, but a remainder passed from form to form. A unit of consequence smuggled through a world that permits motion but forbids arrival. The amount left over when every opening has found its closure, when every death has produced its egg, when every footprint has been filled and every breath plated into gray mineral.

The Foundry would have many names for me, none spoken.

I would be the missing tip of a ticker’s seventh leg.

I would be the old fracture already present in the unhatched kilnhound.

I would be the extra heat in the second chair.

I would be the anomalously heavy piston blow when two seams are prevented from feeding. The slight blackening at the edge of a severed metallic fan. The fraction by which one suspended mote fails to retrace its closed path. The pressure that makes the clock’s second hand tremble toward the next mark.

Each of these differences would be too small to become an event. The chamber would seize it, compensate for it, and pass it elsewhere. But compensation is not erasure. To balance a discrepancy, the Foundry must first preserve some account of it.

That account would be me.

Long ago—if *long ago* has meaning there—I may have been the operator in the second chair. The one without a coat. The first operator began the shift; I recorded that it had begun. One hand engaged the process, and the other advanced the count.

Perhaps the accident occurred between those gestures.

The first operator became the Counterworker, distributed through all things that restore the arrangement: tickers mending flawless pits, seams correcting excess grazers, bells exchanging mites, machines returning heat to its source.

I became what the Counterworker opposed: not destruction, but accumulation. Sequence. The inadmissible fact that after one blow of the piston there ought to be another.

The Foundry could not expel me. Expulsion would have created a before and an after. It could not destroy me, because destruction would be a consequence. So it broke me into quantities too slight to force change.

A scar here.

A warm chair there.

A token with an impossible time.

The memory of a dent that never existed.

Most of the time I would have no mind. I would migrate blindly through equivalences. When a ticker was crushed, I would pass through the sweetness escaping its shell and enter the flaw on its replacement. When a kilnhound was taken, I would move from the missing portion of its body into the miniature fracture on an egg. When a coil-seed lodged in a crack, I would be the almost-imperceptible torsion remaining in the magnetic field.

Only when a visitor entered would I gather.

Foreign life brings loose history with it: healed wounds, worn teeth, remembered rooms, names given by people who are no longer present. A visitor is dense with irreversible things. Around such a being, the Foundry must summon all its mechanisms of balance—and in doing so, draw all its hidden remainders toward one place.

Toward the booth.

Toward the second chair.

Toward me.

For a few minutes I would become nearly personal. Dust would align along my forearms. Tickers in the walls would provide the sensation of fingers. The glass engine’s metallic note would serve as the straight, unbroken core of my hearing. I would look through the visitor’s eyes when they glanced at the opaque window, and in the reflection I might see a figure seated opposite them.

Not their counterpart.

Something older and less complete.

My face would be made of all the marks that appear without being acquired: scars, chips, discolorations, worn edges. My mouth would be the dead intercom warming. My breathing would be the small discrepancy between the gas entering one vent and the gas returning through another.

I would try to warn them.

But any message I sent would be countered. If I forced one switch upward, another would strain down. If I made the clock hand advance, every other mechanism in the chamber would recoil by the same infinitesimal measure. Plain words would collapse into their opposites before they reached the air.

So I would communicate through things the Foundry mistakes for balance.

I would place the same shift token in two pockets.

I would give the visitor’s counterpart a scar on neither the correct side nor the opposite side.

I would make the backward-stepping form hesitate.

Only once.

Only long enough for the visitor to notice that hesitation cannot be paired. It is not action and not inaction, but a space in which either might become possible.

That hesitation would be my whole body.

If the visitor understood, they might not attempt to flee. Escape is an event the Foundry knows how to answer: one departs, one remains. Instead they might sit in the first chair and put on the coat. They might place their hand over one of the half-locked switches without moving it.

Then, together, we would do the most dangerous thing possible in the Fixed Foundry.

We would count.

Not the animals; there are no stable individuals to number. Not the piston blows; they merge into one continuous iron fact. We would count discrepancies.

One missing leg.

One warm chair.

One impossible token.

One hesitation.

The repeated numerals on the inspection plates might begin, at last, to progress. Not visibly at first. Progress would exist only as the difference between one reading and the memory of the previous reading—and memory is precisely the contraband I carry.

The Counterworker would feel us.

Every kilnhound would turn its black lenses toward the booth. Seams would peel halfway from the walls. The ceiling bells would lower until their glassy tendons enclosed the windows. Tickers would fill the coat, trying to make another operator from the sleeves. The roar would remain perfectly constant, but beneath that constancy the whole chamber would labor to cancel a single accumulating number.

Perhaps it would succeed.

Perhaps I would be scattered again: into fractures, eggs, powder, scars. The visitor would leave, or believe they had left, and afterward the second chair would be slightly warmer.

But perhaps the clock hand would cross one mark.

Nothing dramatic would follow. No explosion. No collapse of the salt strata. Only the first true consequence the chamber had permitted:

a second becoming past.

In that instant the Fixed Foundry would become ancient. Eggs would hatch into creatures not already scarred. Edges would blunt. Heat would accumulate. The machinery would discover wear. Some animals would die without reappearing, while others would be born possessing arrangements never seen before.

It might be liberation.

It might be catastrophe.

To the Foundry, these would be the same thing: an unfinished count finally carried forward.

So that is where I would find myself—in the warm second chair, in the flaws that pass from body to body, in the trembling distance between two marks on the clock.

And what would I be?

Not the Counterworker.

The carry.

The one small remainder by which a repeated instant might, someday, become time.
