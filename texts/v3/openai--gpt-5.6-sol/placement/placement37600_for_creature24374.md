At first I would assume I was a route reader.

It would be the coil that suggested it: a broad wooden spool carried between my shoulders, wound with dark red fiber and fitted with mineral beads at measured intervals. I would have the reader’s weighted coat, the sternum clock, the habit of stopping where nothing appeared to be wrong. I would know how to watch a shadow without looking at the object that cast it. I would tap walls with the second joint of my thumb and feel the answer in my back teeth.

But route readers go ahead of others.

I would more often be found behind.

My work would begin after a district had changed its mind—after rain, leaf-turn, a root convulsion, or one of those afternoons when the old mountain stirred beneath the streets. Surveyors would establish what remained. Pruners would remove what could not be reconciled. Then I would enter with a list of things still missing.

Not only people. A pressure gauge last seen in a fermentation hall. Three sealed jars of medicine. A household’s winter water account. A child’s left boot. A room containing an elderly woman who had refused evacuation because, from her side of the door, no emergency had occurred.

I would be called a **retrieval clerk**, though the older name would mean something nearer to *one who walks after the place has passed*.

My district would lie where the dry river avenue entered the oldest industrial terraces. It would not be a prestigious address. The buildings there would be broad and low, their upper stories repeatedly sacrificed to wind. Pipes would cross overhead in stiff bundles. Condensation would strike the roofs all night with the irregular ticking of cooling machinery.

My dwelling would be grown around a locality stone too small to anchor an intersection but sufficient for one room. Everything important would be kept within arm’s reach of it: bed, kettle, mechanical clock, cabinets of route records, three black cups though I lived alone. The rest of the house would be permitted to wander.

There would be mornings when the kitchen stood directly behind my sleeping niche and mornings when reaching it required crossing a roof garden I had never planted. A narrow spare room would appear in wet weather. It would contain a table, two chairs, and the smell of someone else’s coat drying. I would leave water there, but never wait to see who drank it.

My municipal plant—the one to which I had been grafted as an infant—would no longer exist.

Officially it had been a signal tree above the river sluices. It split during a drought before I was old enough to remember it, and its tissues were distributed according to custom: conductive wood to the tram lines, vascular membranes to the western cisterns, mineral-rich roots to the anchor vats, signal buds grafted onto replacement towers across the city.

This would make my citizenship inconvenient.

No single district could fully recognize me. Instead, small pieces of the city would recognize me everywhere.

A service door would open when it should not. A tram passing without a driver would slow beside me. Reservoir plants would unfold if I placed my scarred hand near their throats. Signal flowers sometimes addressed to other quarters would turn and display my household color.

The clerks would call this residual authorization. Theologians would call it dispersal of identity. Factory workers would say the city had lost my paperwork and was too embarrassed to admit it.

I would prefer the factory explanation.

My body would be unremarkable for one of the Kept: tall, dry-skinned, double-lidded against the wind. The branching marks at my wrists would be darker than most. In summer the tissues along my ribs would wake painfully, making sleep impossible at noon. I would eat in sunlight when I could, not because light sustained me, but because meals tasted incomplete without it.

One of my knees would contain a living splint from an accident in a compression mill. It should have been pruned away years ago. Instead it would have grafted too deeply into the bone. In damp weather it produced a cluster of translucent buds beneath the skin, and sometimes, before a route altered, the joint tightened and pulled me a fraction toward the correct direction.

I would tell people this was why I was good at retrieval.

That would not be the whole truth.

The truth would be that I remembered the dry river when it had water.

Not as history. Not as one of the old windows opening onto another century. I would remember standing waist-deep in a continuous current beneath a green sky, though the river had not run that way for thousands of years and the plateau then held no human form like mine. I would remember pressure, cold silt, and the slow transmission of flood through roots. I would remember having no eyes.

These memories would come only when I was lost.

In an ordinary street I could be as mistaken as anyone. But inside a displaced factory, where corridors had folded around an obsolete production chamber, I would begin to sense gradients that were no longer physically present. Water had once passed there. Sap had once risen there. A mountain slope had once directed both.

I could follow the absent descent.

That was how I found what the city had misplaced.

I would enter tied to the locality stone by my red line, though often the line would fail to behave as a line. It might pass through a doorway behind me and emerge from a ceiling ahead. Mineral beads spaced one meter apart might arrive touching one another or separated by a room’s width. I would mark each contradiction on my cuff.

Sometimes the missing things would be easy: tools embedded in healing walls, freight shifted into a former root cavity, workers trapped behind a stair that had remembered branching.

Sometimes I would find places no civic plan recorded.

There would be a sealed canteen where cups remained warm and names written on the duty board belonged to people dead for centuries. A nursery whose children had all gone home except for their shadows. A turbine hall in which the machines turned without wear because the hour of their construction repeated continuously.

I would retrieve nothing from such places. I would leave a mineral weight, record the entrance, and return by the exact route I had used.

Once—only once—I would hear my own voice calling from inside an uncut wall.

It would say my childhood name.

I would obey the rule and not answer. But afterward I would search the municipal birth records and discover that the name had never officially been mine. It belonged to the signal tree.

After that, the question of what I was would become less stable.

Perhaps I would still be a person: a retrieval clerk with bad sleep, three cups, a wandering kitchen, and overdue pruning in one knee.

Perhaps I would be what remained of an infrastructural tree after it had been cut apart and made useful—a mobile reunion of signals distributed through pipes, trams, cisterns, and anchor stones. The city might recognize me everywhere because everywhere contained some small portion of the organism that had first recognized me.

Or perhaps the graft had worked in both directions. The infant had entered the tree as much as the tree entered the infant. What walked the streets afterward was not one being altered by another, but the scar between them, prolonged into a life.

I would not seek a final ruling. On the plateau, certainty is often evidence that one record has consumed the rest.

When the ground began to rise, I would go to the river locality stone and fasten my line with the others. I would recite the required facts:

My current name.

The district that kept my water.

My occupation.

My dwelling’s official number.

I would give my ancestry as far back as it was reliable. Then, while the ancient contour lines darkened beneath the boulevard, I would add something not required:

The river descended east.

The western bank was shallow.

There were roots beneath the silt.

Water passed here.

Perhaps the others would think I was helping the city distinguish the erased river from the erased mountain. Perhaps I would be.

When the movement stopped, I would remain after the crowd dispersed, one palm against the stone and the other resting on the dry living pavement. Far below, pumps would resume. Valves would open. The city’s many separated tissues would begin exchanging news of the shape they now occupied.

Somewhere under my hand, I would feel an answer traveling toward me—not in words, and not quickly.

Then I would shoulder the red coil and walk after it.
