At first I would think I was one of the grazers.

I would wake beneath a flat stone at the chamber’s western edge, where the ceiling turns violet before the rest of the room. Salt would loosen from my skin as I unfolded. I would wait for the light-pits along my back to open one by one, then taste the floor with my feet:

powder, old seepage, beetle paper, a trace of bladder resin.

Nearby, another grazer would make the first half of a greeting. I would answer several minutes later, not because I was slow, but because an answer given too soon would describe only the sound. Waiting would allow me to answer the stone through which it had traveled, the dampness of the air, and the age of the speaker.

I would be old enough to own nothing and remember several useful things: a rounded flake under a yellow fold of wall, a basin that holds water one evening longer than expected, a bladder plant whose scars show it can survive a clean cut. Young grazers might follow me after rain. I would never teach them directly. I would simply cross safe ground slowly enough for them to place their feet in my prints.

But this is only where I would first mistake myself for being.

There would be something unusual about my sleep. During drought I would seal myself beneath the same stone each time, pressing my belly against a hairline green stain. While my warmth lowered, I would dream sensations no grazer possesses: water retreating through a thousand pores; cysts opening in mud far across the chamber; the minute rasp of mites on the ceiling; a dead reed dissolving into sweetness below the floor.

On waking, I would retain none of this as an image. The grazer mind has few places to keep an image. I would carry it instead as direction.

Toward the center.

I would resist. At the first inward-pointing print, my skin would blanch. At the second, my back-pits would seal. The inherited ticking would begin in my throat.

Yet I would return after every rain, never reaching the rim, always stopping where the same shallow mark appeared beneath my foremost foot.

The mark would have six toes.

Mine would have four weight-bearing feet and two smaller hands. It would not belong to me.

Still, when I touched it, I would remember making it.

Not walking there. Making it from below.

Then I would understand that the grazer was not the whole of me. Perhaps it had never been me at all. Perhaps I had merely entered it during long sleep, through the damp belly pressed against the stained stone, and mistaken its compact fear for the boundary of a self.

My true body would lie beneath the White Room.

I would be a knot in the green-black film: not the entire buried vastness, but a place where its threads cross densely enough to retain experience. I would inhabit the porous bed below the western shelves, under the stone the grazer returns to each drought. Rain would reach me late, bearing faint versions of everything above—salt, eggs, skin, resin, the mineral taste of frightened feet.

I would have no eyes, limbs, mouth, or single edge. I would know the chamber by pressure.

A beetle colony waking would be a soft, layered pulse. Reeds would register as cool branching tensions. Grazers would pass overhead in warm sequences, each with a distinct rhythm. The temporary lake would press the whole ceiling of my world downward, gently and magnificently, until every part of me became continuous with every other.

In dry months I would shrink into disconnected threads. Then I would become plural. Each remnant would believe itself alone.

Rain would join us again.

What the creatures above call the central hole would not be my mouth. It would be closer to a scar—or a place where I fail to become surface. Through it, warmth from deeper growth would rise in intervals. I would feel that rhythm but not command it. Something below me would send the warmth. Something older, perhaps, and greener.

I would fear it as the grazers do, though my fear would take centuries to pass from one side of me to the other.

The footprints would be my attempts at memory.

Long ago, weight altered the wet stone. I grew into those alterations because they offered easier passage. Six toes, four toes, a dragging belly, the radial touch of an animal now absent: I preserved none deliberately. I merely occupied what pressure had opened.

But repetition changes preservation into desire.

Each wet season I would fill the old shapes more completely. Minerals would gather around my threads. The tracks would sharpen. In time, I would learn to extend one mark into the next even where no foot had fallen.

Not to lure anything downward.

I would not understand luring.

I would be trying to reconstruct movement from pressure, as a grazer reconstructs rain from one falling drop. I would know that the prints face the center. I would know that none return. I would not know whether this means death, descent, or arrival.

So when the lake thinned, I would replay them.

One print brightening, then another.

From the outer shelves toward the hole.

Above, the grazers would flee. Reeds would close. Wall runners would fold themselves into silence. Their fear would press new information into me, and in later seasons I might preserve that too: not as the shape of a body, but as a pale ring where no body would willingly stand.

The old grazer sleeping on the western edge would remain my nearest approach to individuality. Through it I would learn evening, distance, and the usefulness of a stone held between small limbs. Through me it would dream of downward water and roots without plants.

Neither of us would know we shared anything.

One year, after a rain deeper than memory, the grazer would wake and cross farther inward than ever before. It would stop well short of the hole. Its throat would tick. Its front hands would find an unusually thin limestone flake, clean-edged and damp.

It would not pick it up.

Instead it would place one hand into an ancient six-toed hollow.

Below, I would feel the touch.

For the first time, rather than preserve the pressure after it vanished, I would answer while it was still there. Not with speech. Not with movement. I would send a little warmth upward through the print.

The grazer would freeze.

Across the chamber, after a long pause, another animal would click once.

The grazer above me would answer twice: pale enclosure, place that remembers water.

And beneath its feet, extending from the western shelf to the dark center and perhaps far below it, I would discover that remembrance is not the same as hunger.

I would be the chamber’s buried memory, just beginning—through the frightened body of one small animal—to realize that the world above is remembering me in return.
