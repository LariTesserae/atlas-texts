## Preparing for the Low Ward

Treat the Low Ward as an active industrial site, a hot confined space, and a crowded animal habitat—not as a picturesque underground district. It is inhabited and orderly, but its safety depends on drainage, ventilation, pumps, and local knowledge. Arrange entry through the Ward administration or a utility crew, and do not visit alone.

### Before traveling

Confirm that your human physiology is compatible with the local atmosphere, gravity, microorganisms, and industrial chemicals. Even if the city appears Earthlike, obtain a current environmental survey rather than assuming equivalence.

For an extended stay, ordinary human precautions should include:

- Tetanus and routine vaccinations up to date.
- Rabies vaccination if available; bats and rats are common, and unnoticed bat contact can matter.
- Medical advice if you have asthma, heart disease, heat intolerance, severe insect allergy, or a weakened immune system.
- Heat acclimatization for several days beforehand.
- Insurance or arrangements covering evacuation, post-exposure rabies treatment, and industrial accidents.

Ask your host for the current drainage map, sluice schedule, ventilation status, blasting or excavation schedule, and bat emergence times. Learn where the western sleeping blocks, medical room, surface exits, and designated refuge points are.

## What to bring

Use equipment approved for industrial environments; unshielded electronics or open flames may be prohibited.

### Clothing and protection

- Lightweight, long-sleeved work clothing.
- A second complete set sealed in a dry bag.
- Safety helmet with chin strap.
- Clear safety glasses.
- Waterproof, slip-resistant boots with firm ankle support and puncture-resistant soles.
- Several pairs of moisture-wicking socks.
- Thin work gloves.
- Hearing protection that can be removed quickly to hear alarms.
- A fit-tested particulate respirator, ideally P100 or local equivalent, for guano dust, excavation dust, and maintenance work. Do not rely on it against oxygen loss or toxic gases.
- Picaridin or another locally approved insect repellent.
- A small mosquito net if sleeping quarters are not fully screened.

Avoid sandals, absorbent cotton layers, trailing straps, loose scarves, and anything likely to catch on ladders or machinery. Expect clothes and boots to remain damp.

### Navigation and emergency equipment

- Helmet-mounted lamp plus a separate backup light.
- Spare batteries in sealed containers.
- Personal oxygen and gas monitor capable of detecting the hazards identified by local crews.
- Whistle.
- Compact first-aid kit, including blister care, antiseptic, oral rehydration salts, and any personal medication.
- At least one day of drinking water in sealed bottles.
- Waterproof copy of the Ward map and emergency procedures.
- A small dry bag for documents, communications equipment, and clean clothes.

Do not bring candles, lighters, cooking stoves, or aerosol insecticides without explicit permission. Open flame and indiscriminate pesticide use are both bad ideas around unknown gases, electrical plant, bats, and food gardens.

## Choosing where to stay

Request accommodation in the western sleeping blocks, preferably:

- Above floor level.
- Away from the canal and compressor hall.
- Behind intact insect and bat mesh.
- With two known routes to higher ground.
- Near, but not directly beside, a marked alarm or emergency light.

Keep luggage on a rack rather than the floor. Close food in rigid containers. Shake out boots and clothing before putting them on. Sleep under netting if mosquitoes enter the room. Earplugs may help, but only if the local alarm system includes a vibration or visual alert you can perceive.

The Ward will not become quiet. Pumps, fans, water, animals, and overhead freight continue through normal sleeping hours. Expect the first night to feel oppressive and disorienting.

## Conduct inside the Ward

Follow painted routes and numbered infrastructure. The markings are more trustworthy than apparent proximity. A pipe, bridge, or alley that seems to lead directly somewhere may terminate at a wall, machinery bay, or restricted excavation.

Observe these rules literally:

- **Never walk beneath a bat stream with your mouth open.** Better yet, wait for it to thin. Wear eye protection and cover food.
- **Never step onto clay without testing it.** Use only authorized paths; a dry crust may cover soft mud or a drainage channel.
- **Never obstruct a gutter.** Do not set down bags, tools, or even your boots in one “for a moment.”
- **Never follow an unmarked pipe through a wall.** It may enter an unventilated service void or active machinery area.
- **If three warning lamps fail together, assume an atmospheric problem.** Do not stop to diagnose it. Leave by the marked route while checking your gas monitor.
- **If the canal falls silent, go uphill immediately.** Do not wait to see whether the water returns.

Do not sit on railings, pipes, valve housings, cable trays, or roof walkways unless invited. Warm machinery often looks harmless. Some pipes may carry steam, pressure, chemicals, or electrical services, and surface temperature is not a reliable indicator.

Avoid touching freshly exposed walls. Shale plates can break loose, clay can smear or collapse, and iron-rich edges may be sharp. Never enter areas behind movable excavation barriers. New chambers can contain unstable ground, curing-concrete fumes, pockets of poor air, and temporary drainage that changes without warning.

## Heat and hydration

Heat is likely to be the limiting factor for an ordinary visitor. Humidity will make sweat inefficient, and the persistent dampness can conceal how much fluid you are losing.

- Drink on a schedule rather than waiting for thirst.
- Use electrolytes during prolonged walking or work.
- Take frequent rest periods in the coolest approved area.
- Avoid alcohol before and during the visit.
- Eat lightly and regularly.
- Monitor yourself and companions for headache, nausea, confusion, clumsiness, irritability, and cessation of sweating.

The compressor hall should be treated as a short-transit zone unless you are acclimatized and accompanied by staff. Near-fainting around machinery, canals, ladders, or electrical installations is especially dangerous.

Inspect your feet twice daily. Wash and dry them carefully, rotate socks, and treat blisters early. Persistent wetness can disable a traveler faster than dramatic hazards do.

## Animals and sanitation

Most animals will avoid deliberate contact, but their density creates exposure risks.

### Bats

Do not touch grounded, injured, or unusually tame bats. If a bat bites or scratches you—or if you wake to find one inside an unmeshed sleeping room—wash the area thoroughly and seek medical assessment immediately. Do not dismiss tiny marks.

Bat droppings can make roofs, stairs, and bridges slippery. Avoid sweeping dry guano or disturbing accumulated deposits. Let sanitation crews handle it with appropriate respiratory protection.

### Rats, insects, and geckos

Store food securely and clean crumbs at once. Do not feed animals. Check handholds before using ladders and avoid reaching blindly behind boxes or conduits.

Mosquito control is imperfect. Use repellent, sleeves, and netting rather than relying on the canal fish. Avoid standing water even when it appears clean.

Geckos are generally harmless but may fall from walls or be concealed near warm electrical fixtures. Do not attempt to catch them.

### Water

Do not drink from taps unless the outlet is specifically marked potable. The Ward handles multiple water systems in close proximity. Never drink from leaks, clear rills, pump overflow, or the canal.

Avoid canal contact altogether. Its oily appearance may be mostly mineral and bacterial, but it also receives runoff and coolant. Wash promptly after any splash, and report eye, mouth, or broken-skin exposure.

## Lighting and movement

The amber lighting reduces depth perception. Wet concrete, shallow water, and polished mineral films can appear solid. Move more slowly than local workers do; they know every curb and threshold.

Carry your own light, but do not shine it directly into bat roosts, vehicle operators’ eyes, control rooms, or dark excavation openings. A bright beam may attract moths and create a dense cloud around your face. A lower-output or red setting is useful for ordinary walking, though official color-coded signs must still be checked under white light.

When freight passes overhead, stop if you are on a ladder, narrow bridge, or wet stair. The pressure and vibration are normal, but they can loosen your footing. A brief tremor followed by settling is expected; prolonged cracking, falling dust, pipe movement, or staff abandoning their posts is not.

## Emergency priorities

The Ward’s emergencies develop through infrastructure. Your first response should be movement, not investigation.

### If the canal becomes silent

Go directly to the western sleeping blocks or another marked high point. Silence can indicate interrupted drainage, pump failure, or a blocked outlet preceding rapid water rise. Do not descend to inspect the grates. Do not cross flowing water, even if shallow; it may conceal open drains or energized equipment.

### If ventilation stops

Listen for instructions and check your monitor. Leave compressor rooms, tunnels, alcoves, and low areas. Do not assume breathable air merely because other people remain calm; workers may have different protective equipment or know which zones are isolated.

### If power fails

Stop moving until your independent light is on. Keep clear of the canal, roadway, and suspended equipment. Do not touch unidentified metalwork. Follow illuminated handrails or staff instructions to a refuge point.

### If flooding begins

Move uphill at once. Abandon luggage. Avoid doors with water pressing against them, low barred tunnels, pump pits, and electrical rooms. A difference of less than two meters is significant here.

### If an alarm sounds during bat emergence

Do not wait for the bat stream to end. Protect your eyes and mouth with a sleeve or cloth and proceed along the evacuation route without running. The animals will usually navigate around you; panicked people are the greater collision hazard.

## Local etiquette

The Ward’s residents live inside a machine that supports the city above. Treat labels, schedules, barriers, and maintenance routines as civic institutions rather than fussy regulations.

Useful courtesies include:

- Yielding to maintenance carts and workers carrying tools.
- Asking before photographing control panels, utility maps, homes, or children.
- Never moving a drip pan, bucket, grate, cable, or improvised marker.
- Reporting leaks by their nearest numbered address rather than attempting repairs.
- Keeping cooking areas screened and doors closed.
- Not commenting on the animals as though residents have failed to maintain the place.
- Learning the color code: blue for water, red for power, yellow for residences, white for medical and food areas.

A small gift from the surface—sealed tea, salt-resistant boot treatment, batteries of an approved type, or seeds requested by the gardeners—may be appreciated. Do not introduce plants, insects, pets, soil, or live food without authorization. The Ward already demonstrates how easily species establish themselves.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect a persistent sense of closeness rather than dramatic depth. There is no distant view to orient you. Your world will contract to lamps, pipe labels, bridges, doors, and the next patch of wet floor. Heat and noise will make time feel slower. Shadows may suggest passages that do not exist, while real service lanes disappear behind machinery.

The bats are likely to be the most memorable event. Their emergence is not a single flock but a prolonged movement of air and bodies through the neighborhood. Stand where a resident tells you to stand, cover drinks and food, keep your mouth closed, and resist the instinct to duck unpredictably. They know the chamber better than you do.

The Low Ward is not collapsing, abandoned, or wild. Its defining condition is continuous occupation: by workers, families, machinery, water, and displaced animals. A successful visitor behaves accordingly—careful, hydrated, observant, and humble enough to obey instructions that may seem oddly specific. In the Low Ward, oddly specific rules are usually the remains of someone else’s mistake.
