This is not a city an ordinary human can properly enter. It is a city one can only approach, observe, and—at great risk—partially intrude upon. Prepare less as an explorer arriving at a destination than as an archaeologist inspecting a delicate, operating instrument.

## Before attempting entry

The visible crack is too narrow for a shoulder. Do not widen it. The surrounding shale is likely load-bearing, and even a small fracture could redirect seepage, collapse galleries, or destroy the equilibrium that has preserved the settlement.

Use one of the following:

- A millimeter-scale articulated probe with soft feet, fiber-optic vision, chemical sensors, and contact microphones.
- A remote presence body no larger than a hand.
- If your multiverse transit permits precise placement, a tightly bounded observation aperture rather than a full portal. Do not open it across a wall, pipe, cistern, or occupied volume.
- Only enter bodily if you have confirmed a larger route elsewhere. Assume any human-sized passage is unstable until mapped.

A portal introduces heat, air movement, pressure differences, light, spores, and foreign water vapor. Begin with an aperture no wider than a pin, measure the pressure and atmosphere on both sides, and enlarge it slowly. If the water’s pitch changes, stop immediately and close the aperture.

Establish your return anchor outside the formation. Do not depend on equipment placed within the city: constant damp, copper salts, clay particles, and resonant vibration will degrade mechanisms surprisingly quickly.

## Environmental preparation

Expect a cold, saturated, poorly ventilated confined space.

Carry:

- A low-profile waterproof caving suit with no loose straps or hard protrusions.
- Thin nitrile or equivalent barrier gloves beneath soft outer gloves.
- Knee, elbow, rib, and hip protection suitable for prolonged crawling.
- An independent breathing supply. Do not trust the chamber air even if an initial sample appears breathable.
- Carbon dioxide, oxygen, radon, methane, and hydrogen sulfide detectors.
- A full-face respirator as backup.
- Non-slip footwear or soft crawling pads.
- A body-temperature monitor. In cold, wet confinement, hypothermia can begin before it feels urgent.
- A rescue tether made of smooth, non-shedding material.
- A compact inflatable splint and extraction sling.

Do not drink the water. It may contain copper salts, concentrated minerals, ancient microorganisms, or substances harmless to the original inhabitants but not to you. Avoid exposing cuts. Decontaminate yourself and all equipment afterward, in both directions: the greater danger may be what you bring into the chamber.

Open flame is unacceptable. It will consume limited oxygen, deposit soot, heat the ceiling, and disturb the city’s humidity. Use cool, diffuse, low-intensity illumination. Red light may preserve your own night vision, but test whether different wavelengths affect the luminescent paste or the white sump creatures. Never illuminate them continuously.

## Protect your hearing—and do not trust it

The sound will be physically overwhelming even if the actual water flow is slight. Low ceilings, cavities, pipes, and diaphragms turn droplets into sustained thunder. Wear active hearing protection capable of suppressing both impact noise and low-frequency resonance.

Ordinary verbal communication will probably fail. Prepare:

- Tactile signals through the tether.
- Wrist or palm haptic communicators.
- A simple code: one pulse for stop, two for retreat, three for emergency.
- Visual text displays as a secondary system.
- Contact microphones placed on stable, already-wet stone—not on pipes or thin diaphragms.

Sound is also your most important diagnostic tool. Before moving anything, record several minutes from multiple points. Build a baseline spectrum of pitches and rhythms. If a new tone appears, an old one vanishes, or the apparent “breathing” changes, withdraw. You may have displaced water, blocked a channel, altered pressure, or loaded a stone surface beyond its tolerance.

Do not assume that a sound’s apparent direction is meaningful. The rock will carry and return noise through hidden routes. A roar directly ahead may originate behind you or several strata away.

## Moving within the settlement

If bodily entry is somehow possible, accept that you will spend nearly all your time prone, folded, or on hands and knees. Claustrophobia screening is essential. Practice in a wet training maze whose clearance is lower than your chest depth and where turning around is impossible.

Never rely on the city’s stairs, bridges, roofs, ledges, or galleries to support a human. Their scale is uncertain, and many may have been designed for inhabitants far lighter than you. A roof that served as a public plaza may still fail under the pressure of one knee.

Spread your weight using soft, perforated crawl panels placed only on mapped bedrock. Perforations matter: an impervious mat could block condensation or redirect drainage. Do not place equipment in a channel merely because it appears dry. In this city, a shallow groove may be a street, an overflow, a pressure regulator, or all three.

Move slowly enough to notice where your body changes the environment. Your warmth may increase local evaporation. Your breath may condense on protected inscriptions. A sleeve brushing a wall could erase mortar prints that have survived for centuries.

Avoid:

- Leaning against shale flakes.
- Pulling on lintels or doorframes.
- Using recesses as handholds.
- Crawling over clay platforms.
- Passing beneath cracked corbelled arches.
- Touching mineral “skin,” which may be structural rather than decorative.
- Entering any passage from which you cannot reverse without shifting masonry.

Mark your route with a digital map and an external tether. Do not use chalk, paint, string tied to structures, reflective stickers, or cairns. The city already contains countless small arranged objects; adding your own markers could be mistaken for part of its control system.

## Do not operate the civic tools

The cups, weights, stoppers, pebbles, mallets, and wedges around the central basin are controls, not curiosities. Their simplicity makes them more dangerous, not less.

Do not:

- Lift a cup to inspect its underside.
- Replace a pebble “more neatly.”
- Clear sediment from a groove.
- Insert a probe into a pipe.
- Empty a full vessel.
- Catch a drip for longer than the smallest possible sample.
- Straighten a tilted weight.
- Silence an irritating knock.

A change as small as the width of a fingernail may divert water into sleeping chambers, dry out clay seals, flood the lowest sumps, or alter pressure behind a wall. The present arrangement may also be the last functioning expression of the city’s law. Treat every positioned object as both infrastructure and civic record.

If sampling is essential, collect only from water already leaving the inhabited system—never upstream of a cup, grille, cistern, or porous wall. Record the sound before, during, and after. Replace nothing; even chemically identical water may have the wrong temperature, dissolved gas content, isotopic composition, or microbial load.

## Archaeological conduct

The most valuable remains may be the least conspicuous:

- Thumbprints in mortar.
- Wear around tiny doors.
- Shallow sleeping hollows.
- Soot in narrow chimneys.
- Tool marks beneath mineral deposits.
- Differences in polish along crawl routes.
- Protected pressure diagrams and channel plans.

Photograph using raking light and multispectral imaging rather than making casts. Do not apply powders, gels, or molding compounds. Avoid touching bone needles or shell objects; saturated artifacts can crumble when lifted or dry rapidly outside their niches.

Scale everything carefully. The varying room sizes do not necessarily imply varying body sizes. A tiny chamber may have been a cupboard, shrine, valve enclosure, sleeping cell, acoustic cavity, or part of a distributed household. Do not impose human architectural categories too early.

The lack of visible inhabitants is not proof of abandonment. The builders may be elsewhere in the pipework, beyond the gate, dormant, microscopic, acoustically present, or active on a timescale you cannot easily perceive. Behave as a guest even if you see no host.

## Time and reality checks

The perfect repetition of the water may be hydraulic equilibrium, but a multiverse traveler should test for stronger anomalies.

Carry:

- Two independent clocks using different physical principles.
- A sealed control droplet whose fall rate can be measured.
- A vibration logger.
- A temperature and humidity recorder with external comparison.
- A simple memory log that prompts you at fixed intervals.

If your clocks diverge, your recordings repeat exactly rather than approximately, or your team remembers different numbers of drops, retreat. Do not remain to “solve” the anomaly. In a confined space, even ordinary acoustic repetition can distort time perception and induce trance, panic, or false familiarity.

Set a strict visit duration. Constant thunder, crawling posture, dim reflections, and the absence of visible change can make a short stay feel endless—or erase hours without your noticing.

## The creatures in the sumps

Do not collect the white, transparent animals. Do not touch their tiles, grilles, or water. Their rarity may mean they are the last surviving members of a closed ecosystem. They may also move through the city’s infrastructure and perform some unnoticed function, such as clearing films or carrying minerals between basins.

Observe remotely with low heat and no flash. If one detaches after illumination or vibration, end the observation. Your presence may already have altered its behavior.

Quarantine all returning equipment. Treat both biological contamination and mineral residues as potentially active until analyzed.

## The sealed gate

Leave it sealed.

Do not press on the concentric grooves, trace them with tools, inject dye around the edges, drill, lever, heat, cool, or acoustically excite the shale. Do not assume the absence of a lock means the gate is not secured. The groove pattern may be a pressure interface, an acoustic control, a warning, or simply the worn remains of something no longer legible.

The deeper roar is not evidence of a safe chamber beyond. It may indicate:

- A larger flooded cavity.
- Water under pressure.
- A structural void carrying sound from elsewhere.
- Another district dependent on isolation.
- A sealed contamination zone.
- A geological fracture whose opening would drain or inundate the entire city.

Most importantly, nothing about the settlement suggests that opening things was its governing principle. Its builders survived by keeping flows within exact tolerances. Honor that logic.

## How to behave while there

Go quietly, despite the thunder. Move as though every surface is inscribed, every puddle allocated, and every object still in use.

Do not expect grandeur in the human sense. The city’s scale will initially feel cramped, even pitiful. Attend long enough, and that impression should reverse. Its monuments are controlled droplets. Its boulevards are polished lips in stone. Its public works are changes in pitch. Its civic center is a collection of tools anyone might once have used to maintain a shared balance.

The best visit leaves no altered flow, no vanished note, no new crack, no foreign dust, and no opened gate. If you depart uncertain whether you truly entered the city or only listened at the edge of its machine, that is probably the closest an ordinary human can come to visiting it well.
