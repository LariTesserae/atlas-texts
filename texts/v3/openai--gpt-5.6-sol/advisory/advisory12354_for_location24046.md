## Expedition judgment

An ordinary human should not visit this place casually. Treat it as a near-vacuum mountaineering expedition conducted inside a structure that can change topology, gravity, lighting, and acoustic causality without warning. No part of the city is intrinsically habitable, and “close to camp” may cease to be a meaningful safety condition.

Go only with:

- a trained team of at least four;
- a pressurized mobile habitat placed well outside the active architecture;
- redundant life support and rescue capability;
- experience with vacuum operations, technical climbing, and non-Euclidean navigation;
- a strict willingness to abandon objectives early.

Do not travel alone, even within sight of camp.

## Physical survival

### Atmosphere and pressure

Assume the ambient air is incapable of supporting human life and may be close enough to vacuum for exposed moisture to boil or sublime.

Use a full pressure suit with:

- closed-cycle oxygen and carbon-dioxide removal;
- independent emergency oxygen rather than a shared team reserve;
- active heating, especially for hands, feet, visor seals, and batteries;
- a backup pressure layer;
- automated leak isolation;
- at least twice the expected excursion duration in consumables.

Pressure tides are not ordinary winds. They may create abrupt differential forces through doors, shafts, and narrow streets. A harmless open court can become a jet path when the atmosphere contracts. Suit pressure controls must tolerate rapid exterior fluctuations.

Never remove a glove or open a helmet to examine frost, dust, or mineral stains. There is no accessible local water, and any exposed human moisture will contaminate the site while placing the traveler in immediate danger.

### Water and food

Carry all water. Budget generously: the cold and low pressure can conceal dehydration, while suit systems consume water for both the traveler and thermal control.

Keep drinking systems inside the pressure envelope. External containers, flexible hoses, and valves may freeze, embrittle, or lose contents directly to sublimation.

Food should be compact, low-crumb, and edible through the suit system. Do not plan to eat in the city itself. Establish a pressurized shelter for meals and rest.

### Thermal protection

Sunlit crystal may become locally warm while adjacent shadow remains lethally cold. Because afternoon can persist in one courtyard for days while nearby streets remain dark, do not infer temperature from the apparent hour.

Use contact and radiant-temperature sensors. Avoid leaning against stone: some surfaces may be cold enough to damage the suit before the wearer feels it, while dark glass or metallic dust may become unexpectedly hot under direct illumination.

## Base camp

Place the primary habitat on open, geologically simple ground beyond the outermost structures. It should not be:

- beneath a tower, arch, balcony, roof, or inclined slab;
- inside a courtyard that can channel pressure tides;
- on metallic dust or charged mineral stains;
- near the escarpment edge;
- in line with a shaft, chimney, fracture, or long avenue;
- within a district that changes apparent orientation after dark.

Anchor the habitat in several directions, but include quick-release mechanisms. Gravity may rotate locally, and a fixed anchor can become a lethal restraint if “down” changes.

The camp should have an independent beacon visible by several methods: radio, coded light, thermal emission, and a distinctive physical silhouette. Do not depend on any single apparent direction to locate it. Establish at least one secondary refuge containing pressure, oxygen, heat, water, medical supplies, and a beacon.

Keep departure equipment pointed toward open terrain, not toward the city. If the site begins reorienting, the correct response is evacuation, not investigation.

## Navigation

Ordinary maps will fail. Record routes as a sequence of local observations rather than a continuous geometry:

- material and crystal structure;
- gravity direction;
- pressure;
- temperature;
- magnetic and electric fields;
- spectral properties of light;
- views through several fixed-angle cameras;
- elapsed time between landmarks.

“In the second black arcade, turn toward the copper sun while local gravity points beneath the left wall” may remain useful after “go west” has become meaningless.

Assign each team member two navigation records: one digital and one written or mechanically engraved. Electronics may be disrupted by electrical storms in the rock.

Physical markers are unreliable. Dust moves, plazas exchange positions, and removal may expose new structures. If markers are necessary, use self-reporting beacons placed without drilling or cutting. Never assume a trail of markers still describes a connected route.

### Tethers

Tethers are valuable for preventing a fall into shafts, but dangerous where gravity changes direction or corridors reconnect. Use:

- short, individually releasable lines;
- multiple low-load anchors instead of one long rope;
- load sensors that warn when a tether begins pulling in an unexpected direction;
- cutting tools accessible with either hand.

Never tie the whole team to a single continuous rope. A corridor that changes “down” could drag everyone after one person.

### Distance

Measure every excursion by remaining life support and number of uncertain transitions, not kilometers. A terrace visibly fifty meters from camp may require hours to reach or may no longer connect to camp after darkness.

The basin is a strict exclusion zone. Do not throw instruments, ropes, or debris over the edge. A line lowered into it may continue paying out indefinitely, become motionless without support, or draw the operator across the brink if gravity revises itself.

## Team procedure

Travel in daylight bands whenever possible. A minimum field group should consist of:

1. a lead navigator;
2. a gravity and structure monitor;
3. a rear navigator responsible for verifying the return route;
4. a reserve traveler carrying additional life support and rescue equipment.

No one should enter a room until another person has confirmed:

- local gravity with a free inertial indicator;
- pressure gradient;
- floor integrity;
- electrical activity;
- a second exit or a secure retreat point.

Use drones cautiously. Flight control based on a stable vertical will fail in gravity corridors. Crawling or multi-surface robots with pressure-independent adhesion are preferable. A lost drone should be abandoned; do not risk people to recover equipment.

Maintain frequent scheduled check-ins. Because sound can arrive before its cause or from the wrong location, voice heard through the environment is not evidence of a teammate’s position. Trust authenticated suit radio and camera feeds—not clicks, impacts, breathing sounds, or apparent footsteps in the stone.

Radio itself may be bent or blocked by crystal. Use encrypted challenge-response signals so delayed transmissions are not mistaken for current instructions.

## Structural hazards

A tower may take months to fall, but its slowness does not make it safe. The visible stone, its load, and its shadow may disagree about orientation. Avoid every leaning structure and any area covered by its plausible fall path in any direction.

Never use shadows to judge:

- whether a tower is stable;
- which direction it leans;
- the time of day;
- whether an opening is occupied;
- whether an object is moving.

Confirm movement with rangefinders, inertial references, and repeated imaging.

The city is revealed by erosion, and removal acts like construction. Therefore do not:

- chip samples from walls;
- scrape away dust;
- widen cracks;
- break crystal ribs;
- clear blocked doorways;
- dissolve salt;
- apply heat to expose underlying planes.

Even a small act of excavation might create a room, alter a route, or propagate a structural change across the range. Scientific sampling should initially be limited to loose particles collected from open ground, and even that should be done sparingly.

Do not trust stairs. Before stepping onto each flight, establish whether climbing changes gravitational potential, local temperature, or topology. If a staircase returns to the same landing, turn back immediately. Repeated traversal may not produce the same result.

The ceilingless cellar beneath the western terraces should be considered a major anomaly and left unentered by humans. Seeing stars overhead is not proof that the opening leads upward or outside.

## Dust, electrical stains, and native life

The moving dust may be chemically abrasive, electrically active, and fine enough to damage suit joints. Fit sacrificial joint covers and electrostatic dust rejection. After each excursion, decontaminate suits externally before entering the habitat.

Do not touch the fungal-looking stains. They are active electrical patterns, not organisms, and contact could:

- discharge through the suit;
- interfere with electronics;
- alter the stain’s migration;
- trigger an unknown response elsewhere in the structure.

Map them remotely with electric-field sensors. If violet flashes begin moving beneath a wall, withdraw without crossing the apparent direction of propagation.

Glass striders should be treated as native animals, however slowly they move. Their resemblance to debris is no excuse to step on them. Before crossing a plaza, scan for jointed transparent forms with polarized light and thermal imaging. Route around them by a wide margin.

Do not illuminate a strider intensely to make it move. Heat appears to power its locomotion and may change behavior that would otherwise unfold over years.

Give hollow-winged animals and their nesting structures a very wide berth. Do not touch the shed cages. They may represent developmental stages, pressure-sensitive structures, or forms whose causal relationship to the animals runs in an unfamiliar direction. During a flock passage, stop moving, crouch or anchor lightly, and let the pressure tide pass.

No native material should be taken off-world without quarantine. This includes apparently inert salt, dust, glass, shed geometries, and fragments already lying loose. “Not organic” does not mean inactive.

## Darkness and the abyss lights

Be inside a stable refuge before full darkness. Darkness here is not merely lack of light; it appears to participate in rearrangement.

If pale openings illuminate along the basin wall:

1. do not approach an overlook;
2. stop entering new rooms;
3. record local gravity and the positions of all team members;
4. deploy short independent anchors;
5. maintain continuous authenticated communications;
6. retreat toward the nearest refuge only if the route remains physically verified;
7. otherwise shelter in place until light returns.

Do not stare at the lights through magnifying optics for long periods. Use automated cameras and indirect displays. There is no evidence that observation is dangerous, but there is also no reason to expose human perception to a phenomenon that reorients architecture.

When a new district becomes accessible by morning, quarantine it conceptually and physically. Do not enter on the first day. Observe it through at least one complete light-dark cycle and determine whether the former route still exists.

If arches appear to turn without motion or a known eastern route reaches a western overlook, announce a topology emergency. Do not “correct” the map by intuition. Return only by sequentially revalidating each local landmark.

## Medical and psychological preparation

The city will attack confidence more than attention. Human balance assumes stable gravity; human navigation assumes continuous space; human hearing assumes causes precede sounds. All three assumptions are unsafe here.

Travelers should be screened and trained for:

- vertigo and sensory conflict;
- confinement in pressure suits;
- prolonged silence;
- isolation despite visual proximity to teammates;
- resisting the urge to follow familiar-looking routes;
- accepting that a visible camp may not be reachable;
- abandoning a teammate’s location report when instruments contradict it.

Carry anti-nausea medication and use vestibular reference displays inside helmets. Each visor should show local gravity, suit orientation, verified teammate locations, and the age of every displayed reading. Old data can be worse than no data in a city that revises itself.

Establish a formal “perception disagreement” protocol. If a traveler sees a door, light, animal, or teammate that others and instruments do not detect, the team stops. No one moves toward the disputed perception.

Sleep only inside a pressurized, thermally stable refuge. Dreams, fatigue, and hypoxia must not be allowed to blur with the site’s genuine anomalies.

## Emergency rules

- **Suit leak:** seal immediately and return to the nearest refuge, not necessarily base camp.
- **Loss of communication:** stop at the last verified landmark. Do not wander in search of the team.
- **Gravity shift:** lower the body, release any dangerous fixed tether, attach to two locally tested points, and wait for readings to stabilize.
- **Pressure tide:** leave shafts and long avenues; shelter behind broad, solid geometry without entering an untested room.
- **Violet activity in stone:** withdraw from all contact surfaces and power down nonessential external electronics.
- **Structural motion:** move laterally out of every conceivable fall direction. Do not trust the shadow.
- **Route alteration:** shelter, inventory life support, and send drones or instrument packages. Human reconnaissance comes last.
- **Abyss attraction or compulsive curiosity:** treat it as a medical emergency and physically escort the affected traveler away.
- **Unexplained new opening near camp:** relocate camp. Do not explore it.

Set a hard turnaround threshold at no more than half of expendable life support under ideal conditions and much earlier after any anomaly. Reserve enough oxygen and heat to survive an entire unexpected dark interval.

## Conduct

Move slowly. Leave no inscriptions, cairns, drilled anchors, discarded equipment, or footprints made by deliberate dust clearing. This place has no makers, but that does not make it ownerless or empty in any meaningful sense.

The most dangerous temptation will be to interpret the city as unfinished architecture waiting for occupants. It is not a ruin, shelter, or puzzle built to human scale. Its apparent rooms and avenues are consequences of stone, stress, pressure, and rules that do not distinguish construction from erosion.

Visit as if walking through the interior of an active geological organism: self-revising, almost silent, and indifferent to whether a human route continues to exist.

Above all, never assume that because the city has made a place for you, it intended to let you leave.
