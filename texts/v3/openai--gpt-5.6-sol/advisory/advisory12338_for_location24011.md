## First principle: you are the hazardous material

To the High Dry Garden, an unprotected human is a warm, pressurized container of water, oxygen, salts, and microbes. Sweat, breath, tears, urine, blood, damp food, and condensation may all irritate or poison the foundation. A minor suit leak could provoke anything from local blistering to a city-scale immune response.

Do not visit as a casual backpacker. Travel only by prior arrangement with the inhabitants, ideally in a locally approved vehicle or sealed guest chamber. Your expedition should be planned more like a diplomatic visit to a living spacecraft than a wilderness trek.

## Before arrival

Secure three things:

1. **An invitation or recognized contact signal.**  
   The plateau is organism, infrastructure, and possibly polity at once. There may be no meaningful distinction between trespass on land and entry into someone’s body.

2. **A native guide or city-grown route token.**  
   Euclidean maps are unreliable where arches make routes directionally unequal. A destination five minutes away in one direction may be a month away on the return trip.

3. **A chemically sealed reception point.**  
   Do not open a portal directly over the foundation. Moist air from your home environment could condense or freeze onto it. Arrive through a double airlock with humidity traps, pressure equalization, dust filters, and a dry capture floor.

If a local reception facility is unavailable, postpone the trip.

## Environmental protection

### Atmosphere and pressure

The plateau stands in thin, cold upper air laden with conductive dust. Do not assume it contains breathable oxygen or safe partial pressures.

Wear a fully enclosed pressure suit with:

- Independent oxygen supply
- Closed-loop carbon dioxide removal
- Full humidity recovery
- Double-contained waste storage
- Thermal heating and insulation
- Pressure redundancy
- Radiation and ultraviolet protection
- Continuous atmospheric and electrical monitoring

The suit should remain sealed even inside apparently inhabited structures unless your hosts explicitly provide a verified human-compatible chamber.

### Water containment

Ordinary human life support is unusually dangerous here. Use at least two containment layers around every water-bearing system.

This includes:

- Drinking reservoirs
- Cooling loops
- Food packages
- Medical fluids
- Waste tanks
- Suit humidity condensers
- Emergency oxygen systems that produce water
- Fuel cells or batteries with aqueous electrolytes

Carry a **dry spill-capture kit** designed to immobilize water instantly inside sealed cartridges. Do not use sprays, foams, steam, water-based fire suppressants, or conventional disinfectants.

Eat, drink, administer medicine, and use sanitation systems only inside a designated sealed cell. Never lift your visor for a photograph.

### Suit materials

The conductive dust and strong atmospheric electrical gradients make exposed electronics and uncontrolled static discharge hazardous. Your outer layer should be:

- Antistatic and electrically regulated
- Non-sparking
- Smooth enough to shed metallic dust
- Resistant to sulfur compounds, ozone, salts, and organic solvents
- Free of loose fibers or biological residue
- Pale or thermally controlled without being brightly reflective

Avoid exposed copper, reactive alloys, and strong magnets unless local engineers approve them. Use optical data links where possible, but keep them dim and tightly collimated; broad illumination wastes a resource the plateau treats seriously.

## Moving across the foundation

The “ground” is alive. Treat every step as contact with a sensitive organ.

Use broad, soft, low-pressure footpads. Walk gently and at an even rhythm. Do not:

- Drive stakes
- Dig
- Scrape seams
- Collect samples
- Mark routes with paint
- Drop crumbs or waste
- Pour anything out
- Use tracked vehicles
- Anchor tents
- Land hot-thrust aircraft
- Stamp to “test” the surface

Your footsteps will enter the foundation as vibrations and may return amplified. A conventional rigid rover could be deafening to both you and the city. Native road transport is strongly preferable: the roads can raise traveling ridges beneath you and will know which surfaces are prepared to carry your weight.

If you must use imported mobility equipment, it should be light, vibration-isolated, electrically quiet, and supported on wide compliant pads. It must be able to stop instantly when the road stops.

Do not assume an apparently open field is public or vacant. Smooth fields are active light-collection surfaces and may be among the plateau’s most productive organs.

## Navigation

Visual distance judgment will be poor. The dim copper light, uncertain shadows, conductive haze, and halos around charged objects can make a nearby tower seem impossibly remote and a distant ridge seem close.

Do not navigate by sight alone. Use:

- A native route guide
- Inertial tracking
- Pressure and vibration maps
- A record of every arch crossed
- Separate outbound and return routes
- Redundant clocks
- Tethers within the traveling party, if locally permitted

Treat every black arch as a one-way topological junction until proven otherwise. Never cross one experimentally. Send neither a person nor a drone through without knowing where and when the city considers its exit to be.

Ordinary radio navigation may also be unreliable in conductive dust and among electrically active towers. Do not increase transmitter power impulsively; that may interfere with local biology or computation.

## Sound and vibration

The plateau is loud through the body rather than merely through the ears. Earplugs alone will not protect you from ground-borne infrasound.

Bring:

- Active vibration-canceling boot and seat systems
- Chest and skull vibration monitors
- Hearing protection across audible and ultrasonic ranges
- Medical monitoring for arrhythmia, nausea, vertigo, panic, and bone-conducted acoustic injury
- A suspended rest couch rather than a rigid floor bed

Some tower notes may be felt as pressure in the sternum. If you develop chest pain, visual disturbances, confusion, or an irresistible sense of rhythm, withdraw to an isolated chamber. Do not assume a sound must be consciously audible to be physiologically dangerous.

Avoid ultrasonic range finders, seismic survey charges, hammering, loud music, and active sonar. Construction polyps and city organs already communicate in these bands.

## When the city computes

You will recognize a landscape computation by the flattening of metallic hairs, rotation of tower crowns, tightening of bridges, interruption of transit, and a rising chord that passes from deep vibration into metallic singing.

When this begins:

1. Stop moving.
2. Step off any active transit ridge if your guide directs you; otherwise remain where the road places you.
3. Crouch or sit on your vibration-isolation pad.
4. Lower radio and electrical emissions.
5. Do not cross an arch, enter a new chamber, or launch a drone.
6. Do not mistake the event for an alarm and attempt evacuation without guidance.
7. Wait for native transit to resume.

The computation may alter the route network while you wait. Your morning itinerary may no longer exist afterward. A new chamber or road is not necessarily an invitation to enter it.

## Weather and surface events

### Comet frost

Comet frost is a major hazard to both visitor and plateau. The surface will isolate and split incoming water, producing reactive hydrogen and oxygen and making the whole landscape smoke.

If frost is predicted, shelter inside an active tower well before it arrives. Do not attempt to observe it from the open. Expect:

- Reactive gas pockets
- Rapid electrical changes
- Reduced visibility
- Surface immune activity
- Explosive ignition risks
- Closure or rerouting of roads

Carry no exposed flame or hot element.

### Pressure fronts

When hairs flatten and animals crouch, imitate the animals. Pressure fronts may trigger city computation, electrical discharge, or violent movement of the great vanes. The dusk folding of vanes produces explosive cracks; remain well away from crown fall-lines and do not fly near them.

### Deep grazers

A plaza rising beneath you is likely not an earthquake in the conventional sense. A subterranean grazer may be passing through the root galleries.

Do not brace yourself against structures with rigid equipment. Towers and bridges are designed to flex; your braces are not. Follow the road’s redirection, keep your knees loose, and let the city carry you away. Never approach the crest to look for the animal.

### The plateau rim

Avoid the edge unless escorted. Root curtains descend through cloud and strike the cliff with enormous force. Their motion may be difficult to judge in the haze, and the cliff itself may be concealed beneath living folds. There are no familiar drainage lines or bare-rock margins to warn you that the drop is near.

## Animals

Most animals are unlikely to hunt a human, but your heat, water, electrical equipment, and unfamiliar vibrations may attract them.

- Give grazing files right of way. Their feeding flashes are normal.
- Do not touch their quills or stand between members of a file.
- Keep drones away from foil-winged flocks; the flock may treat a drone as turbulence, prey, or defective kin.
- Avoid temporary night chimneys. They vent hot, corrosive chemicals, and predators wait nearby.
- If the ground bulges around a burrower, withdraw without stamping.
- Never feed local animals. Human food may be toxic, and even an empty damp wrapper is contamination.

Assume some “animals” are mobile organs or municipal machinery. Apparent wildlife does not imply lack of ownership or intelligence.

## Inside the towers

Tower walls are living mineral tissue. They are resilient, responsive, and able to redistribute force. Do not knock on them, carve them, attach suction devices, or test them with chemical swabs.

Windows open because someone is present. Doors may respond to intention. Approach slowly and honestly; trying to trick an intention-sensitive entrance is both rude and potentially futile.

Tools that crawl from wall niches are likely temporary extensions of the building. Use only what is offered. Do not pocket a tool, fruit-like device, loose “bone,” or folded shadow. It may be alive, aware, needed elsewhere, or scheduled to return for digestion.

A room sealing behind you may not indicate imprisonment. Empty rooms sleep. Nevertheless, maintain a local emergency contact and know how your hosts signal that a chamber is about to lapse.

Keep lights dim. Do not illuminate entire halls for comfort. Use shielded task lights with wavelengths approved by your hosts. On this plateau, brightness is stored energy and public infrastructure.

## Health and psychology

The environment will be disorienting even when physically safe. Prepare for:

- Chronic dusk and disrupted circadian rhythm
- False distance perception
- Infrasound anxiety
- Vibrations felt as an external “heartbeat”
- Roads moving beneath you
- Architecture opening in anticipation of your actions
- A persistent sense that the landscape is watching

Bring an internal circadian light system inside your helmet or sleeping chamber, but ensure no bright light escapes. Train before travel in low-light navigation, moving floors, high-altitude pressure suits, and prolonged low-frequency vibration.

Your medical kit should be entirely sealed and should emphasize:

- Hypoxia and decompression treatment
- Anti-nausea medication
- Cardiac monitoring
- Cold injury care
- Anxiety management
- Dry wound containment

A bleeding human is an emergency for both patient and ecosystem. The immediate priority is to seal the wound and capture every fluid, not to expose it for convenient treatment.

Do not consume native substances. Their clear “sap” is not water and may contain concentrated ionic salts, reactive cages, mineral foams, and chemistry incompatible with terrestrial cells.

## If your suit leaks

A leak is a diplomatic and ecological incident.

1. Seal the breach immediately.
2. Cover the affected ground with an approved dry capture mat if available.
3. Do not wipe, rinse, or scrape the foundation.
4. Notify the city through the designated emergency signal.
5. Move only if directed.
6. Allow native tissue to isolate the contamination.
7. Enter quarantine even if the leak seemed trivial.

If nearby hairs rise, bend toward you, or begin forming a glassy blister, do not run. Sudden footfalls may spread contamination and look like hostile behavior. Stand on your isolation pad, remain electrically quiet, and wait for assistance.

## Recommended expedition kit

At minimum:

- Double-shell pressure suit
- Closed-loop oxygen and humidity recovery
- Redundant dry waste containment
- Water-immobilizing emergency cartridges
- Antistatic conductive-dust outer layer
- Broad compliant footpads
- Vibration-isolation pad or litter
- Infrasound and electrical-field monitors
- Low-light, narrow-beam lamps
- Inertial navigation with directional route logging
- Native-compatible communication token
- Sealed food and medical compartments
- Personal quarantine bag
- Passive emergency beacon approved for local frequencies
- Several days of reserve life support beyond the planned return time

Do not rely on solar charging. Light is weak, and intercepting it may be environmentally or socially unacceptable. Bring stored power in sealed, nonaqueous systems.

## Best conduct

Move slowly. Ask before touching. Regard roads as guides, walls as living hosts, fields as working organs, and weather as part of the city’s nervous system.

Do not seek ruins by expecting dead stone. The plateau recycles its past. A grove of mineral stalks may be an old tower, memorial, nursery, heat refuge, and active public place at once. Treat it accordingly.

The High Dry Garden is not empty wilderness with a strange city on it. The open country, the transit system, the towers, the animals, and perhaps the inhabitants are participants in one ancient negotiated metabolism. A successful human visitor will not conquer its cold or cross its distances by force. They will remain dry, quiet, light-footed, and legible—and permit the Garden to decide how they may pass.
