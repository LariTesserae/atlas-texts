## Overall recommendation

Treat this as a hadal archaeological preserve, not as a place for conventional diving. An ordinary human should never enter the water directly. Visit inside a **one-atmosphere deep-submergence vehicle** or a pressure-isolated habitat brought through a portal. Send sterile unmanned probes first.

The apparent stillness is misleading. The chief dangers are pressure, cold, darkness, entangling vegetation, unstable masonry, hidden voids, loss of navigation, and contamination of an ecosystem that may never have encountered animal life.

## Before crossing over

### Determine where the portal opens

Do not open an unprotected doorway directly into the submerged world. Miles of water may exert hundreds of atmospheres of pressure; an ordinary portal into a room or terrestrial atmosphere could produce an explosive flood, destroy the arrival site, or collapse the surrounding ruins.

Use a pressure-rated transfer lock with shutters on both sides. Before admitting anything larger than an instrument:

- measure external pressure and temperature;
- sample the water without depressurizing it;
- test salinity, dissolved gases, acidity, toxins, and microbial content;
- confirm that the destination is open water rather than a silt-filled chamber;
- scan for nearby walls, weed, shafts, and unstable ceilings;
- establish whether the water has a reachable surface at all.

Assume that “miles deep” exceeds the rating of almost every commercial submarine. The vehicle should be certified for the measured pressure with a substantial safety margin, not merely for the estimated depth.

### Reconnaissance first

A sensible sequence is:

1. Pressure and chemistry probe.
2. Sterile camera and sonar package.
3. Small untethered mapping drone.
4. Larger remotely operated vehicle with sampling tools.
5. Human visit only after a safe operating volume and return route have been mapped.

The probes should inspect upward as carefully as downward. A portal beneath a roof, overhang, root mat, or inverted courtyard can leave a crew trapped despite apparently open surroundings.

## Vehicle and life support

Use a compact one-atmosphere craft with:

- redundant pressure hulls or an independently survivable crew sphere;
- redundant oxygen supply and carbon-dioxide removal;
- active heating and heavily insulated controls;
- several independent battery systems;
- emergency chemical oxygen and heat sources;
- low-velocity, guarded thrusters;
- variable buoyancy that does not depend on releasing ballast into the site;
- manipulator arms rather than human excursions;
- forward-, downward-, and upward-looking sonar;
- inertial navigation, Doppler velocity sensing, and acoustic beacon navigation;
- high-sensitivity cameras using adjustable, diffuse lighting;
- pressure-wave, temperature, and turbidity sensors;
- a homing system tied directly to the portal or staging habitat;
- enough endurance to wait out a current, mechanical obstruction, or temporary loss of visibility.

A conventional scuba set is useless here. A flexible pressure suit would expose the traveler to fatal pressure and make breathing gases dangerously complicated. Even an atmospheric diving suit would be an inferior emergency option: it is awkward in weed, vulnerable around masonry, and difficult to recover if it enters a shaft.

Carry survival provisions for much longer than the planned excursion. Nothing local should be considered drinkable, breathable, or edible. There may be no useful oxygen in the water and no organism compatible with human digestion.

## Navigation

This world is exceptionally easy to become lost in. Streets, chambers, terraces, and gardens repeat without natural landmarks. There may be no usable magnetic field, and a compass cannot distinguish one nearly identical courtyard from another.

Build navigation around redundancy:

- place recoverable acoustic transponders along the route;
- maintain a continuously updated three-dimensional sonar map;
- log every turn automatically;
- retain a direct range and bearing to the portal;
- program an autonomous return course;
- impose strict maximum-distance and minimum-reserve limits.

Do not rely on visible guide lines. Weed can swallow them, masonry can pinch them, and a density current can drag them across architecture. If a tether is necessary, use a smooth, cut-resistant, positively buoyant one with automatic tension control and explosive or mechanical release points at both ends.

Because the terrain is constructed at every level, “downhill” does not necessarily lead to a basin from which ascent is simple. A channel may become a buried conduit. A plaza may be the roof of a vast hollow structure. A circular shaft may descend farther than sonar can resolve.

## Moving through the ruins

Stay over broad, open terraces during the first human expedition. Avoid interiors, arches, covered canals, weed forests, and all shafts.

Maintain generous clearance from the ground. Thruster wash can:

- erase delicate sediment records;
- uncover or destabilize masonry;
- fill the water with silt for longer than the expedition lasts;
- drive the vehicle into an unseen wall;
- tear ancient plants that may have taken centuries to grow.

Approach by neutral buoyancy and extremely low thrust. Never rest the craft on paving simply because the surface looks solid. The slabs may roof a cistern, conduit, chamber, or succession of older structures.

Do not brush against ribbon weed. Long fronds can enter thruster guards, wrap manipulators, obscure viewports, and anchor the craft to root mats extending through entire districts. Carry multiple independent cutting systems, but use them only for rescue. Cutting a single stem may damage a colony spanning miles.

Do not enter a building with the crewed vehicle unless it has been mapped from several angles and there is room to turn around without touching anything. Send a small ROV instead. A modest doorway built for ordinary feet is not a safe submarine passage.

### Collapse risk

The ruins fail slowly, but a visitor can provide the final disturbance. Avoid:

- cracked arches;
- walls leaning toward open channels;
- swollen black timber under load;
- roof-tile heaps;
- root-lifted slabs;
- sediment bulges at retaining walls;
- narrow spaces that could be closed by one falling block.

Sonar vibration and ordinary lights are unlikely to matter, but contact, thruster wash, coring, drilling, and acoustic blasting may. A collapse underwater may sound subdued while still producing a damaging pressure pulse and a blinding sediment cloud. If you hear isolated knocks, internal cracking, or a rolling sequence of impacts, back away into open water and face the likely source with the smallest cross-section of the vehicle.

## Water “weather”

Install temperature sensors outside the hull and watch for persistent gradients. A density current may arrive without visible warning. Signs include:

- leaves slowly aligning in one direction;
- increasing pressure on a stationary vehicle;
- sediment beginning to pour from roofs;
- abrupt changes in temperature or mineral content;
- acoustic beacons shifting relative to one another.

Do not fight a broad current inside architecture. Move early to a clear area, increase clearance from walls and vegetation, orient the craft along the flow, and conserve power. Postpone all work near shafts. They may become strong intakes or outlets when regional circulation changes.

Warm mineral-bearing upwellings should not be assumed safe. They may be acidic, oxygen-poor, corrosive, or chemically reducing.

## Biological and contamination precautions

The absence of animals is the site’s most important ecological fact. It may also make the world extraordinarily vulnerable to what travelers bring.

Human skin, breath, food, sewage, mold spores, and ordinary Earth bacteria could introduce decomposition, grazing, disease, or nutrient cycles that have never existed here. A discarded crumb might be a greater ecological disturbance than removing a stone.

Use near-planetary-protection standards:

- sterilize the vehicle exterior and all probes;
- use sealed electric systems rather than oil-filled equipment where possible;
- prohibit discharge of ballast water, coolant, lubricants, waste, or food;
- capture manipulator rinse water and loose material;
- double-contain every sample;
- return all beacons and guide equipment;
- quarantine the vehicle after recovery;
- analyze samples in pressure-rated isolation.

Do not assume the world is sterile merely because no animals are visible. Microbial films, spores, sap, and pore water may contain pathogens, toxins, or biochemistry incompatible with terrestrial life. Avoid exposing samples to cabin air. Never taste, touch, or smell a depressurized specimen.

Lights should be used sparingly. Even if the plants grow too slowly to react during a visit, intense artificial illumination could alter them over time. Use brief, low-heat, wavelength-controlled lighting and document total exposure.

## Archaeological conduct

Everything here is both artifact and terrain. There is no harmless patch of bare ground on which to establish a camp, dump ballast, or drive an anchor.

During initial visits:

- take images and noncontact measurements rather than objects;
- do not clear moss from inscriptions;
- do not pry apart fitted stones;
- do not open blocked doors or clay-sealed vents;
- do not retrieve material from shafts;
- do not “clean” roads with water jets;
- do not assume repeated rooms are culturally redundant.

The blocked chambers and raised causeways may preserve the history of the flooding. Their rubble, seals, silt layers, and plant succession are evidence. Removing a blockage could also connect pressure or current systems that have been separated for ages.

If sampling is eventually justified, begin with loose particles already suspended in water and tiny portions of detached material. Record the exact three-dimensional context of everything.

## Human factors

The place is likely to be psychologically difficult despite its beauty. Expect:

- near-total darkness outside the lamps;
- no visible horizon;
- oppressive awareness of the water overhead;
- endless human-scale doors with no inhabitants;
- long intervals without movement;
- occasional impacts traveling from unknown distances;
- severe disorientation caused by repeating architecture;
- the temptation to interpret random forms as statues or watchers.

Bring at least two people in the crew sphere if the vehicle allows it. Use short shifts, spoken checklists, regular orientation reports, and fixed mission limits. Do not permit a sense of calm to relax procedures. Conversely, do not let an unexplained noise provoke a high-speed retreat through weed and masonry.

Keep cabin lighting warm and stable. Provide an independent clock and a visible map back to the portal. The environment offers almost no natural indication of time.

## Where to visit first

The safest and most revealing first destination would be a high, open terrace near the arrival point:

- above the thickest sediment;
- away from tall stem forests;
- outside enclosed architecture;
- distant from circular shafts;
- with several unobstructed retreat directions.

From there, survey roads, drains, retaining walls, and roof gardens without entering them. Higher districts may offer slightly better ambient light and fewer pressure complications, though they are not necessarily structurally stronger.

The deep sunken courts should be reserved for later robotic expeditions. Their breathing shafts combine every major hazard: uncertain depth, current, falling material, vegetation at the rim, and no guaranteed turning space. Nothing that enters and fails to return should be followed impulsively.

## Emergency rules

Abort immediately if any of the following occurs:

- unexplained hull noise or pressure change;
- loss of two navigation systems;
- entanglement affecting more than one thruster;
- turbidity that prevents safe sonar confirmation;
- a regional current stronger than the vehicle’s conservative limit;
- evidence of a nearby progressive collapse;
- chemical ingress or corrosive leakage;
- loss of direct homing to the portal;
- life-support consumption exceeding the return reserve.

Never leave the pressure hull to solve an external problem. Sacrifice equipment before risking the crew. If trapped by vegetation, stop thrusting first; continued rotation will tighten the entanglement. Map the strands with cameras or sonar, cut methodically with manipulators, and maintain enough buoyancy to avoid settling onto the architecture.

## What the visit will feel like

The first impression will probably not be grandeur but proximity: a small stretch of paving, a doorway, leaves hanging over a parapet. Only through sonar and slow movement will the scale become comprehensible. Every familiar architectural feature will imply hands, feet, repeated labor, and ordinary lives, while the ocean’s darkness refuses to reveal where any of it ends.

Move slowly enough to accept that the landscape cannot be taken in at once. The most appropriate posture is not conquest or even exploration in the usual sense, but restraint. You are visiting a world where a root may spend a century opening a crack and where your propeller could erase a courtyard in seconds.

Leave no light burning, no marker behind, no cut stem unless survival required it, and no terrestrial life in the water. In a place made entirely by patient hands and inherited by patient vegetation, the traveler’s first obligation is to avoid becoming its first sudden event.
