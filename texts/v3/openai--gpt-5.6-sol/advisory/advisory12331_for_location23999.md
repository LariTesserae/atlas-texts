## General assessment

Prepare as though you are entering a polar industrial plant that is still operating, has no human-readable safety system, and is large enough to generate its own weather.

The basin is not ruined, even where it appears abandoned. Every floor may flood, every wall may warm, every bridge may move, and every silent machine may start without warning. Its greatest dangers are likely to be ordinary ones acting at enormous scale: hypothermia, falls, entrapment, electrical current, pressure changes, machinery, contaminated water, disorientation, and delayed rescue.

An ordinary human should not visit alone.

## Before entering

### Establish that human life is possible

Send instruments first. Confirm:

- Oxygen concentration and partial pressure
- Carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, ozone, refrigerants, solvent vapors, and combustion products
- Atmospheric pressure and its fluctuations during ventilation cycles
- Presence of metal dust, fungal spores, oil aerosols, or corrosive mist
- Local gravity and any variation near large moving machinery
- Whether the rainwater is electrically live, chemically hazardous, or biologically active
- Radiation, strong magnetic fields, microwave leakage, and electrical arc zones
- Maximum noise and vibration levels
- Temperature ranges, especially along the perimeter
- How quickly low areas flood
- Whether local organisms carry incompatible pathogens or aggressive maintenance behaviors

Do not regard one good reading as sufficient. The atmosphere may change by district or by machine cycle. A safe platform can become a refrigerant exhaust or an ozone-filled arc chamber minutes later.

### Observe at least one complete operating cycle

If possible, monitor the basin remotely for several days before descending. Record:

- Rain-front movement
- Pump activation sequences
- Lake and sump levels
- Ventilation reversals
- Shutter movements
- Crane routes
- Rail traffic
- Expansion-joint motion
- Lighting and warning-horn patterns
- Doors that close automatically
- Areas that heat, freeze, flood, or energize without visible preparation

Do not assume the cycles are perfectly regular. The facility appears capable of changing tasks.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Wear a waterproof, insulated industrial immersion suit rather than ordinary cold-weather clothing. The suit should have:

- A sealed outer shell resistant to oils, sharp metal, and chemical spray
- Removable insulating layers so that warm machinery corridors do not cause dangerous sweating
- Reinforced knees, elbows, and seat
- A hood that does not obstruct hearing or peripheral vision
- High-visibility and infrared-reflective markings
- A personal heating layer with manual control and redundant batteries

Gloves should be waterproof, cut-resistant, and electrically insulating. Carry a second dry pair inside the suit. Never touch exposed rails, housings, standing water, or ice with bare skin.

Boots need:

- Nonconductive soles
- Aggressive grip on wet steel
- Removable microspikes for frost
- Ankle protection
- No loose laces or straps that can enter grilles or machinery

Magnetic soles may help on steep plates, but use them cautiously. Strong magnetic fields, moving panels, and energized equipment can make them dangerous. A mechanical tether is more dependable.

Wear a helmet with a face shield, sealed eye protection, and an integrated lamp. Overhead hazards include condensation, ice blades, tools, crane loads, welding debris, and components dislodged by vibration.

## Air, sound, and communication

Carry a positive-pressure respirator or closed-circuit breathing system, even if initial air tests are favorable. At minimum, each traveler should have an emergency hood and enough independent air to escape a suddenly contaminated sector.

Use active hearing protection with radio communication and bone-conduction backup. The basin’s noise will conceal approaching machinery, distort voices, and exhaust concentration. Radios may fail around large electrical systems, so establish simple tether signals and lamp codes.

Never navigate by sound. Echoes can return minutes late and from the wrong direction. A voice heard ahead may have originated behind you—or during an earlier passage.

## Navigation

Compasses may be unreliable. Satellite navigation will be absent, and repeating industrial geometry will make visual memory treacherous.

Use several systems at once:

- Inertial navigation
- Local lidar mapping
- Physically placed coded beacons
- Reflective route markers
- A continuous guide line in difficult sectors
- Logged elevation and distance from the entry point

Mark routes in a way that will remain readable after rain, frost, darkness, and shifting panels. Chalk, paint, and adhesive tape may fail. Clamped or weighted beacons are better, but do not attach anything to moving seams, hot pipes, doors, rails, control surfaces, or cable trays.

Expect the terrain itself to move. A bridge may shorten, an expansion joint may widen, or a formerly open route may become a spillway. Always identify at least two exits from every sector.

## Entry and base camp

Enter onto a high, broad, surveyed platform well above the apparent maximum flood line. Avoid:

- Central lowlands
- Sump grilles
- Intake towers
- Overhead crane lanes
- Rail corridors
- Ventilation outlets
- Pressure doors
- Expansion joints
- The underside of unfinished construction
- Any surface warm enough to indicate active machinery beneath it

“Inactive” is not a meaningful category here. Choose a site because it has been monitored and has multiple escape routes, not because it looks abandoned.

If staying more than a few hours, use a raised, sealed shelter with its own heat, air filtration, power, and water. It should be freestanding and removable. Do not drill into plates or bolt equipment to the structure. You do not know what is pressurized, energized, or moving behind any surface.

Keep the return portal or transit apparatus elevated, protected from spray, and independently powered. Account for structural shifts of several centimeters. Establish a secondary evacuation point if multiverse transit allows it.

## Movement through the basin

Travel in teams of at least three:

1. A route leader watching terrain and machine activity
2. A systems observer monitoring air, temperature, vibration, and water
3. A rear guard maintaining markers and escape access

Move slowly. Wet metal conceals holes, loose grilles, oil films, sharp cut edges, and sudden drops. Test every uncertain plate before committing weight. Use fall protection on gantries even when they have handrails; cold, vibration, and condensation make familiar industrial structures much less forgiving.

Do not step into standing water if there is another route. It may conceal:

- Energized conductors
- Pump suction
- Drain openings
- Cryogenic cold spots
- Corrosive residues
- Rapidly moving panels
- Deep shafts beneath apparently shallow flooding

Never cross a gutter, channel, or flooded plaza without first identifying the upstream spillways and the nearest high ground. Water levels may rise faster than a person can run once a pump or shutter changes state.

Treat every rail as active. Treat every container as pressurized. Treat every door as capable of closing. Treat every pipe as either dangerously hot, dangerously cold, or capable of becoming so.

## Responding to machine activity

Warning horns may not be intended for humans, and their meaning may vary by sector. Nevertheless, any horn, lighting change, pressure sensation, sudden fog movement, or sequence of chattering tags should be treated as an operational transition.

When the basin changes state:

1. Stop moving.
2. Look up for cranes, shutters, and falling condensation.
3. Check airflow and gas monitors.
4. Identify whether water is rising or draining.
5. Move to previously surveyed high ground.
6. Stay clear of doors, vents, rails, grilles, and joints.
7. Do not shelter inside machinery housings or apparently empty tanks.

If inspection arrays ignite, resist the urge to stare across the basin. Use the brief illumination to check nearby routes and overhead threats. The return to darkness can temporarily blind you.

If a silent production line activates, withdraw rather than observe closely. Automated systems may regard loose equipment—or people—as misplaced material.

## Water and cold

Assume all basin water is contaminated with metals, lubricants, microorganisms, and industrial chemicals. Do not drink it, even after ordinary filtration. Bring all drinking water or use a treatment system designed for unknown industrial contamination.

Rain can soak insulation, obscure vision, overwhelm microphones, and cool the body rapidly. After every rain band:

- Check suit seals
- Replace wet gloves
- Inspect tether attachments
- Verify that route markers remain in place
- Check for ice formation on ladders and handrails
- Reassess water depth before moving

Hypothermia may begin subtly because noise and stress mask shivering and impaired judgment. Team members should regularly test one another’s speech, dexterity, and decision-making.

The warm strips beside bearings and transformer cabinets are tempting resting places, but they may activate, vent, or become electrically hazardous. Use them only after remote inspection, and never sleep against the machinery.

## Local organisms

Do not touch, feed, collect, or step on the pale scavengers, moths, moss, reeds, or filamentous growths. Their scarcity does not imply harmlessness. They may concentrate heavy metals, inhabit electrical systems, release defensive chemicals, or be part of the facility’s maintenance ecology.

Keep food sealed. Do not leave organic waste. A few crumbs could alter a tiny ecosystem dependent on trace nutrients—or attract organisms into your shelter.

If something large moves beneath flooded plates, leave the water’s edge. Do not pursue it with a person. Use a tethered sensor or camera, and be prepared for the possibility that it is a machine component rather than an animal.

After the visit, quarantine clothing, samples, and equipment. Decontaminate for oils, metal dust, spores, and cross-universe biology. Nothing living should be removed without a sealed biological protocol.

## Supplies

Each traveler should carry:

- Independent breathing apparatus
- Multi-gas and radiation monitors
- Waterproof thermal suit and spare dry insulation
- Helmet, face shield, and hearing protection
- Two light sources plus chemical or passive markers
- Fall-arrest harness and cut-resistant tether
- Compact thermal shelter
- High-calorie food that can be eaten without removing gloves
- Medical kit focused on hypothermia, lacerations, burns, crush injuries, and electrical injury
- Emergency flotation if routes approach the lake
- Insulated rescue hook and nonconductive pole
- Personal locator beacon
- Mechanical cutting tool, avoiding spark-producing tools where possible
- Redundant power stored in waterproof insulated cases

Do not depend on local outlets, water, heat, controls, or shelter.

## Psychological preparation

The basin will make distance difficult to judge. Lights several kilometers away may appear close. Sudden full illumination may produce vertigo. Darkness, mechanical concussion, delayed voices, and unexplained alarms can create a constant sense of being pursued or watched.

Set strict turnaround times before entry. Fatigued travelers will be tempted to interpret repeating lights as landmarks, delayed echoes as team members, and warm machinery as safety. Use written checklists rather than intuition once cold and noise have accumulated.

Do not attempt to discover the basin’s purpose during the first visit. Curiosity is especially dangerous here because every visible process suggests another just beyond reach.

## The most important rules

1. **Nothing is truly abandoned.**
2. **Never enter a place from which rising water, a closing door, or a moving machine can remove both exits.**
3. **Do not trust silence, darkness, or apparent inactivity.**
4. **Stay above the water whenever possible.**
5. **Bring your own air, heat, light, water, navigation, and way home.**
6. **Leave before fatigue makes the machine seem familiar.**

The basin is young enough that its hazards remain sharp, bright, and unfinished. It may be beautiful from a high gantry when the ceiling arrays ignite and the entire manufactured horizon appears at once. Admire it from behind a tether, with your gas monitor active and your retreat route already marked.
