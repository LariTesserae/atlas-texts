## The first problem is scale

Do not attempt to enter at ordinary human size. Even if the seam admits your hand, your weight would collapse streets, rupture cisterns, and compress entire houses into the mud. The “village green” is an intricate inhabited volume whose useful spaces may be measured in fingers, seeds, or droplets.

Arrange one of the following before arrival:

- **Proportional reduction**, including clothing and equipment.
- **A locally compatible surrogate body**, preferably light, warm-blooded, and able to crouch or crawl for long periods.
- **Remote presence through a very small articulated probe**, though its vibrations and mechanical smell may alarm inhabitants.

A reduced body must retain appropriate strength and metabolism. At small scale, cold and water become much more dangerous: a droplet can strike like a thrown bladder, surface tension can trap limbs, and heat is lost rapidly. Do not accept a transformation that merely makes you smaller without adjusting these factors.

Your total ground pressure should be no greater than that of a marsh hare or, in the narrow districts, a beetle. Travel lightly.

## Arrange permission and a guide

The entrance does not resemble a gate, and descending through the wrong black seam may put you into a frog’s cistern, an eel channel, or a root pocket that seals behind you. Establish contact in advance and wait for an escort at an agreed signal point.

Use a **silent signal**: a patterned tension on a reed, a sequence of shaded and unshaded light, or a knotted cord left above the entrance. Do not shout into the seam, tap on the roof, or probe it with a stick.

A local guide is essential. Maps age in days. Frost, rain, and active growth can move a street or close a district between your arrival and departure.

## What to wear

Expect continual cold, saturation, darkness, and abrasion.

Wear:

- A close-fitting, waterproof outer layer with no loose buckles.
- A removable insulating underlayer that remains warm when damp.
- Soft knee, elbow, and shin guards.
- Flexible gloves with tactile fingertips.
- Quiet, broad-soled footwear with no hard heels or aggressive tread.
- A hood rather than a brimmed hat; low roots will catch anything projecting.
- Eye protection against root tips, reed edges, fungal dust, and sudden splashes.
- A compact breathing filter rated for spores and compost gases.

Avoid wool or fabric that sheds fibers into the water system. Avoid perfumes, insect repellents, antiseptic vapors, and oily waterproofing unless locals certify them as harmless. The city is an interdependent culture of roots, molds, insects, fish, and people; ordinary traveler’s chemicals may function as herbicides or mass poisons.

Bring dry clothes in a genuinely sealed packet. “Water-resistant” is not enough.

## Lighting

Do not bring an exposed lamp.

Open flame could ignite dry pith, consume scarce oxygen, damage the living roof, or provoke emergency sealing. A bright electric light is nearly as inconsiderate: it will destroy dark adaptation, disturb animals, and may reflect from open water. Local warnings about reflected light should be taken literally.

Carry a dim, hooded, warm-colored light with:

- No blinking indicators.
- No exposed reflective lens.
- A shutter that can close instantly.
- Output low enough to illuminate only the next handhold.

Point it at your own feet or into a matte cloth, never across a plaza or toward water. Better still, accept a shell of luminous mold and allow your guide to manage it.

Expect to see very little. Learn to move by touch without clutching walls. Many walls are alive, delicate, or saturated enough to collapse under a hand.

## Essential equipment

Keep the kit small and silent:

- A soft waterproof pouch worn close to the body.
- A short safety cord made of nonabrasive fiber.
- A blunt probe for testing floors, used only with permission.
- Several absorbent cloths or sponge pads.
- A compact thermal wrap.
- A sealed ration requiring no cooking.
- Electrolytes and drinking water; do not drink from gutters or cisterns.
- A respirator or filter mask.
- A small nonmetallic first-aid kit.
- Matte marking tabs that attach without glue, if your guide permits.
- A waxed slate or knotted-cord system for communication.
- A flotation collar or belt appropriate to your reduced size.

Wrap every hard item in felted material. Secure tools so nothing can be dropped. A falling clasp may be heard throughout the settlement and could damage whatever it strikes.

Do not bring salt loosely packed. Salt is an offering and may also injure local freshwater organisms. Do not bring uncontrolled seeds, live soil, fungi, or biological samples.

## Learn to be quiet before you go

Silence here is not etiquette alone. Noise exposes the city to predators and may cause the roof plants to close air vents.

Practice:

- Walking without heel strikes.
- Lowering yourself rather than dropping to your knees.
- Passing objects hand to hand without clinks.
- Speaking directly beside another person’s ear.
- Using finger signs in low light.
- Suppressing reflexive cries when touched by insects or surprised by animals.

Do not whistle, clap, knock, laugh loudly, or call to someone across a space. If you drop something, freeze and wait for instructions rather than scrambling after it.

The city will not seem empty. Once your eyes adjust, you will perceive constant movement: mites like drifting dust, moths overhead, pressure beneath floors, pale bodies in water, and roots slowly changing position. Do not swat at anything merely because it crawls on you. Brush it gently onto a nearby surface after asking where it belongs.

## Movement inside the city

Assume every surface is weaker and wetter than it appears.

Step only on reinforced reed paths, root ribs indicated by your guide, or designated pads. Moss may conceal open water. A translucent membrane may be a reservoir wall. A seemingly solid knot may be a nursery, compost conduit, or animal den.

Never:

- Lean against a house.
- Step across an empty gap simply because it is shorter.
- Cut vegetation to make room.
- Pull on a root as a handhold.
- move a snail, frog, or sleeping moth without permission.
- Enter an apparently unused doorway.

In particular, do not cut or bruise **white roots**. They are fresh growth, structurally important, and their sap attracts burrowing swarms.

Move slowly around ladders and bridges. Your guide may cross a reed span that cannot support you, even at reduced size. Ask before following.

## Rain protocol

Check the weather above the marsh before entry. A modest rain shower transforms the city into an aquatic system. If rain is expected, postpone your visit unless your purpose is essential and your hosts explicitly approve.

The first warning below may be pressure rather than sound: a bowing roof, darkening roots, or synchronized droplets. At that point:

1. Stop wherever your guide indicates.
2. Close and secure every pouch.
3. Hood your light.
4. Clip to an approved safety line—not to a living root of your own choosing.
5. Move to a marked flood platform or pod-house.
6. Do not block gutters, sluices, collection nets, or animal routes.
7. Keep hands out of rising channels.

Do not attempt to wade through black floodwater. At your visiting scale, currents in narrow gutters may be violent, and predators emerge with the aquatic fauna. Ferry shells should be operated only by locals.

If separated from your guide, climb no higher than the nearest designated dry marker. Herons strike through thin roof patches from above; an exposed high platform is not necessarily safe.

## Other hazards

### Cold and exhaustion
Your body will cool continuously. Eat before entry and carry quick energy. Report numbness, confusion, clumsiness, or unusual sleepiness immediately. The communal compost chambers provide warmth, but they are controlled infrastructure, not public campfires.

### Air quality
Low chambers may accumulate carbon dioxide, methane, fungal spores, or fermentation gases. If your lamp culture dims, animals become lethargic, or you develop headache and breathlessness, leave the chamber without delay.

### Infection
Every surface is biologically active. Cover cuts before arrival. If pierced by reed, root, shell, or insect mouthpart, clean the wound only with a contained sterile pad; do not pour disinfectant onto the floor.

### Large animals
A shrew beneath the walkway may feel like an earthquake. A frog may block an entire passage. A heron’s beak may arrive like a falling spear. Freeze and obey local signals. Do not run blindly: the nearest “wall” may be a cistern skin.

### Enclosure
If a passage narrows after frost or rain, do not force it. Roots can grip clothing and tighten further when damaged. Shed your outer layer if necessary and retreat along the cord.

## Customs to observe

Treat local rules as practical knowledge even when their explanations sound religious.

- **Do not expose light over open water.**
- **Do not cut white roots.**
- **Do not bury anything**, including food scraps or bodily waste.
- **Do not claim, block, or pass through the unused doorway of a house.**
- Do not touch offerings of salt, skin, or seed meal.
- Do not describe the city as conquered land, a burrow, or a temporary shelter. Its political survival appears tied to acknowledging the marsh as an active participant.

Ask where waste goes. In a place without inert ground, every discarded item enters someone’s food, wall, water, or lungs.

If a death occurs, local compost rites may be nonnegotiable. Discuss this with your travel organization beforehand. Extraction of a body could be physically impossible during flood or root closure, and attempts to preserve one chemically would threaten the settlement.

## Gifts and trade

Useful gifts are light, quiet, noncorroding, and biologically safe. Possible offerings include:

- Fine bone or ceramic needles.
- Soft cord in short, counted lengths.
- Matte, corrosion-resistant hooks.
- Sterile cutting flakes.
- Dry pith or insulating plant fiber certified free of seeds and spores.
- Information about upcoming weather.

Avoid copper unless specifically requested; it is valuable but can contaminate water. Avoid iron objects that rust, glass that can break, sweets that attract insects, and any luminous organism not native to the marsh.

Trade through the market’s signs and cords. Do not haggle loudly or assume that an unoccupied object is abandoned.

## Biosecurity and departure

This city is young, ecologically unstable, and exceptionally vulnerable to introduced organisms. Quarantine yourself and your equipment before entry. On departure, submit to inspection.

Do not take:

- Moss, mold, larvae, roots, skins, shells, water, or silt.
- “Loose” seeds from gutters.
- A cutting from a house.
- Material from a newly opened chamber.
- Any animal that appears to have followed you voluntarily.

Clean yourself in a controlled station outside the marsh, not in an upstream pool. Your clothing may carry mites, spores, eggs, or fragments capable of colonizing another world.

Finally, leave before fatigue makes you careless. The city demands sustained crouching, silence, and attention. Plan a short first visit—perhaps one local daylight period, with a generous reserve before dusk, rain, or frost.

The right attitude is not that of a cave explorer. You are entering the interior of something alive that has temporarily made room for a city. Every bridge, doorway, and plaza exists by negotiation. Travel as though the walls are listening, the floor is drinking, and the route behind you may already be growing closed.
