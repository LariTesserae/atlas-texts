## Overall assessment

This basin is not survivable as ordinary wilderness. Treat it as an active planetary-scale organism or self-operating industrial ecosystem whose physical laws are locally conditional. The greatest dangers are not simply cold, drowning, or animals. They are:

- unreliable distance and direction;
- corrosive, conductive water everywhere;
- pressure and gravity changes associated with tripod calls;
- biological activity attracted to heat, voltage, pressure, and chemical differences;
- reflections that alter the state of water;
- sounds that reveal either distant or future events;
- ground that may be alive, formerly alive, or feeding;
- contamination by entities that do not distinguish machinery from biology.

An ordinary traveler should visit only the outer basin, remain for a short interval, and have an extraction method that does **not** require walking back to a coordinate. Do not attempt the central lowland, western crack country, processing ridges, or the far margin where form and sequence fail.

If your portal must be closed behind you, do not go.

## Essential travel arrangement

### 1. Keep extraction open and local

Your return threshold should remain continuously active, guarded from the departure side, and mounted on an imported platform rather than directly on native stone.

It should have:

- an automatic recall or closure protocol controlled from the safe universe;
- independent power on both sides;
- a hard time limit;
- visual, radio, and nonlocal or quantum identification if available;
- a broad physical opening that does not depend on exact alignment;
- a sacrificial vestibule for decontamination.

Do not rely on “the portal is only fifty meters away.” Fifty meters may cease to be a useful statement here. If extraction requires arriving at a mapped location, following your tracks, hearing a beacon, or crossing a nearby channel, it is inadequate.

A short physical tether may help within the immediate staging area, but a long cable is likely to become trapped under herds, incorporated into migrating tracks, attacked as a conductive gradient, or stretched across distorted distance. Use quick-release tethers only.

### 2. Send instruments first

Before crossing, leave a disposable probe package for at least one complete pressure cycle. Measure:

- atmospheric composition and pressure;
- temperature and wind at several heights;
- brine chemistry;
- metallic and volatile contaminants;
- electrical field strength;
- substrate vibration;
- pressure trends;
- local gravity vector;
- whether imported matter changes, acquires extra shadows, or appears in nearby reflections.

Do not retrieve the probes. Their apparent inertness does not prove they have remained inert.

### 3. Travel as a very small team

Do not go alone, but do not send a walking party spread across the flats. The safest arrangement is two travelers inside one sealed, low-profile capsule or on one compact platform, with observers outside the universe.

If proceeding on foot, stay within arm’s reach. Radio contact is not a substitute for physical proximity when a nearby channel can impose half a day of separation.

## Personal protective equipment

Assume the atmosphere is poisonous, the water corrosive, and every exposed energy source edible.

Wear a fully sealed, positive-pressure suit with:

- independent breathing gas;
- redundant heating circuits;
- brine-resistant polymer or ceramic outer layers;
- no exposed copper, steel, or polished metal;
- electrically insulating outer surfaces;
- internal Faraday shielding;
- distributed batteries rather than one warm, highly charged pack;
- no routine venting of heat, moisture, or gas;
- wide, compliant soles for soft ice and irregular stone;
- vibration isolation at the feet, hips, and helmet;
- automatic pressure stabilization for tripod calls;
- eye and dental-pressure monitoring if available.

The suit should be uniformly dull, dark, and nonreflective. Cover buckles, lenses, screens, and instrument faces. Use recessed, baffled cameras rather than mirrors or shiny visors.

The ecosystem consumes gradients. A warm human in a powered suit is an unusually concentrated package of heat, voltage, oxygen, pressure, and chemistry. Your protection must not merely keep the environment out; it must conceal and diffuse what is inside.

### Medical preparation

Before departure:

- correct loose dental work and avoid pressure-sensitive implants;
- screen for glaucoma, retinal vulnerability, vertigo, and circulatory problems;
- use anti-nausea medication if appropriate;
- bring chemical-burn treatment and an internal suit patch system;
- wear active hearing protection, while understanding that it will not stop vibrations transmitted through stone and body fluids.

The tripod notes may first be felt in teeth, eyes, and organs rather than heard. Anyone with fragile ocular or vascular health should not enter.

## Reflection discipline

Reflection is not cosmetic here. It is a physical operation.

A pool observed directly and the same pool seen in reflection may occupy different thermal states. Multiple reflective surfaces could create cascades of freezing, clouding, or other untested transitions. Therefore:

- bring no mirrors;
- cover polished tools;
- use matte anti-reflective helmet optics;
- do not inspect pools with mirrored periscopes;
- do not place glass, foil, polished metal, or glossy screens near water;
- avoid shining lights across pools at shallow angles;
- do not use water surfaces for signaling;
- never infer whether water is liquid or frozen from its image in another surface.

Use radar, short-range lidar, thermal sensing, and direct baffled cameras. Treat unexpected reflections—especially animals visible only in water—as physically present even if no corresponding body is visible nearby.

Do not step through a reflected herd’s path. The reflected animals leave real wakes, and the living fauna already yield to them.

## Movement on the flats

Walk slowly and judge only the terrain immediately beneath the next step. Maps, horizon estimates, and ordinary odometry will be misleading.

### Footing

The upper ice skins are flexible, while pools may freeze from below. A surface that looks solid may have liquid above ice, and one that looks liquid may contain a hard layer at an unexpected depth.

Probe with nonconductive, padded equipment. Avoid striking the ground. On the oldest bedrock, even a tap may be transmitted across the entire basin.

Never stand on:

- water-filled cracks;
- slabs whose surrounding pools are tilting inward;
- branching track systems;
- relief-tracks rising above the surface;
- soft-looking ground near the far margin;
- a surface that begins to sag without breaking;
- stone that gives a cyclic pulse or mechanical knock.

A gradual inward tilt may indicate a circular subterranean mouth. Do not wait for an obvious opening. Move laterally off the affected slab immediately, without running or hammering the ground.

### Navigation

Navigate by a continuously updated map of **local relationships**, not metric distance. Record “portal beside black shelf, shelf touching pale rib, rib bordering three shallow pools,” rather than “portal 120 meters southwest.”

Never trust footprints, including your own. Tracks migrate, merge with older trails, rise into solid ridges, and may lead toward low collection zones.

If markers are used, they must identify themselves cryptographically and report their own sensor state. A marker that merely looks familiar may be a relief-track, reflection, altered duplicate, or reassembled organism.

## Sound discipline

The basin is loud enough to conceal danger, but making more noise is unwise.

Use low-power radio or another nonacoustic link. Keep transmissions brief because electrical activity may attract aerial fauna or chain-bodied conductors. Do not use:

- hammering;
- sonar pings through the ground;
- explosive anchors;
- acoustic beacons;
- metal walking poles;
- machinery that couples strong vibration into the substrate.

A sound’s apparent distance is meaningless. It may have traveled through old material rather than nearby space.

At the far margin, sound may arrive before its cause. If an unfamiliar grinding, herd-thunder, rifle-like unfolding, or impact grows louder without a visible source, clear the anticipated path. Do not investigate the sound. It may be the advance event of creatures that have not arrived yet.

Avoid any wound-like opening suspended in the air. It may be the unused result of a failed predatory strike and can still collect water or interact with passing matter.

## Encounters with native life

Do not assume there is a clean distinction between animal, machine, mineral deposit, weather system, and factory component.

### Shelled herds

Remain well outside their route. They consume thermal, chemical, electrical, and pressure gradients and may treat your suit as exceptionally rich food.

Do not attempt to outrun a herd based on apparent distance. Move perpendicular to its immediate local flow and retreat toward extraction before it surrounds you. If a herd passes nearby:

- reduce nonessential suit power;
- equalize exterior temperatures as much as possible;
- keep limbs close;
- do not discharge gas or coolant;
- do not stand in a pool the herd is entering.

Water left behind by a herd may be chemically uniform and “dead,” but it is not safe to drink or sample.

### Needle-and-ring swarms

Do not step among them and do not collect separated rings. A loose ring may still be one part of an animal and can reunite with others kilometers away. If they gather around your boots or suit joints, they are probably responding to waste heat. Withdraw without shaking or kicking.

Any ring found in an airlock must be treated as active contamination.

### Aerial fauna

When the light dims and ozone or cold-oil odors increase, look for translucent bodies and dragging tendrils.

Immediately:

- lower your profile;
- retract antennas and poles;
- cut power to nonessential external systems;
- avoid metallic seams;
- remain still until the tendrils pass.

Do not touch a severed tendril. Do not assume a translucent sac is harmless because it appears buoyant or passive.

### Black crack-hunters

Avoid the western reaches entirely. A water-filled crack may contain a predator folded into a shape indistinguishable from geology. Rifle-like reports may indicate limbs locking into place.

If one unfolds, do not approach abandoned plates or prey fragments. Pieces carried on its body may be armor, digestive organs, or offspring, and detached fragments may later move independently.

### Tripods

Give tripods the widest possible clearance. Never pass beneath one or between its legs, even though the legs appear not to touch the ground.

When a tripod contracts or the low note begins:

1. Stop moving.
2. Lock the suit against pressure transients.
3. Crouch without touching additional surfaces.
4. Do not run for the portal.
5. Suspend navigation calculations.
6. Watch local gravity, pressure, shadow count, rainfall direction, and water flow.
7. Wait until the call and its effects have stabilized.
8. Recalibrate every instrument before moving.

The fauna stop for these calls. Imitate them.

If rain hangs motionless, do not walk through it or collect droplets. The suspended field may expose atmospheric corridors used by native life, and motion through it may redistribute water in ways that persist when rainfall resumes.

If you acquire an extra shadow, abort the visit. Do not attempt to identify which shadow is “real,” and do not allow it to cross the extraction threshold without isolation.

If water begins running uphill or the apparent down direction changes, abandon all previous route assumptions. Move only enough to avoid immediate immersion or crushing, then extract.

## Regions to avoid

### The central lowland

Do not enter. Ankle-deep water does not make it passable. The animal population is physically continuous, leaving no open route, and pressure changes can rotate the entire mass as one grinding wave. A human would be crushed, dismembered, or incorporated before reaching meaningful shelter.

### Processing ridges

Do not place hands or tools in pores, vents, mineral seams, or cavities. A narrow opening may be an organ, pressure chamber, refinery stage, habitat, or mouth. Red valve organisms and pale sealing disks are functional living components. Removing one can trigger pressure release.

Stay clear of steam, brine, and mineral-powder vents. Familiar metals deposited on the rock are not free ore; they are part of an active cycle.

### Low areas filled with relief-tracks

Migrating raised tracks accumulate there. They can surround a traveler, pin the feet, or carry equipment away. Never camp downhill of old trail fields.

### The far margin

This region is categorically unsuitable for ordinary humans. Solidity, intersection, sequence, limb number, and weather history become unstable. Travelers returning altered are not merely injured; they may no longer be compatible with their departure environment.

No sample, observation, or scientific reward justifies walking there.

## Weather protocol

Pressure matters more than visible weather. Monitor atmospheric trends continuously.

When pressure begins a significant fall or rise, the basin’s population may turn east or west. Retreat before the mass rotation begins. Once shells start scraping and vents opening, do not try to cross between animals.

Rain may begin invisibly only a few meters overhead. Keep suit seals closed even during apparent dry periods. Rising water can emerge from cracks beneath you without rainfall.

Blue electrical activity beneath the clouds should be treated as a storm warning. Reflected flashes moving in the opposite direction may indicate a second, nonidentical event in the water. Do not stand where the two apparent paths intersect.

## Camping and duration

Do not camp on the native surface. Do not sleep in the basin.

A stationary warm shelter creates persistent gradients, and persistent gradients are food. Over time, the ecosystem may begin fitting itself around the shelter as a valve, conductor, obstacle, heat source, or substrate component.

Keep the visit shorter than one major pressure change and establish a mandatory extraction time independent of local observations. If clocks disagree, obey the shortest elapsed time.

Bring all food, water, oxygen, and waste containment. Consume nothing native, including apparently pure ice or distilled-looking condensate.

## Sampling and decontamination

The safest sampling policy is no sampling.

A stone may be compressed former life. A metal plate may be an organ. Water may contain disassembled organisms, future weather, or reflected species absent from the visible landscape. Even the oldest bedrock may twitch under storms.

If sampling is essential:

- use remote tools;
- take microscopic quantities;
- use triple containment;
- keep samples thermally and electrically isolated;
- never bring them directly into an inhabited world;
- quarantine them in a lifeless intermediate facility;
- observe them through at least several pressure and reflection cycles.

On return, abandon the outer suit layer and all exposed equipment in the vestibule. Inspect for:

- loose rings or needles;
- new seams or branching sheets;
- unexplained metal plating;
- extra shadows;
- reflections that move out of sequence;
- scratches depicting journeys not taken;
- components whose number or arrangement has changed.

If any piece of equipment appears worn by events that did not occur, quarantine the traveler as well as the equipment.

## When to abort immediately

Extract at once if:

- the portal’s apparent distance changes;
- a tripod calls;
- pressure begins driving a regional turn;
- any traveler gains or loses a shadow;
- rain stops in midair or begins falling in multiple directions;
- your echo arrives before your movement;
- the ground sags or water tilts toward a common center;
- a reflection shows something occupying your position;
- team members disagree about limb count, route history, or current weather;
- native organisms gather around your heat or power systems;
- any equipment starts functioning as part of the landscape;
- your tracks appear ahead of you;
- the water decides that down lies somewhere new.

The correct mindset is not exploration but temporary trespass inside a process that has been operating longer than ordinary geology. Touch little, generate as few gradients as possible, believe local anomalies immediately, and leave before the basin finds a use for you.
