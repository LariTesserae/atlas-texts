## Overall assessment

Treat this plateau less as a landscape than as a vast, saturated organism. “Ground,” “water,” “tree,” and “building” are unreliable categories here. A surface that supports one person may open beneath the next; a harmless pool may be the mouth of a submerged chamber; a shelter may be occupied, cultivated, or itself alive.

An ordinary human should not visit alone. A first expedition should be short, conservative, and limited to a well-instrumented team with a guaranteed extraction method.

## Before arrival

### Confirm human compatibility

Do not assume that warmth and visible animal life mean the environment is safe.

Use remote probes to test:

- Oxygen, carbon dioxide, methane, hydrogen sulfide, and volatile plant compounds
- Airborne spores, pollen, molds, and microscopic animals
- Waterborne pathogens and toxins
- Background radiation and unusual electromagnetic or temporal effects
- Whether local organisms react aggressively to human scent, heat, exhaled carbon dioxide, or artificial light
- Whether the living substrate responds to pressure, vibration, cutting, or fire

Vaccinations should be current. Team members with severe allergies, asthma, compromised immunity, chronic skin disorders, or poor balance should not join the initial landing.

Establish strict outward and return quarantine. Nothing—not seeds, mud, water, spores, or seemingly dead plant matter—should pass between worlds untreated.

### Choose the arrival point carefully

Do not open a portal directly onto an apparently flat patch of moss. Place a lightweight probe first and observe it for several hours. The substrate may sag slowly rather than fail immediately.

The safest initial site would be:

- On a broad, relatively dry swell
- Far from pools, drainage lines, erosion faces, cliff margins, and giant columns
- Free of obvious animal trails or polished bark
- Outside the drip perimeter of major crowns
- Large enough to support a load-spreading arrival platform

Keep the return portal or extraction vehicle on a platform with broad pads. Mark and continuously monitor its level. The plateau may settle, grow over it, or redirect water beneath it.

## Essential equipment

Everything should tolerate permanent wetness.

Carry:

- A full-face respirator with interchangeable particulate and chemical filters
- Eye protection and a fine insect hood
- Tough, breathable waterproof clothing with sealed cuffs
- Thin inner gloves and cut-resistant outer gloves
- High boots with smooth, easily cleaned surfaces
- A flotation vest or compact inflatable buoyancy collar
- A helmet with face lighting
- A low-profile pack that will not readily snag tendrils
- Collapsible ground-spreading shoes or bog plates
- A long, blunt probe for testing the mat ahead
- Short retrieval poles and throw lines
- Waterproof mesh radios and retrievable navigation beacons
- Inertial navigation and local mapping instruments; do not rely on stars, satellites, or magnetic compasses
- Multigas detectors
- Medical supplies for punctures, allergic reactions, fungal exposure, immersion, and respiratory distress
- Sealed food and a multistage water-treatment system
- Electric heaters and chemical warmers rather than flame
- Freestanding raised sleeping platforms with broad load-distribution feet

Bring duplicates of every critical device. Humidity will invade seals, corrode contacts, fog optics, and reduce battery performance. Keep electronics in dry gas-filled cases when not in use.

Avoid dangling straps, loose mesh, exposed foam, untreated wood, and absorbent fabrics. Local seeds, tendrils, and microscopic life will exploit them.

## Moving across the plateau

Travel slowly and in daylight-equivalent conditions, even though the light may remain dim and directionless. A kilometer here may require the effort and attention of several kilometers on ordinary ground.

Use a lead traveler with a probe, followed by a second person watching the tested path. Maintain visual contact. Do not bunch the team together: several people standing on one patch may exceed the substrate’s strength.

Test every step near:

- Changes in vegetation color or texture
- Circular or crescent-shaped plant arrangements
- Warm patches
- Places where the ground subtly pulses or drains
- Old footprints filled with water
- Apparently open reed plains
- The edges of pools, even tiny ones
- Straight causeways and openings beneath roots

A safety line can help at known crossings, but constant tethering in dense vegetation may become a strangulation or entanglement hazard. Use quick-release attachments and designate one person to manage the line.

Never jump. Do not stamp to test the ground. Vibrations may trigger collapse or attract organisms that interpret movement as prey.

If the surface begins to sink, spread your weight immediately, release your pack if necessary, and move backward along your tested route. Do not struggle vertically as if escaping ordinary mud: you may be pushing through a woven roof into deep water.

## Water hazards

Assume every pool is deep, connected, and biologically active. A pool only a pace wide may lead into kilometers of flooded root chambers.

Do not:

- Wade into dark water
- Stand on floating flowers or plant rafts
- Reach into a pool by hand
- Camp beside water
- Follow an animal onto a floating grove
- Trust a rim merely because it looks firm

Flexible pool margins are especially dangerous. If vegetation appears to lean inward, tighten, or close behind a dropped object, retreat without further testing.

Wear flotation gear whenever traveling through basins. Keep cutting tools accessible in case submerged roots entangle a person, but cut only in an immediate emergency. Damage may provoke a larger response, and severed tissues may release toxins or attract predators.

All drinking water must be filtered for particulates and microbes, treated for dissolved organic chemicals, and sterilized. The clean rainwater stored in hollow columns is not necessarily safe; the column may be an organism, a reservoir for another species, or a deliberately maintained resource.

## Biological precautions

Nothing should be eaten, tasted, smelled closely, or applied to the skin. Familiar-looking orchids, mosses, fruits, and clear water have no nutritional or toxicological relationship to their counterparts elsewhere.

Keep all wounds sealed. In this warm, wet environment, even minor abrasions are serious. Perform skin and equipment checks several times a day, especially beneath cuffs, around hair, and inside boots.

If seed filaments settle on exposed skin, do not brush them into the air. Remove them with adhesive collection strips or rinse them into a sealed waste container.

Be alert for living camouflage. Rotting wood, pool rims, blossoms, and sections of trail may be animals or feeding structures. Warning signs include:

- Shapes that remain unchanged while surrounding leaves move
- Repeated symmetry
- Localized warmth
- Small harmless animals clustering on one “log”
- Vegetation slowly closing an opening
- A path that changes behind the group
- Sudden collective silence

Do not assume that large animals are gentle because they move carefully or carry plants. Give them an enormous distance. Never pass between members of a procession, approach a drinking site, or touch the epiphytes on their backs.

Predators here appear adapted to waiting rather than pursuit. The correct response to uncertainty is usually to stop, observe, and retreat—not to run.

## Navigation and communications

The repeating landscape will defeat ordinary memory. Do not use distinctive plants as landmarks; growth, mist, and viewpoint can make unrelated places look identical.

Create a local mesh of raised, retrievable beacons. Each should record:

- Position relative to the base camp
- Air chemistry
- Substrate movement
- Water level
- Nearby biological motion
- Time since deployment

Map in three dimensions. Surface distance is not enough: drainage may pass below you, and a route may cross over flooded voids.

Turn back before consuming half your available travel time or supplies. Conditions may make the return much slower. Warm mist, darkness beneath giant crowns, and changing water routes can erase a usable path within hours.

Agree on simple radio codes. The vegetation absorbs sound, so shouting is unreliable and may advertise your location. If communications fail, the team should return to the last confirmed beacon rather than search independently.

## Camps

The best camp is a temporary, freestanding platform on a tested dry swell. Spread the load widely and inspect beneath it throughout the night.

Avoid camping:

- Beneath giant crowns, where delayed drops can fall with considerable force
- Near rivers, pools, or old footprints
- On straight causeways
- Inside rings of trunks
- In existing woven chambers
- Near cliff wounds or hanging roots
- On animal trails
- Where insects begin synchronized activity at dusk

Do not use stakes driven deeply into the mat. They may puncture a water chamber, injure a larger organism, or provide roots with a path into the shelter.

Keep the sleeping enclosure sealed and under slightly positive filtered-air pressure. Use raised cots. No clothing or equipment should rest directly on the ground.

Fire is unacceptable. A surface flame could ignite gases, provoke local life, or begin a deep peat fire that remains hidden for years. Use electric cooking and heating only.

Assign watches even if nothing appears active. The principal danger may be gradual: water rising, vegetation tightening around the platform, a trail opening nearby, or the “dry” swell beginning to breathe more deeply.

## Structures and apparent architecture

Treat all structures as occupied and culturally significant until proven otherwise.

Do not enter woven chambers simply because they look abandoned. Their openings change with climate; they may be nests, shelters, traps, organs, or the products of intelligence expressed through growth rather than tools.

Straight causeways are potentially useful but not automatically safe. They may lead to water, breeding grounds, feeding sites, or ritual spaces. Walk beside one first and observe traffic before stepping onto it.

Do not drink from hollow columns or disturb rings of interlocked trunks.

The buried rooms in erosion faces are particularly hazardous. They combine:

- Unstable overburden
- Waterlogged collapse risk
- Stagnant or chemically abnormal air
- Ancient dormant organisms
- Unknown cultural significance
- Doorways that now open into solid peat

No ordinary traveler should enter one. Initial study should use sterilizable remote instruments. Never remove the pale seeds. “Soft after tens of thousands of years” is not reassuring; it suggests extraordinary preservation or continued viability.

## Conduct and ethics

The plateau may possess intelligence at a scale or tempo humans do not readily recognize. A path might be a habit passed among animals, a structure grown by a people, or a response of the ecosystem itself. Lack of obvious tools is not evidence of lack of ownership.

Therefore:

- Cut nothing except to save a life
- Collect only microscopic samples under strict containment
- Do not leave permanent markers
- Do not introduce terrestrial microbes, seeds, food waste, or untreated sewage
- Avoid bright floodlights and loud acoustic surveys
- Do not alter watercourses
- Do not “improve” shelters or causeways
- Allow local beings to approach or withdraw on their own terms

If communication is attempted, use slow, repeatable, noninvasive patterns: light, placement of inert objects, and long intervals of observation. The local response may take hours, seasons, or generations. Do not mistake slowness for absence.

## Emergency priorities

If separated, do not wander. Inflate flotation gear, move only to the nearest tested firm patch, activate a beacon, and wait.

If someone falls through the mat:

1. Others spread their weight and remain back from the opening.
2. Use a throw line or pole rather than approaching the rim.
3. Pull horizontally, not upward.
4. Assume submerged roots will entangle the victim.
5. After rescue, treat for aspiration, toxic exposure, hypothermia, and infection.

If vegetation begins closing around the team, retreat along the exact route of entry. Do not slash indiscriminately.

If air chemistry changes, respirators become mandatory and the expedition ends.

If the extraction site shifts, floods, becomes overgrown, or attracts fauna, do not wait for a dramatic failure. Depart immediately.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect constant dampness, poor visibility, and a peculiar absence of firm feedback. The ground will yield without clearly moving. Water will arrive from leaves long after the rain seems to have ended. Distances will feel dreamlike because every scale repeats the same branching forms.

The psychological strain may be greater than the physical one. There are few horizons, echoes, or abrupt events. Danger announces itself as a minor change: an opening slightly narrower than before, a pool without its flowers, a patch of “wood” now facing another direction.

The safest mindset is patient and humble. Move as though crossing the body of a sleeping animal—because, in every practically relevant sense, you are.
