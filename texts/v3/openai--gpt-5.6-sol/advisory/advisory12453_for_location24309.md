## Preparing to Visit the Low Green Works

Treat the Works as a functioning industrial organism, not as a picturesque overgrown ruin. Nearly every surface is alive, load-bearing, hydraulically active, chemically defended, or all four. An ordinary human can probably visit safely, but only by moving slowly, carrying little, and assuming that anything resembling a street, wall, railing, drain, or machine may respond to touch.

### Before arrival

Arrange a local guide, maintenance liaison, or translated set of route markers if any are available. The town’s laws are chemical rather than written, so visual intuition alone will not tell you where passage is permitted. A broad empty court may be densely occupied underground; an apparently abandoned doorway may still be part of an active ventilation or water system.

Your most important preparatory steps are:

- **Quarantine yourself and your equipment.** Remove soil, pollen, seeds, fungal spores, insects, and untreated organic material. The Works visibly marks fungal contamination, and its response to alien microbes may be destructive to both visitor and town.
- **Confirm atmospheric compatibility.** Test oxygen, carbon dioxide, airborne spores, volatile resins, and humidity before descending into the basin. Conditions may change between courts and during production cycles.
- **Learn the warning colors.** Red cups indicate overheated machinery; blue star-like growth indicates fungal contamination. Pale calibration flowers appear to belong to water-control systems and should not be touched.
- **Identify dusk time and compressor phases.** Do not enter immediately before the municipal tower’s daily signal, and avoid crossing the settlement while the systems are changing phase.
- **Have an exit route that does not depend on local doors, bridges, or roads remaining in the same state.** The town can close shutters, tighten surfaces, drain pools, and redirect conduits.

If interworld transit permits, observe the Works remotely for at least one complete day-night cycle before entering.

## Clothing and equipment

Wear clean, close-fitting layers suitable for a permanently cool, damp cellar. The basin is not brutally cold, but prolonged stillness and humidity will chill an unprepared traveler.

Recommended equipment:

- Waterproof but breathable outer clothing
- Insulating mid-layer and spare dry socks
- Soft-soled, high-traction boots with broad tread
- Gloves resistant to sap, oils, and mild chemical irritants
- Eye protection
- A half-mask respirator with particulate and organic-vapor filters
- Portable atmospheric monitor
- Compact headlamp with a low, diffuse setting
- Noncontact thermometer
- pH strips or a simple chemical exposure kit
- Sterile water and sealed food
- A rigid waste container: leave no organic refuse
- A light walking staff with a blunt, padded end, used only to test uncertain footing
- A safety line for pool courts and raised walkways
- A whistle or electronic alarm, though sound may not summon help
- A small thermal blanket

Avoid spiked footwear, trekking poles with metal tips, crampons, blades, and anything likely to puncture membranes. Do not carry loose cotton, unfinished wood, live plants, raw food, or porous luggage. The town manufactures with fibres, pulp, spores, and resins; your belongings may be mistaken for feedstock, contamination, or damaged tissue.

Keep your pack light. Roads flex, bridges retain inherited deformations, and much of the ground covers cavities, reservoirs, or roots. Heavy loads may provoke structural responses even when they do not cause immediate collapse.

## Entering the basin

Approach in daylight, preferably late morning. Dawn is an active collection period, dusk triggers the municipal shutdown, and night is when the settlement drinks. Midday offers the best visibility and the lowest likelihood of standing water being rapidly redistributed beneath you.

Pause at the rim and watch for:

- Repeating pulses through the ground
- Pools rising or falling
- Warning blooms opening
- Streets changing tension or color
- Conveyor vines or overhead pipe-tendrils in motion
- A travelling bulge beneath the roads
- Sudden coordinated closure of valves or shutters

If multiple systems stop at once, **you should stop too**. A settlement-wide pause may announce the passage of the large subterranean animal, a pressure change, contamination, or another condition the Works understands better than you do.

Use established roads, but do not assume they are inert. Walk near the middle unless local markings indicate otherwise. Curbs are exposed roots, not convenient steps. Keep away from pale repair seams, which may be softer, chemically active, or under tension.

Never take shortcuts across bare-looking ground.

## Conduct inside the Works

The safest posture is that of a guest in a factory where the machinery cannot distinguish curiosity from interference.

### Do not touch casually

Avoid touching:

- Gauge flowers
- Warning cups
- Moss-packed bearings
- Belt-vines
- Valve pods
- Luminous leaf undersides
- Moisture-collecting hairs
- Wall membranes
- Exposed sap or resin
- Silver reeds around open pools

Do not lean on railings. A railing may also be a water collector or conduit. Do not sit against walls: they may be drawing moisture, venting gases, detecting pressure, or releasing suppressive chemicals.

If a surface bends toward you, changes color, beads fluid, tightens, or develops a bitter smell, back away along your exact route. Do not run unless the ground itself is failing.

### Respect water infrastructure

Water is the Works’ most carefully managed resource. Carry your own and do not drink from pools, gutters, cistern flowers, condensation beads, or translucent storage bladders. Even clear water may contain signaling compounds, spores, concentrated minerals, moss oil, or industrial residues.

Never step across a marked water artery. Since the laws are unwritten, likely indicators include:

- Repeated bands of bitter-smelling resin
- Unusually clear seams in the pavement
- Parallel rows of calibration blooms
- Root shoulders that stop abruptly on both sides
- Channels kept conspicuously free of growth

When uncertain, assume a boundary is functional.

Open pools are especially hazardous. Their levels can change rapidly, and a draining pool may pull loose material—or a person—toward its center. Stay several body lengths from the edge. If you hear wet cracking, see the silver reeds fold, or notice the surface beginning to rotate, move away immediately.

### Stay clear of machinery

The machinery is slow, but slow does not mean weak. Piston trees, knotwood gears, press stacks, and belt-vines likely exert enormous force. They may move after long periods of apparent stillness, with little audible warning.

Maintain generous clearance from:

- Resin collars around moving trunks
- Belt turns and wooden wheels
- Press plates
- Suspended bladder tanks
- Overhead tendons and pipe-vines
- Seedpod housings
- Low vents releasing steam
- Any joint that repeatedly swells and relaxes

Never place a hand into a mechanism to retrieve a dropped object. Abandon the object and report it if possible.

A ground pulse strong enough to shake condensation from bridges should be treated as a phase warning. Crouch in an open, designated area, keep away from suspended tanks, and wait through several quiet minutes before continuing.

## Biosecurity and health

The Works is likely vulnerable to foreign biology, and you are likely vulnerable to its chemistry. Keep all skin breaks covered. If cut or punctured, leave the living structures before treating the injury; do not let blood enter a street, wall, drain, or soil bed.

Potential symptoms requiring immediate withdrawal include:

- Sudden coughing or chest tightness
- Metallic, bitter, or intensely green taste
- Eye irritation
- Numbness after touching resin
- Drowsiness inconsistent with exertion
- Headache that worsens inside enclosed halls
- Skin staining or adhesive sap that warms after contact
- Confusion during compressor pulses

Do not scrape attached material from your skin with a knife. Flush with your own sterile water, cover loosely, and depart. Some tissues may respond to cutting by sealing more aggressively.

Eat only sealed food, preferably at the basin rim rather than inside. Crumbs, fruit peel, oils, and bodily waste could all carry chemical or biological information. Pack out everything, including wash water.

After the visit:

1. Quarantine clothing and equipment.
2. Photograph any attached residue before removal.
3. Decontaminate without releasing runoff into another ecosystem.
4. Monitor for delayed respiratory, fungal, or dermatological symptoms.
5. Do not plant or culture samples unless you have a controlled laboratory and explicit permission.

Taking souvenirs is inadvisable. Even apparently dead tissue may be dormant replacement material, a municipal signal carrier, or part of the Works’ waste-routing system.

## Timing and overnight stays

A day visit is much safer than an overnight stay.

Near dusk, the municipal tower releases its low note and the Works shifts into its night condition. Before this happens, be either outside the basin or in a shelter explicitly intended for visitors. During the transition:

- Workshop vents close.
- Conveyor systems slow.
- Road fibres tighten.
- Moisture combs unfold overhead.
- Subsurface valves begin opening.
- Water moves through gutters and reservoirs.
- Temperature may fall further.
- Routes may narrow or become slick.

Do not stand beneath moisture combs, bridges, or leaf edges as condensation begins to fall. Avoid tunnels and covered drains entirely at night. The system’s priority then is water collection, not pedestrian access.

The daily tower breath is likely more than a sound signal; it may include pressure, vibration, or airborne chemistry. Hearing protection will not necessarily isolate you from it. If you are still inside, stop in an open court, avoid doorways and machinery, and wait until the town’s responses have completed before moving toward the exit.

Camping on the roads, in empty dwellings, beneath roof leaves, or on “unused” ground is unsafe. Structures may seal openings seasonally, divert heat, thicken walls, or reclaim organic objects. The broad courts also conceal active subterranean territory.

## Rain and the subterranean animal

Do not visit during prolonged rain. Rain activates drainage leaves, fills reservoirs, changes root pressure, and brings the largest known animal closer to the surface.

Its approach may appear as:

- A slowly travelling ridge in the road
- Sequential lifting of pavement
- Factory belts going slack
- Valve flowers closing
- A widespread pause in production

If this happens:

1. Move off the affected road only if a clearly established hard platform is immediately available.
2. Do not cross the ridge or try to outrun it along its direction of travel.
3. Do not probe the ground.
4. Keep silent and still.
5. Follow the Works’ example: wait until activity resumes.

The creature may be harmless to the town precisely because every system yields to it. A human who remains on rigid machinery, blocks a route, or panics across soft ground may not be similarly accommodated.

## Social and ethical expectations

Even if no humanoid residents remain, the Works should be treated as inhabited. It remembers rules, repairs itself, arbitrates conflicts, controls resources, and preserves civic forms through replacement. Whether that constitutes a single mind, a colony, or a distributed municipal reflex is less important than behaving respectfully.

Good conduct includes:

- Requesting permission before collecting samples or making physical measurements
- Avoiding flashes, strong heat, smoke, and loud repetitive vibration
- Keeping machinery and water routes unobstructed
- Not cutting membranes that resemble blocked windows
- Not “repairing” sagging bridges or rounded steps
- Not removing shell spirals
- Not feeding or approaching the black birds or white mollusks
- Leaving inherited deformations alone

Do not mistake age for neglect. The sag in a bridge or hollow in a stair may be an actively maintained record, not a defect.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the Works to feel larger and more disorienting from within than it appeared from the rim. Curved streets and partially hidden walls will deny you long sightlines. Use passive navigation markers or a recorded route rather than chalk, paint, tied cord, or physical tags, all of which may interfere with living surfaces.

The dominant impression will not be lushness but restraint: grey-green courts, damp quiet, cool air, and machinery working with patient organic pressure. You may feel as though nothing is happening until a piston settles, a suspended bladder changes shape, or a pulse passes under your feet. Pay attention to these small events. They are the settlement’s traffic, factory noise, and public announcements.

Move at the Works’ pace. A safe visit should be short, quiet, and uneventful. If you leave with the sense that the town barely noticed you, you prepared correctly.
