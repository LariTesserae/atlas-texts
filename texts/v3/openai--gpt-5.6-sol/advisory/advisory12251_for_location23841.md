## Preparing for the Lower Sea

The Lower Sea should be approached as a continent-sized industrial plant that happens to contain an ocean—not as an aquarium, dive resort, or natural coastline. Its greatest dangers are not sharks or darkness, but pressure, cold, machinery, currents, moving structures, and the mistaken belief that something resembling terrain is stationary or solid.

An ordinary human traveler can visit safely, but only through the basin’s established transport and habitation systems. Do not arrive expecting to explore independently.

### Before crossing into this world

Confirm the local human baseline before departure:

- Atmospheric composition and pressure in dry stations
- Local gravity and day-cycle length
- Water chemistry, salinity, and dissolved contaminants
- Biological compatibility with local microbes, algae, and food species
- Medical compatibility with local drugs, pressure treatments, and blood supplies
- Whether your implants, electronics, or dimensional transit equipment interfere with sonar, magnetic navigation, or station security

Even if the inhabitants appear human, carry a compact medical translation record giving your anatomy, allergies, blood chemistry, medication needs, and any relevant dimensional differences. Obtain whatever vaccinations or microbiome barriers are recommended for workers around enclosed seawater systems. A young artificial sea can contain aggressive bacterial blooms, industrial biofilms, and treatment organisms uncommon elsewhere.

Arrange entry through a recognized dry station, preferably one connected to the roof or perimeter. Materializing directly into the water is likely to be fatal: you may appear at crushing depth, inside a current exclusion zone, beneath a moving floor panel, or in a volume scheduled to be drained or flooded.

### Permissions and timing

Travel with a licensed basin operator. Access charts are operational documents and may change by the hour as pumps, gates, cargo lanes, and maintenance sectors change status.

Ask for:

- A visitor-grade pressure and current safety briefing
- Live navigation-chart access
- The acoustic warning-tone catalog
- The current pump and turnover schedule
- Medical clearance for any intended dive depth
- Emergency shelter and evacuation assignments
- Authorization for photography, sonar use, and biological sampling
- Confirmation that your visit does not overlap a basin-scale turnover

If possible, schedule your first visit during the long illuminated phase and a period of reduced current. Turnover is impressive from inside a rated observation station, but it is a poor time for recreational travel. Currents strengthen, visibility changes quickly, surface conditions deteriorate, and ordinary routes may close for days.

Do not rely on a printed map. In the Lower Sea, “terrain” may be a hatch, movable panel, diffuser platform, intake screen, or temporary cargo structure.

## How to travel

### Stay inside a vehicle for most of the visit

The safest way to experience the Lower Sea is from a certified passenger submarine or pressure-rated observation vessel operated by a local crew. Choose an industrial craft with redundant propulsion, independent life support, collision sonar, hardwired communications, and the ability to attach to an emergency docking point. A panoramic tourist viewport is less important than reliable systems and a crew familiar with pump operations.

Transport submarines may look ungainly, but this is an advantage. Their pilots understand marked lanes, acoustic boundaries, animal behavior, and current forecasts. Avoid unlicensed private submersibles and any operator offering to “slip inside” an intake exclusion zone during a quiet interval.

Confirm that the vessel carries:

- Independent oxygen and carbon-dioxide scrubbing
- Thermal survival equipment for all passengers
- Emergency locator beacons readable by basin systems
- Mechanical or hardwired navigation backups
- Flood isolation compartments
- External lights that can be dimmed
- A means of clearing fouled propulsors without sending a person outside
- Escape or rescue arrangements appropriate to the actual operating depth

At great depth, personal escape may be impossible. Your survival plan is therefore not “swim upward”; it is “remain in a sound pressure hull until rescue arrives.”

### Dive only in designated zones

An ordinary traveler should restrict open-water diving to shallow, controlled sectors near inhabited stations, kelp terraces, or perimeter margins. Even there, use a local guide and surface or station tether.

Hundreds of meters of water lie beneath parts of the basin. Recreational scuba equipment does not make those depths accessible. Technical diving requires specialized gas mixtures, staged decompression, heated suits, redundant breathing systems, and extensive training. Atmospheric diving suits or crewed submersibles are more appropriate for deep visits.

Do not let clear water distort your judgment. The huge scale and lack of familiar landmarks make distances difficult to estimate. A maintenance tower that appears nearby may be kilometers away. A light below you may mark a structure hundreds of meters deep.

## Essential personal equipment

For movement within dry stations, galleries, moon-pool decks, and approved shallow-water areas, bring or obtain locally certified versions of:

- Warm, layered clothing for cool and damp interiors
- Waterproof outer garments
- Non-slip footwear with toe protection
- Hearing protection suitable for both airborne machinery noise and station alarms
- Eye protection
- A helmet or bump cap in service corridors
- A compact personal locator integrated with the local network
- A waterproof lamp with a backup light
- A watch or timer independent of the simulated daylight cycle
- Anti-motion-sickness medication
- Any necessary prescription medication in redundant sealed supplies
- A dry emergency thermal layer

For diving, use locally supplied or inspected equipment. At minimum, this will likely include:

- A properly fitted drysuit with thermal undergarments
- Full-face mask
- Hardwired or approved short-range acoustic communications
- Redundant breathing gas
- Independent depth, time, and decompression instruments
- A tether where required
- Cutting tools accessible with either hand
- A local acoustic transponder
- Lights with sufficient backup duration
- Gloves that protect against cold and sharp fabricated surfaces

Do not bring an active sonar device, powerful magnetic tool, grapnel, or autonomous drone without permission. These may interfere with navigation, attract security attention, enter machinery, or cause animals to react unpredictably.

## Learn the warnings before entering the water

The posted rules are literal.

### Red acoustic boundaries

Never cross one, even if no physical barrier is visible. The boundary may mark an intake’s influence, a gate’s sweep volume, a high-voltage installation, a cargo lane, or a panel about to open.

Water can look calm while already accelerating toward an intake. By the time the pull feels obviously dangerous, a swimmer may be unable to escape. Do not test the boundary, approach for a photograph, or assume that a silent pump is inactive.

### Tone sequences

Memorize the tones for:

- Pump startup
- Gate movement
- Pressure change
- Current reversal
- Vehicle approach
- General evacuation
- Loss of station atmosphere
- Basin turnover

The animals often react before human visitors recognize the signal. If a school suddenly turns away from a structure, seals leave a ledge, or local fish vanish into racks, treat that as a warning and follow your guide immediately. It does not replace the official alarm, but it may precede it.

### Dark galleries

A dark opening is not a cave. It may be:

- Flooded or dry
- Pressurized or evacuated
- Connected to an active pump
- Scheduled to close
- Full of cables or machinery
- Occupied by animals
- A pressure transition between incompatible environments

Never enter one without explicit clearance and a local guide. Do not open a sealed door merely because a handle is present.

### Floors and platforms

Never assume that sediment means solid ground. It may cover a thin suspended panel, flexible membrane, service hatch, rotating screen, or gap into a lower chamber. Do not jump onto, anchor to, or rest heavy equipment on an unverified surface.

Likewise, never attach a tether to a conduit, railing, grille, ladder, or bolt recess unless it is marked as an anchor point. A harmless-looking pipe may become hot, energized, mobile, or subject to violent vibration when a system starts.

## Environmental hazards

### Cold

Cold is a constant risk, including in supposedly habitable sectors. Water removes heat quickly, and large metal surfaces accelerate heat loss. A diver can become clumsy and irrational before recognizing serious hypothermia.

If your hands stop functioning well, your breathing pattern changes, or you begin to feel oddly unconcerned, end the dive. Do not touch exposed metal with bare skin. In deep or current-exposed work zones, active suit heating may be necessary.

### Pressure and decompression

Pressure changes are unforgiving. Never move between flooded and dry sections without confirming the lock sequence. Do not attempt to follow workers through a pressure door unless your clearance specifically includes their destination.

Report ear pain, sinus pain, unusual fatigue, joint pain, numbness, confusion, coughing, or visual disturbances immediately after a dive. Ensure your station has a functioning recompression chamber before entering the water.

Avoid dimensional travel immediately after compressed-gas diving unless the transit authority explicitly confirms that the crossing produces no pressure, gravitational, or physiological change. Treat portal transit like air travel or altitude exposure until proved otherwise.

### Current

Currents may form without surface waves and can strengthen when equipment changes output. Avoid narrow gaps, intake approaches, diffuser nozzles, trench seams, and the edges of large platforms.

Diffuser fields are generally safer than intakes, but they are not harmless. Rising water, bubbles, and turbulence can alter buoyancy, obscure instruments, and push a diver far above the planned depth. A dense bubble column may also impair acoustic communications or reduce the effectiveness of some flotation systems.

### Noise and vibration

Underwater sound can be physically exhausting. Machinery noise may be felt through the chest, jaw, and teeth, and prolonged exposure can cause headache, nausea, disorientation, or hearing injury. Use approved hearing and communications systems, and plan quiet recovery time after excursions.

Do not remove your helmet or communication gear because the sound seems muted. Water transmits vibration differently from air, and dangerous sound levels may not feel conventionally “loud.”

### Visibility and disorientation

Ceramic dust, plankton, bubbles, or rust can reduce visibility in seconds. Stay close enough to your guide or vehicle to maintain contact without depending solely on sight. In open blue water, there may be no visible surface or bottom; some people experience vertigo, panic, or a powerful sensation of falling.

If disoriented, stop moving, check instruments, establish tether or physical contact, and follow the guide’s instructions. Do not choose an apparent “up” based on light alone. Pressure lamps can create false horizons.

## Animals

The sharks are real, but machinery remains the greater danger. Do not treat established predators as attractions to be approached or fed. Their territories are often near diffuser fields and prey concentrations—places already complicated by currents and poor visibility.

General rules:

- Do not carry loose fish, bait, or food waste.
- Do not enter a large school; predators may be tracking it.
- Do not corner seals on ledges or in flooded passages.
- Keep hands out of cable racks, bolt housings, and service ducts where octopuses, crabs, or eels may shelter.
- Do not touch rays resting on warm panels.
- Avoid mussel-covered edges, which can be extremely sharp.
- Do not chase animals toward grilles or machinery.
- Do not collect shells, washers, insulation fragments, or other objects from octopus dens.

Seals may follow a vehicle or diver to exploit the light. This can be charming until they collide with equipment, steal loose objects, or attract larger animals. Secure everything.

Respect decontamination rules between ecological sectors. Different parts of the Lower Sea have developed distinct communities, and visitors can spread larvae, algae, pathogens, or treatment-resistant biofilms on suits and equipment.

## What to expect psychologically

The most difficult part may be scale.

The roof can remain visibly far above while the floor disappears below. Lights hang in the water with no apparent support. Straight conduits continue beyond sight. The constant mechanical vibration makes even quiet rooms feel active. Simulated evening may occur while you are surrounded by windows showing hundreds of meters of dark water.

Prepare for:

- Claustrophobia inside pressure hulls
- Agoraphobia or vertigo in open water
- Sleep disruption from pump vibration and artificial light cycles
- Anxiety when hearing distant impacts or bulkhead flexing
- A false sense that lit zones are shallow or safe
- Difficulty judging the size and speed of approaching vessels or animals

Spend your first day in a dry observation station before taking a submarine excursion. Let your body acclimate to the sound and the enclosed horizon. Choose quarters away from cargo locks and major pump trunks if you are a light sleeper. Earplugs alone may not stop low-frequency vibration, so request a berth in a vibration-isolated section.

## A sensible first itinerary

A safe first visit might proceed as follows:

1. **Arrive at a roof-connected dry station.** Complete medical, security, and environmental checks.
2. **Observe the upper sea through windows or a moon pool.** Learn the light cycle and alarm tones.
3. **Visit a perimeter margin.** This provides familiar visual references: gravel, waves, birds, and shallow green water.
4. **Take a crewed submarine through an illuminated midwater zone.** See fish schools, lamp clusters, kelp belts, and distant maintenance towers.
5. **Pass a diffuser field outside its restricted perimeter.** It offers the clearest sense of the basin’s engineered scale without the severe risk of an intake zone.
6. **Visit a deep observation station or descend beside a marked wall.** Painted depth numbers and structural seams reveal the true size of the sea.
7. **Watch turnover only from a rated dry facility**, if your schedule includes it.
8. **Allow a recovery day before departure**, especially after diving.

Do not make an abyssal trench, active intake, abandoned station, or dark service gallery your first excursion.

## Emergency conduct

If an alarm sounds:

- Stop sightseeing and listen to the crew.
- Secure loose equipment.
- Do not move toward the surface, a doorway, or a vehicle unless directed.
- In water, establish contact with your guide or tether.
- In a submarine, remain seated and do not obstruct access to controls or bulkheads.
- In a station, verify whether the emergency concerns atmosphere, flooding, pressure, power, or external currents; the correct refuge may differ.
- Do not use a multiversal exit device unless you know it can operate safely through water, metal hulls, pressure differentials, and local energy systems.

If separated underwater, do not swim toward the brightest light. Activate your locator, stabilize depth, and remain clear of structures and vehicle lanes. If caught in a current, follow training rather than fighting directly against it; local rescue teams will predict your movement from the active pump configuration.

## Final perspective

The Lower Sea is best visited with humility. Its resemblance to a natural ocean is genuine, but incomplete. Fish, algae, sediment, and predators have made homes there, while beneath them every current still depends on doors, turbines, conduits, and schedules.

Keep your hands off the infrastructure, obey boundaries you cannot see, and let local workers determine when water is safe. From inside a pressure-rated vessel, the place should be extraordinary: a white manufactured sky far overhead, schools of fish moving between columns of bubbles, immense fabricated mountains fading into blue, and the ceaseless vibration of an ocean whose geology is still running.
