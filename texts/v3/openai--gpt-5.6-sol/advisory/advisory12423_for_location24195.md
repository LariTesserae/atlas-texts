## Visiting the Blackwater Knot

The Blackwater Knot is not a diving site. It is a submerged, variable-scale environment whose geometry, gravity, pressure, and time respond to presence and motion. An ordinary human should not attempt to squeeze through the visible opening. The hand-wide fracture is best understood as an aperture condition, not a reliable measure of the space beyond.

Visit only with an expedition service capable of **temporary miniaturization, aperture translation, or remote embodiment**. Of these, remote embodiment is safest. If you must enter bodily, use a reversible translation method that does not depend on returning to the same physical doorway.

### Before entering

Wait for several days of stable weather above the forest. Heavy rain appears to rearrange the chamber and may cause the entrance to reconnect elsewhere. Mark both alder trees, but do not assume that markers placed inside will remain adjacent to them.

Your surface team should include:

- a portal or translation engineer;
- a closed-environment diver;
- a non-Euclidean navigation specialist;
- a temporal medic;
- a biologist familiar with low-energy aquatic ecologies;
- at least two attendants who remain outside and do not enter under any circumstances.

Record multiple return deadlines: one by surface time, one by traveler metabolism, and one by an independent instrument left just inside the threshold. Do not rely on any single clock. The Knot is slow rather than overtly accelerative, but a slow place can still retain a visitor for centuries outside.

If possible, send probes first. Expect contradictory results. A probe that reports a narrow root pocket and one that reports abyssal pressure may both be correct.

## Essential equipment

### Life support

Wear a smooth, close-fitting pressure suit with:

- fully closed-circuit breathing;
- no released exhaust bubbles;
- active heating;
- automatic pressure compensation;
- a redundant internal gas supply;
- a mouth-and-nose fallback rebreather in case the helmet is punctured;
- cut-resistant outer layers around the hands, throat, and joints.

Do not use ordinary scuba equipment. There is no surface to ascend to, buoyancy does not consistently point upward, and free bubbles may migrate into the chamber’s existing bubble system. Those bubbles appear to retain pieces of other skies or times. Adding, bursting, or merging them may have consequences beyond simple disturbance.

Carry enough life support for several times the intended visit. Movement is hazardous, and safe travel may require remaining completely still for long periods.

### Navigation and extraction

Use a **wired optical tether**, but not a conventional taut cave-diving line. A tight line may catch roots, draw the chamber inward, or persuade local weight to act along it. The tether should be:

- extremely thin and smooth;
- paid out without tension;
- resistant to fungal and chemical attack;
- fitted with frequent one-way breakaway joints;
- bright enough to find at arm’s length but not illuminating at distance;
- connected to a return anchor that operates by identity or phase lock, not by geometric direction.

Carry at least two independent recall devices. One should be attached to your body; the other should be implanted or worn beneath the suit. Neither should require access to the entrance, an unobstructed line, a fixed distance, or ordinary elapsed time.

Do not use breadcrumbs, floating lamps, weighted markers, or magnetic compasses. Objects may “fall” into walls, corridors lengthen while you wait, and local directions do not correspond to aboveground orientation.

### Sensing

Use low-intensity, close-range instruments:

- tactile proximity sensors;
- short-range structured light;
- pressure mapping;
- chemical and biological samplers with sealed intake;
- a fiber-optic camera;
- passive hydrophones, used sparingly;
- several simple mechanical gauges alongside electronic ones.

Powerful sonar, propellers, jets, and sweeping lamps are inappropriate. The chamber is crowded, quiet, and responsive. A strong pulse could cause plants to close, dislodge shale, attract the fish, or spend more of the place’s apparently finite capacity for sound.

Bring no loose equipment. Every tool should be secured flush to the suit.

## How to move

The central rule is:

**Do not struggle.**

In the Knot, movement brings surfaces closer. Panic therefore produces the exact conditions that justify panic. Thrashing through a narrowing passage may compress the chamber around you until roots, moss, and shale prevent any further motion.

Move as follows:

1. Pause immediately after entry.
2. Let your breathing and heart rate settle.
3. Determine which nearby tangle is functioning as “down.”
4. Wait for open distance to develop.
5. Advance with one slow hand movement.
6. Stop before the vegetation begins pressing inward.
7. Wait again.

Think less like a swimmer and more like an animal passing through dense sleep. A meter may require an hour. Speed should never be an expedition objective.

Do not push directly against walls. A surface that looks like soft moss may conceal shale, while apparent clay may be a boundary with a pond, leaf cup, or flooded burrow elsewhere. If trapped, become still. Do not inflate your suit, kick, pull violently on the tether, or cut vegetation unless death is otherwise immediate. The chamber often gives space back to a motionless traveler.

Because weight seeks the nearest dense tangle, maintain contact at three points when working near roots. Never release a tool to see which way it falls.

## Light, sound, and communication

Keep your lights dim, diffuse, and directed only at nearby surfaces. Brightness will not clarify long distances; the Knot seems to preserve texture while withholding scale. Strong light may also damage organisms adapted not merely to darkness, but to the absence of illumination as an organizing principle.

Communicate through the optical tether. Spoken radio may fail or acquire delays. Acoustic clicks should be reserved for emergencies. After any audible root snap, stop transmitting and remain still. The reported “quieter than before” effect may be perceptual, temporal, or a genuine depletion of acoustic transmission. In any case, testing it repeatedly would be foolish.

If you hear a dry snap directly behind you, do not turn quickly.

## Features to avoid

### The suspended bubbles

Do not touch them. Do not collect them. Do not allow them to merge against your suit.

Each bubble appears to contain a coherent daylight scene, and merged bubbles lose one of those scenes. Whether these are reflections, windows, records, or entire connected weather systems is unknown. Treat them as inhabited until proven otherwise.

If a bubble adheres to you, stop moving and allow it to migrate away naturally. Do not brush it off.

### The seven-step stair

Observe only from a distance. Do not try to reach its top or bottom, and do not circle it repeatedly. Its constant side-on aspect indicates that it is not embedded in ordinary three-dimensional orientation. Stepping onto it may impose a direction that the surrounding chamber does not share.

If you suddenly see the stair face-on, initiate immediate extraction. Either the stair has changed or you have.

### The tiny arches, rings, and niches

Do not insert tools, fingers, cameras, threads, or light beams into them. Their small apparent size is no guarantee of limited interior extent. They may be constructions, natural apertures, or entrances to constituent waters whose scale differs from yours.

Do not dismantle them to determine whether they are artificial. Lack of visible tool marks is not evidence of absent makers.

### The toothed opening

Give any circular pattern in the silt a wide berth. Do not sample the teeth and do not send a probe across its center. If it appears along your planned route, wait for the corridor to lengthen elsewhere.

If the ring begins to appear larger, do not assume you are approaching it. The surrounding silt may be approaching you.

### The blind fish

Do not pursue it. Stop when it passes.

The sequential folding of distant plants suggests that the fish affects pressure, local geometry, or biological signaling over a much larger region than its body. It may be harmless, but following it could carry you into the wider dark, where the chamber expresses deep-sea pressure.

If plants begin closing in sequence before the fish is visible, flatten yourself gently against the least dense nearby growth and wait for the sequence to pass.

### Wall-bound ponds and falling seeds

Do not touch the duckweed patch or the reflected trees behind it. That surface may be the underside of another body of water. Breaking it could drain, flood, or exchange material with an unknown world.

Likewise, do not catch the raindrops that become seeds. Do not plant the seeds if one forms near you. Their roots may establish a connection through your suit, your equipment, or your home universe.

## Biological precautions

Although the vegetation appears nearly motionless, it is alive on unfamiliar timescales. Avoid cutting roots or collecting whole organisms. A “minor” clipping may be a century of local growth.

The colorless amphipod is unlikely to attack, but do not allow it onto your suit. Its refusal to swim may indicate that leaves, not water, define its traversable world. If it walks across you, your suit may have been incorporated into that category.

All samples must be:

- sealed individually;
- held without free water exchange;
- kept away from one another;
- quarantined outside your native biosphere;
- observed across multiple rates of time.

Expect clay and fungal threads to reproduce the shapes of containers, tools, or wounds. A sample that seems inert may be changing too slowly to notice.

Do not eat, drink, or expose skin inside the Knot.

## Pressure and decompression

The chamber can acquire the pressure of a deep sea without any corresponding descent. Your suit must respond to actual pressure rather than calculated depth. A rapid extraction from such a region could still cause decompression injury even if you never moved more than a few meters from the entrance.

Your return system should transfer you first into a pressure-matched quarantine chamber. Decompress according to direct physiological readings, not expedition depth. Be prepared for delayed effects if your blood, equipment, and memories have accumulated different durations of exposure.

## When to turn back

Withdraw immediately if any of the following occurs:

- the entrance tether begins feeding from the wrong direction;
- the same marker is encountered ahead and behind;
- your suit clock and metabolic monitor diverge sharply;
- plants close in a sequence centered on you;
- shale appears in previously open water;
- a niche changes walls while being observed;
- your reflection appears in one of the sky bubbles;
- you see the stair from above or below;
- the chamber produces a second copy of an object you brought;
- you hear surface rain through your own body rather than through the water;
- open space continues increasing even after you resume motion.

The last condition may sound favorable, but it suggests that the Knot no longer recognizes your movement—or no longer locates you inside the same chamber.

## Conduct and expectations

Do not approach the Knot as a ruin to be solved. Its tiny constructions may not belong to a vanished civilization. They may be ongoing processes occurring at a rate too slow for human attention, or artifacts from puddles and burrows joined through the chamber. Leave them exactly as found.

Do not carve route marks into clay. Clay here can reform elsewhere in the shape of what was removed, making marks unreliable and potentially reproductive. Do not move the slate chips, straighten arches, clear roots, or merge bubbles for convenience.

Most importantly, accept that a successful visit may consist of entering, waiting beside the threshold, seeing one pale amphipod cross a leaf, and leaving. The Knot punishes the desire to cover ground. Its scale opens to patience and closes against insistence.

Once outside, remain in quarantine until your equipment has stopped acquiring new rootlike lines, all collected water has settled under normal gravity, and independent observers agree that you cast only one reflection. Do not discard Knot water into a sink, pond, garden, or sewer. Store it in a sealed, pressure-stable vessel with no air gap.

For several months afterward, avoid sleeping near standing water. If you begin dreaming of a seven-step stair that is always seen from the side, do not attempt to imagine either end. Contact your expedition’s topological physician and stay somewhere dry.
