## Overall recommendation

Do not visit the High Hollow casually, alone, or without a prearranged extraction route. It is habitable only because an ancient machine happens to maintain local pressure, heat, drainage, and gravity. None of those should be mistaken for natural constants.

The safest visit is a short, daylight survey of the inner terraces, timed between valve-rains and conducted with at least three people:

- one traveler moving,
- one watching the route and machinery,
- one remaining near the entry or extraction point.

If a keeper can be located, wait for one. A recently greased hinge or freshly cut vine is more valuable than any map.

## Prepare your body

The dominant stress will be vibration rather than altitude. The roar passes through floors, rails, tools, and bone. Even with excellent hearing protection, expect fatigue, nausea, disrupted balance, jaw pain, and difficulty thinking clearly.

Before traveling:

- Update tetanus and any broadly applicable respiratory or fungal precautions available to you.
- Do not go with an ear infection, balance disorder, severe asthma, heart condition, panic response to heights, or sensitivity to low-frequency vibration.
- Practice communicating entirely without speech.
- Train in industrial fall arrest, confined-space rescue, and movement on wet grating.
- Establish conservative exposure limits. For a first visit, an hour on the terraces may be enough. The Hollow’s apparent calm encourages people to remain until their judgment has degraded.

The chamber may be warm and humid, but its open margins border extremely thin air. Carry emergency oxygen and a compact positive-pressure escape hood. Do not assume acclimatization will help if the machinery abruptly loses pressure.

## Wear and carry

Use close-fitting equipment with no loose cloth, dangling straps, scarves, unsecured pockets, or exposed cords. The outer airflow can pull objects—and eventually people—through gaps.

Essential equipment includes:

- A sealed helmet with chin strap, eye protection, and particulate filtration.
- Earplugs plus high-attenuation earmuffs. These protect hearing but will not remove the bodily vibration.
- A full-body fall-arrest harness with two independent lanyards.
- Cut-resistant, chemically resistant gloves.
- High-traction boots suitable for wet grating, rust scale, oil, and ceramic dust.
- Lightweight waterproof clothing that vents heat without flapping.
- Emergency oxygen or a pressure-rated escape respirator.
- A sealed supply of water and food. Consume nothing from the chamber.
- A voltage detector, thermal imager, gas monitor, inclinometer, and compact vibration meter.
- At least two lamps, including one capable of deliberate colored signaling.
- A physical slate or luminous writing panel.
- A first-aid kit emphasizing eye irrigation, burns, punctures, fungal exposure, and suspension trauma.
- Retrieval line and hauling equipment positioned outside the active chamber.

Ordinary radios may be nearly useless. Use visual signals, tether pulls, touch codes, or contact transducers pressed against a known pipe or plate. Agree on a very small signal vocabulary before entering: stop, return, danger above, danger below, machinery moving, pressure loss, and medical emergency.

Secure every tool. A dropped object may fall through machinery, strike someone several terraces below, or be drawn outward and upward by the thermal plume.

## Never trust appearances

The Hollow rewards the wrong intuitions.

Dense foliage often indicates low heat and low disturbance, but it does **not** prove that a surface can bear weight. A lush bed may be growing over a hinged panel, a corroded grate, or the oily crown of a moving piston. Conversely, clean metal may indicate lethal heat, vibration, chemicals, electrical activity, or recent maintenance.

Before every step:

1. Inspect visually.
2. Scan for heat and voltage.
3. Probe from a stable position.
4. Verify where the load will actually transfer.
5. Maintain an independent attachment.

Do not use old yellow railings as primary anchors. Their polished surfaces show repeated contact, not structural integrity. Avoid anchoring near dense colonies of red rivet mites: their concentrations may be marking stressed fasteners or active mineral films around moving joints.

Treat every pool as contaminated and every floor panel as potentially operable. Warm tea-colored water may contain metal salts, insulation fibers, animal waste, spores, lubricants, or condensate from unknown industrial processes. Do not kneel in it or allow it inside boots and gloves.

Do not cut vines simply because they obstruct the route. Some conceal cables, preserve the remains of insulation, or may be the only visible warning that a seam is cool and undisturbed. Never cut anything under tension.

## Timing the visit

Enter in morning daylight if possible. The rectangular shadows and direct illumination make grating edges, suspended lint, moving cables, and open gaps easier to read.

Avoid:

- valve-rain,
- the period immediately before it, when overhead condensation is heavy,
- the first minutes after it, when terraces are flooded and animals are active,
- darkness,
- cloud incursions,
- any interval when counterweights or drive systems change their rhythm.

Obtain a rain schedule if one exists, but do not trust it completely. Post a watcher to monitor overhead pipes, animal behavior, and plant movement. Cistern-like leaves opening together, insects emerging from plates, or increasing droplets may provide more warning than dead gauges.

If rain begins unexpectedly, do not run across the terraces. Move to a previously surveyed, roofed position away from drains, exposed edges, opening panels, and the central void. The pale predator is reported to climb during valve-rain. Slipperiness may still be the greater immediate danger, but both should be respected.

## Wildlife conduct

Do not feed, touch, capture, or closely photograph the animals. Avoid flash illumination.

- **Rivet mites:** Do not brush away colonies. Record their patterns from a distance.
- **Glasswing roaches:** Keep helmet seals closed. Their flight paths may carry them toward warm equipment or lights.
- **Condenser frogs:** Avoid striking or tapping plumbing casually. Their signaling travels through the same medium as your contact communications.
- **Cable lemurs:** Secure food and soft gear. They may investigate insulation, straps, and exposed padding. Do not reach behind junction boxes after them.
- **Fiber knots:** Treat every woven knot as both a territorial marker and a possible maintenance marker. Do not remove or “correct” it.
- **Sailbacks:** Give upper basins and siren hollows a wide berth. A rigid wing crossing a narrow walkway could knock a person from it even without aggression.
- **The pale inhabitant of the void:** Do not attempt observation during rain. If plants begin closing in a descending spiral, withdraw laterally toward a known inner route. Do not stand above the central void to look for it, and do not follow shell arrangements.

All biological material should be considered ecologically active and potentially invasive. Take no fruit, spores, mites, feathers, water, or rust-bed samples unless you have genuine multiversal containment. Clean boots, ropes, filters, and suit seams before leaving.

## Keep away from the open sides

The outer railing is not a viewing platform.

Pressure is maintained within the chamber, while the air outside is extremely thin. Near the boundary, the outward flow can seize cloth and unbalance a person. The tower’s thermal plume may carry light objects upward rather than allowing them to fall, so familiar visual cues are unreliable.

Remain clipped to two independent points whenever approaching an outer terrace. Better still, observe the horizon from several meters inside, through a secured optical instrument. Do not lean over the rail, release test objects, or attempt to fly a drone unless it is tethered and expendable. Propellers and pressure sensors may behave unpredictably across the boundary.

If ears pop, breath becomes difficult, mist begins streaming outward, or loose fibers suddenly align toward an opening, retreat inward immediately. These may indicate a pressure change.

## Respect maintained gravity

Bring a weighted plumb instrument and inclinometer, but use them only as current references. Painted arrows, drainage channels, settled deposits, hanging chains, and plant growth may record different historical directions of “down.”

For a short visit, gravity is unlikely to change appreciably. The more practical danger is that old architecture was laid out under another inclination. A walkway that appears level may drain sideways; a hanging chain may swing toward an unexpected opening; pooled water may migrate when machinery cycles.

Never use “water flows that way” as your sole route-finding method.

## The silent circle

Do not enter the smooth black clearing at the lowest terrace.

The cancellation of physical vibration, avoidance by animals, perfect dryness, and deflection of falling water all indicate an active field rather than an acoustically fortunate spot. It may affect balance, fluid motion, instrumentation, gravity, or the coupling between the chamber and the lifting engine.

Observe it from outside its boundary. Do not place a hand, rope, probe, or drone across the edge unless loss of the equipment is acceptable. Do not jump for the ladder. The missing first meters may be a deliberate access control, or the ladder may only become reachable during a machinery cycle, gravity shift, or movement of the chamber. Any of those possibilities makes improvisation unacceptable.

If an object enters the circle, leave it.

## Suggested visit plan

1. **Establish extraction outside the chamber.**  
   Record the entry state, pressure, temperature, machinery rhythm, and position of movable structures.

2. **Survey an inner route without descending.**  
   Mark only with removable, nonfibrous indicators that cannot be confused with keeper tags or lemur knots.

3. **Choose a refuge point.**  
   It should be structurally surveyed, away from edges, drainage paths, overhead hooks, moving housings, and dense vegetation.

4. **Proceed terrace by terrace.**  
   One person moves while another watches overhead and the third maintains route awareness.

5. **Do not cross the gantry or ascend the ladder on a first visit.**  
   The apparent emptiness magnifies exposure, and rescue from the upper shaft would be exceptionally difficult.

6. **Withdraw at the first sign of fatigue or altered machine rhythm.**  
   Do not wait for obvious danger. Confusion, irritability, clumsy hand signals, repeated checking, and fascination with the void are sufficient reasons to leave.

7. **Decontaminate and inspect afterward.**  
   Check for mites, spores, cuts from rust scale, damaged filters, and insulation fibers. Document any unexplained knot, grease mark, fresh vine cut, or change in alignment marks.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect beauty, but not serenity.

The High Hollow will seem simultaneously vast and cramped: a garden no deeper than its trays, surrounded by hundreds of meters of resonant emptiness. Sunlit leaves and warm rain may suggest a greenhouse until the floor trembles through your teeth. The open wall may look peaceful until cloth begins pulling toward it. Every patch of moss will reveal an old leak; every tree will outline a wheel or conduit; every animal will behave as though metal were earth and vibration were air.

The greatest psychological hazard is forgetting that the forest and the engine are the same place. Do not treat the vegetation as wilderness or the machinery as ruin. Both are alive in different senses, both are still working, and neither was made with an ordinary human visitor in mind.
