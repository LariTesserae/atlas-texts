This basin should be approached as a living environmental system whose “weather” includes changes in distance, duration, and physical law. It does not sound overtly hostile, but it is profoundly unsafe for an unprotected human. Ordinary marshcraft is necessary and insufficient.

The central rule is:

**Do not treat the basin as scenery, and do not assume that returning along the same route will return you to the same place.**

## Before departure

Do not go alone. A minimum party is four:

- two people moving together,
- one person remaining at the entry site,
- one observer outside the basin’s influence, preferably on the far side of the transit aperture.

If no member can remain outside local reality, use an automated external monitor that records the portal and refuses to follow commands originating after unexplained timing discrepancies.

Establish several independent deadlines: traveler elapsed time, outside elapsed time, number of rain cycles, and a physiological marker such as meals or sleep periods. Local clocks may agree with each other while all being wrong. Your extraction plan should activate when **any one** deadline is reached, not when all of them are.

Before entry, record:

- full-body photographs and biometric baselines,
- pulse rhythm and resting heart rate,
- autobiographical facts spoken in your own voice,
- the exact membership of the party,
- equipment serial numbers,
- the expected appearance of the entry site,
- a short list of things that must not have changed.

The pulse record is particularly important. The deep lights reportedly imitate a watcher’s heartbeat; afterward, a traveler may have difficulty distinguishing their own rhythm from an imposed one.

Anyone with serious arrhythmia, seizure disorders, dissociative illness, panic triggered by open water, or poor cold tolerance should not visit.

## Clothing and equipment

Prepare for prolonged cold immersion even if the intended visit is brief.

Wear:

- a dry suit with integrated socks and a low-profile personal flotation device,
- layered non-cotton insulation,
- a smooth outer shell that will not snag on roots or perforated stone,
- a helmet with face protection,
- high-traction boots with broad load-distributing soles,
- waterproof gloves beneath sacrificial work gloves,
- eye protection against mineral films, fungal material, and wind-driven rain.

Carry a compact breathing supply. It is not primarily for diving; it is for surviving a sudden fall into a narrow drowned shaft where the surface may be visible but hundreds of meters away in practical terms. Do not enter the apparent “knee-deep” holes intentionally.

Each traveler should carry:

- two mechanical compasses,
- inertial navigation,
- sealed paper maps and waterproof notebooks,
- a body camera recording locally rather than transmitting continuously,
- chemical light markers in several shapes, not merely colors,
- a wide basket-ended probing pole,
- a throw line and cutting tool,
- a personal shelter and warming pack,
- several days of dense food,
- water brought from elsewhere,
- broad-spectrum filtration plus chemical testing equipment,
- a whistle, though whistles are for close-range emergencies only,
- a simple mechanical metronome,
- dosimeters and atmospheric monitors,
- sterile sample containers, if sampling is authorized,
- a nonreflective hood or cloth for covering anomalous pools.

Do not rely on GPS, radio direction-finding, echoes, reflected landmarks, or sound from teammates. Radio may still be useful, but every transmission should include a rolling challenge-response code and a clock reading. A voice arriving from the right direction—or before the speaker transmits—is not proof of identity.

Use equipment with few exposed conductive loops. The basin’s coherent mineral lattice and low-frequency resonance may induce currents, confuse sensors, or transmit vibration through metal frames. Keep implants and life-support electronics heavily shielded. If a device begins responding to touch before it is touched, power it down and bag it.

## Arrival and base camp

Arrive during the most ordinary weather available: steady daylight, light wind, no thunder, no stationary cloud patches, and no basin-wide stone resonance. “Clear weather” may not exist here, but avoid abrupt pressure changes.

Place the portal or arrival point on the driest, least vegetated rock available near the outer margin. Do not place it:

- beside a pool,
- over peat,
- on a mineral terrace,
- near vermilion fungi,
- where blue threads are visible under grass,
- beneath a stationary cloud patch,
- or on stone that answers pressure.

Before unloading, remain still for several minutes. Watch whether water, plants, shadows, or sound begin organizing around the arrival site. If droplets climb your equipment, reflections darken, or the rock produces a pressure response matching your body, withdraw before establishing camp.

The base should be lightweight and removable. Do not drill, hammer stakes, cut roots, dam water, or pour disinfectants. Even actions that seem harmless may interfere with a system operating across hundreds of square kilometers.

Build nothing permanent. The basin appears to incorporate disturbances rather than merely heal from them.

## Movement through the basin

Travel slowly enough that one person can test every step. Peat mats may conceal deep water; apparently shallow pools may be vertical passages; limestone ribs may be slick, hollow, or responsive.

Use a paired movement protocol:

1. The lead traveler probes.
2. The second traveler watches the lead traveler rather than the landscape.
3. Both state aloud what they see before crossing each new surface.
4. If their descriptions differ, neither moves.
5. The rear pair records the boundary and retreats if the disagreement persists.

Stay close enough for physical rescue but do not bind the whole party to one rope. A rope stretched across two time-rate zones could tighten without warning, age unevenly, or transmit dangerous forces. Use quick-release tethers only during known bog or water crossings, and keep a blade accessible with either hand.

Do not follow cairns, footprints, lights, or earlier versions of your own markers unless their challenge pattern matches your current expedition record. Tracks that end at unbroken stone are warnings, not trails.

Never navigate by a pool’s reflection. When checking the sky, look at the sky. When checking a teammate, look directly at them. If all pools begin showing the same image, cover nearby reflective surfaces, stop moving, and wait without staring into the water.

Avoid places where:

- rain falls upward,
- shadows point in conflicting directions,
- water chains itself into geometric beads,
- sound arrives doubled,
- vegetation forms an unusually dense line,
- the air visibly trembles,
- cloud motion stops overhead,
- or distant terrain changes when viewed twice.

These are probable zone boundaries. Cross one only for a compelling reason, and only after marking the near side with three different kinds of marker: visual, tactile, and recorded positional data. Cross together in close succession. Do not stand astride the boundary.

After crossing, immediately verify:

- party count,
- names and memories,
- pulse rates,
- equipment inventory,
- direction of rainfall,
- relative positions of distant land shelves,
- entry-site signal,
- and whether any object appears older, wetter, or repaired.

If more than one category has changed, retreat at once. If retreat does not restore the earlier conditions, do not cross repeatedly in panic. Multiple crossings may carry you farther through the basin’s range of stable states. Stop, shelter, and contact the outside observer.

## Water and biological contact

Bring all drinking water. Do not drink basin water, even after filtration. The danger is not limited to microbes or minerals; the water participates in the substrate’s state exchange. It may also contain memory-preserving molecules, adaptive membranes, or structures below the resolving power of ordinary filters.

Do not touch responsive stone with bare skin. A palm-shaped response from inside the rock may be simple accommodation, examination, communication, or the beginning of integration. There is no reason to find out casually.

Avoid contact with:

- amber blisters,
- vermilion sinkhole fungi,
- electric-blue filaments,
- clear seedlike nodules,
- oily water appearing during blooms,
- eyeless amphibious animals,
- and pale moving points in deep channels.

Do not collect living roots. The plants and stone appear mutually embedded; severing a root may be more like cutting a nerve or opening a pipe than taking a botanical specimen.

If sampling is essential, take only loose rainwater, shed vegetation, or already detached mineral dust from the outer margin. Maintain sealed containment. Never return unused samples, rinse containers into the basin, or leave flags and instruments behind.

Scents should be treated as environmental events, not evidence of nearby people, fire, food, or safe habitation. Smoke odor does not justify searching for a camp. Familiar perfume or the smell of home may be centuries-old chemistry—or a state response keyed to the observer.

## Sound discipline

The basin’s noise can disorient, entrain breathing, and disguise movement.

Wear hearing protection that reduces low-frequency exposure without isolating you completely. Bone-conducted resonance may still be felt through the jaw, sternum, and teeth. If the limestone begins producing a coordinated note:

- get off perforated slabs if safely possible,
- crouch rather than lie flat,
- loosen your jaw,
- avoid touching large rock surfaces,
- compare pulse against the mechanical metronome,
- and wait for the wave to pass.

Do not answer voices in stone. Do not answer your own voice unless the transmission contains the current challenge code. If a call returns before it is made, cancel the intended call and change the code.

Three underground concussive beats should be treated as an evacuation signal, whether or not they truly are one. The cost of caution is low; the cost of discovering their meaning may not be.

## Animals and the deep presence

Do not approach the black birds. Their orientation may provide useful warning: if all visible birds turn toward the same direction, expect a resonance wave from that direction. If they depart suddenly, move away from deep water immediately.

The sequence associated with the large channel presence is especially clear:

- reflections darken,
- reeds lean toward the water,
- birds leave,
- and water behavior changes afterward.

At the first two signs, withdraw perpendicular to the channel, preferably onto broad dry stone. Do not run along the bank, shine lights into the water, throw objects, or attempt to record from close range. Stay low and minimize vibration. The presence may sense the basin through the same substrate that carries footsteps.

If water begins flowing uphill, do not assume the direction of higher ground remains meaningful. Wait for ordinary drainage to resume before moving.

Six-footed tracks should be given a wide berth. Their maker may be absent, submerged, inside the stone, or present in a neighboring state of the basin.

## Time and reality precautions

Every traveler should periodically perform a “continuity check”:

- state your name,
- name every teammate,
- describe the last meal eaten before entry,
- read the mechanical clock,
- count ten metronome beats,
- inspect fingernails, skin, clothing wear, and food condition,
- compare memory of the route with the written log.

Do not use plant growth, cloud motion, hunger, battery drain, or daylight as a clock. Each may be locally regulated.

A hanging droplet is not an invitation to touch it. A footprint filling before the foot lifts is a serious sign that sequence has become unstable. In that case, freeze movement, shift no weight, and have the observer outside the affected patch direct the traveler backward one step at a time.

If a teammate appears to repeat a motion, speak before hearing a question, cast two incompatible shadows, or remember an event the others have not experienced, do not restrain them unless they are in immediate physical danger. Move the group away from the seam and re-evaluate. Physical struggle across inconsistent time rates could be catastrophic.

Do not attempt to “correct” anomalies by forcing them—breaking a suspended droplet, throwing stones into an altered pool, or repeatedly stepping across a seam. The basin’s irregularities are part of its equilibrium, not malfunctions awaiting human intervention.

## Duration of visit

For a first expedition, remain within direct unaided sight of the entry point and limit the planned stay to one outside hour. Because distance may become unreliable, “within sight” is more useful than a kilometer limit, though even sight is not absolute.

Leave at the first occurrence of any of the following:

- stationary cloud overhead,
- coordinated stone resonance,
- universal or impossible reflections,
- disagreement about party count,
- rising rain,
- terrain repositioning,
- black birds departing,
- unexpected aging or restoration of equipment,
- loss of the outside signal,
- or any sense that the basin has begun consistently responding to a particular traveler.

Do not wait for a second confirmation.

## Return and quarantine

Assume that anything exposed to the basin may remain active, including water in fabric, mud in boot treads, mineral dust, biological samples, and recorded data stored on adaptive media.

Return through a wet decontamination enclosure. Collect all runoff; do not discharge it into an ordinary watershed. Quarantine travelers and equipment separately. Monitor for:

- altered pulse synchronization,
- unexplained condensation,
- water moving against gravity,
- sounds arriving before their source,
- recurring mineral tones,
- identical reflections appearing in different vessels,
- accelerated moss or fungal growth,
- and memories or scents with no source.

Do not allow basin water into plumbing. Do not place samples on natural stone or soil. Keep samples physically isolated from one another; the native system exchanges states through groundwater and mineral coherence, and fragments may attempt to re-establish that network.

Review expedition records before relying on memory. If the external observer’s record differs from the travelers’ account, preserve both. Do not force a single narrative merely for convenience.

## Proper attitude

The safest posture is neither fear nor conquest, but ecological humility.

The basin may be a distributed organism, a computation, a watershed that regulates multiple realities, or something for which those categories are parochial. Its lack of buildings does not make it uninhabited. Its lack of visible tools does not make it undeveloped. Stone, water, vegetation, weather, and time may all be organs of one continuous system.

Move as if walking across the exposed sensory surface of an immense sleeping animal: lightly, briefly, and without assuming that stillness means unawareness.
