# Preparing for the Unfinished Escarpment

The Unfinished Escarpment is survivable for an ordinary human, but only if approached as an active industrial city whose geography, weather, and causality are still under revision. It is not a place for improvisational sightseeing. Its residents live safely by moving deliberately, using established routes, and trusting local procedures over personal intuition.

## Before departure

### Arrange a local sponsor and guide

Do not arrive intending to navigate independently. Hire a licensed guide—preferably a freight courier, lift worker, or municipal route-reader—before entering the city. Ordinary maps are of limited use, and conventional coordinates may become misleading once recorded.

Ask your host to provide:

- A route to your lodging expressed as landmarks, sounds, smells, and surface changes.
- The current gravity revision notices.
- The latest Hesitant River forecast.
- Locations of newly painted geological seams.
- Any registered unexplained detonations expected in the districts you will visit.
- Confirmation that your lodging has no recent gap incidents.
- An official tram and lift timetable, understood as advisory rather than exact.

Allow several extra days at both ends of the journey. Delays here are not merely administrative. A station may be temporarily absent, rain may arrive between you and a departing lift, or the route to a terminal may cease to be adjacent to itself.

### Do not record your altitude

Avoid writing down elevation figures while in or near the city. Spoken readings and written readings can place the same platform at radically different heights. This does not necessarily mean you will be physically moved, but there is no reason to invite the issue.

If a form demands altitude, let a local survey official complete that field. Do not copy the number into your own notebook afterward.

Judge altitude stress by symptoms rather than figures:

- Headache
- Confusion
- Nausea
- Breathlessness at rest
- Poor coordination
- Blue lips or fingertips

Carry a pulse oximeter, but treat the reading as a medical indicator, not a measurement of where you are. If symptoms worsen, report to a clinic rather than attempting to “descend” on your own. Downhill is not sufficiently dependable.

### Bring flexible identity documentation

Carry several forms of identification:

- Passport or transit papers
- A sealed photographic copy
- A stamped metal identity tag
- Emergency contact information
- A card describing allergies, blood type, and medications

Keep one copy on your body and another with your local host. Names are usually stable among the living, but lettering and official relationships can drift near funerary districts and spatial gaps. A photograph and physical description are useful supplements.

Do not engrave your name on local stone as a souvenir.

## What to pack

### Clothing

Dress as the residents do:

- Layered wool or synthetic insulation
- Waterproof oilskin or a dark, matte rain shell
- Gloves with good grip
- Warm hat
- Spare socks
- Traction cleats for wet metal and moss
- Weighted, ankle-supporting boots

Bright white or highly reflective clothing is unwise in enclosed spaces. Choose muted colors and non-glossy surfaces.

Attach a small bell to one ankle. It serves as a simple confirmation that each step has completed. A clear, ordinary chime is preferable to an electronic motion alarm.

Use a safety harness only where local workers recommend it. Never clip yourself to an unmarked railing, a future bridge, or any structure mounted on relocation rails.

### Lighting

Do not bring powerful flashlights, photographic floodlamps, flares, or high-lumen headlamps.

Brightness attracts gaps.

Carry instead:

- A low-output amber lamp
- A hood or filter to reduce its beam
- Spare low-intensity lamps rather than one powerful lamp
- A simple chemical marker light for emergencies, if approved by customs

Local duskglass lamps and insect lanterns are safer than imported high-intensity equipment. Rent one after arrival.

Keep phone and camera screens at minimum brightness. Do not use flash photography. If a room suddenly appears unusually clear, sharply illuminated, or unnaturally well-defined, leave it calmly without touching walls or furniture.

### Navigation and communication

Conventional GPS, compass headings, and street grids should be secondary tools at most. Bring:

- A paper notebook with matte pages
- Soft pencil rather than reflective ink
- Chalk in several muted colors
- A length of cord
- A mechanical whistle
- A small tuning fork if you know how to use one
- A voice recorder for landmark sequences

Record routes in the local style:

> Warm oil smell, riveted arch, five grated steps, dripping pipe, violet lamp, bees behind wall, red door.

Do not rely on “turn east” or “continue downhill.” If you encounter your own footprints approaching from ahead, stop and call your guide. Do not follow either set experimentally.

### Medical and environmental equipment

Bring:

- Regular prescription medication, with substantial reserve
- Basic first-aid supplies
- A particulate respirator for industrial dust
- Eye protection
- Hearing protection that can be removed quickly
- Water purification tablets or a compact filter
- Electrolytes
- Anti-slip dressings and blister care
- Medication for altitude symptoms only if prescribed beforehand

Do not wear noise-canceling headphones while walking. Important warnings include cable noises, horns, cliff-hare screams, and impacts that may precede their causes. Filter the industrial din when stationary, but preserve situational hearing in transit.

### Equipment reliability

Do not make your safety depend on a newly manufactured, rapidly assembled, or highly intricate device. Machinery given insufficient time to “become certain” can misbehave in the Escarpment. Imported mass-produced items may not fail, but critical mechanisms deserve suspicion.

For climbing, lifting, braking, and transport, use locally certified equipment that has been in service long enough to establish consistent behavior. A heavy local harness is safer than a clever new one unpacked that morning.

Avoid carrying firearms, fireworks, pressurized novelty devices, or anything explosive. Sudden events are particularly liable to become temporally misplaced.

## Arrival

Enter through an official lift, funicular, or tram terminal. Do not take an unscheduled private ascent merely for the view.

On arrival:

1. Remain at the station until your guide meets you.
2. Verify that the station is physically present; let the conductor perform the tuning test.
3. Register with the appropriate visitor office.
4. Obtain current gravity and geological notices.
5. Ask whether any sounds you heard during ascent are listed in the detonation archive.
6. Acclimatize for at least a day before visiting the rim or industrial quarter.

If practical, step outside with your guide and spend a few quiet minutes looking at the horizon. The formal horizon introduction is intended for newborns, but establishing yourself openly in relation to the city is a harmless and sensible precaution. Do not turn it into a joke or imitation ceremony.

## Moving through the city

### Walk deliberately

The Escarpment rewards patience. Do not run unless instructed by local emergency personnel. Keep one hand free and test unfamiliar surfaces before transferring your full weight.

Pay attention to:

- White-painted seams in stone
- Hinged bridges resting over apparently solid ground
- Stairs descending into walls
- Buildings mounted on rails
- Newly stretched cables
- Streets crossed by fine cracks
- Areas where rain or echoes behave out of sequence

Never step across a painted geological seam merely because the crack beneath it looks too narrow to matter. The marking describes what the terrain is becoming, not only what it currently is.

Likewise, do not camp, picnic, or park on a future bridge span. Solid ground beneath it may be temporary on a geological schedule that has recently accelerated.

### Use established transport

Trams, cage lifts, and funiculars are generally safer than shortcuts. They are operated by people accustomed to uncertain gradients and absent stations.

In a lift:

- Obey the operator immediately.
- Do not lean out to photograph the cloud.
- Do not disturb nets kept near the controls; they may be used to remove fish from condensation ponds.
- If the cage appears to stop while the shaft continues moving, remain inside.
- Do not attempt to determine whether you are ascending or descending.

Freight vehicles and machinery have priority in industrial districts. So do chimney stags.

### Learn your route in both directions

Because neighboring places may not share space symmetrically, the route home may not be the reverse of the route out. Have your guide teach each direction separately.

Never assume that a shared wall means two buildings are directly adjacent. Do not enter utility doors hoping for a shortcut.

## Gaps

Gaps are the most serious unfamiliar hazard.

They usually appear in overlit interiors as thin vertical failures in connection: not black openings, but places where nearby surfaces no longer properly relate. Warning signs include:

- Edges that cannot be visually joined
- A line that seems neither near nor far
- Objects appearing complete on both sides but discontinuous between
- Sudden, excessive visual clarity
- A sensation that part of your body is optional or unrelated to the rest

If you see one:

1. Do not stare into it.
2. Do not shine a brighter light on it.
3. Do not touch it.
4. Do not reach through for any visible object, even if you recognize it.
5. Dim nearby lamps if this can be done without approaching.
6. Back away along your exact previous path.
7. Close the room if possible and notify municipal inspectors.

If someone passes a limb through a gap and it becomes “irrelevant,” do not pull violently. Keep the person still, reduce illumination, and summon local emergency services. Ordinary anatomical assumptions may temporarily be more harmful than helpful.

Treat any long-lost object recovered from a gap as contaminated evidence, not as a gift.

## Weather and water

Carry rain protection even beneath a roof. Rain may reach the building, street, and intervening air on different days. A dry sky does not prove the absence of an ongoing storm.

Avoid collecting unusual rain without a license. “Backward-falling” precipitation is regulated for good reasons.

Do not drink from:

- The Hesitant River
- Faults or rock seams
- Condensation ponds in lift towers
- Factory gutters
- Pools occupied by bellbacks
- Roof cisterns containing suspended river fish

Use municipal water or sealed supplies. The river is bitterly cold and may disappear from the bottom upward, making wading especially dangerous. Never enter its channel once an accumulation notice has been issued, even if no current is visible.

If staying in the dry riverbed market, identify the nearest marked bank exit as soon as you arrive. Vendors dismantling stalls are your cue to leave, not an invitation to help unless asked.

In the Condenser Ward, expect constant slickness. Moss-covered walkways may conceal grates, rails, and gaps between roofs. Use handrails only after confirming they are load-bearing and not botanical growth shaped like metalwork.

## Animals

Most local creatures should be observed without interference.

### Cliff hares

Do not chase or feed them. Their scream often precedes cable failure by several minutes. If several hares begin screaming or evacuating a structure, follow the workers’ response immediately.

A hare licking condensation from a pipe does not mean the water is safe.

### Chimney stags

Yield completely. Do not touch their antlers, stand in their smoke path, or try to redirect a herd. They follow roads that may no longer be visible but may remain spatially significant.

Their dusk resonance is normal. A sudden break in the chord may indicate industrial trouble.

### Dark-winged gliders

Do not scrape stain-like forms from masonry; resting gliders may be folded into them. Keep windows screened and lamps hooded. If one enters your room, dim the light and call building staff.

Do not attempt to count its wings. The count is not useful and may not remain true.

### Bellbacks

Watch from a boardwalk or firm stone. Do not handle them. After rain, their ringing is normal and may continue after the visible weather has passed.

### Fish

Do not stand under suspended fish when the Hesitant evaporates. Children catching them are experienced and have right of way.

## Industrial districts

Wear eye, ear, and respiratory protection. Stay within marked visitor lanes. Machinery here is immense, slow, and not necessarily synchronized with its sounds.

A distant hammer blow may arrive long after or before the motion that produces it. Therefore:

- Do not investigate a noise by walking toward it.
- Do not assume silence means a machine is idle.
- Watch operators and signal lamps.
- Treat chains and cables as loaded at all times.
- Never cross a conveyor because its next component appears far away.

If you hear the distinctive sound of an object you are carrying being dropped, stop and secure it. It may be advance notice rather than an echo.

Do not remove ore, duskglass fragments, slag, or “unfinished” metal as souvenirs. Duskglass conducts gravity unusually well, and unmounted pieces should be handled only by trained personnel. Metal that still remembers being ore is not appropriate for luggage.

## Social conduct

Residents are practical and generally unlikely to be impressed by dramatic speculation about whether their city is “real,” “upside down,” or “cursed.” Avoid philosophical arguments with workers who are trying to keep a lift operational.

Good etiquette includes:

- Speak quietly.
- Wait for machinery and horns to finish sounding.
- Yield to freight crews and chimney stags.
- Respect barricades even when no present hazard is visible.
- Ask before illuminating anything.
- Do not ridicule permits for impossible structures or future terrain.
- Do not demand exact travel times.
- Do not move route markers.
- Do not touch other people’s ankle bells.
- Do not describe slow manufacturing as inefficient.

At funerary houses, do not copy names from stones, correct rearranged lettering, or ask whether a family’s kinship is “really” valid. Those matters are handled locally and often delicately.

## Food and lodging

Choose lodging near a staffed tram station rather than at the outer rim. Confirm that it has:

- Low amber illumination
- A current structural inspection
- More than one independently learned exit route
- No room directly over a murmuring seam
- Secure windows against gliders
- Municipal water
- A staffed desk at night

The Condenser Ward offers the most abundant produce and the wettest conditions. Pale orchard fruit is generally suitable if purchased from established vendors; expect an iron taste. Avoid foraged mushrooms, electrical algae, black needles, and anything growing directly from a power conduit.

Keep a modest food reserve in your room. A missing station or temporary river can make a nearby restaurant difficult to reach without making it geographically distant.

## If something goes wrong

Follow local emergency workers even when their instructions contradict your visual sense of the landscape.

- **Cable warning:** Move away from the projected line of snapback, not merely from the cable itself.
- **Detonation heard without damage:** Note the time and character of the sound and report it to the municipal archive.
- **Damage without a detonation:** Evacuate; the noise may still be forthcoming.
- **Unexpected river accumulation:** Leave the channel by the nearest marked bank route.
- **Vertical wind:** Enter a recessed doorway or designated wind shelter; do not shelter beneath asymmetrical roof edges.
- **Spatial looping:** Stop moving, lower your light, ring your ankle bell once, and call your guide.
- **Missing station:** Remain with the tram or conductor. Do not disembark onto where the platform ought to be.
- **Uncertain body relation after a gap exposure:** Stay still and summon specialists.
- **Severe altitude symptoms:** Seek a clinic. Do not self-evacuate “downhill.”
- **Rearranged identity papers:** Present your metal tag and sealed photograph to the visitor office.

## What is safe to enjoy

With a guide and sensible caution, the Escarpment can be extraordinary rather than merely hazardous. Good experiences for an ordinary traveler include:

- Watching the lower world through a clear break in the clouds
- Riding a staffed funicular through the industrial quarter
- Hearing chimney stags resonate at dusk
- Visiting the dry-bed market of the Hesitant under safe conditions
- Walking the orchard roofs of the Condenser Ward
- Listening to bellback colonies after delayed rain
- Touring a slow assembly works from a protected gallery
- Seeing hinged bridges waiting over solid stone
- Observing miners shave an arriving ore seam
- Standing on a certified outer platform beneath low, hooded light

At the rim, stay behind the barriers. The plains may appear below, overhead, or in disconnected pieces. Do not use the lowland lights to orient yourself unless a resident explains the current constellation.

Above all, resist the temptation to force the city into certainty. Do not demand a final altitude, a correct downhill, a complete map, or a firm distinction between what has happened and what is preparing to happen. The inhabitants remain safe because they allow terrain, machinery, and events enough time to settle into themselves.

Move slowly. Keep your light dim. Listen to the animals. Trust the painted seams. And if you hear something deep inside the mountain beginning to open, tell an engineer rather than going to look.
