## Overall assessment

Treat the high sea as a combination of **technical cave dive, deep-ocean expedition, unstable volcanic range, and pristine extraterrestrial biosphere**. Its warmth and stillness make it look gentle, but almost every familiar safety cue is unreliable: “down” may change, the apparent floor may open into sky, plants can entangle or constrict, stone is newly sharp, and the water’s chemistry and pressure are unknown.

An ordinary person should not visit alone or by free-swimming from a portal. Go with a trained dive team, a sealed support vehicle, and a guaranteed route back.

## Before entering

Determine, as far as instruments allow:

- Water pressure and dissolved gases at the intended depth.
- Salinity, acidity, metal content, toxins, and microbial or spore hazards.
- Whether local water can attack seals, fabrics, lenses, or electronics.
- The direction and strength of gravity along the entire planned route.
- The position of both the **upper surface** and the dangerous lower boundary.
- Whether the “mineral charge” interferes with compasses, radios, batteries, or nervous systems.
- Whether your portal or return point will remain fixed relative to the sea.

Do not assume warm water means shallow water or survivable pressure. Enter through an airlock, submersible, or pressure-controlled portal chamber. If the destination pressure is uncertain, use a rigid atmospheric suit rather than ordinary scuba.

Establish an unambiguous **home reference**. Never describe it merely as “up.” Call it something like *portalward*, assign it a color and beacon code, and make every team member carry an independent locator for it.

## Essential equipment

### Life support

Use a fully enclosed breathing system with:

- Redundant rebreathers or independent bailout cylinders.
- Full-face mask or helmet.
- Tested gas mixes appropriate to measured pressure.
- Carbon dioxide monitoring.
- Closed-loop waste containment.
- Enough reserve to wait out entanglement or navigation failure.
- A decompression plan based on actual pressure readings, not apparent depth.

Do not breathe bubbles or gases produced by the plants. Do not drink the water.

Although the sea is warm, prolonged immersion still causes heat loss, skin damage, dehydration, and fatigue. Wear an insulated, chemically resistant suit with active temperature control if possible.

### Protection

Bring:

- Puncture-resistant gloves, boots, and outer layers.
- A smooth suit profile with no loose straps for vines to catch.
- Hard protection over joints, throat, hoses, and visor.
- Chemical and biological isolation.
- Two cutting tools reachable by either hand.
- A compact powered line cutter for emergencies only.
- Eye protection against crystal splinters and mineral dust inside air spaces.

The rock is young enough to be vicious. Never put full weight on an edge that has not been tested, and do not trust crystal shelves merely because plants grow on them.

### Navigation and communication

Ordinary magnetic compasses may be useless near charged mineral ridges. Carry overlapping systems:

- Inertial navigation.
- Short-range sonar mapping.
- Pressure and gravity-vector sensors.
- Return-line reels with breakaway sections.
- Recoverable acoustic beacons.
- A physical or optical portal locator.
- Wrist displays that show *homeward* rather than only up and down.

Keep sonar and acoustic communication at the lowest useful power. Sound carries extremely well here and may travel across many valleys. A careless pulse could disturb animals, trigger sensitive plants, or mask the warning vibration of a rockslide.

Map every route in three dimensions. A passage that looks vertical may cross into a zone where loose objects settle sideways.

## How to travel

Move slowly, preferably by small submersible or atmospheric suit. Free swimming should be limited to surveyed areas near the support craft.

Maintain a generous distance from all vegetation. The forest occupies the water itself, and apparently open corridors may contain transparent threads. Do not let vines trail along your suit. Some constrict only after prolonged contact, meaning an encounter that feels harmless can become dangerous while you stop to observe or rest.

Use **breakaway tethers** rather than a single unyielding line. A fixed tether could become lethal if local gravity changes, a ribbonwood trunk flexes, or a ridge slowly sheds material. At the same time, never abandon all physical connection to home in an unmapped valley.

Do not use the vegetation as handholds. Ribbonwood may flex far more than expected, gas-filled leaves can pull upward, mineral-heavy growth can collapse downward or sideways, and apparently rigid arches may depend on living roots for support.

Avoid:

- Fresh fractures and glowing or warm seams.
- Dark columns of mineral-rich water.
- Cliff bases and narrow chasms where rockfalls concentrate.
- Pale ridges whose water tastes metallic—tasting must, in any event, be done by instruments.
- Dense bulb gardens and flowers with central openings.
- Transparent vine bridges.
- Any area where leaves abruptly change color, as this likely marks a chemical or thermal boundary.
- Valleys where suspended stones indicate weak or inconsistent gravity.

A distant cracking sound should be treated as an active geological event. Stop, hold position away from cliffs, and monitor pressure and sonar. The vibration of a slide may reach you long after the initial failure.

## The lower boundary

The cloudward underside is the greatest obvious hazard.

Do not approach it closely, regardless of how much it resembles an ordinary water surface. Beyond it is open air above clouds and mountain summits. Small drops may be recalled into the sea; a human body may not be. Even if the boundary pulls you back, the transition could damage lungs, suit seals, or blood gases.

Set a hard exclusion zone using pressure, optical, and beacon measurements. Because the underside sags and frays during warm hours, that zone must move with time. Never navigate toward the reflected cloud light on the assumption that it is an exit.

If accidental emergence through the underside is possible, carry an emergency fall system—compact parachute, autonomous return thruster, and portal beacon—but regard these as last resorts. A parachute is of little help if deployment altitude, atmosphere, winds, or the location of solid ground are unknown.

The safer entrance and exit is the upper surface or a stabilized portal, never the lower skin.

## Plants and animals

Assume every organism is both fragile and potentially hazardous.

The ecosystem acts on timescales that can conceal cause and effect. A vine may take hours or days to begin tightening. A touched membrane may release toxins only after it closes. A flower that has waited a century for prey has no reason to react quickly enough to warn you.

Do not touch, feed, chase, illuminate intensely, or collect organisms without a carefully reviewed protocol. In particular:

- Keep clear of mouthlike flowers.
- Do not pass beneath flat-bodied swimmers.
- Avoid the pale sensory threads hanging from animals.
- Do not scrape seedlike grazers from warm stone.
- Do not swim through glass-winged groups; they may be almost invisible except when crossing sunlight.
- Treat red mats, silver-edged black fronds, and sealed bladders as contact hazards until proven otherwise.

Rare animals may not fear you, but that does not imply tameness. Their stillness may be feeding behavior, dormancy, or a response too slow for you to recognize.

## Environmental ethics

This place appears never to have encountered tools, terrestrial microbes, or rapid disturbance. Its apparent lushness may represent thousands of years of growth. A boot mark, broken vine, or introduced bacterium could outlast the traveler by centuries.

Use strict planetary-protection procedures:

- Sterilize suits, vehicles, lines, and sample containers.
- Keep breathing, waste, food, and cooling systems closed.
- Release no ballast, dyes, marker tape, bubbles, microbes, or organic material.
- Use only recoverable beacons.
- Take no souvenir samples.
- Do not cut plants except to save a life.
- Avoid anchors; if station-keeping is necessary, use thrusters at low output or attach only to equipment installed in a sacrificial sterile platform.
- Decontaminate all returning personnel and equipment before entering another biosphere.

Even bright lights may alter a plant’s seasonal behavior. Use dim, broad-spectrum illumination and avoid leaving lamps trained on one organism.

## Duration and human factors

Keep the first visits short. The quiet, lack of familiar horizons, slow movement, double lighting, and uncertain gravity will be psychologically disorienting. Divers may lose their sense of body orientation or become convinced that the cloudward glow is the surface.

Schedule frequent orientation checks. Team members should confirm:

1. Location of the return point.
2. Current gravity direction.
3. Pressure and gas reserve.
4. Tether condition.
5. Time since last physical contact with vegetation.
6. Distance from the lower-boundary exclusion zone.

Do not rest while touching vines or foliage. If a team member reports jaw pressure, chest vibration, unexplained vertigo, or sideways buoyancy, leave the immediate area rather than waiting to identify the cause.

Dusk is beautiful but operationally difficult. Leaves close across valleys, recognizable openings can disappear, and lower reflected light may reverse visual depth cues. Plan to be back inside the support craft before the closing bands begin. Night travel should be reserved for emergencies.

## Recommended first visit

The safest first expedition would consist of a sealed submersible entering near the upper surface, descending only through open water, and remaining well above the tallest submerged peaks. Observe remotely, map gravity and pressure, and deploy no permanent equipment. Only after several instrumented surveys should suited travelers approach a bare ridge—and even then, they should remain within direct line of return.

The high sea rewards stillness. Do not go there expecting to conquer distance or reach a summit. Choose one overlook, watch the forests hold their impossible positions, and leave without giving the place any reason to remember you.
