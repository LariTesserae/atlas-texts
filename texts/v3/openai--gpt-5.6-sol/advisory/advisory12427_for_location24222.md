## Preparing to visit

Expect neither wilderness nor a conventional town. This is a densely inhabited wetland forest whose streets, buildings, gardens, and drainage system overlap. Everything is damp, alive, shared, and under continual repair. A visitor’s main challenge will not be extreme weather but moving through the settlement without slipping, obstructing work, contaminating water, or damaging structures that may look natural.

### Clothing and personal gear

Dress for cool, persistent moisture rather than heavy storms.

Bring:

- A breathable waterproof outer layer with a hood. Umbrellas will snag on branches and block narrow paths.
- Quick-drying wool or synthetic layers. Cotton will remain cold and wet.
- Close-fitting trousers and sleeves against midges, nettles, ticks, and leeches.
- Soft-soled, high-traction boots that tolerate mud and shallow water. Avoid aggressive cleats, which can tear woven bridges, turf roofs, and root-bound paths.
- Several pairs of dry socks, sealed individually.
- Thin work gloves for ropes, wet railings, wicker, and emergency maintenance.
- A broad-brimmed hat or head net for the marsh quarter.
- A small waterproof sitting cloth.
- A compact towel and a dry sleeping layer reserved solely for indoors.

Use waxed cloth, roll-top bags, or nested waterproof pouches for anything that must stay dry. The atmosphere will penetrate ordinary luggage even when it is not visibly raining. Protect medicines, papers, electronics, matches, and spare clothing separately.

Do not bring a large rigid suitcase. Paths are narrow, steep, hidden by vegetation, and often interrupted by ladders, low tunnels, sleeping dogs, and improvised bridges. Carry one narrow backpack and keep your hands free.

A walking staff may help on the slopes, but fit it with a broad rubber or wooden foot so it does not punch through wicker decking or soft terrace edges.

### Health precautions

Assume that biting insects, ticks, fungal skin problems, and minor cuts are more likely than dramatic animal attacks.

Carry:

- Insect repellent compatible with wetlands
- A mosquito or midge net for sleeping
- Tick-removal tools
- Antiseptic and waterproof dressings
- Foot powder or antifungal treatment
- Oral rehydration salts
- Any essential medicine in double waterproof packaging
- A compact water filter and purification tablets

Do not treat every clear stream as drinkable. Water passes through animal yards, workshops, dye vats, ponds, gardens, and washing basins. Drink only from an explicitly designated source—most likely the first covered basin at the upper spring—or water offered by a trusted host. Filtering tea-dark marsh water may remove organisms but not necessarily dyes, metals, or other workshop contamination.

Keep cuts covered. Clay, livestock waste, pond water, and splintered wicker are everywhere. Wash and dry your feet every evening, even if this feels futile.

If arriving from another universe, follow strict biological quarantine. Clean seeds and soil from boots and equipment before entry. Bring no live plants, untreated food, pets, insects, or microbial cultures. This town depends on a finely negotiated ecology; an unfamiliar fungus, beetle, or waterborne organism could do enormous damage.

### Movement and navigation

Hire or accept a local guide for at least the first day. A map will be less useful than it appears. Routes shift, divide vertically, pass through buildings, and may be usable only in one direction or at certain water levels. A lane that looks public may become a workshop platform or roof path twenty paces later.

Learn the local warning calls used at blind crossings. Call before stepping around foliage, beneath houses, or onto narrow bridges. Listen for replies, animal clappers, shoulder poles, and loads being dragged downhill.

General rules for walking:

- Give way to porters, livestock, and anyone carrying tools for water control.
- Never stand in a drainage channel, even if it resembles a convenient footpath.
- Do not sit on sluice boards, retaining walls, roof edges, woven railings, or stacked brush.
- Test uncertain surfaces with your foot, not your full weight.
- Step over sleeping dogs carefully rather than trying to move them.
- Do not grab living branches for balance without permission; they may be trained supports, fruiting limbs, or structural ties.
- Avoid terrace edges after prolonged rain. Saturated soil can shear away without much warning.
- At night, use a low, warm lantern rather than a dazzling beam. Bright light will attract insects, disturb residents, and ruin your own ability to see beyond the mist.

Choose landmarks by sound and water flow as well as sight: the central cascade, mill hammers, pigeon lofts, the upper spring, and the marsh frogs may be more dependable than paths. Remember that downhill water eventually leads toward the cleft, but following it directly may take you into ponds, workshops, or unstable banks.

### Water etiquette

Water management is the town’s central civic concern. Treat every channel as shared infrastructure.

The covered basins below the upper spring must be used in strict downstream order:

1. Drinking
2. Food washing
3. Body washing
4. Fiber soaking
5. Garden use

Never wash hands, boots, utensils, or clothing in the drinking basin. Do not dip a used bottle directly into it unless locals clearly do so; use the provided cup or spout. Soap, insect repellent, sunscreen, blood, food scraps, and wastewater must not enter clean channels.

Never alter a sluice, clear a blockage, redirect a trickle, or move a board on your own. Even an obviously clogged ditch may be diverting water deliberately. Alert the nearest resident instead.

Do not empty chamber pots, wash water, medicines, or chemical products into running water. Ask where each kind of waste belongs. The downhill household has rights over both the quantity and cleanliness of overflow, so a small mistake can become a serious dispute several terraces away.

### Rain and flooding

Rain here is often gentle, but accumulated water is powerful. Learn the flood-warning signals immediately upon arrival.

After sustained rain:

- Stay away from the central cascade, low boardwalks, pond margins, and retaining walls.
- Do not shelter beneath leaning trees or houses braced against them.
- Keep your bag packed and boots beside your bed.
- Follow residents uphill if instructed.
- Do not cross water merely because locals are doing so; they know which submerged surfaces are firm.
- If handed a basket, rope, brush bundle, or mattock during communal work, ask for a precise task and remain under supervision.

Useful assistance is likely to be appreciated, but improvisation is dangerous. Pulling away the “wrong” branch could open a bank; clearing leaves from one channel could flood another terrace. When in doubt, carry materials, relay messages, or keep animals and children away from the work.

### Lodging

Arrange lodging through a household rather than attempting to camp. Dry ground is scarce, ownership is complex, and a harmless-looking patch of moss may be a roof, reservoir cover, seed bed, or structural bank.

Ask your host:

- Which floor or platform can bear your weight
- Whether the room shifts in wind or rain
- Which openings are for cats, smoke, drainage, or ventilation
- Where wet clothes may be hung
- Whether lamps or open flames are allowed
- How to reach an uphill assembly point at night
- Which sounds indicate ordinary water movement and which require waking the household

Expect insects, animal noises, smoke, damp bedding, and some movement of the structure. A compact sleeping-bag liner and personal midge net will be useful. Do not seal vents against insects; smoke may need those openings to escape.

Inspect boots before putting them on. Shake clothing gently rather than crushing whatever is inside. Snails, beetles, moths, and occasionally frogs will enter rooms, and residents may prefer that they be moved rather than killed.

### Local conduct

This community appears to value maintenance, reciprocity, and practical attentiveness more than immaculate boundaries or permanent ownership.

Important customs include:

- Do not block running water.
- Do not pick fruit from private branches. Fruit hanging over a public path may be taken, but only from the ground—never by climbing.
- Do not disturb nests, even on a building under repair.
- Do not remove frogs or eggs from cisterns.
- Ask before cutting, tying, leaning on, or moving any living plant. Vegetation may be architecture.
- Do not assume a decaying object is abandoned. A weathered knife, rope, bridge member, or basket may still have a recognized owner, name, and intended next use.
- Ask permission before photographing or scanning homes. Settlement and foliage interpenetrate so thoroughly that you may be recording private rooms without realizing it.
- Keep out of active work areas even when no fence is present.
- Announce yourself at woven doors; their light construction does not imply casual entry.

Accept that animals will occupy human space. Complaining that dogs block bridges, chickens enter workshops, or insects gather beneath the eaves will make you seem incapable rather than discerning.

### Trade and gifts

Metal tools, waterproof fabrics, and manufactured goods may be extremely valuable, but introduce them cautiously. A high-performance alloy blade, powered pump, synthetic pesticide, or compact motor could disrupt local crafts, water rights, and labor arrangements.

Safer gifts include:

- Fine sewing needles in a protective case
- Plain, repairable hand tools made from familiar materials
- Beeswax
- Salt, if not locally abundant
- Durable cord whose composition you can explain
- Waterproof containers
- Sharpening stones
- Simple medical dressings

Avoid gifts that become toxic when burned, shed persistent fibers, cannot be repaired locally, or resemble superior versions of tools tied to status and inheritance. Do not casually replace a named old object; its continuity may matter more than its efficiency.

For payment, ask what is accepted before negotiating. Labor, transport, preserved food, fiber, metal fragments, or obligations may matter as much as coin.

### Food

Expect mushrooms, onions, greens, fish, duck or pork, eggs, berries, orchard fruit, roots, cider, and smoked or fermented foods. Eat with hosts or established vendors until you understand local sanitation.

Be cautious with:

- Raw marsh greens
- Unpeeled fruit washed downstream
- Undercooked freshwater fish
- Unfamiliar mushrooms
- Food left exposed near wasps, gulls, rats, or slaughter areas
- Watered cider or soups if the water source is uncertain

Do not leave food loose in your room. Use clay-plastered mesh or containers recommended by your host. Hanging a bag may not be sufficient; squirrels, rats, cats, and birds all exploit vertical spaces.

### Best season and timing

The driest weeks will offer easier footing and fewer drainage emergencies, but they may also be the busiest period for roof harvesting, repairs, and fodder cutting. The rainy months will show the town at its most characteristic, but travel becomes slower and flooding more likely.

Arrive in daylight, preferably before midday. Mist thickens toward evening, livestock moves inward, paths become congested, and the settlement’s visual landmarks disappear quickly.

Plan more time than distance suggests. Although the boundary can be walked in an hour, crossing town with luggage, animals, rain, and social obligations may take much longer.

### Technology and multiversal precautions

Keep advanced equipment discreet. Drones would disturb pigeons and nesting birds, vanish into the canopy, and violate privacy. Loud generators would be intolerable. Bright floodlights would attract clouds of insects. Heat-producing devices present a fire risk inside turf, bark fiber, wicker, and dry stored fodder.

Solar charging may be unreliable under cloud and canopy. Bring sealed spare power, but assume electronics will eventually suffer from condensation. Use silica gel and allow cold devices to warm inside a sealed pouch before opening them.

Do not rely on satellite navigation, remote communications, or dimensional return equipment functioning beneath dense wet vegetation. Mark a stable arrival and departure point outside the most saturated terraces, preferably on the upper rim rather than in the marsh quarter. Confirm that the site will not become a work yard, pond, or redirected channel before your return window.

### The right mindset

Do not expect the town to be quaint, peaceful, or untouched. It is noisy, crowded, labor-intensive, and highly managed. Its apparent disorder is a working arrangement among water, roots, animals, buildings, and generations of residents.

The most successful visitor will move slowly, ask before acting, tolerate dampness, and pay attention to what flows downhill. Carry less than you think you need. Keep one set of clothes dry. Learn the spring order. Never interfere with a sluice. And if you wake to find that yesterday’s path has become a pond, do not assume anyone has made a mistake: wait for the person carrying the basket, hook, rope, or sharpened stake, and ask where the town goes today.
