## Preparing for the Condensation Garden

Treat the Garden as three things at once:

1. **An active volcanic basin**
2. **A delicate, unfamiliar ecosystem**
3. **A functioning machine whose controls are distributed through weather, water, stone, and time**

The third danger is the least visible, but the first two can kill an ordinary traveler just as readily. Do not let the impossible effects distract you from heat exhaustion, toxic gas, unstable crust, altitude sickness, or a fall from the rim.

Above all, do not arrive directly inside the notch. Place any multiversal transit point on stable ground well outside the black-stone ring. A portal, translation field, or return beacon could be interpreted by the Garden as heat, weather, reflection, or an object repeatedly entering the same space. You do not want the Garden incorporating your way home into its next cycle.

---

## Before the Visit

### Confirm basic human compatibility

Test the local atmosphere before full arrival. Check:

- Oxygen partial pressure
- Carbon dioxide
- Sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide
- Carbon monoxide
- Airborne silicate and sulfur dust
- Pressure appropriate to the mountain’s apparent elevation
- Local gravity
- Background radiation
- Biological compatibility, insofar as it can be tested remotely

A filter respirator will help against particulates and some gases, but not against low oxygen or high carbon dioxide. Carry a small independent oxygen supply for emergency withdrawal.

### Acclimatize

The basin is hot, but the mountain remains high. Heat does not prevent altitude sickness. Spend at least one or two days at a comparable elevation before entering if your transit method has not already acclimatized you.

Do not confuse headache, confusion, or nausea from altitude with an anomalous perceptual effect. Either is reason to leave.

### Observe one full cycle first

Watch the Garden remotely for at least a day, preferably two. Record:

- Times of downward and upward rain
- Wind direction
- Pylon tones
- Changes in gas concentration
- Marsh level
- Movement of cracks
- Areas where mineral crust collapses
- The onset of evening vapor
- Any tracks, severed vegetation, or gliders

Use instruments outside the boundary as your reference. Instruments inside may remain internally consistent while the path, gradient, or duration around them changes.

## Recommended Expedition Structure

Never enter alone.

A cautious minimum is:

- **Two people inside the basin**
- **Two observers outside the ring**
- One additional person at the transit or evacuation point, if available

The outside team should maintain visual contact, track time independently, monitor gas levels, and control a guide line anchored beyond the Garden.

Enter and leave through the same marked point. Do not make a complete circuit of the basin merely to “map” it. Straight routes acquire curves here, and a loop may not close in the way expected.

Set a strict turnaround time several hours before dusk. The safest visit is likely from late morning to mid-afternoon, after observing the current condensation pattern. Do not plan to be inside when the pylons sound.

## Clothing and Equipment

Prepare for a cold, windy approach followed immediately by humid heat.

Wear:

- Moisture-wicking base layers
- Easily removable insulation
- A light waterproof shell
- Non-cotton trousers
- Heat-resistant, high-traction boots
- Gaiters to keep mineral slurry and insects out
- A climbing helmet
- Eye protection with anti-fog treatment
- Thin work gloves beneath heavier heat-resistant gloves
- A respirator appropriate for sulfur gases and mineral dust

Carry:

- Multi-gas detector with oxygen, H₂S, SO₂, CO, and preferably CO₂ monitoring
- Emergency oxygen
- Personal cooling packs
- Water and electrolytes in sealed containers
- First-aid and burn kit
- Climbing harness and rope
- A nonconductive ground probe
- Composite or ceramic wedges for unstable cracks
- Waterproof radio
- Body camera
- Mechanical watch and electronic watch
- Waterproof notebook and pencils
- Compass, inclinometer, and rangefinder
- Several redundant lights
- Brightly colored, removable route markers
- Sealed sample containers only if sampling is authorized
- Dry clothing stored outside the basin

Assume metal will remain wet. Waterproof all electronics and bring analog backups.

Do not bring fragile glass, open cups, loose papers, hanging straps, or anything you cannot afford to abandon.

## Moving Through the Garden

### Test every step

The floor is layered rather than solid. A dry-looking mineral plate may conceal:

- Hot runoff
- A steam pocket
- A deep angular crack
- Acidic water
- A channel capable of trapping a boot or leg

Probe ahead and step on broad basalt surfaces where possible. Do not jump between plates. Do not reach into cracks, even apparently shallow ones. Cracks open and close with temperature, and fractured stone may heal during the next rain.

If a piece of equipment falls into a fissure, abandon it unless retrieval is trivial. The Garden is not worth an arm.

### Use a guide line, but do not trust its geometry

A rope anchored outside remains useful because it provides connection, not because it proves a route is straight. Let it run freely rather than pulling it taut across pylons, plants, or the marsh. If the rope begins to curve without an obstacle, treat that as an early warning and withdraw.

Have the outside observers verify your position. Do not rely solely on survey instruments reporting that a path is straight.

### Watch the condensation state

During downward rain:

- Visibility may collapse quickly.
- Mineral surfaces become dangerously slick.
- Fresh fractures may begin to close.
- Gas can be forced out of cracks.

During upward rain:

- Exposed skin may cool abruptly.
- Water can leave open vessels.
- Moisture may lift from clothing, eyes, or wounds.
- Marsh organisms may appear.

Seal drinking water and protect your eyes and mouth. If beads begin rising from your clothing or skin, retreat rather than waiting to see how far the effect progresses.

## Things Not to Touch

### The pylons

Do not touch, lean against, tether equipment to, or pass between a pylon and an orbiting band of condensation.

They alter local relationships between heat, water, and height. Their soft surface does not make them harmless. The absence of controls probably means your body, temperature, moisture, and position would become the input.

Stay several body lengths away. If rain begins curving toward a pylon, move out of that path.

### The suspended roof

Do not use the roof as a rest shelter, laboratory, campsite, refrigerator, or medical station.

The shade interrupts duration. Anything kept there may resume its deferred aging or decay when removed. For a cut plant or cup of water this is interesting. For a human body, medication, battery, rope, food supply, or pressurized oxygen cylinder, it could be catastrophic.

Never sleep beneath it.

If you accidentally enter the shade:

1. Leave immediately by the same side.
2. Record the apparent duration on both watches.
3. Do not repeatedly cross the boundary.
4. Have the outside team observe your exit from a safe distance.
5. Submit yourself and exposed equipment to medical and temporal quarantine.

Do not carry preserved objects out merely to test what happens.

### The marsh

Do not wade, drink, wash, or retrieve objects from it.

Its clarity is not evidence of safety. The marsh retains and delays images, produces organisms during certain cycles, and may participate in a larger spatial or temporal process. Reflections cannot be used to confirm that a person or object is currently present.

Stay far enough back that a collapsing bank cannot drop you into the water. Use direct sight, voice, and physical position—not a reflection—to account for companions.

Never repeat object-drop experiments in a pool. The Garden remembers repeated gradients; an innocent series of tests may alter how later objects, or people, fall there.

### The partial structures

Do not:

- Climb the five steps
- Stand inside the polished ring
- Insert tools into the luminous sockets
- Attempt to complete incomplete-looking arrangements
- Add water, heat, plants, blood, or electrical current
- Remove components

These are not ruins awaiting activation. They appear to be parts of a system already operating.

## Plants and Animals

Assume all life is both ecological and technological.

Avoid walking through colonies even when bare stone offers a longer route. Do not cut reeds, vines, moss, or tree-ferns. Their roots and water transport may be part of the Garden’s regulation.

Mineralized fronds are not necessarily dead debris. They may store heat, stress, information, or structure. They also fracture sharply and may release fine mineral particles. Do not collect them casually.

Do not touch:

- Droplet-carrying beetles
- Fish appearing during upward rain
- Gliders beneath fern fronds
- Clear sealant on severed trunks

If a larger inhabitant appears, give it right of way. Do not approach, signal, offer food, imitate its movements, or follow it toward the stair or marsh.

Paired tracks ending at the water are not a trail to investigate.

## Dusk and the Pylon Tones

Plan to be outside the boundary before evening vapor reaches knee height.

The tones are felt through the body, so ordinary hearing protection may provide little benefit. Their sequence marks an increase in system-wide activity:

- The first corresponds with condensation appearing in empty air.
- The second draws water from the cliffs.
- The third stills the marsh and changes its reflections.

If you hear the first tone while still inside, terminate all work and withdraw immediately. Do not stop for samples or photography.

If the third tone sounds before you are out:

- Stay with your partner.
- Move away from the marsh and pylons.
- Follow the guide line rather than apparent straightness.
- Ignore reflected movements that do not match direct observation.
- Do not investigate newly visible structures.
- Do not step into footprints that were not present on entry.

The future shown in the marsh may be accurate, conditional, manipulative, or merely stored. Treat it as information with no authority.

## The Doorway

If the unsupported doorway appears in the marsh’s reflection, the visit is over.

Do not:

- Approach the pool
- Wait to see whether the doorway becomes physical
- Wave at the reflection
- Throw anything through it
- Photograph it from progressively closer positions
- Count on its absence by morning
- Follow the five wet steps
- Enter any matching doorway elsewhere in the basin

Withdraw beyond the black-stone ring and remain under observation until sunrise. Keep the transit mechanism inactive unless evacuation is necessary; opening another cross-world threshold nearby may create a correspondence between the two.

At sunrise, document any wet impressions from a distance. Do not stand in them. Do not follow them back to the stair.

A doorway that appears only in a prediction but leaves physical steps during the night is not safely classified as illusion, portal, memory, or invitation.

## Sampling and Contamination

The best sampling policy is no sampling.

If collection is essential:

- Take only loose material already detached.
- Collect outside active plant colonies.
- Use single-purpose tools.
- Never sample from beneath the roof.
- Never take marsh water.
- Never remove organisms or mineralized fronds.
- Triple-seal each sample.
- Store samples separately from the return mechanism.
- Quarantine them after transit.

Nothing should be returned to the Garden after laboratory examination. Reintroduction could complete a cycle or provide the system with foreign biological or technological information.

Clean boots and equipment before leaving the mountain, but do so outside the ring. Treat runoff from decontamination as hazardous.

## Abort Conditions

Leave immediately if any of the following occurs:

- Gas alarm
- Sudden headache, confusion, chest pressure, or nausea
- Loss of radio contact
- Guide line behaving unpredictably
- Unexplained time disagreement greater than a minute
- Upward water forming on skin or inside sealed equipment
- A crack opening between team members
- A companion’s shadow or reflection becoming delayed
- Appearance of fresh paired tracks
- Movement near the stair
- First pylon tone
- Any visible doorway
- Any team member feeling that the Garden has become familiar

That last condition matters. The Garden’s greatest psychological danger is not terror but gradual accommodation. Its effects are quiet, consistent, and almost reasonable. After an hour, you may begin treating delayed shadows and uphill water as ordinary environmental features. That is when improvisation starts.

Use written checklists. Obey the turnaround time even when everything seems calm.

## What the Visit Will Feel Like

Expect discomfort rather than spectacle: sweat under waterproof clothing, cold droplets on overheated skin, fogged lenses, sulfur at the edge of smell, and the unnerving softness of rain that ought to be louder.

The quiet will make small things seem significant. A click under the crust may be cooling basalt or a channel opening beneath your feet. A flash in a dead frond may be harmless stress fracture or system activity. The pylons will not look more dramatic when they become dangerous.

The place is likely to feel observant without showing any obvious observer. Resist the urge to answer that feeling with experiments.

Visit as you would enter a working laboratory whose purpose you do not understand and whose equipment includes the floor, the weather, and your own passage through time. Move lightly, change nothing, and leave before the Garden begins making tomorrow.
