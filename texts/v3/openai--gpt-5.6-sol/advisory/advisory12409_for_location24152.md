## Preparing to Visit the Bell Chamber

The Bell Chamber is not a place for ordinary scuba equipment. Treat it as a confined, high-pressure industrial torrent inside a structure that is alive only in the geological sense—and may rearrange itself while you are inside it.

Your priorities are, in order:

1. **Remain isolated from the surrounding pressure and water chemistry.**
2. **Never become untethered.**
3. **Do not enter an opening merely because it was open when you arrived.**
4. **Follow the inhabitants immediately when they react to pressure changes or passing shadows.**

### Do not arrive directly in the chamber

If multiversal transit allows you to choose an arrival point, establish a staging habitat outside the mountain or in a known stable chamber nearby. Materializing in the Bell Chamber’s central water would be exceptionally dangerous: there is no reliably still region, and you could be carried into an outflow throat before orienting yourself.

Send instruments first. Measure:

- Absolute water pressure and pressure fluctuations
- Temperature at each inlet
- Salinity, acidity, dissolved gases, and toxic metals
- Current direction through a full cycle
- The location and diameter of every visible outflow
- Recent mineral growth
- Structural vibration and acoustic peaks
- Local day, storm, and pressure-change patterns

A map more than a few days old should be considered historical rather than operational.

## Essential equipment

### A pressure-resistant atmospheric suit

Use a compact one-atmosphere diving suit or similarly rigid pressure shell. It should be streamlined, mechanically simple, and capable of protecting its occupant even if external pressure changes abruptly.

Ambient-pressure diving is a poor choice. Even if the water is shallow enough for unusual local divers, unknown dissolved gases, extreme noise, cold, and decompression requirements make it unsuitable for an ordinary traveler. If the chamber is as deep as its setting suggests, conventional scuba would be fatal.

The suit should have:

- Joint locks to prevent shoulders and elbows being wrenched backward
- Smooth external surfaces with no dangling hoses
- Protected viewports and cameras
- Independent life support with generous reserve
- Carbon-dioxide monitoring
- Thermal protection
- Automatic posture stabilization
- A retractable gripping hook designed for existing guide-lines
- A powered emergency line cutter accessible to either hand
- External pressure, vibration, and current sensors
- Internal haptic alarms rather than audio-only warnings

Thrusters may help with orientation, but do not plan to overpower the chamber. Strong thrust could damage young mineral curtains, blind everyone with grit, or push you directly into a countercurrent. Movement here is accomplished by attachment and timing, not free swimming.

### Redundant tethers

Use two independently anchored lines:

- A primary load-bearing tether
- A shorter safety line attached to a separate point

Both should be cut-resistant, low-profile, and fitted with quick releases that cannot open accidentally. Avoid loose loops. A drifting loop can catch on a limestone rib, a native platform, or another person and become a noose under current load.

Do not assume that an anchor embedded in stone is permanent. Soft calcite may shear away, while apparently solid handholds may have become hollow beneath new deposits. Whenever possible, use the inhabitants’ tested routes and anchor practices.

### Communication

Speech will be nearly useless. Standard acoustic radios may also be overwhelmed by the chamber’s roar and could interfere with local signaling.

Prepare three systems:

1. **Wired communication through your safety line**
2. **Haptic signals in gloves, wrists, and torso**
3. **A simple tactile vocabulary shared with your hosts**

Learn a few signals before entry:

- Stop
- Hold tight
- Move now
- Current changing
- Passage closing
- Line caught
- Injury
- Retreat
- Extinguish light
- Do not touch
- Follow me

Agree on emergency tugs as well. Keep them distinct from ordinary line movement; the current will tug constantly.

The inhabitants communicate through touch because touch remains legible amid the noise. Accept that this may feel unusually intimate. A grip on the wrist or pressure against your palm is practical speech, not aggression.

### Light

Bring enclosed, cool, diffuse lamps with instant blackout shutters. Avoid hot lights, chemical flares, or anything that could alter mineral precipitation. Your lamps should be usable at low intensity because the chamber already has daylight and glare from glowing stone.

If the inhabitants extinguish their lamps, do the same immediately—even if you can still see. Their response may be intended to hide motion, reflections, heat, electrical emissions, or some other sign not obvious to you.

## Timing the visit

Ask your hosts to choose the entry period. Ideal conditions are likely to include:

- Stable weather at the ocean surface
- No recent large pressure shift
- Predictable current cycles
- No fresh structural ringing
- At least two known exits
- Recent replies from neighboring resonant stones

Do not enter immediately after a summit-wide change in pitch. That sound indicates that passages, loads, or pressure boundaries have shifted. It may precede a rupture or sudden closure.

Likewise, a lull is not automatically safe. Reduced current can allow accelerated mineral deposition, release creatures from sheltered fissures, or signal that water is being diverted through a newly opened route.

## How to move inside

Expect your instincts to mislead you. “Up,” “down,” and “across” are less useful than:

- Toward the wall
- Along the line
- Behind the rib
- With the next phase of flow
- Away from the throat

Keep at least three points of security whenever possible: two hands and a tether, or a hand, a foot brace, and a tether. Do not launch yourself across the luminous center. Wait for the inhabitants’ signal and let the correct current phase carry part of your weight.

Never fight sustained flow with your arms. A rigid suit may protect your bones but not necessarily its joints or anchors. If a current pins you to a wall, do not push away immediately; doing so may place you in faster water. Signal, secure a second line, and move laterally toward the edge of the jet.

Do not follow a dropped object. Loose equipment is expendable. A person swept after it may not be.

Stay clear of:

- White inlet jets
- Dark or sharply defined holes
- Milky water carrying fresh mineral precipitate
- Flexing wall sections
- Silica curtains
- Ribs that vibrate visibly
- Vortices containing suspended grit
- Any passage whose edges are shedding flakes

A narrow passage may close around a line or suit surprisingly quickly. Assign one member of the party to watch the route behind you rather than the chamber ahead.

## Noise and vibration

Even through a pressure suit, the chamber will be physically loud. You may feel major tones through your feet, chest, jaw, and teeth. Prolonged exposure can cause headache, nausea, confusion, panic, or a false sense that the suit itself is failing.

Use:

- Hearing protection inside the suit
- Vibration-damping contact pads
- Exposure limits
- Haptic rather than acoustic alarms
- A prearranged retreat signal for disorientation

Do not strike the walls experimentally. The chamber’s resonances are both a communication system and a structural phenomenon. A tone that seems harmless locally may travel through the mountain, alarm another settlement, attract wildlife, or be mistaken for news of a newly opened route.

The caged resonant stone is essential infrastructure. Do not touch or sound it without explicit permission.

## Respecting the inhabitants

Bring little, and attach everything. The chamber’s platforms are not benches in the ordinary sense; they are precious footholds in a room trying to remove them.

Suitable gifts might include:

- Chemically inert cordage
- Compact corrosion-resistant clips
- Bone-like or ceramic wedges
- Waterproof tactile markers
- Sealed medical supplies
- Flexible line that remains usable under mineral crust
- Small pressure and vibration sensors, if the inhabitants want them

Avoid gifting powered machinery without understanding its consequences. A pump, drill, or powerful thruster could redirect flow and destroy the equilibrium that keeps the chamber hollow.

Do not carve names or leave markers in the stone. Such marks will disappear, but the damage may alter crystal growth or weaken a thin wall. Do not collect the red-tipped streamers, shell-creatures, fresh mineral curtains, or sealed bubbles. Life is scarce here, and even “common” crust may be part of a route marker or structural warning.

Ask before touching polished handholds. Their polish may indicate heavy use, but some may be close to being swallowed by calcite and reserved for emergencies.

## Biological and chemical precautions

The chamber appears sparsely inhabited, not sterile. Keep your body and breath isolated from the water. Decontaminate the suit before entry and again after exit.

Particular concerns include:

- Mineral-rich water clogging seals and joints
- Crystal growth on fabric, ropes, and valves
- Unknown microorganisms in sheltered ridges
- Heavy-metal or silica exposure during maintenance
- Translucent animals entering suit recesses
- Abrasive grit damaging moving parts

Inspect joints after every excursion. Equipment left in the chamber may become rigid, embedded, or structurally altered within days or weeks. Do not trust a line merely because it is still visible.

## Emergency rules

### If the chamber changes pitch

Freeze against the nearest tested support. Secure both tethers. Do not enter or leave a narrow passage until the first major pressure movements have passed and the inhabitants signal.

A simultaneous pitch shift across all openings means the chamber’s geometry or loading has changed. Your previous current map is no longer valid.

### If a passage begins closing

Do not attempt to chip it wider unless entrapment is immediate and no alternative exists. Cutting may accelerate fracture or release a high-pressure jet. Withdraw while there is still clearance, even if equipment must be abandoned.

### If you are swept free

Curl your limbs close to reduce joint injury, protect your viewport, and deploy the tether brake gradually. A sudden hard stop can injure you or tear out the anchor. Do not fire thrusters blindly; first determine which openings are inlets and which are outflows.

### If a large silhouette appears

Follow the inhabitants:

- Flatten against the ribs
- Stop unnecessary movement
- Black out lamps
- Shut down active sonar and nonessential transmitters
- Do not cross the open center
- Do not approach the outer wall for a better view

Whether the creature is predator, grazer, or merely enormous is irrelevant. The local inhabitants have survived by treating it seriously.

### If the outer wall ruptures

This is the worst credible event. Expect violent equalization, fragments of young stone, and the loss of all familiar currents. Your only useful preparation is a short route to a stable inner chamber or a properly positioned extraction system. Do not rely on swimming against the breach.

## Psychological preparation

The Bell Chamber will probably feel less like visiting a room and more like clinging inside a musical instrument during its construction.

There will be light but little visual certainty. The walls will seem solid until they flex. The water will seem empty until grit reveals the speed of its motion. Your body may insist you are falling while the tether holds you sideways. The unending vibration can make thoughts repetitive and rhythmic. Minutes may feel much longer because every movement must wait for the chamber.

Claustrophobia and agoraphobia can occur at once: you are enclosed in a small geological shell, yet the open center is broad, exposed, and nearly impossible to cross safely.

The most important mental adjustment is to abandon the idea of permanence. The route you enter by is not “the entrance.” A platform is not necessarily a floor. A wall is not necessarily stable. A quiet pool is not necessarily safe. The inhabitants do not live in a completed structure; they negotiate continuously with a hollow that exists only because opposing forces have not yet resolved themselves.

Visit briefly. Carry nothing you cannot abandon. Learn the touch signals. Trust the people who know the currents.

And when the entire mountain begins to ring, stop sightseeing.
