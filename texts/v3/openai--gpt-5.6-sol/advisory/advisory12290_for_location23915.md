## Overall assessment

This basin is physically survivable but metaphysically hazardous. Its chief danger is not violence, weather, or wildlife; it is **enforced balance**. The city tolerates activity that confirms its existing state and gently reverses activity that would create lasting surplus, expansion, or novelty.

Treat the visit as an expedition into a living conservation law. Go quietly, stay briefly, and leave with the same people and equipment you brought.

## Before arrival

### Arrange permission and a local sponsor

Do not arrive unannounced if that can be avoided. Ask for:

- A household or civic office willing to count you as a guest.
- Explicit rules about carrying goods into and out of the basin.
- Guidance on whether visitors affect population balance.
- Permission before collecting water, plants, stones, images, measurements, or stories intended for publication.
- A guide for the summit, should access be granted.

The inhabitants may not call the basin supernatural. Avoid describing it as frozen, cursed, stagnant, or imprisoned. To them, uncontrolled change is the disturbing condition.

### Establish an external return system

Local environmental cycles are exact repetitions and cannot reliably measure elapsed time. Do not schedule extraction by “the third rain” or “when the flock crosses,” since these events may tell you sequence without proving duration.

Use several independent safeguards:

- An externally controlled extraction window.
- A portal beacon or tether maintained outside the basin.
- Two personal timepieces using different mechanisms.
- A written sleep-and-meal log kept on your body.
- A companion who independently records events.
- Predetermined recall criteria based on your own physiology and supplies.

Assume clocks may continue normally while the surroundings do not—or may display stable readings that conceal temporal interference. A short planned visit is much safer than an open-ended stay.

Do not visit alone.

### Medical preparation

The basin is high, cool, damp, and probably low in oxygen. Acclimatize beforehand if possible. Carry:

- Layered non-cotton clothing.
- Waterproof outerwear and gloves.
- Boots with excellent grip and puncture-resistant soles.
- Altitude medication if medically appropriate.
- Water purification equipment.
- A compact trauma kit and ordinary medicines.
- Several days of sealed food.

The river may be clear, but clarity is not sterility. Persistent ecological stability could also mean that unfamiliar microbes remain unusually persistent. Filter and disinfect all drinking water. Avoid raw fish, translucent fruit, and local dairy until a host confirms their safety.

Keep wounds scrupulously clean. The sharp scree and freshly fractured rock can cut deeply, and one should not assume healing, infection, or scar formation behaves normally here.

## What to bring—and what not to bring

Bring durable, quiet, personally necessary equipment. Favor felt, cloth, wood, and matte finishes over rattling metal or flashing lights.

Useful items include:

- Soft-soled camp shoes for inhabited districts.
- A slate or writing pad for silent communication.
- Simple pictogram cards.
- A small polished signal board, if locals approve.
- Headlamps with red or low settings.
- A rope and climbing helmet for mountain travel.
- Ear protection for accidental echoes—not because the city is loud, but because a close shout may return unexpectedly from several directions.
- Sealable containers for every scrap of waste.

Do **not** bring:

- Seeds, spores, live plants, soil, insects, pets, or livestock.
- Self-replicating machinery or autonomous drones.
- Construction materials intended to remain.
- Loudspeakers, sirens, fireworks, firearms, or noisy engines.
- Large quantities of tools or manufactured goods.
- Gifts presented as technological “improvements.”
- Anything you are unwilling or unable to carry back out.

Imports may count as excess. A new knife, boat fitting, or length of rope could conceivably cause an older local object to separate or disappear from use. Arrange all gifts as supervised, one-for-one exchanges rather than donations.

## Conduct in the city

### Be quiet

Sound carries exceptionally well. Speak below normal conversational volume and imitate local practice.

Never shout toward the eastern ridge. An echo there may recur for hours and could be mistaken for a person calling, a civic signal, or an emergency. Learn the local hand signs and the conventions of polished signaling boards before traveling independently.

If separated from your group, use:

1. Visual signals.
2. Prearranged rope tugs.
3. Quiet, brief calls aimed toward the ground or a nearby wall.

Do not test the echoes for amusement.

### Respect balance customs

Ask before moving anything, even if it appears discarded. Nothing is truly abandoned merely because it has lain untouched among lichen.

In particular:

- Do not add stones to walls, cairns, bridges, or channels.
- Do not straighten cracked roof clay.
- Do not prune plants.
- Do not clear moss.
- Do not release trapped animals.
- Do not deepen a stream or redirect condensation.
- Do not repair a boat, gate, or tool without being invited.
- Do not take “only one” rock, flower, feather, fish, or seed.

Local labor is often ceremonial confirmation rather than improvement. Offering to make a process faster may be both rude and dangerous. A more efficient sluice, stronger bridge, or higher-yield crop could be interpreted by the basin as an imbalance to be corrected.

If invited to help, reproduce the demonstrated action exactly. Count baskets, stones, branches, and strokes as the locals do.

### Trade cautiously

Use local markets through a host. Prices expressed in knots may encode more than quantity: provenance, replacement, obligation, and the object that must leave circulation in return.

Keep a complete inventory. Recount it before departure, but do not panic if an imported object comes apart cleanly. That may be the basin’s correction rather than sabotage. Do not replace the loss by taking a local equivalent.

Carry all refuse out. Even food scraps, shed fibers, batteries, bandages, and human waste may represent unauthorized additions. Follow local sanitation rules; do not assume burial is acceptable.

### Avoid sensitive social errors

People’s appearance may reveal very little about age. Ask about kinship or civic role, not “how old” someone is. Do not challenge genealogies because a grandparent appears no older than a grandchild.

Birth and death are matters of exact balance. Avoid casual questions about fertility, infant mortality, or population control. Travelers should not attempt conception in the basin. Someone considering long residence should first determine whether their presence must eventually be “answered” by a departure, birth, or death.

Do not promise to cure aging, restore growth, or “free” the city from stillness. The inhabitants may hear such promises as threats.

## Moving through the landscape

The mountains look stable, and no stones are heard falling, but treat them as real young mountains. The absence of observed rockfall does not guarantee that an outsider cannot trigger one—or become the event the landscape has been withholding.

Stay on established routes. The dangers include:

- Sharp, interlocking scree.
- Clean cliff fractures with poor handholds.
- Wet condensation beneath shaded ledges.
- Sudden cold after the daily rain.
- Altitude illness.
- Becoming disoriented because every day’s cloud, shadows, and animal movements repeat.

Use maps and physical route markers approved by your guide. Do not leave your own cairns, paint, tape, or scratches. Navigation systems may be preferable, but compare them against surveyed landmarks in case coordinates repeat or fail to register movement.

The river is shallow but should not be treated casually. Its fixed ripples could conceal holes, slick basalt, or persistent currents. Cross at bridges or recognized fords. Do not disturb the fish holding beneath bridge shadows.

## The summit seed

The red seed is the most dangerous object in the basin, even if it does nothing visibly.

Do not:

- Touch it.
- Water or dry it.
- Warm it.
- Shade it.
- Move the frost around it.
- Sample its surface.
- Introduce pollen, soil, blood, breath condensate, or microbes.
- Place instruments in the split.
- Attempt to make it sprout.
- Attempt to destroy it.

If granted permission to observe it, use the same route, stance, tools, and measurement practices as local scholars. Prefer remote visual observation. Do not lean over the split with loose equipment.

The seed may be the basin’s sole unrealized change, the mechanism maintaining the stasis, or the event for which the entire system is reserving capacity. An ordinary traveler is not qualified to experiment on it.

If the seed changes in any way—swells, cracks, dries, shifts, loses its gloss, or produces a root—withdraw without touching anything and notify local scholars. Begin evacuation. Do not remain to document the outcome at close range.

## Warning signs that should end the visit

Leave immediately if:

- Your clocks disagree by more than their expected error.
- Written notes revert, vanish, or repeat without your remembering writing them.
- Supplies replenish, fail to diminish, or disappear to maintain a fixed quantity.
- A wound remains unchanged for an implausible period.
- You repeatedly experience the same conversation.
- A construction, footprint, or mark you made is reset overnight.
- Local genealogists begin discussing where you fit into a household line.
- An inhabitant dies or a birth begins soon after your arrival.
- One of your party is treated as though they have “always” lived there.
- The river’s ripples change.
- The northern cloud fails to take its customary shape.
- The summit seed changes.

If the basin starts incorporating you into its balance, departure may become increasingly difficult. Do not wait for certainty.

## Best style of visit

The safest visit is modest: arrive under sponsorship, lodge in an established guest space, observe the markets and waterways, accompany residents in ordinary tasks, and depart after a small, externally measured number of sleep periods.

You will probably find the city beautiful rather than overtly frightening. Its discomfort emerges slowly: fruit that never spoils, rain that adds no water, old walls that possess history without wear, and people for whom transformation is more alarming than permanence. Resist the urge to test it. In this place, curiosity expressed through alteration is the principal hazard.

Go as a witness, not an inventor—and make certain that, when you leave, the basin has no reason to keep you.
