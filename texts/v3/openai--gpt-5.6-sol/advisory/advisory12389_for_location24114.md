The Rain House should be approached not as a ruin, cave, or picturesque underground market, but as a working piece of municipal machinery. Nearly every surface is load-bearing, water-bearing, inhabited, cultivated, repaired, or claimed. A careless visitor can contaminate a cistern, startle a pigeon wall, obstruct a flood crew, or damage something that has survived only because generations knew where not to put a hand.

## Before descending

Arrange a local sponsor and guide—ideally someone involved with drainage, plaster work, cistern keeping, or animal management. Do not rely solely on a merchant who knows only the market courts. The Rain House changes with repairs and water levels; maps will become inaccurate, and passages that look public may end at a work site, animal pen, or structurally sensitive wall.

Ask your guide, before entering:

- Which channels are currently “live.”
- Where the nearest high flood platforms are.
- What calls or knocks announce incoming rain.
- Which cisterns contain drinking water.
- Whether any galleries are closed because of bats, bad air, repairs, or unstable plaster.
- Where lamps are permitted.
- Where bodily waste is disposed of.
- What route remains usable if the main stair floods.

Choose a dry, settled day for your first visit. “No rain here” is not sufficient; rain falling elsewhere on the drainage catchment may reach the chamber. Avoid arriving during the first storm after a long dry period. That first runoff will carry roof dust, dung, dead insects, and accumulated refuse, and everyone below will be busy managing it.

If multiversal biological compatibility is uncertain, use whatever quarantine and screening your point of origin provides. The Rain House combines people, livestock, rodents, bats, birds, standing water, compost, insects, and poorly ventilated warmth. It is an excellent place for organisms from different worlds to meet.

For an Earthlike human, sensible medical preparation includes current tetanus, hepatitis, typhoid, and rabies advice, plus region-appropriate precautions for waterborne and insect-borne illness. Bat or dog bites, rat scratches, and floodwater in an open wound should all be treated seriously.

## What to wear

Expect persistent warmth rather than extreme heat. The greater problem is that sweat will not evaporate well.

Wear:

- Lightweight, loose, washable clothing that covers the legs and shoulders.
- Grippy, closed ankle boots with firm toes. Smooth-soled sandals are unsuitable on wet lime, brick, mud, and worn stairs.
- Thin gaiters if snakes, leeches, or biting insects are locally significant.
- A close-fitting padded cap or low-profile climbing helmet. High ceilings alternate unexpectedly with low vaults, projections, ropes, and stairs.
- Work gloves you can remove quickly. Gloves help with rope and rough brick, but should not touch drinking-water equipment.
- A scarf or particulate mask for dry dust, lime scraping, guano, and mold. Do not wear respiratory protection so heavy that it becomes dangerous in the heat.

Avoid long cloaks, dangling straps, wide baskets, and rigid framed packs. In crowded pillar passages, anything projecting from your body will catch on pegs, pottery, people, or animals. Carry one soft, narrow bag, preferably one that leaves both hands free.

Keep cuts covered with waterproof dressings. Wash any wound promptly; channels and floors contain animal waste even when they look dry.

## Essential equipment

Bring only what you can carry through a passage while turning sideways.

Useful items include:

- Drinking water known to be safe, in a securely closed vessel.
- Oral rehydration salts or an equivalent electrolyte mixture.
- A small waterproof lamp with a warm, non-flashing beam.
- A backup light kept sealed.
- A compact first-aid kit.
- Water purification equipment, though local drinking water should still be taken only with permission.
- A whistle for emergencies **only if local guides approve its use and explain where it may be used**. Never whistle near pigeon walls.
- Cord for personal repairs, but not for marking routes or tying to architecture.
- A sealed notebook or wax tablet; ordinary paper will soften.
- A cloth for wiping condensation from hands and equipment.
- A small insect net for sleeping, if you will remain overnight.
- A discreet carbon-monoxide or air-quality monitor, if available and culturally acceptable. Lamp smoke, ovens, blocked ducts, and animal occupation can create local pockets of poor air.

Do not carry a large open flame. If you use a local oil lamp, have a resident show you where it may be placed. Never put it beneath a bat throat, ventilation duct, reed screen, painted ceiling, or hanging mineral deposit.

Do not bring loose seeds, untreated soil, fresh fruit with viable pits, or dirty boots from another ecosystem. The Rain House’s planting beds are small and interconnected with its waste streams. An invasive fungus, beetle, or weed would be difficult to remove. Clean and dry your equipment before entry and again before returning home.

## Conduct inside

Move at the pace of the person in front of you. Let loaded workers, water carriers, animals, and repair crews pass. Where pillars are close, turn sideways and stop rather than forcing another person against the wall.

Observe the local rules literally:

- **Never step across a live water channel.** Use the proper bridge, crossing stone, or alternate route. This is both etiquette and safety: the channel may deepen suddenly, carry drinking water, or be opened without warning.
- **Do not whistle near pigeon walls.**
- **Do not collect first-rain water.**
- **Do not move cracked jars.** Repairing one may create a claim of ownership, but a jar holding water remains protected by a stronger rule. Let your guide handle any breakage.
- **Do not make, widen, probe, or “improve” an opening.** The rule that every opening requires a closure is part structural practice and part law. A visitor has no standing to decide what balances what.

More generally:

- Do not lean against walls, cistern shells, parapets, gates, or plastered “boulders.”
- Do not pull reeds, wooden pegs, bones, pottery sherds, or mineral crusts from cracks. They may be repairs.
- Do not tap hollow walls to investigate what lies beyond.
- Do not scrape pigment or touch painted ceilings. Condensation may have left the surface barely attached.
- Do not sit on sluice boards, rope systems, jar racks, tank rims, or channel covers.
- Do not block stairs, shafts, air passages, or the path between a cistern and its jars.
- Do not feed dogs, goats, tortoises, pigeons, or frogs without permission.
- Do not photograph with flashes around birds, bats, animals, or working crews. Sudden light can cause panic in exactly the places where falling plaster is a concern.

Treat calcite curtains and other “natural” formations as architecture. They may preserve the outline of a gutter, reed mat, peg, or seam still performing a function.

## Water and food

Never assume that clear water is potable. A beautiful oval tank may also serve animals, washing, irrigation, cooling, firefighting, or sediment settling. Floating straw may be deliberate, incidental, or filthy; you cannot infer its purpose by appearance.

Drink only from a source identified by your host. Do not dip your own vessel directly into a cistern. Water may be distributed with a dedicated jar, cup, or ladle to keep hands and personal containers out.

Avoid raw greens unless you know how they were watered and washed. Food prepared hot in busy ovens is generally a better choice than food that has sat in the warm, damp air. Keep your own provisions sealed from rats, flies, and pale crickets.

Wash your hands before eating and after contact with channels, animals, ropes, jars, or repair clay. Do not wash soap, insect repellent, blood, or medicine into a drain without asking where that drain leads. Some channels feed planting beds or tanks.

## Animals

The animals are part of the chamber’s economy and ecology, not background decoration.

Do not reach into pigeon cells, bat ducts, lamp niches, jar stacks, or cracks. A niche that appears empty may hold a gecko, snake, rat, bird, eggs, or a worker’s tools.

At dusk, avoid standing directly under bat exits. If bats begin pouring through a gallery, stop, lower yourself slightly, cover your eyes, and allow them to pass. Do not wave your arms. A bat touching clothing is not necessarily an attack, but any bite or bare-skin contact should be examined.

Shake out footwear and clothing before putting them on. Keep sleeping mats off the floor if possible. Store food in a suspended or rodent-resistant container.

Let resident dogs see and smell you before passing close to pens or sleeping areas. Do not run from them in narrow corridors. Ask handlers to move goats rather than squeezing behind tethered animals; a startled goat can knock a person into a channel or tank.

## Navigation

No visitor should explore alone. The Rain House contains repeating walls, altered stairs, false boulders, overlapping galleries, and routes that may change after a single repair. Light shafts are not reliable landmarks because their appearance changes with the hour.

Do not mark walls with chalk, paint, scratches, string, or tied cloth. Marks can be confused with work instructions, while cord becomes a hazard around feet, gates, animals, and water crews.

Instead, memorize a short sequence of robust landmarks:

1. The entry stair or hatch.
2. The nearest high refuge platform.
3. One staffed court.
4. A major cistern or oven.
5. The direction of the nearest usable exit.

If separated, do not follow hollow sounds or unfamiliar stairs. Stop at a safe, occupied, elevated place and call in the manner your guide taught you. Avoid shouting near animals unless there is immediate danger.

Descending passages ending in warm water or old masonry should be considered closed unless an authorized worker takes you there. Warm flooded spaces may contain bad air, sudden depth, unstable edges, or currents invisible at the surface.

## When rain comes

Rain is the Rain House’s most impressive event and its most dangerous one. A storm above may not be audible until ceramic mouths begin running.

Early signs can include:

- A change in airflow.
- Dust blowing from a shaft.
- Workers uncovering channels or carrying stoppers.
- Frogs becoming active.
- Calls relayed from room to room.
- Black trickles appearing in otherwise dry gutters.
- A deep knocking or coughing from pipes.

At the first organized response, become an obedient piece of luggage. Stay with your guide, keep your hands free, and move to the assigned place. Do not stop to watch water enter a low court. Do not help with gates or stoppers unless directly instructed; the forces involved can crush fingers and shoulders, and closing the wrong gate may flood another gallery.

Move upward, but not blindly. Some stairs end at platforms with no onward route, and some high galleries may lie beneath dangerous plaster or animal colonies. Use known flood refuges.

Never walk in a newly filled channel. Water that is merely ankle-deep can conceal worn steps, open drains, sharp pottery, snakes, or a sudden current. Keep away from wooden sluices while they hammer in their grooves.

If caught by rising water:

- Go to the nearest stable raised platform identified by residents.
- Keep away from tank rims and narrow parapets.
- Do not enter a descending passage.
- Protect cuts from the water if possible.
- Do not brace yourself against a gate or plaster shell.
- Call for help using local signals.
- Wait for instructions rather than attempting to rediscover the exit.

After rain, assume floors remain slippery and contaminated. The chamber may look festive, but repair and collection work takes precedence over sightseeing.

## Structural and air hazards

The Rain House is stable because it is continuously tended, not because every part is intrinsically safe.

Watch for fresh bulges in limewash, falling grit, widening cracks, unusual groans, and workers clearing an area. Small flakes are ordinary; a sudden stream of dust or pottery fragments is not. If material begins falling, protect your head and move away from the wall into a known clear area. Do not run through unfamiliar arches.

Poor air may collect in sealed or low spaces. Headache, dizziness, nausea, unusual sleepiness, confusion, or a lamp behaving strangely are reasons to retreat immediately. Do not enter a newly opened chamber first. Let local specialists test and ventilate it.

Smoke is also a serious concern. Know which way the nearest open court lies, but remember that shafts and galleries can draw smoke unpredictably. Follow residents rather than assuming “up” is automatically safer.

## Staying overnight

If permitted to sleep inside, choose an upper, dry, inhabited platform away from:

- Bat and pigeon throats.
- Animal pens.
- Oven smoke.
- Active channels.
- Tank rims.
- Lime-scaled ceilings.
- Frequently used rope ways.

Use a raised mat and insect net. Keep footwear, light, and water within reach. Seal food. Earplugs may be useful against frogs, goats, pestles, and dripping water, but do not block all hearing; you must be able to hear rain calls and evacuation instructions.

Expect poor sleep. The Rain House never becomes silent, and its night sounds—moving rats, water in pipes, birds shifting behind walls—can be difficult for an unfamiliar traveler to interpret.

## Gifts and exchange

Money may work in the market courts, but practical consumables are often better received if approved in advance: good lamp oil, clean cordage, needles, repair cloth, copper sheet, salt, or medically safe soap. Do not distribute unfamiliar chemicals, plastics, pesticides, seeds, or miracle sealants. A material that appears superior may poison water, trap moisture, prevent later repair, or outlast the knowledge needed to remove it.

The best courtesy is often labor performed under supervision: passing baskets, holding a lamp, carrying clean jars, or sweeping designated mud into a planting bed. Never begin a repair merely to demonstrate helpfulness. In the Rain House, repair is ownership, engineering, law, and memory at once.

Above all, resist the temptation to “discover” what lies behind the next wall. The inhabitants already live with those hollow sounds. Their caution is not ignorance. It is the accumulated judgment of people who know that every sealed void may contain water, foul air, unstable masonry, an old drain, or the weight of half the chamber.

Visit as a guest in a living organism, not as an explorer in an abandoned one. If you leave without changing anything except the contents of your own memory, you have traveled well.
