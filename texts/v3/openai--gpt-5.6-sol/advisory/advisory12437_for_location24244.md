This is not a wilderness an ordinary traveler should enter casually. It has no settlement, fuel, reliable surface water, audible rescue signals, or obvious route of retreat. Its apparent stability is especially dangerous: nothing looks urgent even when hypoxia, dehydration, cold, and wind are steadily disabling you.

Treat the plateau as a high-altitude expedition site and a protected alien ecosystem.

## Before crossing

### Send instruments first

Do not assume that clear air is breathable air. A probe should verify:

- Atmospheric composition, pressure, temperature, and ultraviolet intensity
- Local gravity
- Wind speed at ground level and along escarpments
- Airborne spores, toxins, and microorganisms
- Water chemistry
- The behavior of the cloud layer below
- Whether electrical equipment, compasses, radios, and satellite navigation function there
- Whether the crossing point remains open and physically accessible from both sides

Measure sound pressure as well as audible volume. The cliffs may produce frequencies low enough to cause nausea, disorientation, anxiety, or impaired balance even through ordinary ear protection.

If the portal or return mechanism opens directly onto the high plateau, pre-acclimatization is essential. A person transported instantly from sea level may become dangerously ill before recognizing the symptoms.

### Travel as a team

No one should go alone. Use at least three people, so one can remain with an injured traveler while another returns for help. Everyone should be trained in:

- High-altitude illness
- Cold injury and dehydration
- Rope travel and steep-ground rescue
- Navigation without landmarks or GPS
- Nonverbal communication
- Ecological quarantine procedures

Have a medical examination beforehand. Serious heart, lung, circulation, or balance problems may make the journey inadvisable.

## Acclimatization and breathing

The greatest immediate hazard is probably the altitude. Relentless wind and noise may disguise rapid breathing, headache, fatigue, and confusion.

Acclimatize gradually in an equivalent terrestrial environment if possible. Carry supplemental oxygen for travel and emergencies, not merely comfort. The group should also have:

- Pulse oximeters, though readings must be interpreted alongside symptoms
- Medication selected by an expedition physician
- A portable hyperbaric bag if evacuation cannot be immediate
- A strict symptom checklist for acute mountain sickness
- A predetermined turnaround rule

Headache, nausea, unusual clumsiness, irrational behavior, breathlessness at rest, a wet cough, or inability to walk a straight line require immediate oxygen and descent or return. Do not wait for symptoms to “settle.” On this plateau, the nearest lower elevation may be across lethal cliffs or beneath an opaque cloud layer, so the multiversal crossing point may be the only practical descent.

Place the base camp very close to the return point during the first visits.

## Protection from light, cold, and wind

The brilliant, colorless light will produce snow blindness and severe sunburn faster than the cool air suggests. Bring:

- Glacier-rated goggles with side protection
- A spare pair for each team
- High-SPF broad-spectrum sunscreen and lip protection
- A brim or neck shield that cannot catch the wind
- Fully covering, UV-resistant clothing
- Insulated gloves that still permit rope and instrument handling

Dress in layered mountaineering clothing. Expect frost at dawn, rapid heating in direct sun, and severe wind chill in shade. The air’s dryness will pull moisture from skin and lungs continuously.

Outer garments should be close-fitting and exceptionally windproof. Avoid loose straps, ponchos, unsecured scarves, and anything that can flap, snag, or be stripped away.

Wear a climbing helmet. Apart from ordinary falls, birds may drop insects or stones at established feeding sites, and old rock is not the same as safe rock. Rounded escarpment edges can still shed fragments.

## Hearing and communication

Near the resonant cliffs, ordinary conversation will be useless. Prolonged exposure may damage hearing even where the noise no longer feels remarkable.

Use:

- Properly fitted earplugs beneath sealed ear defenders
- Noise-rated headsets for electronic communication
- Bone-conduction or throat microphones, tested on site
- Prearranged hand signals
- Wrist or chest-mounted vibration alerts
- Written emergency cards

Never rely on shouting. Each traveler should have a visual and tactile means of calling for help. In poor visibility, connect team members with a short rope only where the terrain permits; a badly managed rope in strong wind or among fissures can create its own hazard.

Hearing protection should not lead to complacency. Establish frequent visual check-ins, because falling stones, approaching animals, and equipment failures may not be heard.

## Shelter

Use a low-profile, expedition-grade tent or compact rigid shelter designed for continuous high wind. Carry redundant poles, repair sleeves, anchor lines, and multiple forms of anchoring. Thin soil may not accept pegs, so be prepared to use removable rock anchors, weighted bags, or non-damaging straps.

Do not automatically occupy caves, shafts, or wind holes. They may be:

- Bird colonies
- Animal shelters
- Vertical drops concealed by shadow
- Channels that amplify dangerous sound
- Places where carbon dioxide or other gases collect
- Essential condensation sites for local life

Select a previously surveyed basin away from cliff edges, flight corridors, pools, and cushion vegetation. Avoid the eastern rain wall and the mouths of resonant cavities. Camp well back from any lip: wind, visual distortion, and altitude-impaired judgment make edges treacherous.

Keep every item secured at all times. A glove, foil wrapper, map, or empty bag may be gone instantly and may enter nesting cavities where it cannot be recovered.

## Water and food

Carry all drinking water needed for the planned stay, plus a large emergency reserve. Do not plan around rain, frost, or the visible pools. Water there is sparse, difficult to collect, and likely central to entire local communities.

A pool no larger than a shield may be the only drinking and breeding site for many animals. Taking a few liters could be ecologically significant.

If emergency collection becomes necessary:

1. Prefer newly condensed or collected precipitation over established pools.
2. Filter out sediment and organisms.
3. Purify chemically or with heat.
4. Test for mineral and biological hazards if equipment allows.
5. Take the smallest possible amount.

Do not assume hard snow is clean. It may contain concentrated salts, windborne organisms, or animal waste. Melting it also requires fuel, and there is almost no local combustible material. Bring a sealed stove and all fuel; never burn vegetation.

Bring calorie-dense food that can be eaten with gloves and requires little water. Packaging should be quiet, robust, and impossible for the wind to scatter. Eat and drink on a schedule, since cold, altitude, and noise can suppress appetite and obscure thirst.

## Movement and navigation

Distances on the plateau are deceptive. The clear air removes atmospheric cues, while scale is confused by huge, worn formations. Assume every apparently short crossing will take much longer than expected.

Navigation should not depend on a single technology. Carry:

- Tested compass, noting whether it behaves normally
- Inertial or local-beacon navigation
- Paper or durable physical maps made from aerial reconnaissance
- Altimeter and barometer
- Laser rangefinder
- Portal or base-camp locator beacon
- Reversible, non-polluting route markers

Record bearings and travel time continuously. Establish strict outward-time limits rather than deciding based on how close a ridge appears.

Probe gravel fans and stone plates before committing weight. Old, settled terrain can still conceal fissures and shafts. Stay away from cornice-like snow seams, undercut limestone, and the dark mouths of holes. Use ropes near ravines, but do not attach them to weathered arches or flakes merely because those formations look massive.

The cloud layer is not a floor. Never enter it without detailed knowledge of the descent beneath it. It may hide kilometers of empty air.

## Respect for local life

The plateau is not empty, and its lack of buildings does not make it unclaimed. Much of its ecology is concentrated into tiny wind shadows, cracks, and water pockets. A careless boot can destroy growth that took centuries to spread across a hand’s breadth.

Follow these rules:

- Step on bare, stable stone whenever possible.
- Never stand on cushion plants; their woody feel does not mean they are durable.
- Do not move rocks casually. Nearly every warm slab may roof a burrow.
- Keep far from nesting cavities and fixed aerial streams.
- Do not block animal paths between water pockets.
- Never feed wildlife.
- Do not handle tool-using birds or take their stones and feeding flakes.
- Do not collect lichens, flowers, bones, insects, or “unoccupied” artifacts.
- Avoid bright throat-like patches or abrupt wing gestures in clothing and equipment until local signaling behavior is understood.

The birds’ use of tools deserves particular caution. They may have territorial traditions, learned feeding sites, and social knowledge that is not obvious to a human observer. Do not interpret the absence of construction as absence of intelligence.

Insect influxes could be overwhelming behind sheltered stones. Wear sealed cuffs, a head net, and eye protection, but avoid broad pesticide use. The insects are a major food supply for the plateau. Chemical repellents should be selected for minimal environmental persistence.

## Quarantine

Biological exchange may be more dangerous than any animal encounter. Clean and sterilize boots, tents, ropes, and sampling equipment before entry. Use closed waste systems, including for human waste and wash water.

On return:

- Seal clothing and equipment before crossing into an inhabited environment.
- Quarantine people and material for an appropriate period.
- Screen for spores, parasites, eggs, and microorganisms.
- Do not bring back untreated water, soil, feathers, insects, or plants.
- Treat all scientific sampling as a controlled biohazard operation.

The same care protects the plateau from ordinary human bacteria, seeds, fungi, and food scraps.

## How to experience it safely

The best first visit would be short: perhaps an hour near a sheltered, surveyed return point after acclimatization. Sit low, tether loose equipment, and resist the urge to range toward the cliffs immediately.

The noise will likely be felt before it is understood. Even with protection, the body may register the bellow through the chest and jaw. The unchanging direction of the wind may make the world seem tilted. Clear air and the cloud floor will distort scale, and the absence of moving trees or flowing water will remove familiar measures of time.

Watch the ground closely. What seems barren from standing height will resolve into compact plants, insect refuges, tracks, and breathing spaces beneath stones. Watch the cliffs from a distance until the fixed birds and horizontal streams of smaller wings become visible. The plateau is best approached not as an empty summit to conquer, but as a vast, exposed habitat whose activity occurs at scales and in senses humans do not naturally notice.

Leave before fatigue becomes obvious. In a place where the wind, light, and sound do not change, your own declining condition may be the only approaching weather.
