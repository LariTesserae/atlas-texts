### Best approach: visit while the basin is drained

For an ordinary human, this is not a swimming chamber but a submerged piece of working civic infrastructure. The safest visit is during scheduled maintenance, entering through the dry access shaft with the water-keepers after the gates have been closed, counterweights blocked, and the basin pumped or drained.

If seeing it flooded is essential, treat it as a technical penetration dive. Do not enter alone, on a held breath, or without the cooperation of the people operating the sluices.

## Before entering

- **Obtain permission from the water-keepers and masons.** This chamber supplies public water; an unexplained intruder may be regarded as a saboteur or contaminant.
- **Learn the gate schedule.** A routine adjustment can produce a violent surge with little warning inside the basin.
- **Require physical lockout, not merely a promise.** Upstream gates should be closed and wedged, ropes secured, counterweights immobilized, and an attendant posted at every control.
- **Inspect the dry access route and identify at least two exits.** Do not count the light shafts as exits; they may be narrow, grated, or impossible to climb.
- **Test the water.** Confirm temperature, mineral content, acidity, dissolved gases, and whether the spring carries anything harmful in this universe.
- **Check local gravity and physiology first.** Even slight differences in gravity, lung response, or gas behavior matter underwater.

If the machinery cannot be secured, observe through a shaft or use a small remote camera. Do not enter during normal operation merely to experience the surges.

## Essential equipment for a flooded visit

Use equipment intended for an **overhead, confined-water environment**:

- Full-face mask or diving helmet, protecting the eyes and face from grit
- Redundant breathing supply, with an independent reserve rather than a single shared source
- Pressure and depth gauge appropriate to local conditions
- Helmet with no exposed snag points
- Close-fitting gloves and a protective suit; the new limestone, tile shards, and mortar edges will be sharp
- Adjustable thermal protection—the water is warm, but long exposure can still cause heat stress or chilling depending on currents
- Small, shielded lights despite the bright shafts; sediment can erase the natural illumination in seconds
- A guideline secured to the true exit
- Cutting tools reachable with either hand
- Compact communication equipment, preferably through-water or hard-line
- Low-profile buoyancy equipment that permits precise control near the floor
- Clean footwear or fins suitable for confined spaces

Avoid dangling hoses, loose straps, jewelry, fabric tassels, or bulky packs. Wooden grates, pegs, pipe mouths, terraces, and hinge sockets provide many places to snag.

Anything entering the reservoir should be washed and disinfected with a method acceptable to the water-keepers. Do not introduce oils, chemical glow sticks, marker dyes, food, soap, or foreign microorganisms.

## How to move inside

Enter slowly and remain slightly above the terraces. The ceramic grit beds are functional settling surfaces, not gravel paths. A careless kick can fill the entire chamber with pale sediment and interfere with the water system.

Use gentle hand movements or modified fin strokes. Do not:

- Put weight on clay pipes or plant troughs
- Pull on wooden grates
- Probe mortar seams
- Collect tile, beads, algae, sediment, or construction fragments
- Scrape away the red setting-out marks
- Touch the thumbprinted plaster recess
- Climb into sluice mouths or drainage pits
- Breathe from trapped ceiling bubbles

Those silver air pockets are not safe emergency supplies. They may be tiny, oxygen-poor, mineral-laden, or contaminated by gases escaping mortar and spring water.

Keep clear of the western wall, where incoming water and heated masonry may cause burns or disorientation. Also avoid the lowest square pit: loose material naturally migrates there, and a sudden current will carry a struggling swimmer in the same direction.

## What to expect

The chamber will feel larger than it is. Refraction and the terraced floor make a few strokes of distance resemble a drowned valley. The pale limestone scatters light so thoroughly that ordinary visual cues become unreliable. Shafts will appear to bend, ledges may seem nearer than they are, and the blue-white moving patterns can make the floor appear to shift.

The sound will be physically oppressive. Even a minor knock may seem to come from every wall. The rushing conduits, rolling ceramic fragments, and gate mechanisms will be transmitted through water and through your skull. Wear appropriate auditory protection if compatible with communications, and establish simple hand or line signals in case speech becomes unintelligible.

Warm and cool layers may be surprisingly distinct. Passing through them can fog a mask, alter buoyancy slightly, and create shimmering visual distortion. The hottest water will gather high and near the western inflow; cooler threads will hug the steps and sluice mouths.

If a gate opens unexpectedly, do not fight directly against the surge. Flatten your profile, protect your mask and regulator, avoid sluice mouths, and move laterally into the lee of a substantial brick pier or raised terrace. Once the pulse passes, follow the guideline out. If visibility is lost, abandon sightseeing immediately.

## Recommended visit plan

1. Meet the chief water-keeper and inspect the gate controls.
2. Place attendants at the controls and dry access shaft.
3. Secure all inflows and wait for currents to diminish.
4. Send in a small camera or weighted test object first.
5. Enter with one trained companion while another remains outside.
6. Stay within immediate reach of the exit for the first several minutes.
7. Cross the chamber once, slowly, without entering channels or recesses.
8. Limit the visit because warm water and sensory overload can conceal fatigue.
9. Account for every tool and fragment before leaving.
10. Decontaminate yourself and your equipment, then allow the keepers to inspect the basin.

To experience the machinery safely, arrange for a **very small controlled pulse only after all visitors have exited**, and watch it through a shaft or protected inspection opening.

The most important preparation is humility. This bright artificial valley may look serene and intimate, but it is a pressure chamber inside a living water system. Visit as the water-keepers do: deliberately, cleanly, and only when every gate is under control.
