## Preparing to Visit the Shelf of Hammers

An ordinary human should not visit the Shelf as a conventional diver. Treat it as a deep-industrial environment with an exposed wilderness around it, not as an ocean destination. Its clear water, steady light, and apparent stillness conceal lethal pressure, cold, vibration, distance, and machinery whose tolerances may encompass an entire ecosystem.

The safest visit is made inside a pressure-isolated submersible, preferably with a pilot and systems engineer familiar with equilibrium worlds. Do not rely on a pressure suit except for brief, carefully planned exterior work.

### Before departure

Obtain, if they exist:

- A current bathymetric chart of the plateau and its escarpments.
- Maps of buried cables, ballast chambers, mineral vents, pressure shafts, and restricted maintenance zones.
- The operating frequencies of nearby engines and gates.
- Biological notices identifying spawning, dormancy, and bell-herd territories.
- Confirmation that any inspection shelter you intend to use is still pressurized, supplied, and compatible with human life.
- Formal authorization from whoever maintains the Shelf—or from its automated control system.

The last point matters. An installation that looks abandoned may simply require no attendants. “Incomplete” structures are not necessarily available for salvage or exploration. Empty bolt beds and open gantries may be active parts of load-balancing plans.

Have the expedition physician screen for conditions aggravated by vibration and confinement, particularly:

- Inner-ear disorders and severe motion sensitivity.
- Migraines, tinnitus, and sound-triggered seizures.
- Dental problems, including loose fillings and trapped gas beneath crowns.
- Sinus or lung conditions.
- Joint implants or other hardware susceptible to sustained vibration.
- Panic responses to deep water, featureless distances, or continuous noise.

Even within a rigid pressure hull, the Shelf’s concussion may be transmitted through the vehicle frame. Ordinary ear protection will not be sufficient.

### Essential vehicle requirements

Use a submersible rated well beyond the Shelf’s maximum local pressure, with particular attention to fatigue under continuous acoustic loading. The hull may survive the static pressure yet fail because seals, connectors, battery mounts, or viewing ports resonate with the machinery below.

The vehicle should have:

- Redundant pressure hulls or an independent survival capsule.
- Vibration-isolated seats, bunks, instrument racks, and life-support equipment.
- Active acoustic cancellation designed for both cabin air and structural conduction.
- Dental bite guards or vibration-damping jaw supports for longer visits.
- Independent heating with several days of reserve power.
- Carbon-dioxide scrubbing and oxygen reserves well beyond the planned mission.
- Inertial navigation, terrain matching, and optical navigation.
- A low-power, narrow-beam communications system able to function through overwhelming background noise.
- A physical emergency beacon using several modalities: light, electromagnetic signaling, coded vibration, and releasable buoy.
- Manipulator arms with force limits.
- Broad landing skids or adjustable feet made for angular basalt.
- Thrusters capable of extremely fine impulses.
- Guards over propulsors and intakes to prevent injury to long transparent organisms.
- External cameras able to resolve nearly invisible bodies against evenly lit water.

Bring a second vehicle if at all possible. On a continent-sized plateau, rescue from a distant station may take longer than your life support lasts.

Do not assume the constant industrial sound makes sonar navigation easier. It may obscure returns, produce false geometry, or interfere biologically with animals that use the engine field. Passive optical navigation is unusually practical here because the water is so clear. When active sonar is necessary, use approved frequencies and the lowest effective power.

### Do not expose yourself to ambient pressure

A soft diving suit is wholly inappropriate. A one-atmosphere atmospheric suit might permit exterior work, but it should be treated as a small submersible rather than clothing. It needs:

- A hard pressure shell.
- Heating and redundant life support.
- Powered joints designed for the local pressure.
- A tether carrying power, data, and retrieval load.
- Vibration isolation at the boots, torso, and helmet.
- Smooth outer surfaces that will not catch on basalt splinters or cables.

Never walk directly across the rock unless the route has been inspected. The basalt can cut seals, hoses, gloves, and suit joints. Pale salt crusts may conceal fissures. Nickel and ceramic fragments can be sharper than natural stone. Use a hovering harness, landing mat, or robotic scout.

A human acclimatized to ordinary atmospheric pressure should remain at one atmosphere throughout the trip. There is no reason to undergo saturation or decompression unless the Shelf has verified human habitats built for that purpose.

### Clothing and personal equipment

Inside the vehicle, wear layered thermal clothing that remains useful if cabin heat is lost:

- Moisture-wicking base layers.
- Insulated midlayers.
- A compact immersion or survival suit.
- Gloves that preserve dexterity.
- Soft head protection against prolonged vibration and sudden vehicle movement.

Carry individually fitted hearing protection even if the cabin has active cancellation. The goal will not be silence; it will be to reduce the continuous load enough that you can think and sleep.

Also bring:

- A medical kit including anti-nausea medication, migraine treatment, and dental emergency materials.
- Paper or mechanically displayed emergency procedures, since electronics may be affected by vibration or fields near pylons.
- A non-acoustic personal timer and watch.
- A sleep mask.
- Adjustable warm-spectrum cabin lighting.
- Familiar scents, music delivered through isolated headphones, and other grounding aids.
- High-calorie warm food and ample water.
- Spare spectacles rather than relying only on contact lenses.
- A written symptom log for hearing changes, confusion, irritability, jaw pain, or false sensations of rhythm.

### Timekeeping and psychological preparation

The Shelf offers almost no natural indication that time is passing. Its light does not change, its water does not visibly flow, and its noise has no usable beat. Travelers commonly depend more on dawn, weather, footsteps, moving shadows, and distant human activity than they realize. Here, none of those will help.

Maintain an artificial twenty-four-hour schedule from arrival:

- Change cabin lighting according to a normal day-night cycle.
- Eat at fixed hours.
- Alternate observation shifts.
- Use alarms that flash or touch the skin rather than adding more sound.
- Sleep in a darkened compartment, not in front of the viewing ports.
- Keep written time logs.

The endless clear distance can also distort scale. A ridge fifty kilometers away may seem close enough to reach quickly. A bell animal may look hand-sized until the vehicle approaches its rim. Always trust measured distance over visual intuition.

The noise may produce phantom rhythms, voices, or a sense that the engines have suddenly changed when they have not. Conversely, habituation may make a real mechanical change difficult to notice. Instrument readings should arbitrate; do not act on perception alone.

Keep the first visit short. Several hours of exposure is enough to understand the place. Multi-day stays should be attempted only after observing how each traveler responds.

### Approach and landing

Approach from above the central plateau, not across its rim. The margin combines a vertical drop, a sharp illumination boundary, deep machinery, and potentially complex pressure behavior. It is also psychologically disorienting: the edge can resemble the end of the world even from inside a secure hull.

Before descending:

1. Match your navigation grid to fixed pylons or surveyed mineral faces.
2. Scan for cable-bodied drifters. Their transparent nervous tissue may be almost invisible.
3. Identify bell herds and give them a wide berth.
4. Measure electrical fields around pylons and buried lines.
5. Check the engine-frequency bulletin against actual readings.
6. Select a landing site free of vents, salt-filled fissures, shell rings, and ballast markers.

Hover whenever practical. If landing is required, set down with minimal force on a surveyed slab. Do not rest on apparently loose rubble; the loose matter may contain vents or maintenance apertures, and the underlying stone may be actively reconstructed.

Avoid stirring powdered basalt. The water’s stillness means a cloud you create may persist around sensors and organisms far longer than expected.

### Conduct around the fauna

Do not mistake sparse distribution for rarity. A region that appears empty may contain transparent animals extending across several kilometers.

Maintain a conservative exclusion distance from all large organisms, especially bell herds. They feed on vibration. Your thrusters, pumps, sonar, and hull resonance may attract, repel, nourish, confuse, or injure them. Do not enter the bell cavity, even if smaller creatures appear to use it as shelter. An animal large enough to contain a building may alter its shape slowly but with forces your vehicle cannot resist.

Around electrical pylons:

- Reduce nonessential electrical emissions.
- Do not pass through shoals of silver disks.
- Expect collective movements when your power systems change state.
- Avoid touching the pylons or cable supports.

The white jointed grazers under pump housings are part of the operating ecology, not fouling to be brushed aside. Their mineral feeding may be included in the station’s maintenance balance.

Never attempt to touch a needle-jawed hunter. Its apparent stillness may be the final stage of a months-long approach to prey. Rapid evasive motion could trigger a strike. Withdraw slowly across, rather than along, the dominant acoustic axis if local biological guidance recommends doing so.

Do not feed anything. Imported organic food could be chemically disruptive even if local organisms cannot digest it.

### Protecting the Shelf from yourself

This environment appears to correct disturbances, but that does not mean disturbances are harmless. The machinery may counterweight a fallen object or replenish a mineral loss without regard for nearby organisms—or for the traveler who caused it.

Use strict contamination controls:

- Sterilize external surfaces before entry.
- Contain all human waste.
- Release no ballast, oils, detergents, microbes, paint flakes, or food particles.
- Use closed-cycle cooling if possible.
- Account for every sampling tool.
- Avoid sacrificial markers unless specifically approved.

Take no rock, shell, salt, machine fragment, or carcass without permission. The apparent abundance or inertness of an object is not evidence that it is unmonitored. Even removing a small mineral sample could trigger compensatory precipitation or confuse long-term measurements.

Avoid unnecessary heat discharge. Heat is one of the disturbances the system actively transports away, and a warm vehicle may become a conspicuous anomaly.

Above all, do not alter sound:

- Do not play music externally.
- Do not test horns or explosive acoustic sources.
- Do not strike the rock.
- Do not attempt to “answer” an engine.
- Do not retune, silence, or diagnose machinery unless this is the authorized purpose of the expedition.

One engine note may regulate reproduction across a country-sized region. A frequency change too small for a human to notice could be catastrophic locally.

### Installations and shelters

Assume every structure is active until proven otherwise.

A lit hatch is not necessarily an invitation or proof of breathable atmosphere. Before docking, remotely verify:

- Internal pressure and gas composition.
- Temperature.
- Power stability.
- Structural condition.
- Decontamination requirements.
- Whether the shelter is in maintenance mode.
- Whether opening the hatch changes pressure, ballast, or acoustic behavior.

Do not cross warning stripes merely because the paint looks new. Everything looks new here.

Shafts should never be entered without official plans. They may connect directly to compression systems, reservoirs, or moving components whose operation is continuous and too rapid to interpret visually. A “quiet” shaft is not an inactive shaft; the machinery’s noise is so universal that proximity may be difficult to judge.

Unfinished gantries and empty pits are particularly dangerous. Travelers often read them as abandoned construction sites, but their edges may have no human safety provisions. There are no roads because the machines were not placed for routine human travel.

### Navigation and movement

The still water is not equivalent to stable footing. Once pushed, an untethered object may travel for days. A dropped tool may not sink promptly; it may drift into machinery or an animal long after your departure.

Secure everything with retractable tethers. Every exterior movement should be deliberate, with enough propellant reserved to cancel it. Do not leap or push off the rock in an atmospheric suit.

Set hard range limits. Naked rock can continue for hundreds of kilometers between shelters, and the clarity of the water makes distance easy to underestimate. Keep a continuous return-energy calculation rather than relying on elapsed time.

Stay well back from the plateau rim unless the rim is the specific mission target. Near the light boundary, visual navigation may abruptly degrade. Do not descend toward the red trench indicators. Machinery below may lie at pressures, temperatures, or water states beyond the vehicle’s rating; the description of trench water as “compressed glass” should be taken as an engineering warning, not a metaphor.

### Emergency planning

Your emergency doctrine should favor retreat over intervention.

Abort immediately for:

- An unexplained change in engine frequency.
- New hull resonance.
- Repeated navigation disagreement.
- Failure of thermal control.
- Increased leakage or seal vibration.
- An approaching large animal whose behavior is unknown.
- Loss of optical visibility.
- Uncommanded electrical effects near a pylon.
- Physical symptoms such as vertigo, dental pain, confusion, or sudden hearing changes.

If propulsion fails, do not assume currents will carry you anywhere useful. The water may leave you almost exactly where you are. Release a marked emergency buoy only if it can reach a monitored layer; otherwise use the approved beacon and conserve heat and oxygen.

If the vehicle begins drifting toward the rim, act early. A small corrective impulse is safer than a desperate burn near the escarpment.

If machinery changes state, do not approach to investigate unless authorized. Move away from shafts, pylons, vents, and overhangs, and rise toward the designated extraction depth. Local equilibrium corrections may involve ballast movement or pressure transfer invisible from outside.

### What the visit will feel like

Expect the Shelf to be beautiful in a severe, exhausting way.

At first it may resemble an empty black plain under museum lighting. Then distance will become apparent: a ridge sharp enough to touch, yet fifty kilometers away; a line in the water resolving into an animal longer than a city district; a faint hollow form revealing itself as a bell vast enough to shelter buildings.

The steady illumination removes drama from everything. There is no descending night to end the day and no passing cloud to mark a minute. Nothing sways. Ice crystals hang over the margins as though embedded in clear resin. Your own bubbles, propeller wash, and moving shadow may seem offensively energetic.

Meanwhile, the sound will be inside you. Even when the cabin microphones are muted, it may remain in the seat frame, sternum, jaw, and fingertips. After departure, ordinary silence can feel wrong. Some travelers continue to perceive the blow for hours or days, particularly while falling asleep.

Plan time afterward in a quiet, warm place with natural daylight and gentle movement. Have hearing and vestibular checks if symptoms persist.

The correct attitude is neither fear nor conquest, but restraint. The Shelf is not waiting to be explored in the usual sense. It is already occupied by a planetary arrangement that continuously maintains itself. Visit lightly, observe from a sealed hull, and leave without giving the system a reason to notice you.
