## Overall assessment

Prepare for this as a **warm, high-altitude wetland expedition combined with an archaeological visit**, not as a walk through a harmless abandoned town. The basin appears gentle—mild air, shallow water, intact roads, no predators—but its principal dangers are quiet ones:

- altitude and possibly unfamiliar atmospheric composition,
- constant damp and loss of body heat,
- slippery fresh stone,
- hidden marsh channels and cisterns,
- chemically or biologically unsafe water,
- unstable young geology,
- total lack of inhabitants, food animals, pollinators, or rescue,
- and the unexplained absence of animal life.

That last point should govern the entire trip. It may be a benign biological peculiarity, but it may also indicate a condition that affects animals periodically or selectively. An ordinary human should make the first visit short, instrumented, and fully self-sufficient.

## Before crossing

### Confirm basic habitability

Do not trust warmth, greenery, or standing water as proof that the environment is safe. Send instruments or a probe first if possible. Measure:

- oxygen and carbon-dioxide concentration,
- atmospheric pressure,
- toxic or volcanic gases, especially sulfur compounds and carbon monoxide,
- airborne spores and microorganisms,
- background radiation,
- water pH, salinity, dissolved metals, and organic toxins,
- local seismic activity.

Because the mountains are geologically young, also check for recent volcanism, hydrothermal activity, and ground deformation. Clear water emerging from basalt can still contain hazardous dissolved minerals or gases.

The absence of insects, fish, and birds deserves specific investigation. Test whether local air, water, pollen, or plant compounds are toxic to animal cells. Until there is an explanation, limit initial exposure and maintain the ability to leave immediately.

### Plan for no local support

Assume there is:

- no edible local food,
- no potable local water,
- no fuel worth gathering,
- no medical help,
- no reliable shelter,
- and no one coming if you become injured.

Leave a travel plan with someone outside the basin or outside the universe. Establish fixed check-in times and a missed-check-in extraction procedure. Carry two independent ways to locate the return point.

If multiversal transit equipment can fail when wet, protect it more carefully than anything else.

## Clothing and personal equipment

The climate is warm, but persistent moisture can still produce chilling, skin damage, and exhaustion. Wear:

- quick-drying base layers,
- a breathable waterproof shell,
- light insulating clothing for rest periods,
- waterproof but drainable boots with aggressive grip,
- gaiters for marsh crossings,
- gloves for wet stone,
- a brimmed hood or cap to keep roof drips off the face,
- spare dry socks sealed in waterproof bags.

Avoid cotton. It will remain wet and become heavy.

Bring trekking poles; two are preferable. They will help on slick paving and can probe turf, shallow pools, and marsh margins before you commit your weight. A short rope, harness, and compact hauling system are sensible around cisterns, wells, terraces, and fractured ridges. Do not explore underground conduits alone.

Other useful equipment includes:

- headlamp and backup light,
- compass, inertial navigator, and physical mapmaking materials,
- pressure altimeter,
- atmospheric monitor,
- water-testing kit,
- waterproof field notebook,
- satellite or cross-world beacon,
- compact first-aid and trauma kit,
- blister and foot-care supplies,
- emergency bivouac,
- portable stove and dry fuel,
- insect netting only as a biosecurity barrier, despite the apparent lack of insects.

The dim, uniform light and lack of moving shadows may make time difficult to judge. Carry a watch and follow scheduled breaks rather than waiting to feel tired.

## Arrival procedure

Arrive on one of the raised stone roads or a broad paved area, not in a garden bed, meadow, or marsh. Remain near the transit point for the first hour.

During that period:

1. Verify that the atmosphere remains safe at human breathing height.
2. Compare readings at ground level, especially near wells and hollows. Heavy gases can pool in low places.
3. Mark the return point physically and electronically.
4. Observe whether your presence produces any change: plant closure, water discoloration, condensation on equipment, respiratory irritation, or unusual fatigue.
5. Establish a clean area for supplies so that local mud and water do not contaminate the exit apparatus.

Do not immediately descend to the central well merely because it is conspicuous. Wells, cisterns, old vents, and canal hollows are the most likely places for trapped gas, weak edges, and concealed depth.

## Water and food

Bring all drinking water for the initial visit. The abundance of clear water should not reduce your supply estimate.

Do not drink from channels, roof spouts, springs, pools, or the central well without laboratory-grade testing. Ordinary filtration removes sediment and many organisms, but not dissolved volcanic compounds, plant toxins, or unfamiliar microorganisms. Boiling is likewise insufficient for many chemical hazards.

Do not eat the garden plants, fruits, gourds, roots, or flowers. Their resemblance to familiar crops is not evidence of compatibility. Even if the city’s former residents cultivated them, their biochemistry may differ from yours.

Use a closed stove rather than attempting to light a hearth. Local timber and turf will be wet, smoke may linger in the still air, and old hearths should be treated as archaeological features.

## Moving through the city

Stay on ridge streets, causeways, and open plazas whenever possible. The builders clearly identified the most stable routes; take advantage of their knowledge.

Expect every surface to be slick:

- Basalt may be polished by centuries of moisture.
- Moss can detach from stone under a boot.
- Pale plates at the eastern margin may break sharply.
- Turf can conceal water-filled cracks.
- Porous plaza slabs may be sound while their margins are undermined.

Walk slowly and keep your hands free. Avoid stepping on channel covers or the rims of public pools until they have been inspected. Never assume that shallow-looking clear water is shallow; without ripples, sediment, or fish, depth perception will be poor.

Cross marshes only on established causeways. If a causeway is submerged, turn back rather than cutting across sedges. Saturated ground can swallow a leg without looking like mud, and a simple ankle fracture would be serious here.

Do not travel after dusk. Wet black masonry will merge visually with the surrounding rock, while canals and wells will remain nearly invisible. Establish camp well before the light begins to flatten.

## Buildings and ruins

The structures appear sturdy, but they were built by weight and careful fitting rather than reinforced frames. Do not mistake intactness for modern safety.

Avoid:

- leaning against inward-sloping walls,
- walking beneath cracked lintels or saturated turf roofs,
- climbing stepped towers in wet conditions,
- entering rooms below canal level,
- standing on cistern covers,
- moving jars, bowls, grinding stones, or hardwood pins.

Roof timbers may have decayed internally while retaining their shape. Turf roofs can also become extremely heavy when saturated. Inspect from doorways first, and do not enter alone.

Treat the city as an inhabited cultural site whose residents are temporarily absent, not as an ownerless ruin. Do not take artifacts, drink from ceremonial pools, sleep in prominent courts, or alter channels. The water system may still depend on carefully balanced gradients; a single displaced stone could redirect flow through a building or garden.

If you need shelter, use a freestanding expedition tent on raised, bare ground. Place it away from walls, old vents, trees, and canal edges. Use a groundsheet with a bathtub floor, but avoid digging stakes into archaeological paving or cultivated soil. Weighted anchors are preferable.

## Altitude and physical pacing

Warm air can disguise the physiological effects of elevation. Move slowly on the first day, hydrate from your own supply, and monitor for:

- headache,
- nausea,
- unusual breathlessness,
- confusion,
- poor balance,
- disturbed vision.

Some of these symptoms overlap with gas exposure or unfamiliar toxins. Do not assume they are merely altitude sickness. If symptoms appear in more than one traveler or improve rapidly on reaching higher ground, suspect atmospheric pooling and evacuate.

The silence may also change how hard you work. Without wind, animals, crowds, or traffic, there are few external cues to pace or distance. Use timed rest intervals and objective heart-rate or oxygen measurements.

## Psychological preparation

Expect the absence of animal sound to feel more profound than the visual emptiness. There will be no buzzing near flowers, no startled movement in reeds, and no birds to indicate weather or direction. Your own breathing, clothing, and footsteps may seem intrusive.

Travel with at least one companion. Agree beforehand not to separate, even within apparently small buildings. Use calm spoken check-ins; complete silence can encourage fixation, anxiety, or the false impression that distant water sounds are footsteps.

The city does not appear violently abandoned, which may make it tempting to invent reassuring explanations. Resist that tendency. Record observations without assuming whether the population departed, vanished, died elsewhere, or intends to return.

## Biosecurity

Use strict two-way contamination controls. You may be the first animal organism—or the first carrier of an animal-associated microbiome—to enter the basin in a very long time.

- Clean boots and equipment before arrival.
- Do not release food scraps, wastewater, seeds, skin cultures, or human waste.
- Use sealed waste containers.
- Do not wash in canals or pools.
- Avoid touching flowers or spreading pollen between plants.
- Bag wet clothing and soil samples before departure.
- Decontaminate outside the destination universe rather than pouring disinfectants locally.

On return, quarantine yourself and your equipment until local spores, water, and plant residues have been assessed. The fact that nothing bites does not mean nothing can colonize a human lung, gut, or wound.

## Weather and geological precautions

Although the basin looks fixed at a perpetual post-rain equilibrium, assume that heavier rain is possible. The drainage works suggest water management mattered to the builders. A subtle rise in the water table could inundate low courtyards and isolate roads without producing a dramatic flood.

Watch for:

- water rising toward plaza level,
- newly clouded springs,
- increased dripping from fractures,
- sudden flow reversal in channels,
- cracking sounds in young rock,
- gas odors,
- unexplained plant wilting,
- tremors.

If any occur, move to an established high road and leave. Do not shelter under cliffs or in buildings incorporated into lava ridges during seismic activity.

## Best approach for a first visit

A prudent first expedition would last only a few daylight hours. Follow a raised main street from the arrival point to the central plaza, inspect the well from a safe distance, visit one open courtyard and one outer causeway, and return by the same route. Mark every turn without altering the masonry.

Later visits can extend toward the terraces, towers, and distant hamlets only after atmospheric, toxicological, and biological testing has explained why the basin contains plants but no animals.

Above all, bring more self-sufficiency than the landscape seems to require. This city is accommodating in form—its roads are broad, its water clear, its climate mild—but it offers no margin supplied by living companions. A twisted ankle, contaminated drink, lost return point, or failed waterproof seal would occur in a place where absolutely nothing is coming to help.
