An ordinary human should treat this basin not as a diving destination but as a cold, dark industrial worksite with no floor stable enough to trust and no surface reliably available for escape. The main danger is not spectacular depth or aggressive wildlife. It is the combination of cold, current, silt, poor orientation, shifting stones, and isolation.

## Choose the right way to enter

The safest visit is inside a small, rugged submersible or a pressure-rated atmospheric diving suit deployed from a well-equipped support vessel. Conventional scuba should be reserved for the shallowest margins, in calm weather, with local survey data and professional cold-water divers.

A submersible should have:

- Redundant propulsion, life support, power, and navigation
- Forward-looking sonar, since cameras may become useless in silt
- An acoustic beacon and independent emergency locator
- A drop weight or equivalent means of achieving positive buoyancy after power loss
- Protected propulsors that will not ingest kelp, lines, or gravel
- Strong low-mounted lights placed away from the viewing port to reduce backscatter
- Mechanical current measurement and depth/altitude alarms
- A manipulator or probe for testing sediment and stone stability
- At least double the expected mission duration in breathable atmosphere, heat, and battery reserve

Do not multiverse-transition directly into open water. Arrive inside a sealed craft, or at minimum inside a positively buoyant pressure shelter with a verified return mechanism. A misplaced arrival only a few metres above the floor could still place you in a current, beneath ice, or inside a dense sediment cloud. If interdimensional return depends on visual landmarks, radio contact, or an object remaining where it was dropped, it is unsuitable here.

## Survey before descending

Map the basin remotely first. Use sonar from the surface and, if possible, an expendable or tethered ROV. Identify:

- Depth and pressure throughout the route
- Moraine ridges and narrow channels where currents accelerate
- Boulder fields that can trap a vehicle or tether
- Soft clay pockets and unstable sand banks
- Ice cover and possible openings to the surface
- Current direction at several depths and at both phases of the tide
- Water chemistry, salinity, dissolved gases, and any unfamiliar biological hazards

Do not assume the currents reverse cleanly or on schedule. Basin shape can create eddies, delayed reversals, and dense bottom flows moving differently from the water above them. A calm surface does not imply a calm bottom.

Establish a fixed acoustic navigation grid. Magnetic compasses may be unreliable around basaltic rock, and visual navigation will fail as soon as silt rises. Dead reckoning alone is not enough.

## Time the visit conservatively

Go during the calmest season and weather window available, preferably without overhead ice. Plan the dive around tidal slack, but expect deep water to continue moving after the surface tide appears to pause.

Abort if:

- A storm is approaching
- Ice is shifting or closing over the recovery area
- Bottom visibility is already deteriorating
- Currents exceed the vehicle’s ability to hold position with a large safety margin
- Sonar contact with the support team becomes intermittent
- Any navigation, heating, or buoyancy system behaves unpredictably

The basin rewards patience and punishes attempts to “finish quickly” after conditions turn.

## Dress and equip for cold

Even inside a vehicle, bring insulated clothing, gloves, head covering, chemical or electrical heat packs, emergency oxygen, water, and compact food. A disabled craft will cool steadily through its hull.

For any direct exposure to the water, use a properly fitted drysuit with redundant thermal protection rather than a wetsuit. Include:

- Dry gloves and hood
- Full-face mask
- Independent backup breathing supply
- Environmentally sealed regulators rated for near-freezing water
- Redundant buoyancy
- Cutting tools accessible with either hand
- A strobe and acoustic pinger
- A reel or tether designed not to snag easily
- A heated decompression plan if depth and duration require one

Cold-water equipment can free-flow, freeze, or stiffen. Practice failures before the expedition. An ordinary traveler should not learn drysuit control, tether management, or zero-visibility breathing in this basin.

Because the depth is unspecified, assume pressure may require decompression planning until measured otherwise. If the basin is beyond normal recreational limits, use a closed vehicle or atmospheric suit rather than mixed-gas diving unless you are part of a specialist team.

## Expect blindness, not scenery

The weak blue-gray light will not help much. Powerful lights may make visibility worse by illuminating suspended silt directly in front of you. Use wide, low-angle lighting and rely on sonar for the larger landscape.

Move slowly. Thrusters, fins, boots, or a manipulator can turn a marginal view into total brownout. Once disturbed, fine glacial sediment may remain suspended long enough to erase both your route and your companions.

Do not interpret pale shapes too quickly. Density layers can bend light, and moving curtains of sediment can make stones appear suspended or displaced. Confirm distances with sonar or a physical rangefinder before approaching.

The experience will be intimate rather than panoramic: a few cobbles, a strip of sand moving like smoke, perhaps a fish revealed for a second. The vastness of the basin will mostly be inferred through sonar and the absence of boundaries.

## Do not trust the bottom

Rounded does not mean stable. Cobbles can roll under weight, sand banks can slump, and narrow gaps may conceal strong flow. Never place a hand, foot, landing skid, or anchor where movement could trap it.

Avoid:

- Standing downhill from boulders
- Entering gaps between tightly packed rocks
- Parking on loose sand or shell channels
- Passing under overhangs scoured from moraine material
- Attaching lines to apparently solid cobbles
- Following a channel merely because it offers better visibility

If you must land a submersible, probe the site first and maintain enough power to lift off immediately. Prefer hovering above the floor. Keep all cables and tethers clear of stones; the reversing current can wrap them around obstacles that were harmless on the outward leg.

Small stones will not remain as markers. Flags, weights, sample bags, and tools can move or be buried. Every important object should carry a beacon or be physically secured to the vehicle.

## Navigation and communication

Maintain continuous contact with the surface using acoustic communications. Carry at least two independent ways to locate the vehicle or diver. Establish simple lost-contact rules before descent—for example, hold position briefly, ascend to a prearranged depth if possible, then activate emergency beacons.

Never let the visible glow from above substitute for a bearing. Under cloud, storm, ice, or sediment, it can disappear abruptly. Even when present, it may only indicate “up,” not the direction of safety.

Record the route automatically rather than depending on memory. The terrain repeats: rounded stone, low ridges, sediment channels, and darkness. One gravel plain will look much like another, especially after the tide reverses and alters the sediment patterns.

## Surface support is essential

The support vessel should have:

- Dynamic positioning or a safe mooring plan
- Weather and ice monitoring
- Sonar tracking of the submersible or divers
- Heated recovery space
- Medical oxygen and hypothermia treatment equipment
- A hyperbaric chamber if exposed diving is involved
- A second recovery system if the primary winch fails
- Personnel capable of recovering an unconscious, heavily equipped person
- A written plan for entanglement, loss of propulsion, loss of communications, and unexpected ice closure

Someone topside must have sole authority to cancel the mission. The visitor’s fascination with the basin should never outrank the recovery window.

## Biological and chemical caution

The known animals are mostly inconspicuous and unlikely to seek confrontation. Do not become complacent, however. Crabs can pinch, bottom fish may have defensive spines, seals may investigate, and any unfamiliar shark-shaped animal should be given distance. The larger ecological risk is stepping on or crushing life you cannot see.

Wear puncture-resistant gloves if sampling, but do not put hands beneath stones or into crevices. Hidden animals will defend themselves, and rolling a stone may expose delicate films that took years to establish.

Test the water before trusting local descriptions such as “salt,” “mud,” or “decaying weed.” Another universe may contain unfamiliar toxins, microbes, or dissolved gases. Use closed breathing systems and avoid swallowing water or bringing untreated samples into living quarters.

Decontaminate vehicles, suits, samplers, and ballast before and after the trip. Introduced microorganisms could alter a sparse ecosystem dramatically. Do not release waste, food, lubricants, or terrestrial ballast water.

## Take samples sparingly

This landscape changes slowly in biological terms even though sediment is always moving. A thin crust or dark stain may represent a long-lived community. Photograph and scan before touching anything.

If sampling is justified:

- Take the smallest quantity needed
- Prefer loose sediment over attached growth
- Record exact depth, temperature, chemistry, and position
- Use sealed, pressure-compatible containers
- Quarantine samples on return
- Never overturn stones without restoring them as closely as possible
- Do not collect the rare larger animals merely because they are unusual

Do not build cairns, drive stakes, or leave memorial objects. The basin may eventually round and bury them, but that does not make the intrusion harmless.

## Personal preparation

Before departure, train for:

- Cold-water survival
- Confined-space emergencies
- Zero-visibility orientation
- Sonar interpretation
- Hypothermia recognition
- Entanglement escape
- Emergency ascent and recovery procedures
- The psychological effects of darkness, pressure, and isolation

The basin may feel more oppressive than its measured depth suggests. There are few visual horizons, no recognizable structures, and almost nothing motionless to anchor the mind. Expect mild disorientation and a distorted sense of time. Keep the first visit short and task-focused.

Inside the craft, secure all loose equipment. Deep knocks and transmitted ice groans can sound like hull failure; learn the vehicle’s normal sounds beforehand so that genuine structural alarms are not confused with the environment.

## A sensible first itinerary

A first human visit should be deliberately modest:

1. Descend in a submersible along a previously mapped route.
2. Pause well above the bottom to assess current, visibility, and vehicle response.
3. Cross one gravel plain using sonar.
4. Observe the lee side of a moraine from several metres away.
5. Approach, but do not enter, a current-tightened channel.
6. Make a short stationary observation with engines reduced, listening for stone movement and ice noise.
7. Collect only water and a small sediment core, if conditions permit.
8. Ascend with at least half of all critical reserves intact.

Do not spend the first visit searching for the deepest pocket, following a large animal, or threading between boulders. Nothing here needs to be conquered.

The best preparation is to accept what the place offers: not a grand view, but fragments. A pale stone emerging from silt, kelp bending through a tidal reversal, the tiny movement of a camouflaged fish, a boulder knocking somewhere beyond sight. Visit as though the basin is always in the act of erasing your route—because it is.
