This is not a ruin to explore casually. Treat it as a live industrial plant inside an unstable cave: hot, wet, electrically active, mechanically autonomous, and built without modern human access clearances. Its emptiness removes the usual warning signs—no workers, animals, or voices react before something starts moving.

## Best time to visit

Visit during the middle of a dry interval, after the pumps have cleared the lower alleys but before prolonged drying produces heavy salt dust and brittle surface flaking.

Maintain a weather station on the black-gravel plain throughout the visit. Rain at the surface is an underground hazard even when seepage has not yet appeared below. Because the delay is unpredictable, set a conservative rule: if rain begins anywhere over the relevant fracture field, the underground party starts withdrawing immediately.

Do not enter:

- During surface rain or shortly afterward.
- When ceiling beads are multiplying or dry channels are darkening.
- When lower alleys already contain continuous standing water.
- If ventilation fans, pumps, or indicator systems have stopped unexpectedly.
- During unusual surface venting, tremors, or repeated stone falls.

The illumination of many green indicators at once should be regarded as an evacuation signal. It means the wet cycle is propagating through the district, not that the machinery has made the area safe.

## Survey before entry

Send instruments first. Confirm that local gravity, atmospheric pressure, oxygen chemistry, and ordinary electrical behavior are compatible with human equipment before committing a person.

A remote crawler or tethered drone should check:

- Oxygen, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, combustible vapors, and unidentified aerosols.
- Air temperature and humidity at floor, head, and upper-gallery levels.
- Energized floors, rails, standing water, cabinets, and exposed pipework.
- The condition of the intended exit.
- Active gates, conveyors, flywheels, fans, and sluices.
- Areas of recent rockfall or freshly opened cracks.

Do not use surface exhaust vents as entrances. They may open automatically and can discharge extremely hot, humid, contaminated air.

## Party and communications

No one should enter alone. Use at least three underground travelers, with another person remaining at the surface portal. The surface watch must monitor weather, maintain a travel log, and have authority to order an immediate return.

Radio will be unreliable among basalt, tanks, and steel frames. Lay a protected communication cable or place repeaters as you advance. Carry an inertial mapper and make a three-dimensional route map: ordinary left-and-right directions become confusing when streets are catwalks and roofs serve as roads.

Establish a refuge and turnaround point in a high, dry chamber close to the exit. Mark two withdrawal routes if possible, both avoiding the lowest layer. Never allow the whole team to descend into a sump district simultaneously.

## Clothing and equipment

Wear equipment suited to heat, sharp stone, arc hazards, and confined-space rescue:

- Arc-rated, cut-resistant coveralls with no loose straps.
- A climbing helmet with chin strap, eye protection, and neck protection from flakes.
- Electrically insulating, oil-resistant boots with strong ankle support and puncture-resistant soles.
- Heat-resistant, cut-resistant gloves.
- Knee and elbow protection for low passages.
- A cooling vest or circulating cooling garment.
- A full-face respirator or independent breathing apparatus, selected after atmospheric testing.
- Continuous personal gas, temperature, humidity, and physiological monitors.
- Intrinsically safe headlamps, two backup lights per person, and diffuse area lights.
- A thermal camera and non-contact voltage detector.
- A short nonconductive probe for checking puddle depth and surfaces.
- A compact harness, rescue line, and equipment designed not to snag on valves or pipe brackets.
- Sterile drinking water and electrolytes carried in sealed containers.
- Burn dressings, eye irrigation, trauma supplies, and treatment provisions for heat illness.

Remove rings, necklaces, watches with exposed metal bands, and other conductive jewelry. Handrails may be hot enough to burn and cannot be assumed electrically safe.

Because sweat will evaporate poorly, ordinary intuition will underestimate heat strain. Set work-rest intervals from measured conditions, not from how tolerable the first few minutes feel. A traveler who becomes clumsy, irritable, confused, nauseated, or suddenly stops sweating should be cooled and evacuated at once.

## How to move through the district

Move slowly. Pipes cross at head and shoulder height, floor channels may be hidden by dark water, and sharp basalt can cut clothing with little force.

Assume that:

- Every machine can start without warning.
- Every gate can close.
- Every channel can suddenly carry hot or contaminated water.
- Every pipe may be hot, pressurized, or energized.
- Every puddle may conceal a shaft, rail trench, or boiler foundation.
- Every cabinet has arc-flash potential.
- Every apparently secure slab may have been loosened by salt growth.

Stay outside mesh guards and painted machinery clearances, even when the equipment appears motionless. Do not step over conveyor chains, between piston linkages, beneath counterweights, or through automatic gates. Never use pipes, valves, cable trays, or railings as climbing anchors.

Avoid touching the rock unnecessarily. Do not hammer, drill, drive pitons, or wedge open load-bearing doors. The ceiling is low because the seam is carrying weight; apparently minor disturbance may trigger a fresh fracture. A dry cracking report, falling grit, or sudden new seepage warrants immediate retreat from that chamber.

Use established stairs and catwalks only after inspecting their supports. Older brick may be weakened by salts, while newer steel may be hot or electrically connected to plant systems.

## Water and biology

Do not drink any water found inside, including condensation that appears clean. It may contain dissolved metal salts, hot-water microorganisms, machine oil, ceramic dust, or process chemicals. Avoid skin contact with orange films and black-green gutter mats. They are the settlement’s most active organisms and may have unfamiliar biochemistry.

Do not collect samples during the first visit. If sampling is later authorized, use sealed tools and perform full decontamination. Treat oily water, mineral crusts, and microbial films as both chemical and biological hazards.

Keep food sealed and remove all waste. The absence of animals should not be interpreted as sterility; it is evidence that the environment is unusually selective.

## Reading the machinery

The district communicates through small changes. Learn them before descending:

- **Ceiling points darkening:** seepage is beginning.
- **Pipes and lintels sweating simultaneously:** humidity is rising throughout the network.
- **Float mechanisms lifting:** low basins are filling, whether or not water is visible nearby.
- **Multiple green indicators appearing:** automatic wet-cycle operations are engaging.
- **A deeper pump stroke or water booming through a main:** large volumes are being redirected; keep away from channels and gates.
- **Fans accelerating or vents opening:** heat or vapor is accumulating.
- **A sudden complete silence:** potentially more dangerous than normal operation; power, ventilation, or pump coordination may have failed.
- **Repeated relay chatter or electrical flashes:** withdraw from that gallery and any connected wet floor.

Do not operate switches or valves. The plant is a closed interdependent system, and a control that appears local may regulate water or pressure elsewhere. Likewise, do not remove abandoned tools, braces, baskets, or pieces of scrap. In this place, discarded material may still function as a counterweight, patch, spacer, or flood marker.

## Emergency priorities

If flooding begins, do not race directly toward the portal through the lowest route. Move first to the predetermined high withdrawal path, remaining clear of sluices and automatic gates. Water need not be deep to be lethal if it is hot, electrically energized, or moving through rail trenches.

If an arc occurs, do not approach the cabinet or touch a person still in contact with metalwork. Isolate or withdraw before attempting rescue.

If machinery catches clothing or equipment, use emergency cutting tools rather than pulling against the mechanism. Do not reach through a guard.

If rock begins falling, move deliberately out of the fall line without striking walls or crowding the next person. Running in the narrow passages is likely to create a second casualty.

If pumps or ventilation cease unexpectedly, leave. Do not remain to diagnose the fault. Without active pumping and heat management, conditions may deteriorate faster than the compact size of the district suggests.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the lack of a horizon to be psychologically tiring. The settlement is small in area but visually endless because routes double back above and below one another. Heat will seem to press downward from the ceiling even while moisture rises underfoot. Quiet intervals may make the place feel inert, but they are only gaps between automatic actions.

The safest attitude is to regard the whole district as one enormous machine whose rooms happen to be large enough for people. Observe from its upper margins, never trust apparent stillness, and leave before the rain reaches the lamps.
