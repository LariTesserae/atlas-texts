## General assessment

Prepare as though entering **a flooded factory that is still operating, inside a living organism that may also be operating it**.

The basin is not overtly hostile, but almost nothing in it is passive. Footing can fail into black water; paths can disappear; machinery may move after minutes or hours of apparent stillness; dampness makes every old circuit suspect; and the orderly vegetation suggests maintenance, territorial behavior, or intelligence. The absence of animals is especially concerning. It may indicate toxins, ecological exclusion, or conditions that ordinary Earth organisms cannot tolerate.

Do not visit alone, and do not plan to sleep inside.

## Before entry

### Establish a staffed exit

Choose one passage through the rim and keep a person outside it for the entire visit. That person should have:

- A clear view of the opening
- A radio and hard-line signal cord
- Rescue gear and a mechanical winch
- A record of entry time and scheduled check-ins
- Authority to order immediate withdrawal

Do not assume the doorway will remain open merely because it was open when you entered. Place a freestanding, nonconductive frame in the passage if this can be done without cutting the braid. Watch for the vegetation beginning to incorporate or compress it.

Keep any dimensional return apparatus outside the basin, on firm, dry ground. The interior is too wet, crowded, electrically active, and biologically acquisitive to trust with a portal anchor.

### Observe one full machine cycle

Before stepping far inside, watch until the flywheel has completed at least one revolution. Time:

- The interval between mechanical reports
- The sequence of pump sounds
- Vat-filling events
- Changes in water level
- Flower opening
- Lamps switching on
- Any movement of belts, vines, rails, or walkways

Do not infer that a mechanism is stopped because it has not moved for ten minutes. Here, “operating speed” may be measured in hours.

If practical, observe through both daylight and a rain event before entering. Heavy rain may alter water pressure, electrical leakage, or the tension on roof structures.

## Clothing and equipment

Wear gear suited to warm wetlands, industrial ruins, and unknown biology:

- Waterproof, breathable coveralls with sealed cuffs
- Tough nitrile-lined gloves under cut-resistant outer gloves
- High boots with broad, flexible soles and ankle support
- Gaiters that cannot catch on tendrils
- Eye protection with anti-fog treatment
- A light climbing helmet
- A compact respirator with particulate and organic-vapor filtration
- Hearing protection for the mechanical impacts
- A buoyancy vest or low-profile flotation collar
- A chest harness with a quick-release tether
- No loose scarves, dangling straps, exposed laces, or mesh pockets

Bring your own drinking water. The basin’s water may look clear when first pressed from the mat, but it passes through roots, fungal cords, corroded machinery, and unknown metabolic systems. Do not drink it, wash wounds in it, or allow it into your mouth.

Carry:

- Two waterproof lights, even by day
- A nonconductive walking probe with a broad foot
- A short rescue line and compact hauling system
- Waterproof radios
- Chemical light markers or reflective tags
- A compass, inertial tracker, and elapsed-time watch
- Voltage detector and basic gas monitor
- First-aid supplies, including wound irrigation, antihistamine, and epinephrine if trained to use it
- A hooked rescue knife for cutting your own clothing or tether
- Sterile sample containers, if sampling is authorized
- Sealable outer bags for contaminated equipment

Avoid exposed steel tools where possible. The rails spark, old circuits may still be energized, and metal appears readily incorporated into the local growth. Do not carry open flames, herbicide, insect repellent aerosols, or unnecessary organic cordage.

## Movement inside

Travel in a group of at least three:

1. A lead traveler testing footing
2. A second traveler watching overhead growth and machinery
3. A rear traveler maintaining navigation and contact with the exit

A fourth person should remain outside.

Move slowly enough that each foot placement is tested before weight is transferred. The root mat may support a person in one place and open directly into water a step away. Do not stamp or jump; pressure summons water and may also load buried pipes.

Treat every surface according to its underlying hazard:

- **Overlapping giant leaves:** possibly strong, but slippery and liable to split along veins
- **Lichen-softened planks:** potentially rotten beneath an intact surface
- **Moss-covered stone:** slick and often hollowed by drip cups
- **Root mats:** buoyant or suspended over water
- **Rails:** possible electrical conductors
- **Corrugated roofing:** overloaded by ponds, roots, and trees
- **Vines resembling cable:** assume they may be either alive, energized, tensioned, or all three

Use existing paths, but do not trust them automatically. A path may be a service route for the unseen operator rather than a safe route for a human.

Never place hands inside foliage where you cannot see them. White roots in control boxes, braided vines around pipes, and growth holding switch levers should be considered functional components.

## Navigation

Ordinary visual navigation will be unreliable. Distances are obscured, landmarks repeat, and the vegetation may close behind you.

Use redundant methods:

- Maintain a lightly tensioned guide line back to the entrance, routed high enough not to sink into the root mat
- Attach only temporary, chemically inert markers
- Log turns and elapsed travel time
- Use the six columns, engine house, vats, and surviving rails as major references
- Photograph backward at every junction so the return view is documented
- Check radio contact frequently

The guide line must have a quick release. A tightening vine or moving machine component could turn a safety line into a trap.

Do not use biodegradable breadcrumbs, paint, cut branches, or piles of leaves. They may be absorbed, rearranged, or interpreted as interference.

If your outward route appears substantially altered, withdraw immediately. Do not continue on the assumption that another doorway will be available at the rim.

## Conduct around the installation

Assume the facility is occupied.

There may be no visible workers, but polished bearings, cleared drains, fresh filament, braided vines, and functioning lamps indicate continuing maintenance. The “worker” may be concealed, nocturnal, plant-based, distributed through the basin, or simply operating on a timescale too slow to notice.

Therefore:

- Do not throw switches
- Do not turn valve wheels
- Do not stop or touch the flywheel
- Do not step over or under the belt
- Do not remove fresh spools or fiber cylinders
- Do not clear drains “helpfully”
- Do not unbraid vines from pipes
- Do not enter between vats during a fill cycle
- Do not stand beneath sagging roof ponds
- Do not lean against columns being drawn together by vines

Keep well back from pressure lines and buried pipes when the mechanical report sounds. That report may represent a tension release through the entire installation. Pause on the most stable available footing before each expected impact, with hands free and knees slightly bent.

The amber lamps may be status indicators rather than illumination. If one activates near you, note the time and location and consider withdrawing. Do not cover or inspect it closely.

## Biological precautions

Avoid direct skin contact with sap, seed filaments, fungal cords, and black water. Tendrils touching clothing may be ordinary climbing behavior, but repeated or coordinated contact should be treated as exploratory behavior.

Do not cut living growth unless it is necessary to free a person. Cutting may:

- Release irritating sap or spores
- Damage a functioning pressure or signal system
- Provoke rapid sealing of the route
- Be perceived as vandalism or injury

If something begins winding around a limb, stop moving first. Pulling may trigger tightening. Have another traveler determine whether it is hooked, adhesive, actively contracting, or simply caught on clothing. Cut your outer garment before cutting the organism if possible.

Take no food into the basin except sealed emergency rations, and eat only outside. Do not urinate, spit, or leave blood, hair, bandages, or other biological material. In a system that efficiently incorporates every fallen object, human tissue may become a durable introduction.

Sampling should be minimal and preferably limited to water, shed material, or already detached rust-root composites. Never sample from:

- Growth attached to switches or bearings
- Neatly braided vines
- Freshly wound filament
- Flowers synchronized with the flywheel
- The root barricade of the engine house

Those are the features most likely to be functional or communicative.

## Warning signs requiring immediate withdrawal

Leave at once if any of the following occurs:

- The flywheel changes speed or reverses
- The mechanical knock comes earlier than expected
- Water rises across multiple areas simultaneously
- Leaves throughout the basin turn toward your party
- Tendrils begin making repeated contact rather than incidental brushes
- Your guide line becomes taut without explanation
- The entrance braid begins closing visibly
- Multiple lamps illuminate
- Vats fill out of sequence
- The rails spark in daylight or under your feet
- A mineral, sulfurous, solvent-like, or strongly sweet odor intensifies
- Animal life suddenly appears in numbers—or all minor animal activity abruptly ceases
- Team members experience confusion, unusual calm, time loss, skin numbness, or synchronized auditory sensations
- The basin’s ordinary water sounds stop

The last sign may be more serious than a loud noise. Continuous water is the place’s baseline; silence would indicate a system-wide change.

## Duration and overnight risk

Keep the first visit under one flywheel cycle beyond the observation period. Later visits can be extended only after comparing timing, exposure symptoms, and route changes.

Do not camp inside. At night:

- Navigation worsens sharply
- Sparks travel along buried rails
- roof and plant movement increases
- the pump cycle is harder to localize
- low-frequency impacts may be disorienting
- closing passages will be difficult to detect

If stranded, do not shelter in the engine house, beneath the roof, beside tanks, or on the rails. Choose the highest exposed stone shelf available, away from pipes and large tensioned vines. Use no fire. Maintain a watch continuously and conserve light for route recognition.

## Decontamination

On exit, assume you are carrying viable spores, seeds, fungal threads, conductive slime, and possibly plant structures too small to recognize.

At the rim:

1. Photograph and inspect each traveler before removing clothing.
2. Bag outer garments and boots separately.
3. Remove all seed filaments and visible roots with tools, not bare fingers.
4. Flush exposed skin and wounds with imported sterile water.
5. Isolate samples in double containment.
6. Quarantine personnel and equipment from local agriculture and natural ecosystems.
7. Monitor for rashes, persistent damp patches, unusual fibers in wounds, metallic discoloration, altered sleep cycles, or symptoms recurring at the flywheel’s measured interval.

Do not discard contaminated wash water into the destination world.

## Best attitude to bring

Do not approach the basin as wilderness reclaiming a ruin. That description is probably wrong. The installation and the vegetation appear to have reached an accommodation so complete that distinctions such as machine, crop, infrastructure, and worker may no longer apply.

Be quiet, predictable, and conservative. Follow established paths. Touch as little as possible. Take nothing that appears maintained. Leave nothing that can grow.

Most importantly, remember that the place’s slowness is not inactivity. A human visit may be brief enough that the basin seems indifferent—while still being fully noticed.
