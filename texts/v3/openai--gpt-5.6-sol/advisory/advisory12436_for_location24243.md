Treat the cleft as four hazards at once: a high-altitude climb, a live electrical installation, a confined acoustic chamber, and an uncontaminated alien biosphere. Its stillness should not be mistaken for safety. The garden is actively maintaining an equilibrium that excludes ordinary water-based life—including you.

## Before approaching

Do not attempt the visit alone. Keep at least one partner outside the cleft, secured well back from the precipice, with authority to order an immediate withdrawal. Establish a rescue plan that does not require an unprotected rescuer to enter.

The elevation may be lethal before the garden is reached. Acclimatization is preferable, but not sufficient if the summit atmosphere is extremely thin. Use a closed-cycle pressure suit with independent oxygen, carbon-dioxide removal, cooling, and humidity recovery. Do not rely on respirators or bottled oxygen delivered into an open mask: the air contains ozone, mineral dust, unknown sulfur chemistry, and potentially unusual oxidation states.

Before the expedition, obtain baseline examinations of:

- hearing and vestibular function;
- heart rhythm and blood pressure;
- lung function;
- skin and mucous membranes;
- hydration and blood electrolytes.

Anyone with arrhythmia, severe tinnitus, vertigo, panic reactions in confined spaces, or implanted electronic medical equipment should not enter. The constant low-frequency sound may affect the chest and balance even when it is not painfully loud.

## Suit and equipment

Use a smooth, fully sealed suit that sheds nothing. The exterior should be abrasion-resistant and chemically inert, with no exposed fabric, loose straps, hook-and-loop fasteners, or absorbent padding. Mineral hooks and glass filaments could snag ordinary climbing clothing.

Because the chamber is electrically active, avoid exposed metal and long continuous conductors. A suitable suit should have:

- a segmented, resistively damped electrical shield rather than an unbroken conductive shell that could become part of the chamber’s resonance;
- dielectric outer boots and gloves;
- isolated, shielded life-support electronics;
- no trailing copper communications cable;
- no uncovered metal carabiners inside the chamber.

Carry redundant oxygen and power, but keep the load compact. A large backpack will catch on the growths and may not fit through the slate-concealed opening.

Hearing protection alone will not solve the acoustic problem. Use a sealed helmet with passive low-frequency damping, active cancellation if it has been tested against stable industrial tones, and vibration-isolated contact points. The drone may still be felt through the soles, suit frame, and chest.

Normal speech may be swallowed. Arrange visual and tactile communication:

- helmet text display;
- hand signals;
- haptic alerts;
- a hard-coded “withdraw immediately” vibration pattern;
- continuous biometric telemetry to the outside observer.

Use optical instruments where possible. Avoid powerful radio transmitters, induction equipment, active electrical probes, flash units, and anything that injects a changing field into the chamber. The garden and machinery are already one resonant electrical body; do not discover how it responds to modulation.

## Sterility matters in both directions

Nothing animal has apparently entered this garden before. A human visitor could introduce bacteria, skin oils, salts, fungal spores, textile fibers, and water vapor into a system with no evolutionary experience of them.

Enter in a sterilized suit. Remain closed-cycle throughout. Do not vent breath, sweat, cooling vapor, urine, or equipment exhaust. Even a damp fingerprint is chemically dismantled and distributed into the garden. Exposed skin would be both contamination and feedstock.

The reverse hazard is equally serious. Dust consists of charged mineral grains, and some “moss” is made of hollow glass filaments carrying sulfur compounds. Do not assume that something immobile cannot penetrate a seal or remain attached to it.

No eating, drinking, smoking, open flame, or exposed water should be permitted in or near the entrance. Water may not poison the garden in an obvious way; it may simply be separated and incorporated, producing subtle and permanent chemical changes.

## Reaching the entrance

The threshold is one of the most immediately dangerous parts of the visit. It hangs above a fall of several kilometers, and the slate fan conceals the opening until close approach.

Install redundant anchors in confirmed dead exterior rock, not in mineral vines, machinery, or apparently solid growth. Every person near the opening should remain attached to two independent lines. Use short tethers so a fall cannot develop momentum.

Do not anchor through the chamber itself. A rope laid across crystalline foliage could damage it, acquire reactive dust, or mechanically couple to the standing vibration.

The first scout should inspect the descending steps and the apparent “floor” with noncontact imaging. Much of the chamber may consist of enclosed machinery, hollow mats, corroded walkway, or vegetation bridging open gaps. Stable-looking surfaces may not support human weight. Never test them by stamping.

If a remote probe is used, it should be small, sterile, tetherless, and considered expendable—but only after deciding that leaving it behind would be acceptable. The local equilibrium appears very good at absorbing foreign objects. Do not assume a probe that enters can be recovered unchanged.

## Conduct inside

Make the first entry brief: five to ten minutes, with a predetermined turnaround time. The chamber is only thirty paces across, but movement will be slow and every path is obstructed.

Stay near the entrance until the body’s response to the drone is known. Withdraw immediately for:

- chest pressure beyond simple vibration;
- irregular heartbeat;
- dizziness or a sense of tilting;
- nausea, confusion, panic, or visual flicker;
- unexplained suit heating;
- static discharge or electronic instability;
- sudden loss of communications;
- any change in the drone’s tone.

Do not use the apparent constancy of the sound as reassurance. If the note changes, you may have altered the equilibrium. If it does not change, harmful energy may still be coupling into your body or equipment.

Move slowly. Keep three points of contact only on surfaces previously designated safe, and touch those through inert pads rather than directly with gloves. Dust disturbed by a boot may remain suspended in the charged air and coat the visor, seals, and joints.

Avoid, in particular:

- the central iron mast and its pale fins;
- the converter housing;
- conduits entering the mountain;
- porcelain insulators;
- enclosed portions of the old walkway;
- cabinets held open by roots;
- anything resembling a cable, rod, scale, or glass filament.

These are not separate categories of “machine” and “plant.” Electrically and chemically, they may all be active organs of the same system.

Do not cut, scrape, collect, bend, or mark anything. Reproduction by contact suggests that a fragment may not be inert, and a specimen removed to a wetter environment could behave unpredictably. Sampling might also interrupt exchanges that have remained balanced for geological spans.

Photography should be passive and low-power. Record from fixed positions without flash. A good visit leaves no flag, plaque, chalk mark, piton, sensor, or dropped tool.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the chamber to feel smaller than it is. The foliage will not move aside, and the noise will remove many of the ordinary cues by which people judge space. Your own footsteps may seem incomplete. Speech will be felt in the jaw without sounding external. The fixed gray light and absence of shifting shadows can distort time.

The strongest psychological impression may be that the garden is attending to you. It probably is not doing so in any animal sense. Nevertheless, you will be the only warm, wet, rapidly changing object in a room where every exposed surface has been integrated into a stable network. That mismatch can produce intense feelings of intrusion or surveillance.

Do not follow impulses to touch the central column, “check whether the machinery is alive,” or remain until something happens. The notable fact is that nothing happens. Preserve that fact.

## Exit and decontamination

Count every piece of equipment before leaving the threshold. If anything has been dropped, do not improvise a retrieval that requires touching the growth or crossing uncertain ground. Record its location and withdraw.

At a protected station outside:

1. Place the entire suited visitor in a sealed inert-gas enclosure.
2. Photograph and electrically scan the suit before cleaning.
3. Remove charged dust with controlled electrostatic capture and gentle inert-gas flow.
4. Do not begin by spraying water or solvent onto the suit.
5. Inspect seams and joints for glass filaments, ceramic hooks, crystalline scales, discoloration, or new conductive paths.
6. Quarantine the suit rather than bringing it directly into a vehicle or inhabited world.
7. Monitor the traveler for respiratory irritation, skin changes, tinnitus, arrhythmia, unusual dehydration, and persistent sensations of vibration.

Any adhered growth or dust should remain contained. Do not culture it, hydrate it, or connect it to electrical power.

Finally, compare the recorded drone before, during, and after entry. A change too small to hear may reveal that the visitor left heat, charge, matter, or structural strain behind. If there is any persistent alteration, do not make a second visit.

The safest successful expedition is a short observation from the entrance, performed in a sterile sealed suit, with no contact and no sampling. This is not a ruin with plants growing over it. It is one old, dry, electrically living arrangement—and a human body is exactly the kind of uncontrolled chemistry it has spent its entire existence without.
