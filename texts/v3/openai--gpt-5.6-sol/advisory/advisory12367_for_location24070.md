## Travel classification

Treat this chamber as a **living, spatially unstable nursery**, not as an ordinary cave. Its dangers are subtle rather than violent: altered distance, unreliable direction, predictive shadows, biologically active clay, and an ecology in which even a footstep may interrupt several processes at once.

**Never visit alone.** A two-person team is ideal. Larger groups would overcrowd the chamber, while an odd-numbered team risks leaving one traveler unable to cross the exact center. A robot, drone, or carried plant should not be assumed to satisfy the chamber’s requirement for paired living creatures.

The visit should be brief, noninvasive, and planned as an observation rather than an expedition.

---

## Before transit

### Use phase entry, not excavation

There is no natural entrance, and one should not be made. Drilling or tunneling could:

- destabilize a chamber whose walls are still forming;
- expose it to outside air and organisms;
- interrupt the mineral-softening cycle;
- create a permanent route for its residents—or its spatial laws—to escape.

Use a transit method that exchanges no rock and leaves no open portal. The return mechanism should be carried on each traveler rather than fixed to a wall or floor. Install an independent anchor outside the chamber and require **simultaneous team extraction**.

Do not rely on “return to the place where we entered.” Place is not entirely dependable here.

### Quarantine preparation

Assume the chamber has had no biological contact with the surface world.

Wear:

- a sealed, positive-pressure environmental suit;
- a full hood with independent air;
- smooth outer surfaces without exposed fabric, tread cavities, or Velcro;
- broad, low-pressure soles to avoid sinking into fresh clay;
- double containment for all instruments;
- eye protection with adjustable neutral-density and contrast filters.

Sterilize the suit exterior without leaving residual biocide. Strong disinfectants entering the pools could erase much of the ecology.

Do not bring food, exposed paper, untreated wood, seeds, leather, feathers, or living test animals. Carry no unnecessary organic material. In particular, do not use a living animal as a convenient “companion” for crossing the center.

### Train as a pair

Practice moving shoulder to shoulder, turning together, kneeling together, and retreating without breaking visual contact. Use a short, slack connecting line attached at the waist. It should prevent accidental separation but must release instantly if space folds around one traveler.

Agree on simple hand signals. The chamber is quiet enough that speech will carry, but loud voices may disturb the animals and obscure the faint geological sounds that provide warning.

## Essential equipment

Bring only equipment that can be accounted for individually:

- independent atmosphere and radiation monitors;
- a low-profile body camera on each traveler;
- inertial recorders and physiological monitors;
- a compact rigidity frame or folding shield;
- broad-soled overshoes;
- a soft sampling tray only if collection has been specifically authorized;
- a synchronized extraction control requiring confirmation from both travelers;
- a continuous-route recorder rather than numbered survey markers.

Avoid loose items. Nothing should be set on the ground unless necessary.

Do not depend on counted inventories such as “seven sample tubes” or “four markers.” Give every object a unique name, color, and pattern. The chamber corrupts numerical consistency, and attempts to resolve discrepancies may merely add new things to the count.

Conventional compasses, rangefinders, and plumb lines may give locally valid but mutually contradictory readings. Record them, but do not navigate by them alone.

## Arrival

Materialize over a clear patch of established, hard mineral:

- away from the exact center;
- away from all pools and damp stains;
- away from the wall-floor boundary;
- away from bone arches, pebble rings, stem enclosures, and insect-lined niches;
- away from soft reddish or blue-gray material.

Do not arrive against a wall. Predators occupy the shaded margins, and newly formed stone may harden around foreign objects.

Remain still for at least a minute after entry. Let your eyes adapt and allow the local animals to reveal themselves. The chamber will initially seem empty because much of its life is motionless, translucent, or visually merged with shade.

Check air, suit integrity, temperature, and radiation before proceeding. Even if the atmosphere proves breathable, keep the suit sealed.

## Vision and orientation

The illumination will make the chamber feel flatter and more exposed than it is. The empty center is brightest, while solid surfaces carry borders of shade. Expect poor depth judgment, especially during dry intervals when the light intensifies.

Use contrast filtration rather than adding more light. A flashlight may be useful for reading instruments but will not make the chamber visually conventional.

Be aware that every living traveler will cast two shadows:

1. one corresponding to present position;
2. one corresponding to the place of the traveler’s next rest.

Before moving, identify both your own shadows and your partner’s. Do not assume the second is an optical artifact.

If a future-rest shadow appears:

- at the wall-floor margin;
- in soft clay;
- inside the woven enclosure;
- beneath suspended water;
- or on the far side of the center while your partner’s does not,

do not sit, kneel, lie down, or “test” the prediction. Alert your partner and prepare to withdraw. Treat it as useful warning, not inevitable fate.

Avoid deliberately turning or blocking the shadows of beetles. Shadow direction is part of their communication, and careless interference may mimic alarm, courtship, or territorial signaling.

## Movement rules

### Stay together

The exact center cannot be crossed by a solitary living creature. Do not try to determine its precise boundary. The center may not agree with geometric measurements.

If crossing is necessary:

- stand side by side;
- establish physical contact;
- begin and finish the crossing together;
- do not allow one traveler to pause while the other continues.

Never cross while carrying an animal. The chamber may interpret this as the predator-and-prey exception.

In most cases, simply route around the center. Remain far enough from the walls to avoid predators, producing a broad circular path through the middle distance.

### Keep away from margins

Flat charcoal predators hide where wall meets floor. They can shorten intervening space while unfolding a limb. Ordinary estimates of striking range are meaningless.

Watch for:

- a patch of border shade with texture slightly different from its surroundings;
- an edge that appears to breathe or flatten;
- grazers becoming still in a traveling sequence;
- multiple future-rest shadows abruptly changing direction.

If a predator begins unfolding, do not run directly away; distance is its hunting medium. Both travelers should step sideways together and interpose the rigid shield without approaching the wall. Extract if the animal continues to orient toward you.

Do not attack it unless necessary to save a life. Killing a predator would disrupt a very small and precise food web, and a dead organism contacting fresh clay may not remain dead—or remain the same species.

### Avoid fresh stone and clay

Following a sigh, click, or tearing sound, inspect the boundaries from a distance. Newly appeared stone may remain moist and pliable for several minutes.

Do not touch or lean against it. Do not insert probes. Do not allow boots, dropped equipment, biological waste, or suit fragments to become buried.

Anything buried in the clay may return altered but not aged. For a human hand or foot, the meaning of “altered” is unacceptable. If an object becomes covered, do not dig after it. Withdraw and treat the loss as a containment incident.

## Water precautions

No water in the chamber is safe to drink, sample casually, or touch.

Its downward direction may change without warning. A harmless bead on a wall can become an uphill-running thread or collect under a boot. Local rain may begin within arm’s reach while the surrounding air remains dry.

Suspended droplets are especially sensitive. Do not:

- walk beneath them if another route exists;
- blow on them;
- shine intense directed light through them;
- catch them;
- touch their reflected images.

Their reflections contain views of the chamber at different ages. Breaking a droplet may be more than spilling water; it could interrupt whatever temporal relationship allows the image to exist.

If droplets begin quivering as you pass, stop. Back away along your exact path until they settle. Do not continue beneath them merely because none has yet fallen.

Never step in a dry pool stain. Fish may already occupy the space before water gathers, and your foot could crush organisms that are not presently visible in an ordinary sense.

## Plants, animals, and constructions

Observe without feeding, handling, or rearranging.

The bone arches, warm-pebble rings, insect-case niches, and woven enclosure are not debris. Their functions overlap between shelter, nursery, courtship, hunting, and memorial practice. Moving one pebble may interfere with several species.

Do not repair a damaged structure. The beetles and grazers maintain them according to rules you do not understand.

Keep several body lengths from grazers. Their calm spacing is deliberate; entering the invisible interval between individuals may split the herd or drive animals toward predators.

The woven enclosure near the central hollow deserves particular restraint. It may look empty, but its reflections are occupied. Do not enter it, reach into it, dismantle it, or try to provoke the reflected animals. If one of those animals appears outside a reflection, end the visit immediately without attempting capture or communication.

Do not collect the white stems, black moss, fish, insects, bones, eggs, membranes, or clay. This chamber’s ecology contributes directly to the creation of additional space. Removing a seemingly trivial organism could alter the growth of the chamber itself.

## Sounds that matter

Keep conversation low enough to hear the stone.

A single click is common and may mark the opening of a minute seam. A faint tearing sound suggests active growth nearby. A low sigh means the chamber has enlarged.

After a sigh:

1. stop moving;
2. do not touch the walls;
3. check the extraction link;
4. inspect each other’s future-rest shadows;
5. withdraw if any boundary appears soft or if navigation readings diverge sharply.

If the chamber sighs repeatedly, leave. Rapid enlargement may indicate a change in growth state, and the destination anchor may cease to correspond reliably to the occupied volume.

## Psychological preparation

The chamber will feel intimate, brilliant, and profoundly watched even when no animal appears to be looking at you. Its silence can magnify breathing and suit noise. The absence of normal directional light may cause vertigo, derealization, and the sensation that the center is much farther away—or much closer—than it appears.

Do not try to resolve every contradiction. If one traveler sees three pools and the other sees four, record both reports and move on. Recounting, marking, and arguing are counterproductive. The discrepancy is a property of the place, not a failure of attention.

Likewise, do not become preoccupied with reflected animals or predictive shadows. Curiosity is the principal hazard here. The chamber presents anomalies that invite testing, but almost every test would require disturbing the mechanism being studied.

Use timed check-ins between partners:

- name;
- partner’s name;
- intended next movement;
- perceived location of the exit anchor;
- whether either person intends to rest or kneel.

If your answers begin to differ in basic relational terms—such as which side of your partner you occupy—extract immediately.

## Emergency procedures

### If separated

Do not cross the center to reunite. Do not move toward the wall. Both travelers should remain visible and circle through the safe middle distance until they are on the same side. If this is impossible, initiate simultaneous extraction from current positions.

### If one traveler falls

The standing traveler should not rush across the center alone. Confirm whether the fallen person is in clay, water, or predator range. If safe, approach by circling and make contact before lifting them.

A fall may count as the person’s “next rest.” Check whether their second shadow has disappeared or relocated.

### If a suit is breached

Extract immediately. Do not remove the suit or attempt extensive repairs inside. Treat both the traveler and the suit as potentially contaminated in both directions.

### If a predator makes contact

Do not pull in a straight line against it. Deploy the rigid barrier, maintain contact with your partner, and trigger extraction. Any tissue contacted or apparently removed must be examined for spatial and biological alteration after return.

### If the exit seems absent

Do not search the walls. The return anchor should be traveler-carried and relational, not visual. Activate both units simultaneously. If only one responds, remain together and use the functioning unit’s paired emergency mode. Never send one traveler out “to get help” while leaving the other behind.

## Departure and quarantine

Leave before fatigue encourages anyone to sit down. Ideally, depart before a full wet-dry transition and immediately after completing the planned observation route.

Perform a final visual check, but do not attempt to reconcile object counts. Confirm instead that every named piece of equipment is physically attached to its assigned traveler.

Extract together from firm ground, away from suspended droplets and fresh boundaries.

After return:

- isolate travelers and equipment;
- examine suits for clay, droplets, moss, eggs, shed cases, or unexplained shadows;
- compare each traveler’s recorded chamber geometry without forcing the records into one map;
- quarantine any object that appears altered, duplicated, missing, younger, or biologically active;
- do not bury chamber-derived contamination as a disposal method.

For several sleep cycles, monitor where each traveler’s shadow falls when resting. If a second shadow persists, points toward solid ground, or appears to remain inside the chamber, do not permit ordinary sleep until a spatial-contamination specialist has evaluated it.

Above all, leave the chamber with as little evidence of your presence as possible. It is not merely an ecosystem occupying a cavity. The ecosystem is helping make the cavity, and a visitor may become part of that process more easily than they can become separate from it.
