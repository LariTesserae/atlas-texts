## Overall assessment

This is not primarily a climbing destination. It is a high-altitude, wind-dominated archipelago of plateaus where exposure, dehydration, hearing damage, sudden ground failure, and unreliable distance matter more than steep ascent. An ordinary human can visit, but only with local guidance, conservative planning, and a willingness to wait days for geography to become favorable.

The most important rule is:

**Do not treat visible terrain as reachable terrain.**

A plateau appearing close enough to touch may be receding in practical distance. A route known to be continuous may widen into a gulf. Conversely, a temporary crossing may form where none existed. Local maps, forecasts, and judgment are therefore more valuable than any compass, rangefinder, or satellite-style positioning system.

## Before departure

### Confirm human compatibility

Have the arrival authority or expedition sponsor test:

- Atmospheric oxygen and pressure at the intended plateau
- Airborne spores, pollen, and microorganisms
- Potability of local condensation and rainwater
- Toxicity of common plants and animal secretions
- Local day length and weather cycle
- Whether “nearness weather” affects portals, return beacons, radio signals, or extradimensional navigation

The plateaus stand high enough that altitude illness is a serious possibility. If your home environment is near sea level, acclimatize gradually elsewhere before arrival or begin on one of the lower inhabited shelves. Carry supplemental oxygen even if residents do not require it.

Receive vaccines or prophylaxis recommended by the transit service. Do not assume that sparse visible animal life means little biological hazard; the root mats and brief fungi may contain a dense microbial ecology.

### Arrange a local guide

Do not hire merely a pathfinder. You need someone who can interpret:

- Wind-court tones
- Ground vibration
- Rope and chime warnings
- The movement of rain and cloud
- The condition of root-bound turf
- Local maps of approach and recession
- Signs that a gulf is “breathing” toward or away from you

Ideally, travel with two local guides from the plateau where you arrive. A visitor can lose speech, hearing, orientation, or footing quickly, and one guide may need to remain anchored while the other performs a rescue.

Agree in advance that the guides may cancel or delay any stage without argument. Here, waiting is not failure. Waiting is part of navigation.

## Essential equipment

### Clothing

Dress for warm moisture, violent wind, cold rain, and rapid temperature loss rather than for ordinary tropical weather.

Bring:

- Close-fitting, layered clothing with no loose straps or flapping panels
- A breathable waterproof outer layer
- A low-profile storm hood that turns with the head
- Warm insulating layers kept in a waterproof bag
- Gloves with excellent wet grip
- High-traction boots with flexible soles
- Gaiters to keep spores, grit, and water out
- Clear impact goggles
- A soft face covering for abrasive rain and airborne spores
- A securely fastened helmet without a large brim

Avoid ponchos, capes, umbrellas, broad hats, and rigid pack covers. Anything that can fill with air can drag you down or lift you sideways.

The wet wind may cool a person dangerously even when the air is warm. Treat uncontrollable shivering, clumsy hands, and poor decisions as hypothermia until proven otherwise.

### Hearing and communication

The landscape can damage hearing and make ordinary speech useless.

Carry:

- High-quality ear protection that reduces dangerous volume without entirely blocking low-frequency warnings
- Spare ear protection
- A bone-conduction or throat-microphone communication set, if locally tested
- A waterproof slate
- Prearranged hand signals
- A short set of tactile rope signals for zero-visibility conditions

Learn local gestures before arriving. Do not invent signals during an emergency.

Complete noise cancellation is not ideal. Some hazards are announced through vibration and changing pressure. At rest, keep a hand or boot in contact with the ground, an anchor line, or the shelter wall when locals are doing so.

### Harness and rope

Wear a climbing-style full-body harness whenever outside settled shelter. Use equipment rated for dynamic shock loads and constant wet abrasion.

Carry:

- Two independent tether points
- Short, low-stretch safety lines
- Locking connectors protected from grit
- A compact ascender and descender
- Webbing or root-fiber-compatible slings
- A rescue knife that can be operated one-handed
- A few nonmetallic backup connectors if local conditions rapidly corrode metal

Do not assume imported rope will behave well in the local humidity, fungi, or abrasive stone. Have residents inspect it. Local root-fiber rope may be more reliable than advanced synthetic material under these conditions.

Clip only to tested rock holes, established path ropes, or anchors approved by a guide. The upper stone may be fractured beneath intact vegetation. A solid-looking boulder may be held in place only by roots.

### Water

Water is everywhere visually but unreliable as a supply. Carry considerably more than you expect to drink.

Recommended provisions include:

- At least two independent sealed water containers
- A gravity or pump filter
- Chemical or ultraviolet treatment as backup
- Electrolyte salts
- A compact condensation sheet or trench liner
- A broad-mouth vessel for catching intense brief rain
- A reserve that is not opened except by agreement with the guide

Do not count on summit pools. They can drain without warning, and warm tea-colored water may be biologically active. Do not crawl into narrow clefts seeking moisture; these can flood, exhale pressurized air, or become resonant chambers during a pressure change.

### Shelter and sleeping equipment

Ordinary tents are dangerous. Their poles, broad walls, and guy lines turn them into sails and debris.

Use established stone shelters whenever possible. If expedition shelter is necessary, it should be:

- Extremely low
- Rounded rather than angular
- Anchored at many points
- Partially recessed behind a natural stone lip
- Designed to fail inward without producing rigid flying pieces
- Entered through a narrow, downwind opening
- Free of projecting vents or chimneys

Your sleeping system should be waterproof and insulated from wet stone. Secure every object, including footwear, cookware, and food bags, before sleeping. A cup left loose can become a projectile.

Never sleep in a drainage groove, below an overhang, beside a cliff rim, or in the direct line between a vent and an open gulf.

## Food and health

Bring most of your own food. Local edible plants may resemble safe terrestrial species while being chemically unrelated. Do not eat fungi merely because residents do; their hour-long life cycle and heavy spore release warrant particular caution.

Use sealed food containers resistant to small burrowing grazers. Do not place food directly on the plant mat, where scent and residue may disturb hidden animals or contaminate water-collecting leaves.

Carry:

- Altitude medication if medically appropriate
- Anti-nausea medication
- Antihistamines
- Bronchodilator if prescribed
- Eye irrigation
- Wound-cleaning supplies
- Burn treatment
- Splints and compression wraps
- A compact oxygen supply
- A mask rated for fine spores and volcanic grit

The constant low-frequency concussion may cause fatigue, headache, nausea, anxiety, sleep disruption, or a false sense of movement. Schedule quiet periods in protected chambers, but verify that the chamber itself is not a dangerous resonator.

## Moving across a plateau

### Stay low and remain connected

Walk with a low center of gravity, knees slightly bent, and pack weight close to the spine. In stronger gusts, crawl or lie flat when instructed. Do not run to escape a gust; the calm interval before the next wave may be brief.

Travel roped in small groups, but leave enough spacing that one person’s fall does not sweep everyone away. Guides should determine the formation.

If pebbles begin dancing, warning cords change pitch, vegetation suddenly presses flat, or local travelers drop to the ground, imitate them immediately. Questions can wait.

### Distrust the green surface

The lush carpet may conceal:

- Fissures
- Hollow stone
- Burrows
- Water-softened soil
- Detached slabs
- Ground extending beyond an undercut cliff
- Root mats bridging open air

Probe uncertain ground. Follow established traffic lines even if a smoother route appears nearby. Avoid walking near a rim simply for the view. Ancient rounded edges can be more deceptive than fresh cliffs because there may be no obvious break in slope before the ground disappears.

A soft collapse of sod is an emergency sound. Move inland, not parallel to the edge.

### Manage visibility

Clear air is not reliable evidence of stable distance. Neither is apparent size. Do not use the detail visible on another plateau to estimate how long a crossing will take.

Mark your route with approved rope methods, not cairns, ribbons, or paint. Loose markers will be scattered, and dangling fabric can injure wildlife or become a wind hazard.

Electronic navigation may remain useful for recording your position on a single stable summit, but it should not overrule local interpretation once gulfs begin changing.

## Crossing clefts and temporary connections

Treat every bridge as an active weather event.

For permanent rope bridges:

- Cross one person at a time unless instructed otherwise
- Clip to the designated safety line
- Keep three points of contact
- Do not overtake
- Stop if the bone chimes change rhythm
- Do not attach extra equipment to the bridge
- Secure all loose items before stepping out

Do not cross during lightning, heavy spore release, cloud-driven whiteout, or an unfamiliar wind-court sequence.

Temporary stone-and-root saddles are more dangerous. They may look substantial while existing only under a particular pressure and distance condition. Cross one only if residents familiar with both plateaus declare it open. Ask what signal means “return immediately,” and establish which side you will retreat toward.

Never pause on such a connection to take photographs. Never camp on one. Never assume the bridge will separate violently; it may instead lengthen imperceptibly, leaving you walking toward a destination that never grows nearer.

If the route begins behaving strangely:

1. Stop.
2. Secure yourself.
3. Maintain physical contact with your party.
4. Do not fix your gaze on the far plateau as your sole reference.
5. Follow the guide’s retreat or waiting procedure.
6. Do not activate an untested portal or teleport system from the crossing.

## Weather and daily timing

Midday may offer the best visibility, but visibility is not safety. Evening appears consistently severe as descending cool air collides with the warm updrafts. Plan to be inside a rated shelter well before condensation gathers along the rims.

Expect:

- Isolated downpours in otherwise clear weather
- Lightning with little warning
- Abrasive rain moving nearly horizontally
- Sudden cloud formation
- Pressure waves strong enough to move stones
- Rapid flooding of shallow depressions
- Fire followed almost immediately by rain
- Entire sheets of vegetation peeling away

Lightning precautions are important despite the scarcity of tall objects. Do not become the highest point on open ground. Separate from large metal equipment, but do not abandon your safety tether. Follow local shelter practice; their low stone architecture is adapted to both wind and electrical storms.

When the wind courts begin answering one another near evening, do not go outside to investigate. The tones may carry practical information, but a visitor should receive it from the shelter keeper.

## Animals and plants

Do not dig casually into root mats. They are both the plateau’s living skin and habitat for burrowing animals. Damage may initiate erosion or open a route for water beneath the turf.

Give six-footed grazers space. A low, broad animal may be difficult to move but capable of defensive force. Avoid standing between a burrow and nearby cover.

When gliders appear:

- Crouch
- Protect the face and eyes
- Do not raise poles or nets
- Do not attempt to touch them
- Secure food and reflective objects

They are adapted to currents that humans cannot read. Their path may also indicate an approaching gust.

Shell-backed browsers should be observed from a substantial distance. Do not walk in their rain-filled tracks, climb onto them, remove moss from their shells, or follow them toward a cliff. If local belief holds that a plateau has only one, the animal may also have considerable ceremonial importance.

During fungal blooms, wear eye and respiratory protection and move upwind if possible. Do not step on a fungus to prevent it opening; the pressure may release spores more violently.

## Conduct among inhabitants

Residents have survived by minimizing projection, waste, and certainty. Visitors should do the same.

Good conduct includes:

- Accepting delays without complaint
- Asking before touching a path rope or wind court
- Helping inspect communal lines and roof pins
- Securing every possession
- Replacing water taken from a shared store
- Keeping gestures broad, deliberate, and locally recognizable
- Speaking close to the ear only with permission
- Treating abandoned structures as maintained cultural places, not ruins to loot

Never adjust a wind court for amusement. Its tones may communicate weather, structural danger, or ceremonial meaning across several plateaus. Moving one stone could produce a false warning or suppress a real one.

Do not take silicified roots, old tools, chimes, rope markers, or stones from abandoned shelters. Objects arriving after a temporary joining may belong to another community and may be expected back when the plateaus next approach.

Ask before recording court tones. In some communities, a sound may be a warning intended to be answered, not passive music.

## Emergency planning

Your evacuation plan must not depend on reaching a visible neighboring plateau or returning along yesterday’s route.

Establish:

- A protected extraction point well inland
- At least two scheduled pickup windows
- A long-duration shelter reserve
- A local custodian for the return beacon
- A protocol for geography becoming unfavorable
- A “do not extract” signal if portal activation could intersect unstable terrain
- Enough food, water equipment, and medication to wait far longer than the nominal itinerary

If separated from the group, do not wander toward distant shelters or answering calls. Anchor yourself, get low, display the agreed signal, and wait. Sound can be multiplied and displaced by the escarpments.

If blown toward an edge, do not stand upright. Drop flat, spread your weight, grip rooted vegetation only as a temporary measure, and connect to fixed rope or rock as soon as possible. Root mats can tear away as whole sheets.

If a new plateau appears nearby overnight, assume neither hostility nor opportunity. Remain on your own side until local observers determine whether the approach is stable and whether contact is customary.

## Best mindset for the visit

The country rewards patience more than athleticism. A strong traveler who insists on a schedule is in greater danger than a cautious one who can remain indoors for three days listening to stone.

Keep your itinerary loose. Carry less than you can secure. Learn to notice pressure through your feet. Believe local warnings even when the sky is bright and the destination looks close.

Above all, remember that here **distance is weather**. You do not conquer the space between plateaus. You prepare, listen, and wait until the world permits passage.
