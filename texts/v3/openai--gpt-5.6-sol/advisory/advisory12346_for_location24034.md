Prepare for the White Choir as though entering three places at once: a sealed cleanroom, the interior of a colossal musical instrument, and an archaeological site made of miniatures too delicate to bear a human foot.

An ordinary traveler should not attempt a free-roaming visit. The safest experience would be a brief, tethered observation from the entry point.

## Before entering

Send instruments first. The chamber’s apparent quietness of matter should not be mistaken for safety.

A probe should measure:

- Oxygen, carbon dioxide, pressure, and trace gases
- Airborne ceramic or glass-like particulates
- The full acoustic spectrum, including infrasound and ultrasound
- Vibration transmitted through the floor
- Electromagnetic and ionizing radiation
- The spectral intensity of the white light
- The actual dimensions and scale of the chamber
- Whether opening a gate causes air, heat, or moisture exchange

Do not open a large portal immediately. The White Choir is sealed and apparently has no circulation. A doorway could produce the first pressure gradient or moving air the place has experienced in an immense time. Begin with a pinhole sensor aperture, then a sealed vestibule.

Keep the return gate continuously open or independently anchored from outside. Do not rely on local landmarks or on being able to reopen a portal from within. The statement that “nothing arrives” and “nothing departs” should be treated as a possible physical property, not merely atmosphere.

An external team should monitor the traveler’s clock, physiology, tether tension, and video feed.

## Personal protection

Wear a fully enclosed, self-contained suit. Do not breathe the chamber air even if it proves chemically breathable.

The dryness is an immediate physiological hazard. Exposed eyes, lungs, and mucous membranes would lose moisture rapidly, while sweat or condensation might be absorbed into the masonry. The suit should therefore have:

- Closed-loop breathing air
- Controlled humidity
- A sealed faceplate
- Cooling that does not vent vapor
- Smooth, clean, non-shedding outer surfaces
- Soft gloves and covered joints, with no exposed lubricants

Ceramic and powdered-glass dust may be hazardous if disturbed. No part of the suit should scrape against the architecture.

The helmet needs unusually strong acoustic protection. Use layered earplugs, active earmuffs, and a vibration-isolated helmet liner, but understand that these will not make the Choir silent. Much of the chord may reach you through the feet, skeleton, teeth, and chest rather than through the ears.

Wear vibration-damping soles or, preferably, remain on a suspended platform that does not touch the native floor. Continuous physiological monitoring should watch for:

- Vertigo and nausea
- Disorientation
- Headache or visual disturbance
- Abnormal heart rhythm
- Panic
- Pain in the teeth or joints
- Loss of balance
- A sensation of pressure in the lungs or abdomen

If any one frequency resonates strongly with the body, withdraw immediately. Do not search for one of the fingertip-sized cancellation nodes; a human body spans too many different pressure zones for such a point to offer protection.

The brilliance will also be exhausting. Use a neutral-density visor with adjustable contrast and spectral filtering. Ordinary sunglasses may not be sufficient because the illumination comes from every direction, including below and behind. The lack of meaningful shadow will impair depth perception, so the helmet should provide lidar mapping or artificial edge enhancement.

## Movement

Assume that nearly every visible surface is either culturally significant, structurally functional, or alive.

The “plain” is not a walking surface in the ordinary sense. It is a fitted field of ceramic scales surrounded by architecture that may be smaller than your boot. A single step could flatten towers, bridge multiple courts, destroy black-green colonies, or load a hollow resonator far beyond anything it was designed to support.

The preferred arrangements are:

1. Observe from the portal threshold.
2. Use an overhead harness or cantilevered viewing cradle.
3. Send small, extremely light robots through the narrow spaces.
4. If contact is unavoidable, place weight only on locations surveyed internally and use broad, soft load-spreading pads.

Do not assume the shallow basins are safe places to stand. Their engraved “tide marks,” salts, waxes, and sediments may be among the most fragile records in the chamber.

Maintain a physical tether to the entry apparatus. It should be smooth and suspended so that it does not drag across towers or snag in ducts. Use a retracting reel outside the chamber rather than coiling line on the floor.

Navigation by sight alone will be difficult. White-on-white relief, omnidirectional illumination, and repeated architectural forms will make distances deceptive. Use a live three-dimensional map and display the return route in the visor. Do not leave paint, chalk, tape, beacons, or breadcrumbs. The Choir already turns every residue into part of itself.

## Communication and timekeeping

Spoken conversation will be nearly useless and may tempt you to shout. Use wired or radio text, hand controls, and haptic signals. Establish simple abort codes before entry.

Carry at least two independent clocks, but let the external team control the schedule. The chamber offers no natural temporal cues. Its unchanging light and stationary chord may make five minutes feel endless or an hour feel instantaneous.

For a first human visit, limit exposure to roughly ten minutes after reaching the threshold. Increase duration only after reviewing physiological data. Do not plan to eat, drink, sleep, or remove the helmet inside.

## Conduct inside

Treat the black-green threads as living organisms. They may appear inert, but they are stable systems adapted to precise acoustic nodes. Avoid touching, shading, heating, sampling, or breathing on them. A hand, instrument, or even a tether placed nearby could alter local pressure patterns.

Do not:

- Remove tiles or probe beneath them
- Pour out water to observe absorption
- Scratch inscriptions
- Test the towers by striking them
- Block ducts or resonant openings
- Introduce flames, smoke, dyes, powders, or sound sources
- Attempt to “play” the chamber
- Collect dust without compelling scientific justification

The Choir’s resonance may depend on every cavity. Blocking a hole with a fingertip could change stresses elsewhere in the structure, even if nothing visibly moves. Likewise, sampling one apparently redundant tile may be equivalent to cutting into a load-bearing or acoustic organ.

Move slowly. Rapid arm movements could strike architecture hidden by the poor contrast. Avoid kneeling or leaning. Keep tools secured.

## Psychological preparation

The most difficult aspect may not be fear, but the oppressive sense of completion.

Humans orient themselves by change: moving air, shifting light, echoes that decay, small animal sounds, the progression of music. The White Choir provides almost none of these. Its sound does not begin, develop, or end. It is less like hearing a chord than being inserted into one.

Expect:

- A strong illusion that the architecture is much larger than it is
- Difficulty judging whether you are moving
- Irritation or panic caused by the unchanging pressure
- A sense of being watched despite the total absence of animals
- Compulsive searching for darkness, silence, or an unfinished gap
- The feeling that your own breathing and heartbeat are intrusions
- Reluctance to touch anything, followed by an equally dangerous urge to test whether it is “real”

Agree beforehand that subjective calm is not proof of safety. Infrasound and sensory monotony can impair judgment before the traveler notices anything wrong.

## Emergency withdrawal

Leave immediately if:

- Suit humidity or pressure changes unexpectedly
- The chord changes in pitch, amplitude, or bodily sensation
- Any dust begins to move
- A crack, tile, plant, or tether appears to become fixed in a new position
- Your return portal distorts or narrows
- Your clocks disagree significantly
- You experience chest pressure, arrhythmia, confusion, or loss of balance
- Condensation appears anywhere on the suit
- The architecture shows any response to your presence

On return, pass through containment. Inspect the suit for ceramic powder, salts, waxes, silicone-like material, or fragments of the growths. Do not immediately wash unidentified residue into an ordinary drain. The greater concern is probably contamination of the Choir by the traveler, but the reverse cannot be assumed harmless.

The proper goal is not to cross the chamber or conquer its maze. It is to stand briefly at its edge without becoming one more process that the place converts into structure. Look, map, listen through protection, and leave while departure is still an event available to you.
