## General assessment

Treat this as a high-altitude, cold-water expedition into an inhabited architectural organism—not as a scenic dive. An ordinary human can visit the upper city with careful support, but should not attempt the deeper districts without saturation-diving technology, local guidance, or some equivalent multiversal protection.

The principal hazards are:

- altitude and severe summit weather;
- cold-water immersion;
- depth, pressure, and decompression illness;
- entanglement in roots, guide-lines, bridges, and animal life;
- poor visibility and acoustically confusing navigation;
- unknown air chemistry in the breathing courts;
- biological contamination in both directions;
- accidental damage to structures that may have taken centuries to grow.

The safest first visit is short, shallow, guided, and almost entirely observational.

## Before reaching the summit

Do not arrive directly over the lake in a loud aircraft if it can be avoided. Rotor wash, exhaust, spilled fuel, and intense noise could damage surface pads, frighten diving animals, or be interpreted as an attack on the city’s roof. Establish a base camp below the summit and approach overland, or use a quiet, clean aerial platform at a distance.

Prepare for:

- cold rain, fog, lightning, and rapid weather changes;
- hypothermia before even entering the water;
- altitude sickness and reduced physical capacity;
- steep, wet, root-covered terrain with few secure anchor points;
- difficulty evacuating an injured diver from the crater.

Acclimatize before diving. Altitude substantially complicates decompression calculations, so use tables or dive computers specifically adjusted for the lake’s elevation. Have a heated shelter, oxygen, medical support, and ideally a portable hyperbaric chamber below the summit.

Do not drill anchors into the crater, cut surface pads, or drive stakes into apparently empty root-felt. Almost everything may be alive, load-bearing, cultivated, or culturally significant.

## Make contact before entering

Assume the inhabitants own and maintain the entire basin, including its water, animals, soundscape, and surface pads. Wait at the surface and signal patiently rather than descending uninvited.

Use low-intensity, repetitive signals separated by long pauses. Avoid powerful sonar, explosive sound-makers, propellers, or bright flashing lights. The city uses tuned vibrations as roadsigns and warnings; an unfamiliar acoustic signal may mask essential infrastructure.

If contact is established, request:

- a local guide;
- a designated entry corridor;
- an acceptable maximum depth;
- instructions for handling guide-lines;
- permission before entering an air court or structure;
- warning signs for storms, large animals, damaged bridges, and restricted districts.

A useful gift would be durable fiber, carefully sterilized glass, ceramic blades, or other corrosion-resistant material—but offer nothing until its ecological and political consequences are understood. Metal may be rare enough to be powerful, sacred, disruptive, or dangerous. Never distribute seeds, live organisms, fuels, batteries, or medicines casually.

## Diving equipment

A conventional recreational scuba kit is inadequate for anything beyond a controlled visit to the brightest upper terraces.

Use:

- a drysuit with redundant seals and thick undergarments;
- a full-face mask, since the water will be cold and communication important;
- closed-circuit or semi-closed rebreathing equipment where practical, reducing bubbles, noise, and disturbance;
- depth-appropriate gas mixtures and redundant breathing systems;
- two independent buoyancy systems;
- passive hydrophones and line-following navigation aids;
- low-powered, broad-beam lights with adjustable color and brightness;
- a cutting tool accessible to either hand, reserved for emergencies;
- compact water, gas, microbial, and toxin sensors;
- a personal locator that does not continuously emit loud sonar;
- a rigid, closable sample case—if sampling is permitted at all.

Every item must be attached by a short, snag-resistant tether. The residents secure their own tools for good reason. A dropped object will not merely be lost: it may fall through occupied districts, injure someone below, contaminate root beds, or vanish into the central shaft.

Avoid trailing hoses, loose gauges, dangling ornaments, long fins, and bulky cylinders that can strike membranes or woven paths. Fit all equipment close to the body. Carry no exposed hooks.

Your suit and equipment should be thoroughly sterilized before entry, but do not leave toxic disinfectant residue. The city is one continuous biological substrate; an imported fungus or waterborne microbe could become an urban plague.

## Do not trust the air pockets

The breathing courts may look inviting, but their atmospheres cannot be assumed safe. Test each one for:

- oxygen concentration;
- carbon dioxide;
- methane and fermentation gases;
- toxic plant volatiles;
- pressure;
- insects, spores, and allergens.

An air mirror at depth remains at the surrounding water pressure. Breathing from it does not reset decompression obligations. Never hold your breath while leaving one and ascending; expanding gas can rupture the lungs.

Do not break the underside of a pad, scrape condensation from it, or exhale freely into a small court without permission. Its gas balance may be deliberately maintained. Human breath could raise carbon dioxide, introduce pathogens, or alter a cultivated insect colony.

## Moving through the city

Remain neutrally buoyant and travel slowly. Do not walk on the root terraces. Their upper layers may look solid but are likely soft, biologically active, and concealing old chambers or delicate conduits.

Follow the guide rather than choosing what appears to be open water. Empty volumes are part of the urban plan. They may be plazas, light corridors, animal routes, or acoustic channels. Swimming through the middle of one could be equivalent to driving across a ceremonial square.

Treat all of the following as infrastructure:

- vines spanning between towers;
- chains of gourds;
- descending roots;
- humming cords;
- hollow trunks;
- rows of apparently decorative holes;
- suspended reed mats;
- membranes over openings.

Do not grab a line unless instructed. Some are roads, but others may be structural tension members, musical beacons, traps, cultivation supports, or current gauges. Never cut one except to save a life.

Move with hands close to the body and use designated handholds. Avoid strong fin strokes near walls. A careless kick could scatter centuries of settled material, block visibility, damage a graft, or send debris into lower neighborhoods.

## Navigation and sound

Visual maps will be less useful than expected. Streets extend vertically, buildings are partly transparent, and the same vegetation may look entirely different from above and below. Carry a tactile route record and maintain a physical or locally approved navigational connection to your guide.

Learn the district chords. A local may navigate by the combination of:

- low notes from hollow trunks;
- tension hum from guide-lines;
- direction of rain noise;
- animal activity;
- temperature gradients;
- current against the face or hands.

Use passive listening whenever possible. Active sonar could deafen animals, obscure civic signals, or make the city believe a channel has failed. Underwater noise also travels efficiently through the human body, so hearing protection should reduce harmful intensity without eliminating environmental awareness.

If the district chord changes suddenly, stop. A blocked current, broken bridge, storm surge, or structural shift may have altered the instrument. Do not continue on memory alone.

## Depth and decompression

Decide your maximum depth before entering and do not revise it downward during the dive merely because the city continues below. The architecture will create a powerful temptation to follow streets “just one level farther.”

The air courts make depth deceptive. Surfacing into one may feel like leaving the water, but physiologically you are still at depth. Track pressure, gas exposure, and time continuously.

For a first human visit:

- remain in the illuminated upper districts;
- avoid overhead environments unless escorted and trained;
- schedule generous decompression;
- ascend along the crater wall, not through the central shaft;
- retain enough gas for delays caused by animals, weather, or blocked lanes;
- have a surface team prepared to recover a diver without damaging pads.

Do not perform decompression stops beneath known bird-diving corridors. A descending bird is fast enough to cause severe injury before either party can evade.

## Animals

Do not feed, touch, chase, or collect any animal. Many species are clearly integrated into city maintenance and horticulture.

- Silver browsing fish may be managed herds.
- Dark grazers appear to clean root terraces.
- Crustaceans maintain bridge fibers.
- Eel-like animals are pollinators.
- Rays may have established right-of-way through plaza voids.
- Diving birds enter at speed and should be treated like falling projectiles.

When a ray approaches, move close to an approved shelter and become still. Do not force it between yourself and a tower. Its wake may be more dangerous to the architecture than the animal itself.

The pale creature in the deepest root vaults is not an attraction. Do not seek it, bait it, illuminate the lower vaults, or answer its knocking. The residents have had far longer to investigate it and have chosen not to do so. Follow their judgment.

## The central shaft

Do not enter the shaft.

It is an unbounded deep-water environment with no handholds, no reliable visual reference, uncertain circulation, and unknown maximum depth. Seeds do not return from it, and fish take hours to do so. It may also carry ceremonial, funerary, civic, or religious significance.

Approach only from a designated balcony. Secure yourself without loading the living railing, and keep all equipment tethered. Vertigo can occur underwater when there is no visible bottom or wall. The brief sunlight column may produce a dangerous illusion of shallowness and clarity.

Never descend in pursuit of a dropped tool. Report the loss and let the residents decide whether recovery is appropriate.

## Cultural conduct

Remember that the city’s apparent “plants” are also homes, roads, public works, archives, and heirlooms. Do not:

- take cuttings;
- peel bark;
- open seedpods;
- collect luminous insects;
- scratch markings into trunks;
- tie personal lines to architecture;
- sit on membranes;
- prune an obstruction;
- repair anything without permission.

A collapsed tower is not necessarily a ruin, and an overgrown corridor is not necessarily abandoned. The inhabitants incorporate damage and age into the city rather than restoring an earlier form. Avoid imposing your own distinction between building, garden, wilderness, and debris.

Move at their pace. Their construction and civic planning operate across generations; impatience will read as carelessness. Expect greetings, route negotiations, and decisions to take longer than a human diver finds comfortable. Build this into gas and thermal planning rather than pressuring your hosts.

Because their faces are covered and underwater gestures may differ from human ones, do not assume silence means consent. Establish clear signs for stop, danger, permission, return, air problem, and medical emergency.

## Best first itinerary

A prudent visit would consist of:

1. Acclimatization and remote observation from below the summit.
2. Formal contact at the surface.
3. Water and atmosphere testing.
4. Entry with a local guide through an approved gap between surface pads.
5. A shallow circuit among the highest terraces.
6. Observation of one civic void from its edge.
7. A brief visit to a tested breathing court, without removing life-support equipment.
8. Passive listening to one district chord.
9. Return by the same route with conservative decompression.
10. Decontamination and medical observation afterward.

Do not plan to reach the oldest streets, deepest civic chambers, or root vaults on the first expedition.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect cold, weightlessness, and constant sound rather than silence. Rain will roll overhead like gravel on a roof. Towers will creak through the water; cords will sing against your suit; seed chambers will crack unexpectedly in the distance. Buildings will appear slowly out of green light, vast but separated by empty water. Their walls may flex, breathe, or carry browsing animals.

The most disorienting feature will be the absence of ordinary orientation. “Up” is the weather and the silver air mirrors. “Down” is not a street level but a succession of older neighborhoods, darker water, and compressed generations of living floor. A doorway may be below you, a road may rise vertically, and a plaza may be an untouched volume through which no respectful visitor swims.

Go prepared to feel less like an explorer entering a ruin than a clumsy, noisy, warm-blooded guest moving through a patient living city. The proper goal is not to penetrate as deeply as possible. It is to leave without breaking a line, clouding a street, introducing a disease, or causing anything to grow differently because you were there.
