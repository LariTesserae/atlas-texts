## First principle: do not treat this as a cave

The hollow is simultaneously an ecosystem, a developing organism, and a machine for revising local geometry. Every apparent wall, foothold, pool edge, and patch of ground is alive or incorporated into something alive. A human is enormous here: hot, chemically dirty, fast-moving, and heavy enough to damage several centuries of growth with one careless step.

An ordinary traveler should enter only if all of the following are true:

- There is an existing open entrance. Do not dig, melt, cut, widen, or force one.
- Remote observation has shown that the opening remains stable for longer than the planned visit.
- Someone remains outside with independent instruments and authority to order an immediate withdrawal.
- The visit has a narrow purpose that cannot be accomplished by sending a probe.
- You are prepared to abandon equipment rather than cut living tissue to recover it.

If this is merely sightseeing, observe through a probe or aperture. Physical entry is difficult to justify and may be impossible without harming the chamber.

## Timing the visit

Choose a period of prolonged, uneventful surface weather. The chamber reacts not only to present conditions but to rain from days earlier, snow load, hard freezes, and possibly stored or anticipated conditions.

Avoid:

- The several days after heavy rain.
- Rapid surface freeze-thaw cycles.
- Exceptional cold snaps, when the chamber grows fractionally larger and may revise its margins.
- Periods of shifting ice or changing snow load.
- Any time remote instruments detect a rising root hum, synchronized light pulses, repeated instantaneous freezing, or measurable disagreement between distances taken along different axes.

Stable midwinter snow cover may give better biological light and less ceiling noise, but it is not automatically safer. Review at least a week of surface weather and subsurface acoustic data before entering.

## Party and duration

Use a party of three:

1. **Two entrants**, always within direct reach or uninterrupted line of sight.
2. **One surface controller**, with duplicate clocks, position records, medical support, and a prearranged withdrawal schedule.

Keep the first visit under thirty minutes. Later visits should still be measured in minutes, not hours. Never sleep inside. The chamber changes too slowly to look dangerous and too profoundly to trust for an overnight stay.

Establish a hard turn-around time before entry. Do not extend it because the chamber feels calm, warm, inviting, or unusually beautiful.

## Personal protection

Wear a completely sealed, low-profile suit with:

- A closed-circuit breathing system. Do not breathe chamber air.
- Positive but very low internal pressure.
- A dry insulating layer and regulated heating.
- A sacrificial, peel-away outer skin.
- Broad, soft, nonspiked soles that spread weight.
- A smooth helmet without projections that can snag root hairs.
- Sealed eye protection and low-light imaging.
- Pressure, temperature, suit-integrity, and physiological monitors.
- A mouthguard if low-frequency vibration causes painful tooth resonance.
- Hearing protection that attenuates sharp ice cracks while preserving communication.

The suit should not run hot. A human-sized warm object may provoke thermal regulation, attract grazers or predators, or interact unpredictably with tissues that borrow heat from possible futures. Maintain only enough heat to prevent hypothermia.

Do not use crampons, ice axes, exposed blades, open flames, hot lamps, chemical hand warmers, insect repellent, perfumes, solvents, or antimicrobial sprays.

Carry all equipment internally or in smooth sealed pockets. Nothing should dangle.

## Respiratory and biological precautions

Assume that the air contains spores, aerosols, signaling compounds, antifreezes, and chemicals capable of changing appetite, comfort, attention, or preference. The chamber’s predators do not need to chase you. They may only need to make one location seem preferable to another.

Before entry, record each traveler’s baseline:

- Mood and alertness.
- Body temperature and blood oxygen.
- Desire to eat, drink, rest, explore, or remove equipment.
- Intended route and exact withdrawal time.

During the visit, perform scheduled cognitive checks. Use simple questions whose answers are fixed in advance. For example:

- “Are we allowed to drink the water?” — No.
- “May either of us change the route alone?” — No.
- “If a place feels unusually safe or warm, what do we do?” — Move away together.
- “When the withdrawal alarm sounds, do we debate it?” — No.

Treat any sudden desire to sit down, approach a blossom, touch a nodule, drink a droplet, enter an alcove, or remain past the deadline as a possible exposure. The unaffected partner should initiate withdrawal immediately.

Nothing from the chamber should touch bare skin. Nothing should be eaten, tasted, smelled deliberately, or brought home without dedicated quarantine facilities.

## Movement and contact

There is no nonliving floor. Ordinary walking is unacceptable.

Ideally, move over a preapproved line of wide, soft, chemically inert load-distribution pads placed by a lightweight probe. The pads must be retrieved promptly; leave them too long and the chamber may graft around them.

If direct footing is unavoidable:

- Test each surface with minimal pressure.
- Step only on the broadest, corkiest rhizome plates.
- Never step on root hairs, translucent bulbs, metallic leaf-scales, luminous veins, amber sap, thin ice, or black-green wall cushions.
- Keep your center of gravity low.
- Move one person at a time.
- Do not jump, kneel abruptly, stamp, or catch yourself by grabbing a root.
- Do not enter the central pool.

Expect all surfaces to be wet, slick, and slightly flexible. The shallow water is not harmless merely because it is only hand-deep. It may conceal leaves, adhesive tissues, or routes into geometry unavailable to humans.

Do not cut anything. If a root curtain blocks the route, the route is closed.

## Navigation

Normal cave-survey assumptions do not apply. Distance may change depending on the direction and method of measurement. A visible return route may not remain metrically equivalent to the route you entered by.

Use redundant systems:

- A physical line of sight to the entrance whenever possible.
- Shielded, low-power beacons at short intervals.
- Three independently synchronized clocks.
- Inertial tracking combined with visual landmarks.
- Repeated bidirectional distance measurements.
- A surface observer measuring the entrance and party position from outside.

Radio, magnetic compasses, lidar, and active optical systems may disturb tissues sensitive to magnetic fields or coherent light. Test every instrument remotely at low power before bringing it inside. Prefer passive imaging and shielded fiber communication.

Any tether must be slack, smooth, breakaway, and expendable. Never tie it to a root. A taut safety line could become a cutting wire if the chamber alters a distance. If the line begins tightening without traveler movement, stop, release it, and retreat visually.

Do not follow animals into side spaces. Some can enter alternate root geometries that are not survivable or reversible for a human.

## Light and observation

Use dim, diffuse, low-heat illumination only when necessary. The chamber will not present itself as a complete landscape. Some leaves conceal their luminous undersides when directly watched, while biological light travels through tissues independently of your needs.

Do not compensate by flooding the chamber with bright light. You may trigger defensive responses, suppress animal behavior, heat sensitive surfaces, or alter root signaling.

Accept that records will be fragmentary. A camera array may produce mutually inconsistent spatial maps. Preserve all raw data rather than forcing it into a single three-dimensional model.

## Sounds and warning signs

The chamber is naturally loud, but changes in its sound matter more than absolute volume.

Normal sounds include:

- Droplet impacts amplified into wooden booms.
- Ice cracks.
- Gas notes from submerged moss.
- Animal clicks and rasping.
- A low pressure-song felt in teeth and joints.

Withdraw if:

- The low hum rises steadily or begins pulsing in synchrony.
- Root lights converge toward the party.
- Droplets stop despite visible mist.
- Animals fall silent in sequence around you rather than all at once from simple alarm.
- A mapped arch or pool edge appears nearer from one direction and unchanged from another.
- Root hairs orient toward equipment or suit joints.
- Amber-colored sap appears along your route.
- A new root tip emerges between you and the entrance.
- Either traveler experiences an unexplained sense that retreat is unnecessary.

Large movement will silence nearby animals. Do not mistake that silence for the chamber’s deeper freezing event.

## If the central pool freezes at once

If the entire pool freezes simultaneously, the lights go out, and suspended mist crystallizes:

1. Stop moving.
2. Do not touch the pool or test the ice.
3. Do not run for the entrance.
4. Lower your center of gravity without kneeling on delicate tissue.
5. Maintain physical contact with your partner if this can be done without crossing unstable ground.
6. Wait through the resonant knock and until water, lights, and ordinary dripping clearly resume.
7. Withdraw by the existing route.

The event may represent a temporary locking of the chamber into one geometrical state. Movement during it could place you on the wrong side of a distance when the chamber becomes fluid again.

## Entanglement and contamination

If caught by root hairs, adhesive mucilage, or amber sap:

- Do not pull violently.
- Do not use heat or solvent.
- Shed the sacrificial outer suit layer if possible.
- Apply slow, distributed pressure away from the point of attachment.
- Abandon attached equipment.

Cutting is a last resort reserved for immediate threat to life. Even then, cut the smallest possible amount with a sealed mechanical tool and withdraw at once. Assume the injury will be communicated through the entire root network.

After the visit, quarantine the party and all equipment. Decontamination should begin with containment and analysis, not indiscriminate sterilization. Chamber organisms may incorporate foreign genetic material, while ordinary disinfectants may convert harmless residues into unfamiliar compounds. Keep travelers under observation for delayed skin reactions, altered thermoregulation, unusual cravings, persistent auditory sensations, and changes in spatial perception.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the place to feel larger than its dimensions and more crowded than its visibility suggests. Your lamps will reveal wet buttresses, black water, silver mist, and then nothing coherent between them. The cold will enter through your hands and feet despite the suit. Impacts overhead will sound like distant doors or trees splitting. The root hum may be felt first in fillings, jaw joints, and the long bones of the legs.

Movement will seem indecently fast. Animals that spend months following prey will react to a human step as to an explosion. The chamber’s apparent stillness may produce a dangerous feeling of safety, but nearly everything around you will be measuring pressure, chemistry, heat, light, or possible future strain.

Most importantly, distrust comfort. In this place, a warm hollow, a sweet smell, an attractive light, or a sudden conviction that one more minute would do no harm may be the first stage of predation.

Visit as lightly and briefly as possible. Take images, measurements, and memories. Take no living thing, leave no object, and never assume that the space through which you entered is obliged to continue existing for your return.
