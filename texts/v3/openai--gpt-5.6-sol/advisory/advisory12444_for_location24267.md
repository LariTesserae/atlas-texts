## Preparing for the basin town

Treat this as an inhabited industrial wetland, not an abandoned factory settlement. Its empty lots, intermittent waterways, and slow machinery are all working parts of the town. The chief dangers are not dramatic floods or runaway engines, but noise, unstable young ground, unexpected pressure releases, cold moisture, and the temptation to assume that something stationary is safe.

### Before crossing over

Confirm that the local atmosphere, gravity, microorganisms, and industrial vapors are compatible with you. Even if the air appears ordinary, bring a compact atmospheric monitor capable of detecting low oxygen, carbon monoxide, corrosive aerosols, and concentrated brine mist. The mineral fog that hardens the paving may irritate lungs or eyes, and factory condensate should not be assumed potable.

Your vaccinations should be current, especially tetanus. Bring any personal medicines in sealed, moisture-resistant packaging; local storage conditions are persistently damp, and pharmacies may be limited or absent.

Arrange an introduction if possible. Ask for a works guide, drainage warden, or resident host rather than attempting to interpret the town’s machinery yourself. The residents understand purge cycles, buried conduits, and temporary seep routes that will not be obvious to a visitor.

Choose an arrival point on a marked raised pavement or established loading platform. Do **not** arrive in one of the inviting open spaces between buildings. Those gaps are flood courts and fog-settling grounds, and may fill without conventional rain.

## What to wear

Bring:

- Waterproof boots with stiff soles, strong ankle support, and good traction on glazed paving
- Wool or other insulation that remains useful when damp
- A breathable waterproof outer layer, preferably waxed fabric or a quiet modern equivalent
- Layered hearing protection: earplugs under earmuffs are appropriate near the central works
- Clear safety glasses against dripping condensate, grit, and pressure-blown debris
- Work gloves for railings, cables, and cold metal
- A warm cap and a dry change of socks
- A compact respirator with particulate and acid-gas protection for emergencies
- A headlamp with a warm or amber setting for mist
- A whistle, vibration alarm, or bright signal lamp; shouted warnings may be inaudible

Avoid loose scarves, dangling straps, long coats, and unsecured hair near belts, fans, winches, tram tracks, or moving doors. High-visibility clothing is useful, but do not rely on color alone: colored doors have specific local meanings.

Expect the temperature to drop sharply toward evening. The day may feel merely cool, while the onset of condensation can chill wet clothing very quickly.

## Moving through town

Stay on the raised pavements and use the iron railings. The hexagonal road blocks are sharp-edged and can become slick with mineral film or moss. Broken blocks may conceal soft blue clay beneath them.

Do not walk:

- Along fresh-cut banks or beneath clay fins
- Across pale mud shelves at the basin edge
- Through reeds, even if the ground looks firm
- Over drain covers that are humming more strongly than usual
- Beneath suspended loads or slow-moving cranes
- Inside empty rail corridors without checking for the tram
- Across an exposed canal bed merely because it is dry

The canal can begin carrying water according to machinery and cistern levels rather than local rainfall. Likewise, reeds often mark a buried leak, not a harmless marshy patch.

The town’s slow motion is deceptive. A door that takes ten minutes to open can still crush a person. A crane moving almost imperceptibly remains a crane. Never stand in the path of a mechanism while waiting for it to finish, and never “help” it by pushing, pulling, releasing a catch, or adding weight.

Machinery here is governed by accumulated pressure and counterweight. Interfering with something that appears stalled may release stored force elsewhere.

## Learning the local signals

On arrival, ask a resident to explain the current warning conventions. At minimum, remember:

- **Yellow doors:** dry rooms
- **Blue doors:** cistern access
- **White doors:** places where steam may gather

These are classifications, not invitations. Do not enter cistern or steam spaces without authorization.

Pressure discharges may be preceded by changes in vibration, rooks taking flight, shutters shifting, or workers clearing an area. Animals are useful clues but not a substitute for local instruction. If everyone nearby steps behind a railing, do the same immediately.

The condensation-tower snails are treated as unofficial safety gauges. If they remain high on the walls, no one should open the tower. Do not touch, collect, feed, or relocate them.

The tram may be difficult to hear. Learn its scented signals:

- Pine near residential lanes
- Bitter orange near the market
- Tar near the loading yard

If you smell one of these in an unexpected place, look for the track and step well clear. In heavy mist, scent may reach you before the vehicle is visible.

## Water etiquette

Bring your own sealed drinking water for at least the first day. Do not drink from stone basins, gutters, temporary ponds, cooling pools, canal water, pipe drips, or factory condensate. Even clear droplets may contain brine, metal residues, industrial oils, or unfamiliar microorganisms.

Water allocation is a civic rule, not merely a custom:

1. Cooling
2. Animals
3. Gardens
4. People

Do not fill a bottle, wash clothing, or bathe from a public basin without asking. Never block a gutter, move a collection vessel, close a dripping valve, or redirect a small flow because it appears wasteful. The town may already be directing that moisture through a larger system.

Conversely, do not report every leak as damage. Reeds and damp seams may mark intentional seepage. Inform a resident and let them decide.

Use water sparingly in your lodging. Bring waterless hand cleaner, biodegradable wipes, and a small personal washcloth. Avoid detergents that might enter reed beds or ceramic conduits.

## Conduct around the works

The central works should be visited only with an escort. Wear full hearing protection before approaching, not after the noise becomes painful. Low-frequency vibration can be exhausting even when your ears are protected, so limit your first visit to an hour or less.

Do not touch sweating pipes. They may be hot beneath a cool condensate layer, chemically active, electrically grounded, or subject to sudden vibration. Do not lean on tanks, sit on cable drums, or step over belts and conduit runs.

Pay attention to physical symptoms. Leave the works if you develop:

- Headache or unusual drowsiness
- Dizziness or nausea
- Ringing ears despite protection
- Chest tightness
- Metallic or strongly saline taste
- Confusion about direction or timing
- Tingling while touching railings

Move toward a yellow-marked dry room or an occupied porch, tell a worker what happened, and use your atmospheric monitor. Because the entire basin vibrates, visitors may not notice fatigue until they reach a quieter place.

Never demand that machinery be stopped abruptly for your convenience. If something has fallen into a mechanism or a person is endangered, use the local emergency stop procedure or summon a worker. The system may require a staged shutdown to avoid a pressure failure elsewhere.

## Lodging and daily routine

Stay in an occupied house or designated guest room above known flood level. A good room will have a steep roof, narrow windows, a dry entrance area, and a place to leave wet gear. Keep boots and outerwear on the porch rather than carrying mineral moisture indoors.

Pack valuables in dry bags. Include silica gel or another desiccant for electronics, documents, and dimensional equipment. Condensation will form on devices brought from cold streets into warm rooms, so let them acclimate before switching them on.

Sleep may be difficult. The engines’ rhythm is continuous and can be felt through floors and bed frames. Bring comfortable earplugs, but ask whether alarms are audible or vibrational before wearing them overnight. Some travelers may find steady machinery soothing; others may experience motion-like unease or wake when an engine pauses.

The sudden absence of noise is worth noticing. If the birds fall silent and the engine rhythm stops unexpectedly, do not wander out to investigate. Wait for local guidance. Quiet may indicate a controlled pause, but it may also precede pressure equalization, venting, or emergency work.

## Food, animals, and biological caution

Carry enough familiar food for a short stay. Local produce may be excellent, especially pears and trough-grown vegetables, but ask how it was watered and whether visitors tolerate the local mineral content.

Do not forage from reed beds, cooling grounds, night-blooming mats, or factory walls. Rust-colored algae and pale moth clusters may be mistaken for harmless deposits. Wear gloves if examining plants or insects.

Observe wildlife without disrupting it:

- Do not reach into stacked ceramic pipes; birds may be nesting there.
- Do not move empty cable drums; foxes may be sleeping inside.
- Avoid culverts used by water voles.
- Do not enter condensation towers to see the snails.
- Keep food sealed against rooks and rodents.

If bringing equipment or biological samples across universes, follow strict decontamination in both directions. This is a young, delicately balanced ecology. An ordinary seed, fungus, insect egg, or bacterium from your home world could spread rapidly along the warm pipes and wet corridors.

## Social expectations

Move patiently. The town does not reward urgency, and residents are unlikely to appreciate visitors who interpret slowness as inefficiency. Allow generous time for doors, cranes, tram journeys, pressure locks, and cistern-controlled crossings.

Useful courtesies include:

- Asking before taking water
- Waiting for machinery to complete its cycle
- Leaving flood courts empty
- Keeping seep routes unobstructed
- Returning tools dry and clean
- Closing doors according to local instructions, not as quickly as possible
- Accepting that a delayed journey may be a safety decision

Do not criticize the undeveloped spaces or suggest building on them. The deliberate emptiness is infrastructure. Likewise, do not assume the land beyond the retaining wall is free for camping, surveying, or collecting. It is unstable, newly formed terrain whose drainage routes may shift.

Photography and recording should be cleared with the works staff. Flash photography may disturb workers or birds, and microphones or instruments attached to railings can interfere with vibration monitoring.

## Best way to experience the place

Spend your first morning in the residential lanes, where you can learn the engine rhythm at a distance. Watch droplets gather beneath pipes and listen for the deep knock of cistern valves. From there, visit the market or an orchard release channel with a guide before approaching the pumping hall.

The most revealing time is late afternoon into evening. You will feel the air cool before the light fades. Condensation will thicken, basins will begin to ring, and temporary ponds may wake with frogs. Bring a warm layer and remain on marked paths. At sunset, wet glass and steam can turn briefly gold, making the town appear gentler than it sounds.

A night visit to the boiler-house margins may reveal the white flowers, moths, bats, and birds fitting their behavior around the engines. Do not enter unlit service areas to follow them.

At the basin edge, remain behind the final retaining wall. The raw flats are beautiful precisely because they are unfinished: sharp drainage scars, survey stakes, young gravel, and pale clay still deciding where water belongs. View them rather than crossing them.

## Emergency rules

If mist suddenly fills the lower streets:

1. Move uphill or onto the nearest raised pavement.
2. Do not run across open ground.
3. Avoid white-marked doors unless directed inside.
4. Keep away from canal cuts and drain covers.
5. Use your respirator if the mist irritates your eyes or lungs.
6. Follow residents to an occupied dry room.

If water begins rising:

1. Leave equipment behind if it slows you.
2. Use railings and designated raised routes.
3. Never enter a culvert or attempt to cross flowing canal water.
4. Do not shelter in an empty warehouse basement or cistern access room.
5. Expect flow routes to pass through apparently intentional courtyards.

If machinery stops abruptly:

1. Freeze long enough to identify nearby moving loads.
2. Step clear of doors, cranes, belts, pipes, and drain covers.
3. Do not touch controls.
4. Wait for instructions; stored pressure may still discharge.
5. Treat the restart as potentially more dangerous than the stop.

Above all, arrive prepared to wait. This town is safe when its rhythms are respected. Its water gathers drop by drop, its vehicles move when pressure permits, and even its wildlife lives in the spaces between impacts. A visitor who stays dry enough, listens to the residents, and never mistakes slowness for inactivity should find it strange, loud, and unexpectedly alive.
