## Visitor profile

This is not a wilderness destination, despite its appearance. Treat it as an active construction site, greenhouse, wet laboratory, water-treatment plant, and elevated industrial platform—all sharing the same small, rapidly changing space.

An ordinary traveler can visit safely, but should not arrive unescorted or expect yesterday’s map to remain accurate.

## Before departure

### Arrange authorization and an escort

Contact the summit operations booth before entering the local universe. Confirm:

- The freight lift and at least one independent emergency route are operational.
- Cliff growth, rain, pressure, compost, and extraction cycles for the visit window.
- Which walkways are certified that day.
- Current root-load and floor-load restrictions.
- Whether the northern drainage gallery is sealed.
- Whether any printers are performing “slope revisions.”
- The location of the nearest rigid refuge above the flood level.
- The meaning of local alarms, lights, and floor vibrations.

Request a human guide familiar with both the habitat and the machinery below it. A botanical specialist alone is insufficient; many apparent rocks are active outlets, and many apparent garden structures conceal moving equipment.

Do not visit during unattended autonomous growth. If operations personnel describe the site as “stable enough,” ask for measurements.

### Biological and multiversal quarantine

The ecosystem is young, isolated, and easily altered. Arrive clean.

- Use sterilized clothing and equipment.
- Carry no untreated wood, soil, seeds, food scraps, live cultures, or open organic fibers.
- Inspect boot treads and closures for pollen and spores.
- Do not apply ordinary insect repellent, sunscreen, or antimicrobial spray once inside unless operations has approved its chemistry.
- Confirm that your native microbiome is not hazardous to the site and that its algae, fungi, or mineral-accumulating plants are not hazardous to you.
- If inter-universal biological compatibility is unknown, wear a sealed suit with its own air loop.

Arrange decontamination for departure. The greater danger may be taking something home in wet boot seams, filter cartridges, or equipment vents.

### Medical preparation

Obtain clearance for:

- Extreme humidity and heat.
- Mold, pollen, algae, and compost aerosols.
- Exposure to aerosolized mineral paste, powdered glass residues, and metal-rich water.
- Confined spaces and unstable footing.
- Rapid pressure or ventilation changes.
- Vertigo associated with high structures, even though the mountain itself cannot be seen.

Carry personal medication in waterproof packaging. Anyone with severe asthma, fungal sensitivity, compromised immunity, poor balance, or open wounds should consider observing from the operations booth instead.

## What to wear

Wear light industrial wet-environment PPE rather than hiking clothes:

- Waterproof, breathable coveralls with sealed cuffs.
- High-traction boots rated for wet ceramic, metal grating, algae, and oil-like biofilms.
- Ankle support and puncture-resistant soles.
- A helmet with a chin strap.
- Eye protection that resists fogging.
- Cut-resistant waterproof gloves, plus thin disposable over-gloves for sampling.
- A compact respirator suitable for mineral particulates, spores, and compost aerosols.
- Hearing protection, not because the site is continuously loud, but because pressure releases and nearby machinery can be abrupt.
- A lightweight flotation vest if approaching the tarn or any knee-deep pool.
- A fall-arrest harness if using ladders, upper scaffolds, or routes beside open service gaps.

Avoid loose straps, ponchos, scarves, and dangling bags. Vines, printer arms, fans, and hidden mechanisms can catch them.

Expect to become wet from all directions. Rain can fall under clear sky, condensation can descend from cables, and overflow may cross the floor after a spray cycle. Bring a complete dry change of clothing for after decontamination.

## Equipment

Carry:

- A waterproof headlamp and a backup light.
- A hardwired or intrinsically safe radio on the site frequency.
- A personal gas and oxygen monitor.
- A thermal sensor or infrared camera for distinguishing warm fresh stone, active pipes, and dormant-looking machinery.
- A noncontact voltage detector if approved locally.
- A compact first-aid kit with eye irrigation and treatment for cuts and chemical splashes.
- Drinking water in a sealed bottle. Do not drink site water, even if it appears to be clean condensation.
- A short pole for testing water depth and locating submerged grate edges—used gently, never inserted into drains or vegetation.
- Independent positional beacons or breadcrumb transmitters.

Ordinary GPS may be useless within the fabricated cliffs and steel crown. Magnetic compasses may be distorted by the tower, motors, reinforcement, and cabling. Visual navigation is also unreliable: vegetation obscures paths, mist can erase an entire wall, and the physical layout changes.

Use a site-issued live map, but do not trust it by itself. Establish fixed reference points at the lift, operations booth, pipe pavilion, and emergency shelter. Keep a continuous return route.

Do not use adhesive trail markers, paint, chalk, or biodegradable tape. The system may interpret foreign material as contamination or feedstock.

## Conduct inside the enclosure

### Stay on certified routes

Hexagonal grating is not automatically safe. A walkway may be:

- Hidden by vegetation.
- Undermined by roots.
- Partially detached.
- Submerged beneath opaque water.
- Above active macerators, vents, or service cavities.
- Rated for maintenance machines but not for a person standing at a particular edge—or the reverse.

Test handrails before relying on them. Yellow paint indicates intended human use, not current certification.

Walk slowly, with three points of contact where possible. Never step on molded roots, apparently solid mineral shelves, floating root mats, or newly printed stone. Fresh geology may have a hardened skin over warm paste and can slump under body weight.

### Respect machinery concealed as landscape

Maintain generous clearance from:

- Nozzles and apertures in the cliffs.
- Padded maintenance arms.
- Fan housings.
- Compost perforations.
- Drain shutters.
- Skimming belts.
- Warm seams.
- Areas labeled for cliff growth or slope revision.

A stopped arm is not a safe arm. It may move in response to moisture, root pressure, or a geometry update without any conventional warning.

Never place a hand on fresh stalactites or ledges merely because they look geological. Warmth indicates curing material or active plumbing. The paste may be alkaline, abrasive, or mechanically unstable.

If a printer begins operating nearby, leave by the designated route rather than watching. Lithic paste can sag, eject fragments, redirect water, or close a passage behind you.

### Prepare for artificial weather

Before each scheduled rain cycle:

1. Move onto high, certified grating.
2. Leave low basins and the tarn margin.
3. Secure tools and close all equipment ports.
4. Face away from sprayers and put on eye protection.
5. Wait for both the rain to stop and the drain shutters to complete their sequence.

The most hazardous period may be immediately after the rain, when overflow has concealed edges and new routes are forming across unfinished slopes.

If mist suddenly blooms, stop rather than continuing from memory. Activate your beacon, announce your position, and wait for visibility or an escort. Extraction fans may clear the enclosure from the floor upward, but they can also create unexpected drafts near vines and hanging cables.

Do not shelter in an alcove unless it is marked as a refuge. An alcove may be a future drain, printer cavity, or pressure outlet.

### Treat all water as industrial process water

Do not wade unless your route explicitly requires it. The water may contain:

- Dissolved metals.
- Uncured mineral compounds.
- Algal cultures.
- Compost leachate.
- Hidden hot or electrified infrastructure.
- Cable bundles and sharp ceramic teeth.
- Sudden suction when drainage begins.

If forced to cross shallow water, disconnect from any fixed power source, use the depth pole, shuffle rather than stride, and watch for changes in vibration. Leave immediately if shutters click, pumps change pitch, or the water begins moving as a sheet.

The tarn is a fabricated reservoir, not a swimming pond. Keep back from its interlocking rim and never place hands among the floating roots.

## Plants and animals

Do not collect, prune, move, or lean on vegetation. Some plants store concentrated metals. Sap, pollen, or iridescent veins should be presumed toxic until tested. Even ordinary-looking leaves may be structural components grown around composite spines.

Roots are especially important. They may be:

- Carrying high mechanical loads.
- Routing water.
- Penetrating energized machinery.
- Under observation as part of a succession experiment.
- Stabilizing stone that has not fully cured.

Do not feed or attempt to capture the silver fish. Do not use flash, sonar, electrical probes, or submerged cameras near them without explicit permission. Their registration marks and synchronization with construction machinery suggest they may be tagged components, sensors, maintenance organisms, or something for which the present records are incomplete.

Likewise, do not pursue the possible gecko or kill the moths and snails. In such a sparse ecosystem, one animal can be ecologically or diagnostically significant.

If an organism touches you, photograph it if safe, record the location, and report the contact. Do not automatically swat or remove it inside the habitat.

## The northern passage

Do not enter the unfinished drainage gallery.

Its current designation does not make it a drain in the ordinary sense. It contains:

- Fresh, damp surfaces.
- Embedded lighting.
- Raw scaffold.
- An unguarded drop.
- Airflow from unseen volumes.
- Water sounds inconsistent with the summit’s visible dimensions.

Those conditions indicate an active service void, spatial anomaly, or both. Sound is not a reliable guide to depth there. Do not drop objects, lower cameras, send drones, shout into it, or anchor a rope to its printed walls. Any of these could interfere with construction, provoke a pressure response, or establish an unwanted connection to lower systems.

If you feel drawn toward the passage, hear speech in the water, perceive a route where the scaffold should end, or receive radio traffic apparently coming from below, retreat to the pipe pavilion and notify operations. Do not investigate alone.

## The map-patterned fern

Observe it only from a marked distance.

The fern appears to display future site geometry, and the cliff printers subsequently implement that geometry. Several explanations are possible: the plant may be reading concealed construction signals, the control system may be reading the plant, or causality may operate locally in a less familiar order.

In practical terms:

- Do not touch or shadow its fronds.
- Do not trace the lines with a finger.
- Do not photograph it with structured light, lidar, or active scanning unless authorized.
- Do not sketch proposed alterations beside it.
- Do not place personal objects within its root zone.
- Do not mention desired pathways while standing near it if local systems use acoustic interfaces.

If the fern displays a route, pool, or ledge corresponding to your location or body outline, leave the northern wall immediately. Inform operations without attempting to “test” the prediction.

Do not use the fern as a navigation map. Its contours depict features that do not yet exist and may cause you to walk toward a place while it is being manufactured.

## If something goes wrong

### Lost in mist

Stop, get onto stable high ground, activate your beacon, and call operations. Do not follow water downhill; downhill may lead to a service deck, drain intake, or newly opened void.

### Sudden flooding

Move laterally to certified grating, not automatically toward the nearest wall. Walls contain outlets and printer apertures. Avoid reed beds and drain mouths. Release any tether that could pull you toward flowing water, but retain your flotation vest.

### Ground or ledge movement

Do not run. Step back along your exact approach path, keeping weight low and evenly distributed. Warn others without shouting into machinery apertures. A finger-width slump can precede a larger paste failure.

### Machinery activation nearby

Freeze long enough to identify the arm’s motion, then withdraw perpendicular to its work face if your escort directs. Never duck beneath an arm or pass between it and the cliff.

### Loss of communications

Return immediately by the beacon-marked route. If the route has changed, shelter in the operations booth or pipe pavilion. Do not improvise through vegetation.

### Unexpected spatial effects

If distances lengthen, water sounds move in the wrong direction, the sky is visible where a wall should be, or your route returns you to the same location, stop all movement. Use a physical tether to a certified fixed point and request multiversal retrieval. Do not rely on visual landmarks or the fern.

## Best way to experience the place

The safest and most revealing visit is a short one spanning a single rain cycle. Enter after a route inspection, walk to the pipe pavilion, remain there through rain and drainage, and watch the enclosure change without approaching active construction.

Pay attention to the sequence rather than the spectacle: warm stone darkening, leaves bowing, overflow hesitating over slopes, shutters opening, mist rising, and vegetation immediately occupying whatever the machines have just made. The summit’s defining quality is not that it is artificial. It is that fabrication and ecology have ceased to be separate processes.

Leave before fatigue sets in. Heat, humidity, constant dripping, low-frequency pumps, obscured sightlines, and the subtle movement of the terrain can impair judgment faster than expected.

On departure, compare your exit map with the one issued on arrival. Report every discrepancy, even a new puddle or a ledge shifted by a few centimeters. Here, minor changes are not scenery. They are the principal activity of the place.
