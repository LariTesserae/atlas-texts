An ordinary human should treat this plateau neither as a desert nor as a petrified ecosystem. It is a densely inhabited place whose principal hazard is attention.

The safest visit is short, sealed, guided, and confined to the immediate vicinity of the arrival point. Crossing the plateau should be considered an expedition with no dependable duration and possibly no return.

## Before arrival

### Establish a protected return

Open and stabilize the return passage before entering. Do not rely on activating it later: mechanisms may remain fixed while watched, change while covered, or belong to a different century after a short walk.

The return site should have:

- Several redundant anchors outside the plateau’s reality.
- A continuously staffed extraction watch on the home side.
- Predetermined recall intervals rather than a simple elapsed-time deadline.
- A physical emergency return method that does not depend on local radio, sound, sunlight, magnetism, or geographic distance.
- A visible boundary that travelers can recognize without staring at it continuously.

A ring of painted eyes or polished disks may help preserve the physical location of the gate, following local practice. Do not aim those eyes at moving controls, doors, or anything that must change state. Fix the frame; veil the mechanism.

No traveler should go far enough that the return point disappears behind terrain.

### Do not use your real name

Before transit, assign each person a short hand-sign and a disposable written symbol. Avoid:

- Real names.
- Spoken call signs.
- Naming local animals, formations, shelters, or events.
- Repeatedly describing a companion in identical terms.
- Saying that someone “always” does something.

If names attract continuity, a true name may become less a label than an instruction to persist.

Communication should be by small, close-to-the-body signs modeled on local etiquette. Practice them beforehand. Include signs for:

- Cover your eyes.
- Look here and do not look away.
- Stop observing me.
- Screen this object.
- Shadow hazard.
- Air failure.
- Distance distortion.
- Return immediately.
- I may be fixed.
- Something has changed while covered.

Speech should be reserved for immediate life-threatening emergencies.

## Personal equipment

### Wear a sealed environmental suit

The fact that breathing is possible does not mean the air is safe. Its composition varies over a few paces and may fail to mix even around a moving person. Use a light pressure suit or fully sealed hood with:

- Independent oxygen.
- Carbon-dioxide scrubbing.
- Positive internal pressure.
- Particulate and volatile-organic filtration.
- Thermal regulation.
- A minimum of several times the oxygen expected for the planned visit.
- Mechanical gauges as backups to electronic sensors.

Do not test local air by lifting the mask. The copper taste and feather-like odor are warnings, not curiosities.

The suit should retain all sweat, urine, exhaled moisture, skin flakes, and other waste. Water and flowing fluids may be ecologically unprecedented here. A spilled canteen could be more disruptive than a fire in an ordinary forest.

### Bring all water and food

There is no usable local water. Do not attempt to melt, distill, or chemically reduce the pale deposits. They include biological and mineral material whose status may be closer to dormant organism than soil.

Use:

- Closed drinking systems.
- Low-crumb, low-odor food.
- No open cooking.
- No flame.
- No boiling or venting.
- No food wrappers that can escape.

Do not eat local lichen, eggs, shed shell, mineral fibers, or powder, even if residents appear to harvest them.

### Dress to prevent recognition

Over the suit, wear a loose, matte cloak and a simple mask. Carry at least one alternate cloak or reversible outer surface. This is partly cultural courtesy and partly protection against becoming too visually continuous.

Avoid:

- Reflective visors.
- Exposed mirrors.
- Repeating lights.
- Highly distinctive colors.
- Name patches.
- Permanent insignia.
- Devices that track faces or eyes.

Children are masked here for a reason. Adults should assume they need the same protection.

### Use shuttered instruments

Continuous video is especially unwise. A camera may count as observation, and a permanent recording may preserve a view more steadily than any living witness.

Prefer:

- Brief instrument readings taken behind a hood.
- Cameras with physical shutters and lenses covered between single exposures.
- Notes written from memory after turning away.
- Sensors that do not form images.
- Redundant analog compasses, while remembering that magnetic seams may mislead them.

Do not deploy autonomous drones, trail cameras, lidar sweeps, or panoramic recorders. They could accidentally fix an entire basin, release something by changing observation patterns, or return carrying images whose effects persist elsewhere.

## Attention discipline

This is the essential training.

### Never stare casually

Use a soft, moving gaze. Do not hold an animal, object, companion, doorway, or distant landmark in uninterrupted view merely to see whether it changes.

Work in teams of at least three:

- One person navigates.
- One monitors environmental systems.
- One manages observation.

Rotate these roles. No one should watch another traveler continuously.

If something needs to operate, hatch, warm, open, heal, or otherwise change, veil it. If something dangerous must remain inactive, watch it deliberately and without interruption until relieved.

### Carry screens

Each traveler should carry several opaque folding cloths or rigid panels that can be deployed without approaching closely. These are rescue tools.

If a companion appears to be becoming fixed—holding an involuntary posture, failing to complete a blink, or remaining unchanged while equipment clocks advance—do not stand and inspect them. That may finish the process.

Instead:

1. All but one rescuer look away.
2. The remaining rescuer casts or extends a screen between the group and the affected person.
3. The rescuer then looks away as well.
4. Allow a predetermined interval with no direct observation.
5. Speak no name.
6. Check only briefly, preferably through an indirect sensor that does not produce a continuous image.
7. If movement returns, cover the person and withdraw immediately.

Do not use a mirror to observe them indirectly. Polished disks are used locally as eyes; reflection may still count.

### Do not experiment with the rule

Never conduct tests such as watching an insect to see whether it freezes, covering an animal to see whether it moves, or arranging multiple cameras around an egg. The plateau is already crowded with the consequences of long observation.

In particular, do not interrupt the inward-facing rings of animals. Their centers are containment sites. Assume that an apparently empty center contains an active catastrophe waiting for a lapse in attention.

If one of those animals closes its eyes, turns, or becomes obscured, do not investigate. Alert the inhabitants and leave by the route you came.

## Moving through the plateau

### Hire a local guide

Do not proceed beyond the arrival area without a willing resident guide. Pay only in materials they explicitly request. Suitable gifts might include clean felt, plain hair cord, or nonreflective hand tools, but even these should be shown veiled and offered without naming them.

Never offer:

- Water.
- Seeds.
- living organisms.
- Batteries likely to rupture.
- Clocks.
- Mirrors.
- Recorded voices.
- Portraits.
- Weapons.
- Maps that claim fixed distances.

A guide knows which postures constitute paths, which cairns are current, which air pockets are dangerous, and which neighboring shelters are not contemporary.

### Respect cairn eyes

At a cairn topped with an obsidian eye, cover the eye before passing. Use a cloth rather than your hand, and remove the cloth only if the guide indicates that this is expected.

The eye may stabilize a junction, watch a hazard, or prevent the traveler from becoming part of the route. Do not assume it is symbolic.

### Stay within the postural paths

Paths are corridors formed by the orientation of the animals. Do not squeeze between bodies merely because there is physical space.

Avoid:

- Passing between two suspended birds.
- Ducking beneath antlers.
- Climbing over low animals mistaken for rock.
- Stepping through the line of sight of a predator.
- Crossing the shadows of crouching cats or hook-jawed creatures.
- Allowing your shadow to connect a predator’s shadow with prey.

Predation here occurs by alignment. A human body or shadow may complete an arrangement that has been waiting for centuries.

When the sun’s position is found to have changed, stop and reassess all shadow paths. Do not expect to see the change happen.

### Do not touch the ground unnecessarily

Much of the apparent geology may be ancestral animal matter. A ridge, slab, powder bed, or wall-like spine can be alive on a scale too slow for immediate recognition.

Use no pitons, drills, chalk marks, sample hammers, or adhesive trail markers. Do not overturn stones. Do not sit on warm surfaces.

If a “rock” has regular openings, grain resembling fur, bilateral structure, or warmth unrelated to sunlight, give it a wide berth.

### Do not look through antler forks

Antlers can frame earlier versions of the plateau. Treat any accidental view of mountains, ash fountains, moving stone-swimmers, or other eras as real but inaccessible.

Immediately:

- Close one eye.
- Step sideways rather than forward.
- Lower your gaze.
- Do not try to reproduce the viewing angle.
- Do not photograph it.
- Do not point it out by speaking.

A second look may establish continuity between eras, with uncertain consequences for the observer.

## Distance and time

Carry a clock, but do not trust it as a measure of travel. Carry a map, but do not trust it as a measure of return.

A ridge may be near from one direction and years away from another. Therefore:

- Do not cross ridgelines.
- Do not approach the northern basins or western precipice on a first visit.
- Do not enter shelters merely because they appear adjacent.
- Retrace the exact path and body orientation used on approach.
- At each cairn, record which shoulder faced the sun and which hand covered the eye.
- Turn back at the first discrepancy between expected and observed landmarks.

A physical tether to the portal may help near the arrival point, but it is not sufficient protection. Distance may accumulate along the tether without being visible. If the line becomes unexpectedly taut, slack, warm, mineralized, or much older in appearance, do not pull it. Screen it and use emergency extraction.

Plan for a visit measured in minutes, but carry life support for days. No amount of supplies makes a seven-year one-way path safe.

## Conduct around animals

The animals are living inhabitants, not sculptures or specimens.

Do not:

- Touch eyes to provoke pupil response.
- Cut tissue.
- Pluck feathers or fur.
- Break antler tips.
- Collect eggs.
- Stand under suspended birds.
- Move animals to clear a route.
- Place objects in mouths or breathing pores.
- Cast deliberate shadows between predator and prey.
- Attempt to hear a heartbeat with amplified equipment.

A wound that closes over years is still a wound.

If shed material is offered by local residents, keep it sealed and visually covered. On return, quarantine it as both biological and geological matter. Assume it can alter when unobserved and persist when watched.

## Sound and silence

Avoid unnecessary noise. Not because noise carries far, but because effects may precede causes.

If you hear an echo, dry note, footstep, or voice before making the corresponding sound:

- Stop.
- Do not intentionally produce the expected sound.
- Do not answer a voice.
- Change position without speaking.
- Return toward the last cairn.

An anticipated sound may be a warning, a constraint, or an event seeking completion. There is no reason to supply it.

The pressure below hearing produced by the herds can cause headache, nausea, dread, and false certainty. Use bone-conduction dampers or active protection that does not broadcast sound externally. Establish hand-sign checks for cognitive impairment.

## Shelters and inhabitants

Approach only when invited. Stop outside the first bend of a doorway; the double turn is protective. Never try to see the entire interior at once.

Follow these courtesies:

- Keep gestures small.
- Do not stare at faces.
- Do not remove a child’s mask.
- Do not unwrap tools.
- Do not watch a cooking stone heat.
- Do not look directly at an egg or cocoon.
- Do not ask how old someone is.
- Do not ask whether two neighboring shelters exist at the same time.
- Do not promise to remember someone forever.
- Do not say that you will “always return.”

If residents abruptly cover an object, turn away. If they begin staring at something, help only if clearly instructed; otherwise withdraw without crossing their lines of sight.

## The western precipice

Do not visit it unless the expedition’s sole purpose is to study the edge and the travelers accept that they may not return.

The suspended fall is not safe ground. Do not throw anything over, reach toward hanging debris, or stare down between the birds. The upward-facing eyes below may collectively provide enough observation to fix a human at the brink.

Vertigo there is not merely psychological. Your body may accept the falling geometry even while the rocks do not.

If you accidentally reach the precipice:

1. Do not look down again.
2. Cover reflective surfaces.
3. Face the plateau.
4. Keep clear of all overlapping wings and shadows.
5. Follow the exact line of your own footprints if they remain visible.
6. Request extraction before attempting a long return.

## Contamination and departure

Before leaving, inspect the suit without removing it. Look for:

- Powder adhering in organized patterns.
- Shell-like growth at seams.
- Mineral fibers closing over scratches.
- Lichen diagrams on shaded surfaces.
- Unfamiliar warmth.
- A shadow that does not quite match posture.
- Tiny animals fitted into joints or fabric.

The inspection must be brief and segmented. Do not have several people stare continuously at one suspicious area unless the intention is to keep it from changing.

Return through quarantine. Keep all equipment sealed and alternately veiled and inspected under controlled conditions. Do not introduce plateau dust, animals, recordings, or local names into ordinary environments until specialists understand whether the rules of observation travel with them.

Any traveler whose body temperature feels “dry,” whose pulse becomes difficult to find, or whose wounds show pale fibers instead of blood must remain screened from continuous observation while receiving medical care.

## Final rule

Do not confuse stillness with safety, emptiness with vacancy, or slowness with insignificance.

On the plateau, looking is an intervention. Looking away is also an intervention. Prepare every gaze as carefully as you would prepare a weapon, and leave before the place has enough time—or enough attention—to decide that you have always been there.
