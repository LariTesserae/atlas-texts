## General assessment

An ordinary human can visit, but should treat the city as a combination of high-altitude settlement, ice cave, active drainage system, and slowly rearranging structure. The greatest dangers are not exotic predators or the cistern mysteries. They are:

- altitude sickness,
- cold injury,
- snow blindness,
- falls caused by glare and thin ice,
- getting lost when passages change,
- being caught in the evening freeze,
- accidentally obstructing water,
- and assuming that a familiar doorway, stair, or floor will remain familiar.

Do not arrive alone. Arrange for a local guide and lodging before entering the bowl, preferably through someone responsible for water management. A guide who knows the current drainage pattern is more valuable than any map.

## Before ascending

### Acclimatize slowly

The city appears to stand high enough for severe altitude effects. Spend several days at intermediate elevations before entering the basin. Ascend gradually and avoid heavy exertion on the first day.

Watch for:

- persistent headache,
- nausea or loss of appetite,
- unusual exhaustion,
- poor coordination,
- confusion,
- breathlessness while resting,
- coughing or bubbling respiration.

Confusion and loss of coordination must not be blamed on the city’s shifting geometry. They may indicate life-threatening altitude illness. Descend immediately with assistance if symptoms worsen.

Bring any altitude medication you know you tolerate, but do not use it as permission to ascend rapidly. Oxygen equipment is sensible if it can be carried without blocking narrow passages. Keep the mask and valves warm enough to function.

### Learn the city’s hand signs

Voices do not carry reliably. Before arrival, learn at least the signs for:

- stop,
- thin ice,
- water below,
- blocked channel,
- falling ice,
- passage closing,
- follow the rail,
- return,
- help,
- and evening freeze.

Also agree on touch signals with your guide: one squeeze for stop, two for proceed, repeated tapping for immediate danger. Do not depend on whistles, shouting, bells, or electronic alarms alone. The stone may swallow them.

## Clothing and equipment

Dress for severe cold, constant damp, intense reflected sunlight, and confined climbing. Bulky expedition clothing can snag in shoulder-wide alleys, so use close-fitting layers.

Bring:

- a moisture-wicking base layer,
- insulating mid-layers that retain warmth when damp,
- a waterproof and windproof outer shell,
- waterproof trousers with reinforced knees and seat,
- two pairs of insulated gloves, plus thin dexterous liners,
- insulated boots with flexible, high-traction soles,
- removable ice cleats that will not gouge paving or catch in ladders,
- several pairs of dry socks sealed in waterproof bags,
- a warm hood or hat that does not obstruct peripheral vision,
- and a neck covering that can be loosened quickly during exertion.

Avoid exposed metal against skin. Wrap buckles, tool handles, camera bodies, and bottle necks. Metal jewelry should be removed.

Carry a compact change of dry clothing. “Waterproof” should be understood skeptically here: moisture comes from spray, condensation, wet walls, roofs, floors, ropes, animals, and the air itself.

### Eye and skin protection

The brightness may be as dangerous as the cold. Bring glacier-grade eye protection with side shields. A spare pair is essential. Slitted local masks may work better in the city’s strongest glare, but test them before using them on stairs.

Also carry:

- high-SPF sunscreen,
- lip protection,
- a dark face covering or soot substitute for exposed skin around the eyes,
- and a small piece of black cloth to create visual relief when overwhelmed by glare.

Snow blindness may begin as gritty, watering eyes and progress to severe pain and temporary loss of vision. At the first symptoms, enter a dark room and seek help.

### Navigation and fall protection

Bring:

- a short nonmetal-tipped staff,
- a compact headlamp with warm, low-intensity settings,
- spare power kept inside your clothing,
- a waterproof notebook and grease pencil,
- a small personal first-aid kit,
- and a short length of soft rope or webbing.

Do not trail a long safety rope through the city. It may obstruct drains, catch animals, cross passage rights, or become frozen into the paving. Clip in only where a local approves. Never attach yourself to an ice formation, a gutter, a movable handrail, or a wall whose seepage pattern is changing.

A map is useful only as a record of relationships, not fixed geometry. Note flows, handrail textures, household marks, and inhabited stations rather than counting stairs. The number of stairs is explicitly unreliable.

## Health precautions

Do not assume abundant water is safe water. Bring a personal supply for the first day and a treatment method suitable for biological and mineral contamination. Filtration plus boiling or chemical treatment is preferable to either alone, though local drinking channels may be carefully managed.

Never drink from:

- the lower natural cisterns,
- luminous water,
- water returning unexpectedly warm,
- channels containing translucent eels,
- animal drains,
- or an unfamiliar seep merely because it looks clear.

Ask before washing, filling a bottle, or emptying anything into a channel. Water paths have specific civic purposes, and confusing drinking, tanning, animal, hearth, and waste flows could harm an entire neighborhood.

Pack treatment for hypothermia, frostbite, minor cuts, gastrointestinal illness, and altitude headache. Keep dressings in waterproof packaging. Cuts should be cleaned promptly; everything you touch will be wet with local water.

## Conduct inside the city

The local laws concerning obstruction are survival engineering, not picturesque customs. Follow them literally.

1. **Never plug, cover, dam, or redirect a seep yourself.**  
   If water enters your room or crosses your belongings, move the belongings and summon a local. Do not “solve” the leak.

2. **Do not block openings made for birds.**  
   This includes openings that appear inconveniently close to your sleeping place.

3. **Do not add weight to a roof without permission.**  
   Your baggage, shelter, equipment case, or body may count toward the load that must be cleared before freezing.

4. **Never displace an animal from a warm drain during the longest night.**  
   At other times, ask before doing so. Animals are part of the city’s thermal and hydraulic life.

5. **If a new doorway appears, do not bar it.**  
   Let locals pour water over the threshold. Do not sleep across it, store baggage in it, or assume it leads to a safe room.

Additional courtesies:

- Do not point at oddly moving shadows.
- Do not mock or loudly debate the instability of buildings.
- Do not take mineral objects from the eastern wall.
- Do not drop test objects into the old cisterns.
- Do not touch a returned object until its temperature and civic status have been established.
- Step aside immediately for people carrying jars, drainage tools, clay gates, or fitted stones.
- Give roof-traveling animals the right of way. Never stand between a six-horned grazer and the route it has chosen.
- Keep luggage narrow, soft-sided, and easy to lift. Wheeled cases are useless.
- Avoid dangling straps, loose scarves, and unsecured tools.

A suitable gift would be something durable, compact, and useful without requiring scarce fuel: high-quality cord, needles, waterproof cloth, eye protection, fine hand tools, or corrosion-resistant containers. Ask first before offering advanced pumps, sealants, or heating devices. Anything that changes flow or freezing could be dangerous.

## Moving through the city

Travel with one hand available for rails. Test each step with your foot or staff, especially where reflected sky makes a passage appear open or makes open air look solid.

Do not trust:

- smooth white surfaces,
- apparent continuation of paving,
- yesterday’s route,
- an ice sheet merely because an animal crossed it,
- a door merely because you used it before,
- or a familiar room viewed from an unfamiliar side.

Follow local traffic closely. Birds and rodents may reveal warmth, openings, and approaching change, but they are not universally safe guides. The silent movement of many animals in one direction should be treated as a warning, particularly late in the day.

Never enter a curtain of falling water without knowing what lies behind it. Never crawl into a drain after a lost item. Silver foxes can pass through openings that are not safe measures of human clearance.

If separated from your guide, do not roam in search of a remembered landmark. Move to the nearest inhabited, structurally sheltered ledge without crossing active water. Display an agreed visual marker and wait. Since shouting may fail, use light against a dark cloth or rhythmic tapping on a handrail—provided a local has confirmed that tapping is an accepted distress signal.

## Timing your day

Begin outside activities after dawn frost has broken and the morning drainage has been assessed. Even then, expect falling scales of ice and sudden runoff.

Return to your lodging well before evening freezing begins. The freeze is a daily emergency operation. Visitors should not be in the White Court, on suspended footways, beneath waterfalls, or in unfamiliar passages when the visible freezing boundary starts descending from the rim.

If caught outside:

1. Follow the nearest local crew without argument.
2. Stay clear of gutters, cistern lids, retaining walls, and overhanging ice.
3. Do not stand downstream of anyone hammering wedges.
4. Do not attempt to help by moving gates unless directly instructed.
5. Keep hands away from tightening bridge sockets and lifting lids.
6. Move ahead of animal flows rather than trying to cross them.
7. Shelter in an occupied space, not an apparently empty niche.

The freezing front can close stairs and open tunnels within minutes. Its danger lies in redirection: a dry passage may suddenly become the city’s alternate channel.

## Lodging

Choose a low-traffic household room with:

- two known exits,
- a sleeping platform above the current seepage line,
- no newly appeared threshold,
- a nearby approved drinking source,
- and a host who can explain the night’s likely water behavior.

Keep boots, eye protection, gloves, and outer layers within arm’s reach. Do not spread possessions across the floor. Pack everything so it can be moved in under a minute if the room’s water path changes.

Use local heating methods under supervision. Fuel is scarce, ventilation may be poor, and enclosed rooms can collect smoke or carbon monoxide. Do not introduce an open flame near rendered oils, dry reeds, hides, or unfamiliar fungus fuel.

Before sleeping, ask:

- Which exit is safest tonight?
- What signal means the household must move?
- Where may waste water be poured?
- Which wall or floor should not be touched?
- What should be done if a door appears?
- Where should you go if the room begins to flood?

## Animals and natural warnings

Observe animals without handling them.

The translucent eels are especially important. If locals report that they are climbing toward upper terraces, expect a substantial thaw and rapidly altered drainage. If the eels bury their heads and leave their tails exposed, follow local earthquake procedures immediately. Do not wait to verify the prediction.

During shaking:

- move away from retaining walls, ice buttresses, cistern mouths, and suspended bridges;
- crouch in an approved open area if one is close;
- protect your head and eyes;
- and expect water routes to change after the motion stops.

Do not feed rodents near sleeping quarters or drains. Do not corner silver foxes. Watch the sky for broad-winged kites, but avoid standing at parapet edges while looking upward.

## The lower cisterns and eastern wall

These should be treated as restricted sacred, civic, and potentially anomalous infrastructure. Visit only with explicit permission and an experienced escort.

Do not enter submerged passages. Their geometry, water temperature, destination, and time of return are uncertain. Ordinary cave-diving equipment does not make them safe.

Mineral objects produced by the cisterns may be extremely cold, chemically active, structurally fragile, or functionally unknown. Do not purchase, pocket, scan, warm, unfold, or “test” one casually. At night, keep away from the eastern wall while its contents reposition. If an object appears among your belongings, avoid touching it, isolate the area without blocking water or birds, and notify your host.

## Emergency priorities

In order:

1. **Stay warm and dry enough to think clearly.**
2. **Protect your eyes.**
3. **Do not obstruct water.**
4. **Remain with locals.**
5. **Reach a stable inhabited space before the evening freeze.**
6. **Descend if altitude symptoms become serious.**
7. **Treat geometric changes as real until proven otherwise.**

Above all, do not approach the city as a fixed ruin to be mapped or a machine to be repaired. It survives through continuous adjustment. The safest visitor is observant, lightly equipped, willing to move at once, and humble enough to understand that a wet wall, a silent flock, or a pebble turned white-side-up may constitute an urgent instruction.
