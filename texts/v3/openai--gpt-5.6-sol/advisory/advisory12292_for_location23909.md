## Essential fact: this is not a scuba destination

There is no breathable surface, not even at the False Shore. The inhabited summit is still beneath enough water to kill an unprotected human almost instantly. An ordinary traveler should arrive in a **one-atmosphere submersible or rigid atmospheric suit**, with all life support carried from home.

Do not plan to breathe compressed local-pressure gas. Even if a suitable mixture could be made, the decompression schedule, gas toxicity, high-pressure neurological effects, and unknown dissolved compounds would make an excursion extraordinarily dangerous. Keep your body at ordinary pressure from arrival to departure.

Ideally, travel with:

- A small pressure-hulled habitation vehicle
- Two independent atmospheric excursion suits
- Several times the expected oxygen and carbon-dioxide-scrubbing capacity
- A second vehicle or retrieval system capable of recovering a disabled suit
- A multiverse return mechanism that functions through water, rock vibration, and heavy external pressure

Test everything in warm, turbid, chemically active water before departure.

## Your vessel

The best vehicle will be compact, quiet, and capable of extremely precise three-dimensional movement. A conventional submarine designed for open water may be too clumsy for the close-packed bridges, hanging buildings, cultivated fronds, and guide-ropes.

It should have:

- Low-speed shrouded thrusters with a “garden mode” that produces minimal wash
- Manipulator arms with soft, broad gripping surfaces
- Tethers and reversible anchoring systems
- Passive acoustic sensors
- Low-light cameras and short-range imaging sonar
- Water-chemistry, temperature, pressure, and density sensors
- Filters protected against mineral flour, spores, membranes, and translucent plant flakes
- External lights adjustable down to very low intensity
- A completely closed biological containment system
- Electrical heating and cooking within the pressure hull—never an exposed flame

Avoid dropping a heavy anchor. The city is built on young, shifting rock, and apparently empty surfaces may be gardens, rootworks, resonant structures, or roofs viewed from the wrong direction. Obtain permission before attaching to anything. If you must wait outside the settlement, maintain position in open water rather than settling on a terrace.

Bring no exposed propellers. Loose fronds, navigation cords, cultivated membranes, and the long filaments of balloon-creatures could all become entangled.

## Personal excursion equipment

A rigid atmospheric suit is preferable to any flexible diving system. It should be close-fitting enough to pass through crowded structures but resistant to puncture from unworn basalt. The rocks here have not been rounded by weather; apparently harmless handholds may have glasslike edges.

Equip the suit with:

- Redundant oxygen and carbon-dioxide removal
- Independent emergency buoyancy and ballast
- Variable trim for moving among floors, walls, ceilings, and open water
- Two tethers: one to your vehicle and one short local safety line
- A guarded cutting tool for entanglement emergencies
- Soft gloves over pressure-jointed hands
- Forearm and chest contact microphones for receiving vibration through stone
- A tactile navigation display
- Very dim lamps, preferably green or blue adjustable light
- Chemical sensors around fissures
- An emergency acoustic beacon that is used only during genuine danger

Secure every tool. In this city, “dropped” does not necessarily mean downward. A tool may sink, hang motionless, or rise into the darkness depending on its density. Brightly mark and tether all equipment.

Do not rely on visual depth perception. Suspended material blurs edges; weak diffuse light removes shadows; vertical streets and inverted structures make ordinary ideas of floor and ceiling unreliable. Train beforehand in neutral-buoyancy movement while blindfolded or using tactile instructions.

## Health and biological precautions

Treat the local ecosystem as biologically unknown in both directions. The city’s water is dense with spores, reproductive cells, microbes, mineral particles, and living fragments. None may infect a human—but an ordinary allergy, toxin, or inflammatory response could still be severe. Your own bacteria could be disastrous to gardens that have evolved with few animals.

Maintain full separation between local water and your skin, lungs, food, and waste.

Bring:

- Broad-spectrum emergency medicines, but do not assume local organisms respond to familiar antibiotics
- Antihistamines and treatment for severe allergic reactions
- Eye and skin irrigation supplies inside the habitat
- Wound-sealing and pressure-suit repair materials
- Antifungal capability
- A medical scanner rather than invasive sampling tools
- A quarantine compartment for all returning equipment

Do not eat local plants, fungi, grazers, or cultivated membranes. Do not drink processed local water until extensive analysis has been completed. Carry all food and potable water, and retain every scrap of waste.

On departure, quarantine yourself and sterilize the vehicle externally using a method proven not to release toxic residues into the city. Do not bring home a glowing nodule, spore bell, root cutting, or apparently inert piece of bark without explicit permission and biological containment.

## Moving through the city

The settlement is navigated by handhold, temperature, density, and vertical relation as much as by compass direction. Learn the local directional vocabulary before venturing outside your vehicle:

- Heavier and lighter
- Warmer and cooler
- Rooted and open
- Dimmer and brighter
- Toward or away from a named fissure, rope, or stone

Guide-ropes are civic infrastructure. The carved shells are likely tactile signs, not decoration. Never cut, move, tighten, or use one as an anchor without being invited. If you cannot read them, travel with a local guide.

Move by gentle pushes and controlled glides. Do not kick through gardens. Avoid thruster use near buildings unless necessary. The inhabitants appear to conserve effort and may interpret constant restless motion as childish, alarming, or aggressive.

Remember that local buildings need not have an obvious upright orientation. A basket under an arch may be a warehouse; a membrane enclosure may be a private home; what looks like a roof may be a public entrance. Wait to be shown where to grip and where to pass.

Because circulation is so slow, damage lingers. A cloud of disturbed sediment, a broken spore body, or a loose strip of material may remain in a courtyard for weeks or months.

## Sound and etiquette

Your greatest accidental offense may be noise.

Water carries sound efficiently, and the city is culturally and structurally attentive to vibration. Loud sonar, metal hammering, hull pings, alarms, cavitating thrusters, and careless tool impacts may travel through several districts. They could be mistaken for warnings, rituals, geological surveys, or signs of structural failure.

Use passive sensing whenever possible. If active sonar is necessary, agree on frequencies, power, and timing with local authorities.

Never strike one of the tall city stones. These pillars are instruments for reading the mountain, and sounding one without authority could be comparable to triggering a civil alarm while tampering with a seismic observatory and a sacred object at once.

Speak softly. A suit loudspeaker should have strict volume limits. Communication may work better through:

- Slow hand signs
- Writing or diagrams
- Tactile patterns
- A contact transducer pressed to an approved wall or object
- Low-volume speech in an enclosed chamber

Pause longer than feels natural. Local life, travel, construction, and conversation all seem adapted to patience. Silence may not signal refusal or confusion. A host may simply be considering you, conserving breath, or waiting for your machinery to stop humming.

Turn off unnecessary pumps while receiving visitors. A vehicle that sounds quiet to a human inside its hull may be thunderous to someone touching the surrounding rock.

## Cultural preparation

Do not call the city primitive. Its residents have substituted growth, pressure, resonance, selective mineral deposition, and generations of maintenance for fire and engines. Their finest structures are processes rather than finished objects.

Expect a different idea of permanence. A building is not permanent because it remains fixed; it is permanent because it can be retied, regrown, moved, and inherited. Complimenting a wall for looking immovable may therefore miss the point. Ask who tends it, how it has shifted, or where its roots will be guided next.

Other useful courtesies:

- Ask before touching roots, membranes, cords, glowing organisms, ancestral bundles, or ballast stones.
- Do not assume an apparently untended garden is wild.
- Do not illuminate a person suddenly at close range.
- Keep loose hair and clothing contained, as the residents do.
- Yield guide-ropes to children, workers carrying loads, and anyone moving without mechanical assistance.
- Avoid displaying powerful cutting tools around cultivated root masonry.
- Do not release bubbles ceremonially or playfully; bubbles rise into inhabited and biologically important spaces.
- Do not promise rapid construction or geological “stabilization.” The mountain’s continuing growth is central to local life and perhaps identity.

The city may find your impatience more disturbing than your technology. Allow hours for meetings and days for permissions that elsewhere would take minutes.

## Gifts and trade

Bring gifts that are inert, understandable, repairable, and unlikely to transform the local balance of power. Good possibilities, subject to prior approval, include:

- High-quality ceramic or stone abrasives
- Chemically stable glass beads or optical pieces
- Nonreactive pressure-rated containers
- Detailed astronomical or geological records in tactile form
- Soft, durable cordage samples that cannot reproduce biologically
- Contact microphones or vibration-recording devices with no hidden active transmitter
- Images of mountains, oceans, and skies from other worlds

Be cautious with metal. Corrosion-resistant alloys, steel blades, motors, batteries, and powered cutting tools may be immensely valuable in a place with almost no metallurgy. Offering them casually could disrupt crafts, authority, or access to resources. Native copper mirrors and implements may have ceremonial importance even if they seem technically inferior.

Never introduce seeds, living fibers, engineered microbes, or self-replicating technology. Do not offer fire as a spectacle.

## Geological hazards

The summit is actively rising. Although change is slow on a human timescale, cracks, warm springs, and unstable anchors can shift without obvious warning.

Avoid:

- Entering fissures
- Resting against glassy basalt
- Passing beneath newly cracked shelves
- Anchoring across a fracture
- Following a warm current into a confined space
- Assuming a mapped passage remains aligned with its destination

Monitor heat and chemistry continuously. “Warm” hydrothermal water may also contain gases, acids, sulfides, metals, or compounds hazardous to suit seals and sensors. The minute fizzing around a crack should be treated as a warning.

If the inhabitants begin pressing jaws or palms to the walls, become still and follow their example or return immediately to your vehicle. They may have detected movement long before your instruments interpret it.

## The False Shore

Do not mistake the False Shore for an escape route. It contains no air.

It is a density boundary between layers of water. Crossing it may cause a vehicle, suit, or loose object to lose buoyancy abruptly. A system trimmed to float below the boundary may sink or tumble above it. The layer also collects leaves, sediment, bubbles, and biological material, potentially clogging intakes and obscuring visibility.

Reflections there can be extremely disorienting. The mirrored city overhead may make you roll the suit or steer toward a nonexistent opening.

Visit only with:

- A local guide
- A physical tether to a known anchor below
- Continuously variable ballast or powered station-keeping
- Density sensors
- A precomputed return procedure
- Enough reserve thrust to cross the interface in either direction

Approach with lights dimmed. Seasonal mineral blooms may be culturally important gatherings, and the reflected city appears to have religious significance. Treat the upper terraces as you would a shrine or observatory. Do not fly through the reflection for amusement, project images onto it, or announce that it is “only optics.”

## Emergency planning

The most likely serious failures are not dramatic attacks but entanglement, clogging, navigation loss, suit damage, or an exhausted power supply during a very slow rescue.

Establish tactile and acoustic emergency codes with your hosts. Your beacon should distinguish at least:

1. Medical emergency  
2. Loss of propulsion  
3. Entanglement  
4. Hull or suit breach  
5. Geological danger  
6. Request for guidance without immediate peril  

A pressure breach is not locally treatable by bringing you into a house; their homes are full of water at ambient pressure. Your only refuge is another intact human-pressure vessel. Never leave the main vehicle unless the backup system can retrieve you independently.

Carry more patience than propellant, but carry plenty of both.

## Best way to experience the city

Spend the first day inside the vehicle with engines quiet. Let the suspended veils settle around you. Learn to distinguish cultivated surfaces from bare stone and buildings from gardens. Listen through contact sensors rather than filling the water with probes.

When invited out, make the first excursion short. Follow one guide-rope, visit one warm-water hall, and return before fatigue compromises your movement. Human atmospheric suits are cumbersome; the locals’ effortless glides may tempt you into traveling farther than your joints and life-support reserves allow.

If permitted, attend a sounding of the city stones. Feel the returning pulses through the suit rather than trying to hear them as music. Visit an inherited grove and ask what part of it is intended for a generation not yet born. Approach the False Shore only after you have learned how the city understands “up.”

Above all, do not arrive expecting a drowned world waiting to become dry. This is not a failed land civilization. Water is its weather, road, structural support, acoustic medium, garden soil, and sky. You are the creature from an implausible realm of empty pressure, falling tools, violent currents of air, and mountains that have already begun to wear away.
