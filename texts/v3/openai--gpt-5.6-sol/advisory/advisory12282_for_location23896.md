## Preparing for the Last Plateau

Treat the Last Plateau neither as wilderness nor as a giant organism. It is worse, operationally, than either: a landscape capable of changing its anatomy, weather, distances, and ecological rules without possessing a single mind you can negotiate with.

An ordinary human should not visit alone. A short, carefully supported excursion is possible; an unsupported crossing is not.

### 1. Before departure

#### Establish biological compatibility

Do not assume that apparently fresh air is safe. Test for:

- oxygen and carbon dioxide levels;
- anesthetic, hallucinogenic, and corrosive plant volatiles;
- conductive mist and aerosolized metal salts;
- spores small enough to cross lung or skin barriers;
- local organisms capable of using human tissue as substrate;
- solvents or enzymes that attack common plastics, rubber, or adhesives.

Wear a positive-pressure environmental suit until repeated sampling confirms that exposure is tolerable. Even then, keep the helmet closed. The plateau blurs distinctions between animal, fruit, seed, and host; “nonpathogenic” may have little meaning there.

Complete quarantine planning before you leave. Nothing collected on the plateau should enter an inhabited world untreated—not water, mud, air filters, footprints, or recordings stored in biologically grown media. Assume dormant material can awaken in response to unfamiliar electricity or weather.

#### Bring an anchored return method

A portal fixed only by geographic coordinates may reopen inside peat, above a drainage wound, or several days’ travel away after rainfall alters local distance.

The return gate should be keyed to several independent references:

- the traveler’s transponder;
- the departure world;
- a local physical anchor;
- elapsed personal time rather than local time;
- a continuously maintained extradimensional tether.

Place the arrival point on a surveyed hummock, but do not trust the hummock to remain one. Use a hovering or externally braced gate platform if available. Never allow the portal frame to rest directly on the root-mat: the plateau may envelop, digest, imitate, or biologically incorporate it.

Maintain a second emergency extraction system that does not depend on local radio, local line of sight, or local distance.

#### Keep the first visit short

For an initial excursion, plan minutes or hours, not days. The main danger is cumulative uncertainty. A route that is manageable at noon may become physically longer after rain, migrate sideways with a moss field, or cease to connect to the same place after a pumping cycle.

Set strict turnaround conditions. Do not extend the trip because the gate is “still visible.”

## 2. Clothing and personal protection

The plateau is cold, wet, electrically active, and almost never truly solid.

Wear:

- a sealed, thermally regulated dry suit;
- a rigid internal harness so a soft surface cannot constrict your torso;
- broad, detachable bog shoes with pressure sensors;
- electrically insulating outer layers over a conductive Faraday underlayer;
- a full helmet with active noise reduction;
- eye protection capable of dimming sudden green discharges;
- gloves resistant to acids, enzymes, mineral slurries, and fungal penetration;
- a personal flotation collar and automatic buoyancy bladder;
- a cutting tool accessible with either hand.

Do not rely on down, wool, or exposed woven cloth. Wet insulation will stiffen, gain weight, and provide an excellent rooting surface. Avoid loose straps and dangling cords. Root fibers, migratory tracks, and small animals may treat them as pathways.

Boots should have smooth, sacrificial soles rather than deep treads. Treads will carry organisms and may encourage the mat to close around the foot. If a foot becomes trapped, release the outer boot rather than pulling violently. A forceful struggle may trigger gripping, pressure changes, or attention from nearby predators.

Carry spare outer gloves and boot shells in sealed compartments. Any equipment that has begun sprouting threads, pulsing, warming itself, or matching the rhythm of the underground pumps should be abandoned—not brought home.

## 3. Equipment

### Navigation

Ordinary maps will fail. Carry overlapping systems:

- inertial navigation fixed to your own motion;
- portal-relative direction and range;
- astronomical or extradimensional reference, if accessible through cloud;
- continuous three-dimensional route recording;
- short-range lidar or radar for surface deformation;
- physical markers that report their own position rather than merely standing still.

Do not use biodegradable trail markers. Do not trust footprints. Tracks can persist, speak, or migrate. A line of familiar boot prints may no longer indicate where you walked.

Color-code route displays by certainty. On the Plateau, a precise-looking number is more dangerous than an admitted unknown.

### Ground testing

Bring a long, nonconductive probing staff with pressure, chemical, and electrical sensors. Test every apparently black “rock” before using it as an anchor. It is probably root tissue.

Use distributed pressure whenever possible. Never jump onto a hummock, stamp on a suspicious surface, or drive stakes deeply. The crust may conceal:

- open water;
- red pressure tissue;
- moving peat-swimmers;
- gas chambers;
- valve structures;
- a pool whose subsurface dimensions exceed its visible rim.

Ground radar may help, but reflective membranes and conductive vascular tissue will produce deceptive returns. Use it as warning, not proof.

### Communication

Use tethered optical communication or extradimensional relays. Radio may be distorted by conductive fog, copper-rich veins, electrical storms, and the plateau’s own signaling.

Every traveler should carry:

- an independent locator;
- a dead-man extraction trigger;
- a mechanical distress signal;
- a written or engraved identity card;
- a body camera with local and remote recording.

The identity card matters because voices are not secure evidence here. Seedpods may repeat old calls. If a teammate calls from fog, confirm them using a changing challenge-and-response transmitted by a secure channel. Do not follow a familiar voice merely because it sounds frightened.

### Hearing protection

The combined noise of wind, turbine flowers, bursting chambers, pumps, and tonal vents can disorient or injure. Active cancellation should suppress continuous machinery while preserving alarms and teammate speech.

If a district of turbine flowers begins aligning, retreat before the chord peaks. Besides acoustic injury, the alignment may precede a weather or pumping event.

## 4. Movement across the plateau

Travel in teams of at least three, linked electronically but not physically unless crossing a known hazard. A rope can drag the entire party into a collapsing mat or become incorporated into root movement.

Move slowly and distribute weight. Prefer pale, tightly interlocked moss over:

- glossy surfaces;
- recently darkened peat;
- warm patches;
- rhythmic bulges;
- areas where small animals have abruptly fled;
- apparently dry corridors bordered by water.

The ecology’s “empty” spaces are functional. A clear circle around a stalk is not an invitation to camp. It may be kept open for rotating vanes, electrical discharge, root pressure, falling bladders, or wind focusing.

Do not walk beneath turbine flowers, mast-like stalks, or translucent chambers. Their operating radius may be far larger than their visible structure suggests.

Never assume uphill is safer. Water, organisms, and even saturated peat can move against apparent gravity. Near drainage wounds, test the direction of gravitational pull with a loose instrument before approaching. If rain begins moving sideways, withdraw immediately along your tether bearing; do not approach the edge to look.

### Rain protocol

Rain changes distance. At the first sustained rainfall:

1. Stop advancing.
2. Record the portal-relative range.
3. Deploy a temporary floating or wide-footed shelter.
4. Compare inertial distance, visual distance, and tether delay.
5. Return at once if any two disagree beyond the mission threshold.

Do not use a visible landmark as your sole destination. The journey to it may lengthen while you walk.

### Pool protocol

Do not wade. Pools may have hidden geometries and can be far larger below than above. The surface tension may also be maintained biologically and fail when disturbed.

Never stare into reflections for long. Use cameras with timed shutters rather than direct observation. If a pool reflects different weather, stars, or people not physically present, mark it remotely and withdraw. Do not touch the image, sample the water, or wait for the reflected event to occur.

## 5. Recognizing a pumping cycle

Memorize the signs:

- three deep pulses through the feet;
- simultaneous drainage of nearby pools;
- animals fleeing toward hummocks;
- paling moss;
- rapid sub-surface flow;
- a visible traveling deformation of the horizon.

At the first pulse, do not run blindly. Running increases the chance of breaking through a draining mat.

Instead:

1. Trigger the extraction alert.
2. Move toward the nearest previously surveyed broad hummock, not merely the highest point.
3. Inflate flotation and impact protection.
4. Disconnect any stakes, samplers, or probes embedded in the ground.
5. Keep clear of fissures and newly exposed red or black tissue.
6. Face across, rather than along, the direction of the traveling wave.
7. Extract before the warm mist phase if possible.

The exposed interior is not a cave system to explore. Pressure tissues may close rapidly, braided conduits may carry corrosive or high-velocity fluids, and reflective chambers may disrupt orientation.

Afterward, unfamiliar warm water and organisms may appear. This is the worst time to collect specimens. These arrivals may belong to another climate, another epoch, or another branch of reality, and their tolerances will be unknown.

## 6. Animals and mobile vegetation

Most creatures are not likely to regard a human as familiar prey, but novelty is not safety.

Keep distance from:

- quill-like knots, especially if they seem loose rather than rooted;
- moving ridges in peat;
- juvenile reeds traveling in groups;
- animals gathering around heat or electrical sources;
- any organism that changes direction when you speak or transmit radio;
- footprints that move without a body.

Do not feed, pet, cut, tag, or follow local animals. Do not assume an immobile green form is a plant; it may be an old grazer. Likewise, a fruit may be an animal’s early stage.

If small grazers begin processing around your boots, move away calmly. They may be eating moss, sampling the suit, or laying a trail for something larger. If transparent-winged swarms suddenly disappear into the mat, take cover from weather and electrical activity. Their retreat may be an early warning.

Do not shelter at chemical vents, however welcome the warmth feels. Local animals will converge there at dusk, and the vent may exhaust without warning. Warmth may also indicate fermentation, solvent production, or electrical storage rather than a harmless geothermal source.

If bitten or penetrated, do not apply local water or plant material. Seal the wound, isolate the affected limb if feasible, and extract. A minor puncture could introduce a life cycle rather than an infection.

## 7. Camps, if an overnight stay is unavoidable

The safest camp is suspended above the surface on a hovering platform, with no organic waste discharged below.

If that is impossible, use a broad, sealed raft-like floor placed on a previously scanned mat. The shelter should be:

- electrically shielded;
- acoustically insulated;
- capable of flotation;
- pressure monitored underneath;
- quick to detach from roots;
- brightly instrumented on all sides;
- free of exposed heat exhaust.

Do not dig drainage trenches. Do not bury waste. Do not light an open fire. Fire may provoke chemical defenses, ignite volatile plant products, or be interpreted as a useful condition and actively fed.

Rotate watch. A camp can move without its occupants noticing if the entire moss sheet migrates. Check portal-relative position, inertial displacement, gravitational vector, and local weather every fifteen minutes.

Count team members by sight and authenticated telemetry. Voices outside the shelter are not sufficient. If someone appears to return after being confirmed inside, do not open the shelter until identity and spatial duplication have been resolved.

Sleep only in sealed individual cocoons. The plateau continues working through the night, and its processes may incorporate warmth, carbon dioxide, rhythmic breathing, or dreams into local signaling.

## 8. What not to collect

The desire to take a piece of this place home should be resisted.

Especially avoid:

- mineral-rich “stone”;
- peat;
- water from anomalous pools;
- ice formed inside living leaves;
- conductive resin;
- seeds or bladders;
- animal remains;
- abandoned quills;
- fragments exposed during pumping;
- recordings of repeated voices if the storage medium shows unexplained changes.

Nothing on the Last Plateau is reliably inert. Even objects that appear dead may be stages, memories, components, or instructions.

If scientific sampling is essential, use remotely operated disposable equipment and send data rather than matter. Keep each sample in nested chemical, biological, electrical, and spatial containment. Do not combine samples; proximity itself may complete a life cycle or mechanism.

## 9. Psychological preparation

The plateau can undermine ordinary intuition without offering a dramatic moment of crisis. This is psychologically exhausting.

Expect:

- mild but persistent disagreement between sight and travel time;
- landscapes that seem empty while constantly watching or operating;
- familiar calls emerging from impossible sources;
- uncertainty over whether movement belongs to weather, terrain, or animal;
- the sense that every stable category is temporary.

Before departure, establish simple decision rules. Do not improvise philosophical explanations in the field. Whether a footprint is “alive,” a root “remembers,” or a pool shows the future is less important than whether you should approach it. Usually, you should not.

Keep a private cognitive baseline: a short text, sequence, drawing, and autobiographical statement checked at intervals. If your memories begin incorporating local valleys, unfamiliar weather, or calls you never made, extract immediately. Identity can travel through the ecology; assume humans are not exempt.

Do not answer a voice that repeats something you said earlier unless a teammate verifies the source. Avoid speaking names aloud unnecessarily. The plateau may remember sounds long after you leave.

## 10. Emergency rules

Extract immediately if:

- rainfall changes portal-relative distance;
- three underground pulses occur;
- gravity deviates;
- your suit develops roots, vascular threads, rhythmic motion, or internal warming;
- a teammate’s voice comes from two locations;
- footprints move independently;
- reflected weather begins matching current conditions in reverse order;
- the ground starts arching;
- all nearby animals suddenly converge on—or flee from—you;
- the return gate acquires biological growth;
- any team member feels that remaining is necessary for reasons they cannot clearly explain.

If extraction fails, prioritize flotation, insulation, and separation from direct ground contact. Broadcast continuously through the extradimensional tether. Do not march toward distant structures: the tall growths are machinery, not shelter, and the apparent distance may be conditional.

---

## Final advice

Visit as a guest who cannot assume the host is a person.

The Last Plateau may not be hostile. Hostility requires a distinction between intruder and environment that this place may not maintain. Its oldest tendency is to prevent anything from remaining inert. A human body, a steel probe, a footprint, a spoken word, and a portal frame may all be interpreted as unfinished ecological material.

Your greatest protections are therefore brevity, containment, redundancy, and the willingness to leave before you understand what is happening.

Go to see the horizon move. Listen to the buried pumps through insulated boots. Watch the turbine flowers turn beneath the clouds.

But bring nothing home—and make certain the plateau has not found a way to bring itself home in you.
