The chief preparation is not for cold, darkness, or wilderness. It is for **slowness, closeness, and restraint**.

This city appears to reject anything that arrives as an intrusion. A traveler who treats it as a cave to be entered, mapped, illuminated, and sampled may never find the entrance—or may damage a place where every handhold is part of a house, garden, water system, or habitat. Plan to become a temporary neighbor rather than an explorer.

## Before approaching

Arrange permission in advance if any contact with the inhabitants is possible. Do not expect to arrive at the opening and shout for attention: voices do not carry inside, and shouting would be both ineffective and offensive. A local guide should meet you on the floodplain and lead you down.

Allow an entire day simply to locate and enter the hollow. Walk the final distance slowly. Stop frequently. Do not rake aside vegetation or probe beneath roots. The entrance’s resistance to hurried search should be taken literally: anxiety, repeated circling, and forceful investigation are likely to make it harder to perceive.

Never approach during heavy rain, snowmelt, or a flood warning. Although the hollow diverts ordinary floods, a visitor should not presume that protection extends to unfamiliar bodies, equipment, chemicals, or multiversal materials.

Leave any vehicle, portal apparatus, large pack, and expedition infrastructure far away. A roaring generator, hot exhaust, bright lamp, or anchored gateway could disrupt the hollow even if it cannot physically descend. If a portal is necessary, establish it well outside the floodplain and close it before approaching.

## What to wear

Dress for cellar cold combined with constant damp:

- Several thin wool or similarly quiet insulating layers  
- A soft, close-fitting outer garment that will not rustle or catch on bridges  
- A warm cap without a brim  
- Thin gloves with good tactile sensitivity  
- Soft-soled, high-traction shoes with clean, flexible soles  
- Knee protection, since kneeling and sideways crawling may be unavoidable  
- A scarf or mask if you are sensitive to spores, but nothing that interferes with quiet hand communication  

Avoid hard boots, crampons, loose buckles, dangling straps, jangling fasteners, stiff rain shells, and exposed metal. Bronze chills instantly there, and other metals will likely do the same. Keep metal tools wrapped in felt or leave them above.

Wash and inspect all clothing beforehand. Remove seeds, soil, insects, fungal spores, and burrs. The hollow’s ecology is extraordinarily intimate; an unnoticed seed in a cuff could become a serious introduction. Use no perfume, insect repellent, aerosol, oil, or strongly scented soap on the day of entry.

## What to carry

Everything should fit into one small, soft-sided satchel that can be held against the chest while turning sideways.

Useful items include:

- Your own compact food, requiring no cooking  
- A small supply of your own water  
- A soft cup rather than a rigid bottle, if local custom permits drinking  
- A folded wool pad for sitting without crushing plants  
- A personal medical kit with silent closures  
- Antihistamine and any essential medication  
- A small hand lens, used without touching specimens  
- Wax tablet, slate, or quiet notebook for communication  
- Soft cord, but only for emergency personal use—not for tying to local structures  
- Sealable bags for every scrap of waste, including bodily waste  
- A low-temperature chemical hand warmer for emergencies, kept sealed unless truly needed  

Do not bring open flame, a stove, smoke, pressurized containers, drones, sampling equipment, cutting tools, speakers, bells, or powerful lamps. The city is already evenly lit. A lamp may create a harshness the inhabitants rarely experience, and heat is more intrusive than it seems.

Electronic instruments may function, but assume they are socially inappropriate until invited. A clicking camera shutter could carry less far than expected yet still disturb everyone nearby. Flash photography should be considered unacceptable.

## Health and scale

Expect prolonged crouching, twisting, climbing, and balancing. Train beforehand for:

- Slow ladder use  
- Sideways passage through narrow spaces  
- Remaining still in cold conditions  
- Controlled breathing  
- Moving without placing a hand automatically on the nearest wall  

That last skill matters. In an ordinary city, a wall is inert support. Here it may simultaneously be masonry, herb garden, snail pasture, moisture collector, and someone’s roof.

If you are unusually tall, broad, claustrophobic, unsteady, or unable to kneel comfortably, do not enter without explicit local assessment. “Nothing large enters” may refer not only to physical dimensions but to the way a person occupies space. Bulky protective gear could make an otherwise acceptable traveler too large.

Obtain broad medical clearance and relevant vaccinations before interworld travel, but do not rely on the local water or food. The clear cisterns may host unfamiliar microorganisms, and the inhabitants’ immunity will differ from yours. Equally important, you may be dangerous to them. A pre-visit quarantine is prudent.

## Conduct inside

Move at roughly half the speed that feels naturally cautious. Then slow down again.

Pause before every stair, bridge, doorway, and handhold. Let residents pass. A principal avenue where two people must turn sideways is not a place to stop and study the view. Never back up abruptly; there may be a ladder, child, sleeping hare, cistern, or roof garden immediately behind you.

Keep your voice low and use it only at arm’s length. Since speech fades within a few paces, do not compensate by becoming louder. Instead:

- Face the person you are addressing  
- Keep your hands visible  
- Learn the local signs before arrival  
- Use drawings or objects when meaning is complex  
- Wait through pauses without filling them  

Study the cord signals carefully. At minimum, know the signs for available water, birth, and closed passage. Do not touch, rearrange, copy, or hang anything on a communication cord. A decorative feather to you may be a public warning to everyone else.

Ask before sitting. Ask before drinking. Ask before crossing anything that looks like a bridge, even if others use it. Ask before touching moss, carved stone, animals, water, or buildings. In such a compressed city, there is almost no genuinely unclaimed surface.

## Food, water, and waste

Bring food that is quiet, odorless, and crumb-free. Soft travel cakes or sealed paste are preferable to brittle biscuits. Do not cook. Do not offer unfamiliar food casually; salt, sugar, yeast, or microbes could affect the local ecology.

Treat every drop of water as allocated. Condensation collected over days may supply a household or garden. Never wash your hands in a cistern, dip your own bottle into one, or touch a wet cord. If offered water, let the host serve it with local utensils. Drinking should still be avoided unless its biological safety has been established.

Carry all waste out. This includes crumbs, lint, hair, blood, bandages, and human excreta. There may be no private or ecologically safe place for an outsider to relieve themselves inside, so schedule short visits and prepare accordingly.

## Gifts

Good gifts are small, inert, comprehensible, and offered without creating obligation. Possible choices include a length of clean, untreated soft fiber; a carefully made bone or wooden tool; or a polished stone from a biologically sterile source. Even these should be approved beforehand.

Avoid seeds, living plants, food, manufactured dyes, batteries, steel blades, medicines, plastics, or anything presented as a technological improvement. Their apparent scarcity of machinery should not be mistaken for lack. Their engineering is based on fit, continuity, and generations of accumulated knowledge. A “better” tool may introduce speed that the hollow cannot tolerate.

## The law of gradual change

Take the local principle seriously: **anything changed too quickly will not remain**.

Do not repair a cracked step, straighten a leaning post, clear an overgrown sign, deepen a channel, or transplant a plant, however helpful the act appears. A wall that seems unstable may be supported through a network of roots and neighboring structures you cannot perceive. Sudden correction may cause failure rather than prevent it.

This also affects social behavior. Do not demand immediate answers, decisions, permissions, or judgments. If an invitation is undecided, leave it undecided. If a dispute concerns you, expect symbolic objects, waiting, and biological signs rather than a prompt verdict.

Do not interpret patience as indecision. In this place, patience is an active technology.

## Animals and plants

Most creatures are unlikely to attack, but proximity makes careless contact easy. Check ledges before putting down a hand. Do not reach under bridge rails or into fern beds; spiders and thin snakes wait there. Step over rather than through moss whenever a bare stone is available.

Do not feed voles, birds, hares, snails, or salamanders. Do not attempt to hear the flightless birds more clearly by touching occupied railings. Their low calls may be part of territorial or civic communication.

If bitten, remain calm and let a guide identify the animal. Do not slash the wound, apply local plants, or kill the creature. Use your own medical supplies and leave if necessary.

## The black basin

Treat the central basin as inviolable unless explicitly instructed otherwise.

Do not touch the water, test its temperature, place an instrument over it, collect a sample, toss in an offering, breathe deliberately across it, or attempt to produce a ripple. Its unchanging condition is too conspicuous to regard as ornamental. It may be sacred, structurally essential, temporally unusual, or all three.

Approach only by the indicated street. Stop where your guide stops. If residents wait in silence, wait with them. The proper response to something that has never visibly emptied, frozen, overflowed, or moved is not experimentation.

## Navigation and departure

The city’s density will quickly defeat ordinary spatial memory. A route may pass over a kitchen, under a ladder, through a garden edge, and beside several identical limestone curves. Do not mark walls with chalk or leave trail tokens. Travel with a guide and memorize sequences of civic signs rather than physical turns.

Arrange your return before descending. The entrance may be difficult to find from either side when approached urgently. Never wait until cold, illness, or panic forces haste. Begin leaving while you are still comfortable and unhurried.

In an emergency, resist running. Call only to someone within reach, signal visibly, and permit residents to guide the evacuation. Their routes and structures are adapted to slow movement; a panicked outsider may turn a minor injury into a collapse.

After departure, undergo ecological decontamination without exposing material from the hollow to harsh treatment on-site. Brush and bag debris away from the floodplain. Do not carry out stones, spores, water, shed feathers, shells, or “souvenirs,” even if they seem loose. Report any accidental biological transfer immediately.

Most importantly, do not publish precise coordinates. A place that survives by being overlooked should not be rewarded for admitting one traveler with an influx of many.

The ideal visitor leaves almost no evidence of having entered: no broken frond, no missing drop, no loud story spreading ahead of consent. If you are accepted, the experience will not resemble sightseeing. It will feel like being allowed, briefly, inside a single continuous household—one made of stone, roots, water, animals, buildings, and generations of deliberate attention.
