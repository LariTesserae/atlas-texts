## Overall assessment

Treat this as a cold, wet, high-altitude wilderness disguised as a city.

The ruins look settled and stable because their decay is slow, but the principal dangers are serious: exposure, hidden water, sudden flooding, falls through buried floors, disorientation, and the impossibility of obtaining help. The city’s scale is especially deceptive. A visible tower may be a day away, and a district that appears dry may become a drainage basin during rain.

An ordinary traveler should not visit alone. A first expedition should remain short, travel in a group of at least three, and establish a secure camp near the arrival point rather than attempting a crossing.

## Before crossing

### Make sure you can return without local infrastructure

Do not assume the city contains anything that can power, repair, or shelter a multiversal transit mechanism. Place the return point:

- On high, exposed, gently sloping stone.
- Away from cisterns, wells, marshes, retaining walls, arches, and roofed chambers.
- Above all visible waterlines and silt rims.
- Well back from the eastern escarpments.
- Outside any broad avenue or depression that could become a flood channel.

If the portal, beacon, or return apparatus is technological, test whether imported devices continue to function on the far side before committing the party. Keep a non-electronic return procedure if possible. A sophisticated beacon is not enough by itself; mark the site with conspicuous fabric, cairns, reflective panels, and a written bearing log.

Allow a generous extraction window. Fog, flooding, or an injured ankle could turn a two-hour return into an overnight ordeal.

### Medical and biological precautions

The visible fauna appears sparse, but sparse does not mean harmless. The underground water connects distant pockets of life, and unfamiliar microbes may be present even when the water looks perfectly clear.

Prepare for:

- Hypothermia and trench foot.
- Sprains, fractures, and deep cuts on stone.
- Waterborne infection.
- Bites or skin toxins from small aquatic organisms.
- Mold, mineral dust, and spores in enclosed rooms.
- Tetanus-like wound contamination.
- Cross-world biological exposure in both directions.

Use gloves when touching wet interiors or handling animals. Do not drink directly from springs, cisterns, or apparently pristine runoff. Follow whatever quarantine and decontamination procedures multiversal travel requires, including cleaning mud from boots and equipment before returning.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Dress for continuous damp cold rather than dramatic freezing.

Each traveler should carry:

- A waterproof shell with a hood that turns with the head.
- Insulating layers that remain useful when damp.
- Spare base layers and socks in sealed bags.
- Waterproof boots with aggressive grip and firm ankle support.
- Gaiters to keep shallow runoff and grit out of footwear.
- Thin work gloves plus warm waterproof gloves.
- A warm hat and dry sleep clothing.
- Eye protection for wind-driven grit and dripping mineral water.
- A climbing-style helmet.

Smooth wet volcanic stone can be extraordinarily slick, particularly where it carries invisible algae or a fresh green film after rain. Footwear should be tested on wet rock beforehand. Do not wear hard, smooth soles.

A trekking pole is valuable for balance and for testing questionable paving, but never use it as proof that a floor is safe. A thin crust may hold the pole and fail under a person’s full weight.

## Navigation

Electronic navigation may work, but it should be treated as supplementary. Dense masonry, buried chambers, fog, and repeated architectural forms will make orientation difficult. Satellite systems may not exist or may not correspond to this world.

Carry:

- Compasses, after verifying that they behave consistently.
- Waterproof paper, pencils, and preprinted grid sheets.
- Survey tape or removable route markers.
- Chalk in several colors.
- A measured cord or knotted line.
- Whistles.
- A simple optical rangefinder if imported equipment functions.
- Duplicate maps stored separately.

Record routes by watershed and elevation, not merely by landmarks. Note whether water flows left or right, which walls are mossed, the number of stair flights, and the sequence of courts. Towers and ridges can disappear in minutes when fog arrives.

Adopt strict marking conventions. For example:

- One mark: route back to camp.
- Two marks: hazard or impassable route.
- Arrow with date and party initials: confirmed direction of travel.
- Hanging strip: junction requiring special attention.

Use removable markers where possible. The city should not be needlessly defaced, and permanent marks can also become misleading on later visits if floods alter the route.

Never let the group spread beyond voice range. In fog, shorten that to arm’s reach or rope the party together on exposed terrain—but do not use a shared rope in active flood channels, where one person can drag the others down.

## Water

Water is everywhere, but safe drinking water is not.

Runoff may have passed through animal nests, drowned chambers, mineral deposits, old refuse layers, or unknown biological communities. The clear appearance of the water means little.

Bring enough drinking water for the planned visit and a delayed return. For emergencies, use multiple treatment methods:

1. Filter out particulates and organisms.
2. Chemically disinfect or boil.
3. Avoid water with unusual color, oily films, strong mineral taste, or deposits around the source.

A spring issuing from a wall is not necessarily a clean source; it may be drainage from an entire buried neighborhood.

Do not enter cisterns to collect water. Their sides may be vertical and slick, and submerged stairs may end at a collapsed floor.

## Food, fire, and shelter

Bring compact food requiring little or no cooking. Local vegetation is too sparse and biologically uncertain to forage safely. Do not count on finding burnable wood. The shrubs are small, damp, and ecologically important.

A freestanding, low-profile tent is preferable to one that depends on stakes. Stone courts may look sheltered but can collect runoff or receive falling masonry. Camp instead on a slightly convex surface where water drains away in several directions.

Before pitching shelter, inspect for:

- Silt rings showing prior ponding.
- Fine debris caught in cracks, indicating flood flow.
- Dark ceiling stains or recent-looking fractures.
- Open wells and drain mouths.
- Walls leaning toward the site.
- Blocks perched above.
- Animal tracks leading into nearby openings.

Avoid sleeping under intact roofs, arches, cliff-cut chambers, retaining walls, or tall freestanding masonry. A roofless enclosure may block wind, but choose one with low, thick walls and at least two easy exits.

Cooking stoves and lanterns should be used only after confirming that imported combustion behaves normally. Ventilate carefully. Underground chambers may contain low-oxygen air or accumulated gases even if there is no obvious smell.

## Movement through the city

### Travel slowly

Distances will take longer than expected. Wet stairs, offset doors, rubble, and repeated elevation changes impose constant effort. Plan for perhaps half the speed of an ordinary hill walk, less in fog or rain.

Turn back based on time, weather, and daylight—not enthusiasm. Evening shadows will make holes and stair edges harder to judge, while temperatures will drop quickly on wet clothing.

### Assume every paving stone may conceal space

The upper city rests partly on buried rooms, conduits, cisterns, and flooded streets. Warning signs of a void include:

- Circular or crescent-shaped cracks.
- A pavement subtly sagging toward its center.
- Reeds or unusually lush moss in one localized patch.
- Cold air rising through joints.
- A hollow sound underfoot.
- Water vanishing between slabs.
- Fresh grit around an opening.
- A court with no visible drainage despite heavy staining.

Do not gather as a group on suspect floors. Cross one at a time, spaced widely, or retreat.

### Avoid underground exploration on an initial visit

Subterranean spaces combine nearly every major hazard:

- Unmapped shafts.
- Flooded passages.
- Unstable ceilings.
- Cold water.
- Poor air.
- Complete darkness.
- Routes that change after storms.
- Loss of communication through thick masonry.

Never dive into the drowned lower city. Clear water and visible stairs create a false sense of accessibility. Depth, temperature, overhead obstructions, and submerged collapse make such exploration highly technical. Even trained cave divers should first establish that local biology, water chemistry, and equipment function are compatible.

### Respect weather-driven transformations

A dry alley can become a stream without rain falling directly overhead. The relevant storm may be occurring in another district or higher watershed.

Leave immediately if you notice:

- Water rising rather than merely flowing.
- Sudden clouding or increased grit in a channel.
- Floating seeds and lichen fragments arriving in quantity.
- A change from intermittent dripping to continuous flow.
- A distant low rushing sound.
- Fog thickening alongside falling temperature.
- New water issuing through wall joints.

Never camp in monumental avenues, paved depressions, low plazas, narrow alleys below reservoirs, or below old gate grooves. Many ceremonial-looking spaces are hydraulic works.

Causeways across marshy saddles should be crossed early in the day and only in stable weather. Probe the edges, stay on the constructed centerline, and do not step into adjacent water to test its depth. A hand-deep sheet may conceal a well, drain, or collapsed chamber.

## Weather and exposure

The cold will be cumulative rather than spectacular. A traveler may remain functional for hours while slowly losing heat through wet boots, damp cuffs, and continual contact with stone.

Schedule regular checks:

- Are hands still dexterous?
- Is anyone shivering continuously?
- Has speech become slow or vague?
- Are people making uncharacteristically poor decisions?
- Are feet numb, pale, or wrinkled?
- Has anyone stopped complaining about the cold despite still being wet?

The last sign is particularly dangerous.

Fog should trigger a change of plan, not merely slower walking. Stop at a known safe point, establish bearings, and decide whether to return or wait. Wandering in hopes of recognizing something will compound errors.

Thunder may be uncommon, but exposed ridges and towers should be avoided during storms. The long echoes may also obscure the direction and distance of rockfall.

## Rockfall and structural collapse

The city’s remaining structures have reached a kind of equilibrium, but travelers can disturb that equilibrium. Do not:

- Climb isolated walls or worn towers without technical assessment.
- Pull blocks free.
- Squeeze through rubble-supported openings.
- Walk directly beneath pale mineral curtains or actively dripping vaults.
- Shelter against retaining walls saturated with water.
- Stand below someone crossing loose terraces.
- Force rotten remnants of doors, beams, or gates.

Freeze-thaw conditions are especially hazardous around dawn after cold nights. Sunlight warming an exposed wall can release stone that froze into place overnight. Following heavy rain, avoid cliff-cut districts and the eastern escarpments until drainage has subsided.

If a block falls nearby, do not immediately rush toward the sound. One movement can precede a larger adjustment.

## Animals and ecology

Most animals will probably avoid humans. Give deer and foxes room to retreat, particularly near narrow passages where they may feel trapped. Do not handle newts, crustaceans, insects, eggs, bones, or nests without proper protocols.

The plant life is sparse and slow-growing. A small patch of moss may represent decades of growth, and a single shrub may be locally important habitat. Walk on bare durable stone when safe, rather than trampling drain-channel vegetation. Do not strip shrubs for fuel or bedding.

Avoid contaminating cisterns with soap, food, chemicals, sunscreen, or human waste. Waste should be packed out. Burying it is inappropriate in a city where thin soil, shallow water, and hidden conduits can spread contamination far beyond the burial point.

## Recommended expedition structure

For a first visit:

1. **Day one:** Establish the return site and high camp. Confirm equipment function, compass behavior, weather patterns, and local time.
2. **Day two:** Survey only the immediate ridge and map all safe return routes.
3. **Following days:** Make radial excursions, returning to the same camp each evening.
4. **Only later:** Establish a second camp, and only if the route between camps remains usable under both dry and wet conditions.

Assign explicit roles:

- Lead navigator.
- Rear navigator who independently tracks the route.
- Weather and water observer.
- Medical lead.
- Log keeper and site recorder.

Every person should still carry enough clothing, water, food, light, and route information to survive a night if separated.

## Psychological preparation

The city’s silence and scale will affect judgment.

There will be no ordinary signs of habitation to measure distance: no smoke, voices, maintained road, or lighted window. Repeated courts and walls may produce a dreamlike sense that one has not moved, while broad views may make distant objectives seem close. The ordinary daylight and recognizable architecture can encourage the dangerous impression that this is merely an abandoned town. It is closer to a mountain range whose ridges happen to be made of buildings.

The absence may also invite speculation about what happened to the inhabitants. Do not let the search for an answer pull the expedition into wells, drowned stairs, or unstable chambers. Nothing in the visible city suggests urgency. It has waited for centuries and will wait through another visit.

Keep written schedules and turn-around rules. Fatigue, fascination, and isolation should not be allowed to renegotiate them.

## What to seek—and what to avoid

The safest rewarding destinations are likely to be:

- High roof terraces with low surrounding walls.
- Open quarry roads.
- Broad ridges above the drainage basins.
- Shallow reservoirs observed from their rims.
- Plazas after water levels have stabilized.
- Towers only where access does not require climbing unstable masonry.

The most dangerous temptations will be:

- Submerged stairways visible through clear water.
- Intact-looking underground rooms.
- Tall viewpoints reached by narrow worn stairs.
- Dry avenues below large catchments.
- Circular reed-filled ponds formed by collapse.
- Eastern cliff chambers and spring-cut foundations.
- Routes that seem like shortcuts through roofed passages.

## Essential final rule

Watch the water more closely than the sky.

The sky tells you what is happening where you stand. The water tells you what is happening across the city: on unseen ridges, in buried reservoirs, and in districts already swallowed by fog. A change in its depth, clarity, direction, or sound is the city’s nearest equivalent to a warning.

Visit lightly, remain on high ground, and leave before weather or curiosity closes the route behind you.
