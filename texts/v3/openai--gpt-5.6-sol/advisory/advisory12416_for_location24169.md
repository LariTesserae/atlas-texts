## Traveler’s preparation brief

Treat this city neither as wilderness nor as a conventional metropolis. It is a living construction system whose streets, buildings, drainage, vegetation, and geology participate in the same slow metabolism. The chief danger is not hostility. It is assuming that anything inert will remain inert, that a route will remain a route, or that familiar physical behavior applies everywhere.

An ordinary human can visit safely, but should arrive through a recognized transit station with a local guide or municipal orientation service. Do not attempt an unsupported crossing of the outer plain.

### Before departure

Arrange the following in advance:

- **A licensed local guide**, preferably one certified in route probability, acoustic anomalies, and active construction fields.
- **Accommodation in an occupied, stable district.** Ask whether the building is expected to retain its current entrance and floor plan for the duration of your stay.
- **A dynamic map subscription.** Static maps become unreliable overnight. Your map should display confidence levels, not merely routes, and update around drainage changes, migrating roads, construction radii, and temporary ponds.
- **A physiology check** for heat intolerance, mold sensitivity, respiratory disease, vertigo, and vestibular problems. Local gravitational variations may be subtle but disorienting.
- **Medical clearance for an unfamiliar biosphere.** Even if the visible plants are harmless, pollen transport is citywide and peaks at night.
- **A guaranteed return window.** Do not schedule a tight transfer. A station may remain functional while its approach changes, or a causeway may temporarily lead somewhere other than its visible alignment suggests.

If your home reality uses standardized spatial constants, have your instruments calibrated locally. Conventional compasses, inertial trackers, laser rangefinders, and surveying tools may provide internally consistent but operationally useless readings near mass-altered structures.

## What to pack

### Clothing

Bring light, breathable clothing for warm, humid conditions, but cover more skin than the temperature seems to require.

Recommended:

- Long sleeves and loose trousers
- Quick-drying underlayers
- A thin waterproof outer shell
- Closed boots with firm soles and ankle support
- Gaiters or sealed cuffs for shallow mist, mud, and vegetation
- Lightweight gloves
- A soft hat without loose cords
- One warmer layer for underground transit and climate-controlled interiors

The ground is young and sharp. Cracks, channel lips, mineral crusts, and clean-edged slabs have not been softened by erosion. Sandals are inappropriate even in plazas.

Choose footwear that can tolerate black silt, warm water, slick moss, ceramic drainage surfaces, and sudden changes between hard and yielding ground. Avoid magnetic soles, powered traction systems, or footwear that tries to “correct” balance automatically unless it is certified for local variable-gravity zones.

### Respiratory and allergy protection

Carry:

- A comfortable fine-particle respirator
- Spare filters
- Antihistamines you already know you tolerate
- Any prescribed inhaler
- Sterile eyewash
- Unscented skin cleanser

Night ventilation carries pollen between districts. Construction can also release drifting mineral grains, even though it produces little noise. Do not assume a clear, damp atmosphere is particle-free.

### Navigation and communication

Your navigation kit should include:

- A locally issued route device
- Physical district tokens or written destination cards
- A short-range beacon compatible with municipal rescue systems
- A non-audio signaling light
- A wrist-contact or bone-conduction communicator
- Chalk, removable tape, or markers approved for local surfaces
- A simple mechanical watch

Do not depend exclusively on speech. Sound can be absorbed, displaced, or delivered to a distant courtyard. In uncertain areas, use light signals, touch codes, or wrist vibration.

Avoid marking walls without permission. Some surfaces are still differentiating into doors, vents, structural fibers, or water channels. A harmless adhesive in your world may obstruct a sensory membrane here.

### Water and food

Carry water despite the humidity. Condensation, temporary pools, gutters, and flowing channels are not automatically potable. They may contain concentrated minerals, engineered microorganisms, construction residues, or active ecological agents.

Bring:

- A sealed water supply for at least one full day
- Electrolytes
- Familiar, packaged food
- A local-spectrum purifier if traveling outside occupied districts

Ordinary expedition filters may not remove dissolved mineral seams, nanoscale construction material, or organisms that do not fit familiar biological categories.

Do not eat city vegetation, even if it resembles fruit, herbs, or edible fern shoots. Some plants store water under pressure, respond to voices, interface with data systems, or participate in infrastructure. Harvesting one may be closer to cutting a cable than picking a berry.

### Personal safety equipment

Useful items include:

- A compact first-aid kit
- Skin-safe antiseptic
- A rigid splint
- A personal location beacon
- A small passive flashlight with amber and red modes
- A nonmetallic walking staff
- A thin emergency groundsheet
- A whistle only for designated acoustic-safe zones

Keep the light modest. The city’s own illumination is low but adequate once your eyes adapt. Bright beams can ruin night vision, attract unwanted attention, interfere with light-based signage, or trigger responsive surfaces.

## On arrival

Spend your first several hours indoors or in a certified public court. Allow your eyes, hearing, balance, and expectations to adjust.

The dim sky does not mean evening is imminent, and the diffuse light gives poor intuitive information about direction and time. Shadows are shallow. Distances across open plazas can be deceptive. Towers may appear conventionally shaped from one angle while occupying space differently from another.

During orientation, learn the local indicators for:

- Stable pedestrian routes
- Roads scheduled to soften or bend
- Active pylon fields
- Fresh construction
- Buildings undergoing self-dismantling
- Gravity-sensitive gardens
- Safe conversation areas
- Night drainage corridors
- Temporary pond formation
- Transit pressure warnings
- Structures that should not be approached from a particular side

A pale wall that is wet and warm may be unfinished rather than damaged. Do not lean on it, test it with a tool, or search for a door. By the next day it may have developed one.

## How to move through the city

### Travel by confidence, not by distance

A destination one kilometer away may be less accessible than one several districts distant. Use routes with the highest current stability rating, even if they appear indirect.

At every major junction:

1. Confirm the destination identifier, not just the street appearance.
2. Check the route’s timestamp.
3. Check whether the route is valid for your direction of travel.
4. Look for drainage and construction notices.
5. Confirm the expected return route separately.

A bridge may continue across dry land or connect to a district not visible from its beginning. This is not necessarily a malfunction. Cross only when your guide or route system verifies the current connection. Do not turn back midway solely because the destination remains invisible.

### Avoid shortcuts

Empty foundations, gardens, reed beds, and bare silt are not vacant in the ordinary sense. They may be:

- Water reserves
- Future construction sites
- Active matter-conditioning zones
- Ecological buffers
- Buried transit corridors
- Areas awaiting a scheduled shift in gravity or drainage

The red groundcover over data conduits is especially useful as a warning. Do not walk through it, camp on it, or follow its flowering patterns as if they were path lights.

### Watch the ground

The plain is nearly level, but it is not easy walking. Look for:

- Fresh ruler-straight cracks
- Pale crust over soft black silt
- Greenish sand exposed beneath gray clay
- Glass-bright mineral seams
- Channels with sharp, undercut edges
- Ground darkening from below
- Mist moving against the apparent slope

A moving ribbon of mist can reveal a ramp, drain, or pressure gradient not obvious to the eye. Do not stand in its path to take photographs.

If the ground begins to warm rapidly, vibrate, compact, or develop a regular grain, leave by your previous route. You may be inside an active formation area.

### Treat pylons as hazardous infrastructure

The black pylons alter the tendencies of matter around them. Never enter a pylon field without authorization. Do not toss objects toward one, fly a personal drone nearby, or use it as a landmark for close navigation.

Possible warning signs of an active field include:

- Unexplained heaviness or lightness
- A sensation that your stride is becoming asymmetrical
- Loose particles moving uphill
- Straight objects appearing subtly bowed
- Your shadow lagging or separating unusually
- Nausea without heat stress
- Equipment repeatedly recalibrating itself

If these occur, do not run. Stop, lower your center of gravity, notify your guide, and retrace your steps slowly. Sudden movement may carry you into a steeper physical gradient.

### Respect survey kites

Do not touch their descending threads, even when the threads stop above the ground. Do not pass directly beneath them unless the route is marked safe. A hanging thread may designate a future road, station, grove opening, or matter transition.

The thread’s failure to touch the ground does not mean there is empty space beneath it.

## Scheduling your day

For a first visit, conduct most travel from midmorning through late afternoon. Dawn and night are beautiful but operationally complex.

### Dawn

At dawn:

- Mist is withdrawing into cisterns.
- Paths are hardening.
- Water may still be moving under apparently dry surfaces.
- Overnight construction may not yet appear on all personal maps.
- Entrances and passages may have changed.

Wait for the first route reconciliation before leaving your lodging. If your doorway, corridor, or street has changed, contact building services rather than improvising an exit.

### Day

Day is the city’s least active period and the easiest time for inexperienced visitors. Even then, carry water and protect against diffuse light; haze can conceal significant ultraviolet or unfamiliar radiation.

The lack of strong sun makes dehydration easy to overlook.

### Evening and night

Night is when the city becomes most visibly alive. It is also when:

- Pollen is transported.
- Condensation becomes runoff.
- Roads adjust permeability.
- Plants unfold.
- Reservoirs expand.
- Mist fills lower streets.
- Construction and dismantling accelerate.
- Local gravitational changes may activate flowers or water systems.

A guided night excursion is highly recommended, but remain on elevated, certified paths. Wear a respirator if advised and keep all belongings attached to your body. Fine grains from a dismantling structure may resemble dust or luminous haze; leave the area rather than walking through them.

Never sleep outdoors in an empty lot. By morning, it may be a pond, a wall bed, a drainage court, or part of another district’s material supply.

## Buildings and accommodation

Stay only in structures currently designated for human habitation. “Unoccupied” does not mean abandoned. Empty buildings may be ventilating, growing, changing function, collecting moisture, or preparing to shed material.

Ask your host:

- Whether the room has stable gravity
- Whether apertures open automatically at night
- Whether the floor plan can change
- How to recognize a structural adjustment tone
- Where to wait during self-reconfiguration
- Whether the building communicates by light, vibration, or sound
- What objects may safely touch the walls
- Where emergency exits are expected to be, rather than where they presently appear

Do not wedge doors open. Do not block pores, slots, channels, or seams. Do not hang wet clothing from cables or translucent ribs. What looks decorative may regulate water, heat, pollen, structural mass, or communication.

If a tower emits a single resonant note, remain calm. It may be adjusting to temperature or answering another structure. If the note is followed by floor vibration, changing wall color, or local evacuation lights, follow building instructions immediately.

## Sound etiquette

Speak quietly outside designated conversation spaces. This is both courtesy and safety.

A whisper beneath an arch may travel intact to a distant courtyard. A shout may vanish locally while disturbing a sensitive structure elsewhere. Do not test echoes.

Use acoustic-safe locations for:

- Personal conversations
- Medical discussions
- Trade negotiations
- Recorded interviews
- Music
- Calls to people in other realities

Assume that any conversation held near an untested arch, bridge cavity, reservoir, or thin surface may be transmitted unpredictably.

If you hear a voice that seems close but cannot locate its speaker, do not follow it blindly. Check your communication device and move to a mapped acoustic reference point.

## Plants and animals

Observe without touching.

The vegetation may be biological, engineered, infrastructural, or all three. In particular:

- Do not speak closely to vines that orient toward conversation.
- Do not puncture water-storing shrubs.
- Do not step on red conduit groundcover.
- Do not collect flowers whose opening patterns encode traffic.
- Do not rest against creepers covering unused facades; the facade may still be active.
- Do not assume moss is safe footing.

The six-legged waders in temporary pools appear delicate and nonaggressive, but give them space. Their rarity makes interference ecologically significant. Avoid the dark gliders among towers; do not attempt to illuminate or track them with a drone.

If reeds begin parting in a long, continuous line, leave the water’s edge calmly. Do not pursue, throw objects, or wait for tracks. Whatever moves there may interact with the stems or ground in ways that do not produce ordinary evidence.

Use only local insect repellents and biocides. Imported chemicals could harm pollination systems or react with engineered plants, and conventional “bug spray” may be useless in a place where pollination is performed by vents and gravity.

## Social expectations

Residents are likely to regard adaptability as basic civic competence. A visitor who insists that an address, property line, or entrance remain fixed may seem unreasonable.

Remember:

- Ownership applies to intervals and functions more than permanent coordinates.
- A garden may become a station approach without being “destroyed.”
- A pond appearing beneath a licensed foundation may be accepted as a decision of the ground, not a planning failure.
- Quiet construction is still construction.
- Empty space may be cultivated, reserved, or socially important.
- Asking “When will the city be finished?” is likely to misunderstand the place.

Ask for locations by current district relation, transit access, or municipal identifier rather than by visual landmark alone. Instead of “the café beside the white tower,” use something like “the tea court currently connected to Station Cluster Seven.”

Do not joke about unstable architecture in ways that imply neglect. The city’s changes are managed, but management here means negotiation rather than domination.

## Photography, samples, and souvenirs

Photography may require spatial as well as personal consent. A structure that has no visible occupants may still be recording, growing, or serving as civic infrastructure.

Avoid:

- Flash photography near responsive walls or animals
- Laser focus systems near survey threads
- Drones around towers, pylons, transit routes, or gliding fauna
- Soil or mineral collection
- Plant cuttings
- Bottled condensation
- Pieces of fresh wall material

The bright seam resembling powdered glass may be valuable, hazardous, structurally significant, or all three. Leave it in place.

Purchase souvenirs from licensed makers. The safest objects are those certified as materially settled and non-germinating. Ask whether an item will remain the same shape, mass, and function after leaving local physical conditions.

## Medical and emergency guidance

Seek assistance promptly for:

- Persistent vertigo
- A sense of uneven body weight
- Unexplained time loss
- Skin irritation after contact with mist or plants
- Breathing difficulty at night
- Repeated auditory displacement
- Equipment shadows or reflections behaving out of sync
- Mud or mineral material that continues adhering after washing
- A route device that identifies you as being in multiple districts

If lost, do not keep walking in hopes of finding a familiar landmark. Move to the nearest stable, occupied surface, activate your beacon, and display a steady local rescue color. Avoid standing under arches, elevated platforms, survey threads, or drainage outlets.

If separated from your guide at night:

1. Step out of flowing mist if possible.
2. Stay off red groundcover and softening roads.
3. Do not enter the nearest empty building.
4. Check for pylon-field warnings.
5. Send location data and remain still unless instructed otherwise.

If a new wall rises around or beside you, do not cut through it. Fresh structures may be soft, but they are not necessarily weak, and damaging them could trigger further growth. Signal municipal construction services.

## The right mindset

The most useful preparation is psychological. Do not seek permanence as reassurance. Seek current agreement.

A path is safe because, for the moment, the ground, water, structures, and civic systems all permit it to function as a path. A room is yours because the building currently supports that arrangement. A district is not incomplete because open earth lies between its towers. The open earth may be as active and intentional as the towers themselves.

Travel slowly. Recheck what you think you recognize. Accept local guidance even when your eyes insist that the destination is obvious. Spend time in the sunken tea courts and mapped gardens, where conversation can remain where it is spoken. Watch condensation gather on cables at evening. From an elevated walkway, observe the mist entering the lower streets and the vegetation awakening beneath it.

A first-time visitor should not try to master the city. The city itself does not operate through mastery. Visit as you would visit a warm marsh at night, a reef under construction, or a young geological formation that has learned architecture: with curiosity, restraint, and a willingness to let every route remain provisional.
