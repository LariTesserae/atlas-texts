## Overall assessment

Treat the basin as an active construction site whose machinery includes weather, geology, biology, and probability. It is not merely unfamiliar wilderness. Ordinary assumptions—downhill water, solid rock, harmless reflections, stable maps, predictable decay—are unreliable there.

An ordinary traveler should visit only as part of a supported reconnaissance team. Do not go alone, do not camp in the basin, and do not plan to remain through the evening condensation event. Your exit must stay open and supervised from outside.

## Before entering

### Establish an external base

Place the transit gate, vehicle, or return anchor well outside the basin, preferably on terrain already geologically mature. Do not establish it:

- On the basin floor
- Near the black arch or pylons
- In mist flowing through any structure
- On newly poured-looking rock
- Anywhere visible only during the pale flashes

Keep a person on the safe side whose sole task is maintaining the return route. The basin may gradually incorporate unattended equipment, and a portal fixture left overnight could become part of the mountain or acquire an unwanted duplicate.

Set a strict turnaround time well before the subtle onset of “evening.” Local light is too poor to serve as a clock, so use several independent timers. Vary their alarm tones; never program exactly seven identical beeps.

### Assume the atmosphere is unsafe

The warmth and humidity do not prove the air is breathable. Wear a positive-pressure environmental suit with its own air supply. Ordinary filters may not exclude the droplet-borne mechanisms, especially if they are spatial distortions rather than particles.

The suit should include:

- Full face and eye protection
- Flash-damping visor
- Thermal regulation for hot, saturated air
- Hearing protection that reduces bass concussion without completely isolating you from teammates
- Chest and neck padding against the pressure of nearby wingbeats
- Sealed, nonabsorbent outer layers
- High-traction soles
- No loose straps, dangling tools, or exposed skin

Prefer ceramics, stable polymers, and composite equipment over bare metal. Metal will sweat continuously, may corrode unpredictably, and may encourage conductive mineral growth. All tools should be tethered to the wearer.

Carry no food or drink outside sealed suit systems. Do not drink the pool water, even after treatment.

### Travel light, but redundantly

Bring:

- Two independent navigation systems
- A physical route sketch on waterproof material
- Inertial tracking rather than reliance on local landmarks
- Thermal and multispectral imaging
- A short, tethered probe for testing apparent ground
- A rescue line connected to the team, not fixed into basin rock
- Compact medical and extraction equipment
- Variable-tone communications
- Sample containers only if collection has been specifically authorized
- A complete item inventory

Do not use pitons, buried anchors, trail markers, or equipment caches. The ground may close around them, duplicate them, or reinterpret them as geology.

## Moving through the basin

### Keep the visit short and the group small

Travel in teams of three or four. Maintain visual contact, but spread out enough that one false patch of ground cannot take everyone at once. Use a predetermined route and avoid improvising excursions toward newly revealed structures.

Count personnel and equipment frequently. Do not trust the apparent number of structures, pools, or paths.

### Test every surface

Rock in the basin may change hardness over hours or in response to being stepped on. Walk slowly and probe ahead. Avoid:

- Fine branching cracks
- Fresh mineral seams
- Surfaces that look poured, fibrous, or unusually seamless
- Areas between the pylons
- Copper mats
- Clay cups around the humming reeds
- Flat, leather-like shapes
- Ground whose depth or color changes as you approach

The three-legged predators disguise themselves by altering the apparent ground. Visual inspection alone is inadequate. Compare ordinary sight with thermal imaging, polarization, and physical probing. If the instruments disagree, retreat.

Never kill a predator unless required to save a person. Its remains can become a persistent trapping stain.

### Regard animal behavior as an alarm system

Do not touch, feed, collect, or corner any organism.

If all the pale grazers freeze and face one direction, freeze as well. Observe their orientation, check above and behind you, and prepare to withdraw. They are responding to something you may be unable to perceive.

When swimmers begin gathering or agitating in a pool, move away. Their mass aerial migrations are loud, chaotic, and capable of striking a visor or driving a startled traveler into deeper water or over the open edge.

If the large fliers descend, leave immediately. Their low wingbeats may impair balance, breathing, and communication. Never stand beneath the visible distortions that resemble their overhead lines.

### Stay away from the open edge

Warm mist disguises a major drop, and local water motion makes slope difficult to judge. Maintain a generous exclusion zone even if terraces or towers appear beyond the rim during a flash. Features revealed there may become persistent, but persistence does not make them safe or reachable.

Do not tether to the black arch. Do not walk through it. Do not stand beneath the upward-falling drops.

## Sound discipline

The rule concerning seven repetitions should be taken literally.

Avoid producing the same sound exactly seven times. This includes:

- Alarm beeps
- Knocks
- Sonar or lidar pings
- Foot-stamps used as signals
- Repeated names or calls
- Tool impacts
- Test tones

Configure devices to vary pitch, duration, or interval automatically. Use speech or visual hand signals rather than repetitive coded taps. If a sound has accidentally been repeated six times, do not produce a seventh matching instance. If it has already occurred seven times, do not touch the space where it sounded; droplets and small organisms may be able to rest on the resulting acoustic structure.

The basin is naturally loud, so establish concise communication protocols before entry. Noise-canceling equipment should not erase all ambient sound: the reeds, cracks, animals, and arch may provide the earliest warning of changing conditions.

## Structures

The structures should be treated as functioning environmental organs, not ruins.

### The black arch

Keep well clear. Its effects include altered water direction, low-frequency vibration, disappearance, delayed return, and mineral modification of living bodies. Nothing living should pass through it or remain among the animals gathering nearby.

### The three-sided shrine

Do not enter during routine exploration. The cool, dry interior is attractive precisely because the rest of the basin is physically stressful, but the moving perforations and variable future image indicate ongoing operation.

It may be the least bad emergency refuge if trapped during condensation, but only as a last resort. If forced inside:

- Do not sit on the bench
- Do not place anything in the recess
- Do not touch the image
- Do not alternate observers to test how it changes
- Keep the exit continuously visible
- Leave as soon as exterior conditions permit

### The pylons

Do not touch them. Avoid the ground between them and do not collect newly appearing organisms there. Palm-matching grooves suggest that contact initiates some form of reading or continuation process. Gloves should not be assumed to prevent it.

### The uncertain structures

Do not use the ladder, reflected door, silver pins, buried antenna, or white building. In particular:

- Do not climb down a ladder into apparently solid rock.
- Do not approach a door by following its reflection.
- Do not obstruct or pocket migrating pins.
- Do not dig.
- Do not chase the white building.

Anything that cannot be approached directly should not be used for shelter or navigation.

## Water and weather

All water should be considered biologically, mechanically, and causally active.

Do not wade through pools simply because they appear ankle-deep. Their visual darkness may not correspond to depth, and disturbance triggers light and animal activity. Water also seeks living heat. A breach in the suit may therefore attract moisture rather than merely admit it.

Watch for these signs of the evening transition:

- Heat rising through the soles
- Copper mats opening widely
- New pools appearing on previously dry ground
- Fliers descending
- Increased calls along the cliffs
- Heavy impacts from inside the mountain
- Stronger vibration from the arch

At the first two signs, withdraw. Do not wait for the rain.

### If the condensation event begins

You may receive almost no warning: droplets form throughout open air rather than falling from clouds.

Immediately:

1. Seal all suit ports and stop exposed sampling.
2. Move away from the arch, pylons, pools, cliffs, and open edge.
3. Crouch low on previously tested ground.
4. Clip all team members to the rescue line.
5. Protect visor, air connections, and joints.
6. Do not run unless the route to the exit is unmistakable.
7. Expect water to travel sideways, upward, or toward body heat.
8. Leave as soon as the initial density decreases.

Do not shelter beneath an overhang merely because it would protect against normal rain. Droplets already occupy the air, and water hangs beneath ledges.

## Biological and medical precautions

Nothing from the basin should contact the traveler’s body. Even apparently mineral growth may represent a biological or probabilistic process.

A cut, suit breach, bite, sting, or prolonged water contact requires immediate extraction. Do not wait for symptoms. Record any:

- Mineral deposits around joints
- Changes in flexibility or texture of equipment
- Condensation outlining old injuries
- Duplicated tools or personal items
- New grooves matching skin patterns
- Sensations of weight associated with sound
- Memories of routes or structures other team members did not see

If a person dies, recover the body immediately if this can be done without additional casualties. Dead matter is rapidly revised in the basin. A body left behind may cease to be recoverable in any ordinary sense, while its shape remains active in the weather.

## Preventing incorporation

Nothing should remain in the basin overnight—not boots, waste, blood, food, probes, bodies, or broken equipment. Before departure, conduct a spoken and visual inventory. Inspect soles and suit joints for:

- New stone flanges
- Rootlike mineral growth
- Pale nodules
- Duplicate fragments
- Copper scales
- Condensed outlines that remain after wiping

If equipment has become attached to the ground, do not wrench it free. Detach from it at a designed breakaway point and record the loss. Violent extraction could create a lasting crack or provoke a larger geological response.

## Decontamination and quarantine

Exit into a sealed receiving area rather than directly into an inhabited environment.

Recommended procedure:

1. Photograph the whole team before removing anything.
2. Compare people and equipment against the entry inventory.
3. Check for duplicates, missing shadows, altered reflections, and unexplained mineral seams.
4. Remove outer layers robotically if possible.
5. Isolate all liquid and condensation.
6. Quarantine travelers separately but within communication range.
7. Perform repeated imaging over several days, especially around joints, lungs, old fractures, and suit-contact points.
8. Do not discard contaminated material into soil, waterways, or ordinary incineration systems.

A returning person or object may have acquired only a fractional change in probability. That is enough to justify prolonged observation.

## Psychological preparation

Expect sensory contradiction. The basin is warm when it should be cold, wet without drainage, bright beneath dark water, and crowded with movement that does not obey familiar mechanics. It may also reveal features that become real only after being noticed.

Do not treat every flash, tower, silhouette, or changing future image as a message intended for you. Do not follow apparently familiar buildings. Do not assume that a teammate who remembers a structure differently is confused. Record disagreements without trying to settle them on site.

The correct attitude is neither panic nor wonder-driven curiosity. Move as though crossing a half-finished floor while the builders are still deciding where gravity, rivers, species, and history will go.

Visit briefly. Touch almost nothing. Leave before the rain teaches the mountain a new rule that includes you.
