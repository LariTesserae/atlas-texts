## General assessment

This basin is not overtly hostile, but it is profoundly unrewarding of carelessness. It offers no water, food, dependable shelter, navigational landmarks, or living material that can be ethically harvested. Its warmth and small size may create a false sense of safety. Treat it as a remote salt desert and an exceptionally fragile biological site.

Plan for a short, self-contained visit. Everything needed should come through the crossing with you, and everything you bring—including dust, crumbs, waste, and microbes as far as practicable—should leave with you.

## Before arrival

- **Confirm the atmosphere** rather than assuming it matches your home world. Test oxygen, carbon dioxide, toxic gases, pressure, and airborne mineral dust before removing respiratory protection.
- **Obtain weather information beyond the rim.** The basin itself may appear windless even when conditions are changing outside it. Because it is a closed depression, rare rainfall elsewhere could produce runoff or flooding with little local warning.
- **Calibrate navigation instruments locally.** Do not rely solely on magnetic compasses, satellite systems, familiar constellations, or home-universe timekeeping.
- **Establish a dependable return point.** Place the portal or transit anchor on stable, bare ground where it will not scour the clay, burn vegetation, or cover ancient lichens with dust. Avoid machinery with downward exhaust.
- **Decontaminate boots, clothing, tents, and cases.** Remove seeds, soil, insects, fungal spores, and food residue. A place with so few decomposers and competitors could preserve introduced material for an extremely long time.

Leave your itinerary and return deadline with someone outside the basin. Travel with at least one companion if possible.

## Essential equipment

Carry:

- More drinking water than the planned visit requires, plus reserve electrolytes.
- A wide-brimmed hat and sun protection if arrival or departure may occur near daylight.
- Light-colored, breathable clothing with a warmer layer for unexpected overnight cooling.
- Sturdy closed boots. Natural flint flakes may be extremely sharp even when half buried.
- Eye protection and a particulate mask or respirator for gypsum, salt, and clay dust.
- Two independent lights per person, with spare power. A dim red setting is useful for preserving night vision; retain a strong white beam for route-finding and medical use.
- A freestanding illuminated or retroreflective marker for the return point.
- A locally functional radio or direct line-of-sight communicator.
- First-aid supplies, especially irrigation, dressings, and treatment for cuts and eye contamination.
- A ground cloth or portable seat. Do not assume a gray plant mound is an inert cushion.
- Sealable containers for all waste, including food scraps and bodily waste.
- Weather and atmospheric monitors if available.
- A simple rangefinder, inertial navigator, or spool line for poor visibility. The basin is only about half a kilometer across, but darkness and a nearly featureless floor can make that distance deceptive.

Do not count on the basin’s flint, stems, niches, or mineral deposits as survival supplies. They are possibilities, not provisions.

## Moving through the basin

Enter slowly and pause to let your eyes adjust. In darkness, the rim may disappear into the sky and the plants may not become visible until they are within a few steps.

Choose a route across plainly bare clay and mark it with equipment you will retrieve. Do not make cairns, scratch arrows into limestone, or break vegetation as trail markers. Keep the return light behind you when possible.

The compacted floor should generally support walking, but test pale crusts before putting full weight on them. Salt and gypsum can conceal softer clay. Walk rather than run: there are settled stones, shallow cracks, and protruding flint, all difficult to judge under flat illumination.

Give every plant a wide berth. The visible clump may be small, but its root zone is represented by the bare circle around it. Do not step within that circle if another route is available. Avoid the black lichens on the rim entirely. A bootprint across a handspan of lichen could erase growth older than many civilizations.

Remain within sight or reliable radio contact of companions. Calls and footsteps may not carry as expected in the acoustically dead hollow. Agree beforehand on check-in intervals and emergency light signals.

## Heat, water, and weather

The stored nighttime warmth is likely to be more fatiguing than it first seems. With little air movement, sweat may not cool you efficiently, especially in protective clothing. Drink on a schedule rather than waiting for thirst. Monitor one another for headache, irritability, clumsiness, or confusion.

Do not sleep directly on the warm mineral floor without insulation. It can continue transferring heat into the body, while temperatures later in the night may change faster than expected near the surface.

At the first indication of rain, distant lightning, rising wind above the rim, or unexplained water sounds, leave the floor and withdraw by the prepared route. Do not rely on the shallow limestone niches as flood shelter. They are also too small and unstable to justify using as campsites.

## Conduct in the hollow

This is a place where tiny disturbances may remain visible for generations.

- Do not collect flint, ocher, salt, plants, or lichen unless the visit is explicitly scientific and properly controlled.
- Do not crush stems to smell them. Their faint bitterness is not worth destroying living tissue that may take decades to replace.
- Do not light a fire. There is little fuel, but any scorch, soot, or melted mineral surface could persist indefinitely.
- Do not drive stakes, dig latrines, overturn stones, or widen limestone niches.
- Eat over a container and recover every crumb.
- Avoid urinating on the ground; the sudden addition of water, salts, and organic nutrients could significantly alter the immediate ecology.
- Keep voices and artificial sound modest. Silence is one of the site’s defining conditions, and there is no practical reason to overwhelm it.

Photography and non-contact observation are the most appropriate activities. If samples are necessary, take the smallest possible amount from already detached material and record the exact location.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the basin to seem both smaller and larger than it is. Intellectually, you will know the rim is never far away. Sensually, a lamp will reveal only a small circle of bone-colored floor, isolated plants, and occasional stones, making the darkness beyond feel unbounded.

The silence may become psychologically forceful. Ordinary bodily sounds—breathing, clothing, swallowing, equipment clicks—will seem intrusive. Some travelers may interpret the lack of animal noise as danger or begin imagining movement at the edge of the light. Discuss this possibility in advance. If someone becomes anxious, do not tease them or send them back alone; orient toward the return marker and leave together.

The best way to experience the basin is probably not to cross it rapidly. Walk to a durable patch of bare ground, sit on your own ground cloth, extinguish all but the return marker for a few minutes, and remain still. Let the stored warmth, weak overhead brushing of air, and sparse outlines establish the true scale of the place. Then relight your route carefully and depart without leaving evidence that you were there.

The basin contains the raw beginnings of tools, shelter, and subsistence, but almost none of their completed forms. Do not mistake that latent usefulness for hospitality. Its chief lesson is patience, and the most appropriate preparation is to arrive needing nothing from it.
