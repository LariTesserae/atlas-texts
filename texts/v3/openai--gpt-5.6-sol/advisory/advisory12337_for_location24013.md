## Before you go

Do not attempt an unguided descent. The entrance is easy to miss, the city has no street plan in the horizontal sense, and familiar emergency signals are useless in its noise. Arrange for a resident guide to meet you on the salt plain at a fixed time and marker. Do not rely on shouted names, whistles, radio audio, or directions such as “down the main stair.” Bring a surface beacon so your return fissure can be found even after wind has altered its appearance.

Confirm three things before committing to the crack:

- The atmosphere is breathable by human standards at several depths, not merely near the entrance.
- Your guide understands that you require liquid water and produce liquid waste.
- A quiet, lined place has been designated where you can drink without spilling.

The second point is crucial. To the inhabitants, liquid water may be less like food and more like an exotic corrosive. A leak can dissolve saltstone, weaken a socket, alter a tuned shaft, or kill growths based on oil chemistry. Never pour water onto the ground, wash in public, or offer anyone a drink as a casual courtesy.

Carry your own food. Nothing local should be assumed digestible merely because it is cultivated. The black-green fans contain mineral oils, resins, acids, and metabolic compounds evolved in a waterless chemistry. Even airborne “spores” may be inorganic or toxic to terrestrial lungs.

## Essential equipment

### Hearing and vibration protection

The noise is not just loud. Much of it is infrasonic and conducted through floors, rails, ropes, and your skeleton. Ordinary earplugs will protect against some audible injury but will not prevent chest pressure, nausea, headache, disorientation, or the sensation that your teeth are humming.

Bring:

- High-grade earplugs and over-ear defenders
- A dosimeter capable of measuring low-frequency sound
- A vibration monitor worn against the torso
- A respirator that does not depend on audible alarms
- Haptic or visual communication devices
- Anti-nausea medication you have tested beforehand

Agree on tactile signals with your guide: stop, crouch, follow, danger above, danger below, return, and secure your tether. Learn the city’s principal structural warning patterns, but do not strike beams yourself. A random hammering may be interpreted as an alarm or may interfere with a real one.

Limit your first stay to a few hours. Infrasound exposure can impair judgment before you recognize that anything is wrong. Leave if you develop vertigo, chest pain, visual flicker, panic without apparent cause, or difficulty coordinating your hands.

### Climbing and fall protection

Treat the entire city as a climbing environment. A bridge rail is not proof of safety, and a doorway may open onto a woven floor suspended over a drop.

Carry:

- A comfortable full-body harness
- Two independent tethers
- Padded slings that will not cut local ropes or saltstone
- Gloves with strong grip but good tactile sensitivity
- Close-fitting clothing without loose straps
- Flexible, high-traction footwear
- A compact rescue ascender and descent device
- A helmet with chin strap and eye protection

Do not clip metal hardware directly onto ancient local cables, plant laminates, or carved anchor holes unless a resident approves. Their structures are designed to flex. A rigid connection can concentrate loads and cause exactly the failure you hoped to prevent.

Keep yourself attached whenever crossing open bridges, exposed stairs, garden ladders, or districts built on woven platforms. Never assume that a fall will end on a lower street. Much of the city has only air beneath it.

### Light and eye protection

Carry a headlamp, a chest-mounted backup, and chemical or sealed emergency lights. Darkness begins abruptly beside reflected sunlight.

Use polarized or adjustable-tint eye protection in the upper districts. The reflector network can produce painful glare and localized furnace points. Do not touch or turn any reflector. A disk that appears decorative may be ripening a crop, lighting a stair, or regulating heat in another neighborhood.

Avoid highly reflective clothing or equipment. An accidental moving flash could blind people on bridges or redirect concentrated light onto dry, combustible growth.

### Water and waste

Bring water in several small, double-contained flexible reservoirs rather than one large bottle. Keep each inside a waterproof secondary pouch. A single puncture must not release your whole supply.

Drink only where your hosts direct you, ideally over a glazed or glass-lined catch basin. Use a bite valve rather than an open cup. Account for every drop.

Human sweat, urine, saliva, and wash water are all chemically significant here. Bring:

- Sealable waste bags
- Absorbent, inert pads
- Waterless skin-cleaning materials
- A contained toilet system
- Spare liners for your sleeping area

Do not spit. Do not urinate into the shaft. Do not clean salt dust from equipment with water while in the city.

## Clothing and bodily care

Expect heat, fierce reflected light, abrasive salt, and powerful drafts within a few paces of one another. Wear breathable but tightly woven layers. Cover your skin against crystal dust and weak plant acids. Salt will enter seams, abrade skin, corrode metal, and turn sweat into concentrated brine.

Inspect your feet and harness points frequently. Small abrasions can become severe under salt contamination. Use a waterless barrier ointment before descending, but choose one with little scent and no volatile solvent; local organisms may react to unfamiliar hydrocarbons.

Wear goggles during the noon winds and the evening reversal. A face covering will keep out airborne crystals, resin fragments, and mineral spores. If the aromatic venting causes wheezing, confusion, burning eyes, or numbness, switch to filtered air and move upward.

Do not use flame. The city contains dry fiber, resin, wax, mineral oil, gas-filled bladders, and strong drafts. A match could become a district fire.

## Entering the city

Approach in the morning if possible. Noon brings the hardest downward winds, flying crystals, moving bridges, and inflating gardens. Sunset brings the daily crescendo and current reversal. Neither is a suitable time for a first descent.

The entrance may be shoulder-width. Remove your pack and lower it separately rather than forcing yourself through with altered balance. Once below, pause before proceeding. The sudden combination of vertical exposure, noise, heat, glare, and vibration can overwhelm even experienced travelers.

Do not judge distances by sound. Echoes and tuned shafts make nearby mechanisms seem remote and deep tones seem to come from inside your body. Follow visual or tactile directions only.

Keep one hand free. Residents have worn deep handholds into the stone; use only those your guide indicates. Some grooves may be plant channels, acoustic features, or signs rather than grips.

## Conduct among the inhabitants

Expect conversation at intimate distance. Someone placing their mouth close to your ear is being practical, not aggressive or affectionate. Likewise, residents may take your wrist, shoulder, or harness to communicate through touch.

Learn local hand signs before arrival. At minimum, know:

- Greeting and thanks
- Permission to pass
- “I do not understand”
- “I need to stop”
- “My breathing is impaired”
- “My tether is caught”
- “Water leak”
- “Structural warning”
- “Take me upward”

Do not shout to overcome the city. You will injure your voice without becoming intelligible.

Never adjust a shutter, vane, pipe, rope, reflector, counterweight, garden vent, or clay flap. The city’s infrastructure is one interdependent aerodynamic instrument. What looks like a loose panel may regulate pressure several districts below.

Avoid stepping on root-pads, copper cords, white scale mats, or brittle tubes. Gardens occupy walls, ceilings, bridge undersides, and abandoned masonry; “empty” surfaces may be intensively cultivated.

Ask before touching sail-backed crawlers. Do not encourage them to land on you, and never open a bright membrane to examine it. A gust could carry the animal into the central void.

If a hollow-wing descends, do not point excitedly, pursue it, or congratulate anyone on the sighting. Follow the local response. Its descent is considered a warning that the city’s breath is failing or changing.

## The daily pause

Before sunset, secure all equipment and move to a sheltered, approved position. The meeting of opposing currents produces the day’s loudest and most violent interval. Bridges may buck, loose salt may become airborne, and low tones may cause severe bodily discomfort.

Then the sound will stop for roughly three breaths.

When it does:

- Stop moving.
- Do not speak.
- Do not clap, test the silence, or activate an audible device.
- Keep a hand on a secure rail or structural member if your hosts do so.
- Watch your guide.

The pause is both a physical transition and a civic moment. Residents halt because the air currents are changing and because the silence has cultural weight. A visitor who fills it with noise will appear reckless at best.

The night wind begins afterward, rising warm from below. Do not mistake this reversal for a safe opportunity to explore deeper levels.

## Accommodation

Request a room in an upper or middle district, away from major pipes, spinning vanes, reflectors, and bridge junctions. A local “quiet room” will still be very loud by human standards. Its soft woven walls are designed to absorb higher frequencies, not necessarily the deep vibration that disturbs human sleep.

Bring a padded sleeping sling or mat that isolates you somewhat from the floor. Do not take strong sedatives: structural alarms are tactile, and you may need to wake and evacuate quickly. Secure every loose object before sleeping. Anything dropped through a floor gap may be unrecoverable and could damage structures below.

Expect persistent phantom sound after leaving—a roaring or humming in apparent silence. If it lasts, or if hearing remains muffled, seek medical evaluation.

## Where not to go

Do not visit the Last Floor on your first trip. It is a rope web over an unmeasured shaft, beside pipes powerful enough to heave the platform. It combines fall danger, low-frequency exposure, darkness, uncertain air composition, and profound psychological stress.

If you later go with specialist preparation:

- Use two independent belays anchored in an inhabited district above.
- Carry your own atmosphere monitor and breathing supply.
- Attach nothing to the deep pipes.
- Empty your pockets completely.
- Do not drop an object “to hear how deep it is.” You will not hear it, and the act may be offensive, hazardous, or both.
- Leave immediately if the rope motion ceases unexpectedly. In this city, loss of vibration can be as significant as excess vibration.

Also avoid newly exposed salt surfaces marked by parallel grooves. Whether or not the pale burrowers exist, residents may treat such areas as unstable or inhabited.

## Emergency priorities

If a structural warning begins, do not run. Running causes falls and adds unpredictable loads to flexible bridges. Clip in, lower your center of gravity, and follow the guide’s tactile instructions.

If you spill water:

1. Signal immediately.
2. Prevent it from spreading with absorbent pads.
3. Do not conceal the accident.
4. Keep people off the affected structure.
5. Let local builders decide whether the saltstone, rope, or root-pads must be removed.

If separated, remain at the last secure inhabited platform. Activate a visual beacon shielded from reflector paths and use the agreed haptic or structural signal only if authorized. Do not descend in search of voices.

If the city falls unexpectedly silent outside the normal pause, treat it as an emergency. Secure yourself and look to residents. A stopped draft may mean a blocked shaft, pressure reversal, major collapse, or a change originating far below.

## What to bring as gifts

Useful gifts should be light, flexible, dry, and understandable without liquid technology. Good possibilities include:

- Fine corrosion-resistant cutting tools
- Soft metal foil
- Heat-resistant fiber
- Optical filters
- Small sealed bearings
- Durable cord
- Mineral pigments
- Compact vibration-measuring instruments
- Polished but controllable reflector materials

Ask first. A rigid steel tool is not automatically superior to a copper knife or shell blade in a city that survives by bending.

Do not bring flowers, drinks, moist food, or anything that leaks. Avoid self-heating batteries and volatile fuels.

## The right mindset

Prepare less as if visiting an underground settlement and more as if boarding a vertical ship whose hull, farms, streets, language, weather, and music are all the same mechanism.

Nothing is truly still. A wall may be a crop. A railing may be a warning line. A draft may be irrigation. A pipe may be holding a neighborhood in aerodynamic balance. “Old” does not mean abandoned, “fragile” does not mean poorly made, and “collapse” does not always mean disaster.

Above all, resist the temptation to solve the city. Its people have lived inside vibration, erosion, and controlled instability for generations. Your task is not to make it quieter, firmer, wetter, or more familiar. Your task is to move lightly through its breathing space—and to leave without changing the tune.
