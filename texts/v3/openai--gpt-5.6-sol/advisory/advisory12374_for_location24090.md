## Overall recommendation

Treat the plateau as a functioning industrial installation, an alpine environment, and a slowly deforming spatial anomaly at the same time. Its apparent calm is not safety. Most hazards are quiet, delayed, or disguised as ordinary infrastructure.

An ordinary human should not visit alone. Arrange a local guide from an established settlement, preferably one who works the route you intend to take rather than merely knows the district. Avoid the “new regions” entirely on a first visit.

## Before departure

### Medical preparation

- Confirm that you can tolerate cold, thin air, prolonged glare, ladders, and enclosed industrial spaces.
- Bring any essential medication in duplicate, divided between two people. Printed labels are better than electronic records.
- Update tetanus and other routine vaccinations. The plateau combines sharp metal, standing water, animal contact, and unknown microorganisms.
- If possible, establish a baseline for blood oxygen, hearing, vision, and neurological function. This makes subtle exposure or reality-related changes easier to recognize.
- Do not rely on local plants or animals being biologically compatible. Serial markings and copper-colored veins are warning enough.

### Entry and return plan

Your return arrangement should not depend on a single clock, doorway, bridge, reflection, or rail line.

Set:

1. A primary return point in an old, inhabited district.
2. A secondary return point reachable without crossing a marked unstable zone.
3. Check-in times based on both elapsed time and local events, such as lamp sequences.
4. A maximum absence interval after which your support team closes or abandons the route.

Synchronize several clocks before arrival: mechanical, electronic, and chemical or consumable timers. Keep them separated. If they disagree, do not average them. Record the disagreement and return by the safest known route.

## Clothing and equipment

### Wear

- Layered cold-weather clothing suitable for damp, windy high country.
- A waterproof outer shell that does not conduct electricity.
- A climbing helmet with chin strap.
- High-ankle boots with excellent wet-metal traction and electrically insulating soles.
- Cut-resistant gloves with enough dexterity for ladders and rails.
- Clear and dark eye protection. The glare can be severe even in cool conditions.
- Hearing protection, but not continuously. Important local sounds are often very small.
- A compact respirator for dust, slag particles, mold, sealant fumes, and vent releases.
- Supplemental oxygen if the atmosphere has not been tested for your physiology.

Avoid loose straps, flowing clothing, magnetic accessories, and jewelry. Machinery that appears motionless may still advance, rotate, or close on very long cycles.

### Carry

- Two independent lights, plus a low-output red lamp.
- A nonreflective emergency hood or cloth. Reflections are not always passive here.
- A short insulated probe for testing plates, puddles, grates, and apparent depth.
- Chalk, grease pencil, and physical tags approved by your guide.
- Paper maps and a bound notebook. Use pencil; damp and cold are hard on ink.
- A mechanical compass, though it should be treated only as one observation among many.
- A laser rangefinder and a spool range line for ordinary spaces. Do not stretch lines across anomalous thresholds.
- An oxygen meter and basic atmosphere detector.
- A contact-voltage detector.
- A personal fall-arrest harness for conventional catwalks and ladders.
- Water, sealed food, electrolyte salts, and a compact stove that does not draw from local utilities.
- A first-aid kit emphasizing punctures, cuts, burns, eye irrigation, hypothermia, and electrical injury.
- A rigid sleeping platform or insulated mat. Never sleep directly on a plate, access hatch, cable trench, or warm panel.
- Several small ceramic washers or other locally accepted trade goods, purchased from inhabitants rather than removed from machinery.

Use electronics as recording aids, not as your sole means of navigation. Label batteries and memory cards physically. A device that reports impossible coordinates may be accurately describing its own condition rather than yours.

## Movement on the plateau

### Assume every surface has a purpose

There is no inert ground. A plaza may be a pressure door; a wall may be structural; a dry gutter may activate without rain.

Walk where locals walk. Stay off:

- Black-sealed seams
- Plates that flex visibly
- Inspection trenches
- Painted safety stripes used by pipeline animals
- Warm joints surrounded by red grass
- Bolt patterns that are unusually clean
- Areas where condensation stops abruptly
- Hatches incorporated into public squares
- Rails, even if no cart is visible
- Shadows that do not match the current light

Do not hammer pitons, drill anchors, move counterweights, remove bolts, or “improve” a railing. Even a loose-looking part may participate in a machine whose motion is measured in months.

Before crossing a broad plate, watch a local cross it. Move one person at a time. If cups, puddles, hanging cables, or distant rooflines begin tilting together, leave by the route you entered. Something beneath the plates may be passing.

### Vertical travel

Ladders will often be slick with condensation. Test every rung and use three points of contact. Do not trust a lift unless residents use it routinely and can explain where it stops.

Be especially cautious in lower works. They combine:

- Low oxygen
- Accumulated gases
- Sudden flooding
- Submerged electrical systems
- Corroded catwalks
- Large unseen fauna
- Poor acoustic localization

Never enter a flooded turbine vault or submerged tram tunnel without expedition-grade diving support. Apparent water depth is unreliable.

## Weather and exposure

The light is cold but intense. Expect ultraviolet exposure, snow-blindness-like glare, and dehydration without feeling warm. Wear sunscreen and eye protection.

Rain is localized and may ignore walls. If it begins inside a structure:

- Do not assume the roof leaks.
- Watch where drops travel after landing.
- Move away if water climbs surfaces, gathers around disconnected machinery, or flows against multiple gradients.
- Do not shelter in elevator pits, sumps, trenches, or beneath hanging reservoirs.

Standing water should be treated as electrically live, chemically contaminated, and spatially deeper than it appears. Do not drink it. Do not reach into it after a dropped item. A shallow pool may reflect a nearby tower correctly while containing no corresponding bottom.

Condensation is more predictable than rain but creates severe slip hazards. Frost can retreat with little audible warning, releasing sheets of ice or previously frozen mechanisms.

## Sound discipline

The plateau’s quietness changes how danger is perceived. You may feel machinery through your teeth before hearing anything. Stop periodically and pay attention to vibration through boots, rails, and walls.

Do not shout casually. In echoing valleys, speech may return hours later from another direction and be mistaken for a person in distress. Agree on simple hand signals and use radio sparingly.

If you hear your own party speaking words nobody has yet said, withdraw without answering the sound.

A tool-drop is not a useful test of depth or distance here. The echo may precede the impact.

## Animals and plants

Do not feed, capture, dissect, or obstruct local fauna. Many animals appear to be functional components of the plateau.

In particular:

- Do not remove moths from control panels.
- Do not take washers from birds or their nests.
- Do not block migrating animals from entering factories.
- Do not touch eels near electrodes.
- Give pipeline foxes the painted stripe.
- Avoid ventilation louvers occupied by swifts.
- If animals abruptly leave an area, follow their direction only as far as the nearest established refuge. Do not attempt to imitate their anomalous crossings.

Animals can traverse discontinuities that humans cannot. A fox stepping between disconnected gantries does not demonstrate a safe path.

Treat all plant life as potentially engineered, electrically conductive, or integrated into fluid systems. Do not cut translucent-barked trees. Their roots may be sealing cracks or drawing pressure from inside a wall. Black moss may conceal stamped numbers, seams, or corrosion.

Bring all food with you. Local meat, reeds, roots, and water require extensive compatibility testing before human consumption. Do not assume “warm-bodied” means chemically familiar.

## Settlements and etiquette

The settlements are likely the safest parts of the plateau, but they do not look separate from the machinery. Ask before entering any chamber, touching any panel, or crossing a painted circle.

Useful etiquette includes:

- Do not call reused objects “scrap.”
- Do not praise the inhabitants for “making something from nothing.”
- Ask what an object was, what it is now, and whether it is still connected.
- Never open a public-square hatch, even if it appears unsecured.
- Offer repair materials, sealed food, medical supplies, or information rather than freshly fabricated curiosities unless advised otherwise.
- Do not remove stamped plates, signal pennants, obsolete processors, animal-deposited washers, or rust patterns as souvenirs.
- Ask permission before photographing homes. A former instrument bay may still transmit information.
- Accept that a local route description may use condensation, animal migration, shadow angle, or machinery phase rather than distance.

Hire a guide locally and pay for waiting time. Safe travel here may require standing still for hours while a shadow, bridge, or rain corridor reaches the correct condition.

## The new regions

Do not enter them merely because they look clean. Freshness is one of their warning signs.

At their boundary, watch for:

- Dust ending in a straight line
- Factory-blue bolts
- Clear water without recent rain
- Mismatched stair levels
- Adjacent windows showing different weather
- Extra doors or walls
- Rooms becoming wetter with repetition
- Numbers changing when spoken
- Shadows continuing after their sources leave
- Reflections showing impossible proximity

Yellow triangles indicate instability, but the markers migrate. Regard them as evidence that instability exists nearby, not as precise boundaries.

Never count stamped numbers aloud. Record them silently or photograph them with a physical reference card. Do not enter a warehouse box, regardless of apparent dimensions. Do not cross a bridge whose two ends disagree on date, weather, or wear.

If a room repeats, stop at the first recognized recurrence. Do not continue searching for the “real” exit. Mark the floor without speaking, turn around, and follow your own marks only if they remain consistent in shape, age, and dampness.

If two neighboring windows show different weather, do not open either.

## Reflections

Carry a nonreflective covering and minimize unnecessary mirrored equipment.

Never navigate solely by a reflection, even when it makes a distant object appear near. Do not touch a tower seen within arm’s reach in a puddle. Do not step into water to investigate reflected doors, roads, people, or sunlight.

On mornings when every reflective surface shows the same unfamiliar animal behind you:

1. Do not turn suddenly.
2. Do not address it.
3. Cover or turn away from reflective surfaces.
4. Remain with your group.
5. Return to an inhabited interior by an established route.
6. Let a local determine when ordinary reflections have resumed.

If one group member sees something different from the others, treat that person as exposed, not dishonest. Escort them calmly. Do not ask them to repeatedly describe or count what they see.

## Navigation protocol

Use redundant landmarks:

- Physical route tags
- Plate numbers recorded silently
- Machinery phase
- Lamp sequence
- Local animal signs
- Measured distance
- Time elapsed

No single landmark is sufficient. Long repeated arches can create false confidence, and apparent distance is conditional.

Photograph every junction facing both directions. Include a party member or measurement rod for scale. Before entering a corridor, agree on an abort condition such as “three repeated rooms,” “two clock disagreements,” or “unexpected rain indoors.”

Do not use a safety rope through an anomalous doorway. It can create a false sense of connection, snag on machinery, or link spaces with incompatible lengths. Use ropes only where the geometry has been confirmed ordinary.

## If something goes wrong

### If separated

Stop moving. Do not follow voices unless they give the current challenge-response phrase and are visible in the same uninterrupted space. Use a prearranged light signal. Do not chase a companion seen only through glass, water, or polished metal.

### If clocks disagree

Return immediately to the last inhabited or demonstrably stable location. Record each clock without resetting it. Avoid bridges, repeating rooms, and fresh districts.

### If the route changes

Do not force a shortcut. Wait. The plateau’s failures are slow, and a route may realign. Locals may know the relevant cycle.

### If a structure begins to rise or tilt

Move perpendicular to seams toward a broad, locally occupied structural member. Stay away from plate edges, hanging loads, reservoirs, and rigid bridges between moving districts. Expect secondary water movement.

### If exposed to an unknown vent or liquid

Move crosswise out of the plume or flow, remove contaminated outer layers, rinse only with carried water, and isolate affected clothing. Do not use plateau gutters or showers unless locals certify them.

### If a companion is duplicated

Do not decide identity through personal questions alone; a duplication may share memories. Keep both individuals separated from machinery and reflective surfaces, remain nonviolent, and seek local assistance. Do not allow either to cross the return threshold until the threshold operator understands the situation.

## Best first itinerary

For a first visit, remain in an old inhabited band. Spend one night at most, in a chamber selected by residents. Visit a public concourse, a stable reservoir edge, and a vantage point overlooking the machine-mountains. Avoid lower works, active factories, migration routes, fresh districts, and any summit mirror installation.

The plateau rewards patience more than exploration. Sit somewhere permitted and watch. A crane may move by the width of a hand. A lamp may join a constellation. A fox may cross a pipeline that has no visible continuation. These are safer ways to understand the place than opening a sealed door.

Above all, remember that the plateau is not abandoned simply because no operator is visible. It may be maintained by animals, inhabitants, weather, buried creatures, or processes too slow for a human visit to recognize. Behave as though every surface is occupied, every mechanism is active, and every impossible feature has rules you do not yet know.
