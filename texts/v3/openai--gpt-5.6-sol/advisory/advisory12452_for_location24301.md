## General assessment

Treat the plateau as a high-altitude polar desert combined with a poorly bounded archaeological site and a region of degraded physical law. It may look calm, but calm is not the same as safe. The chief dangers are:

- hypoxia, cold, dehydration, and exposure;
- becoming lost despite remaining in sight of camp;
- entering a building whose interior does not share the exterior’s space, weather, or time;
- releasing stored frost, heat, silence, or other accumulated conditions;
- being carried by remembered gravity or water flow;
- accidental contact with unfamiliar plant chemistry;
- attracting—or becoming incorporated into—the city’s maintenance processes.

This is not a good destination for solo tourism. A first visit should be a short, conservative survey made by a small team with a prearranged extraction method that does not depend solely on geographic coordinates.

## Before crossing

### Acclimatize for altitude

The plateau is described as being at immense elevation. Assume severe hypobaric hypoxia until instruments prove otherwise.

Spend several days acclimatizing in your home reality, or arrive with:

- supplemental oxygen;
- a pulse oximeter;
- pressure and oxygen sensors;
- medication prescribed for altitude illness;
- a portable pressure shelter if available.

Headache, confusion, euphoria, poor judgment, and visual distortion must initially be treated as medical symptoms—not as evidence that local space has changed. Unfortunately, either may be true.

Establish a strict turnaround rule. Any traveler who becomes confused, unusually calm, clumsy, or obsessed with a distant figure returns to the extraction point immediately.

### Establish identity and continuity records

Because people, relief carvings, shadows, trees, and maintenance crews may be interchangeable phases here, each traveler should carry redundant evidence of their own continuity:

- a written personal history;
- photographs from several ages;
- fingerprints and a DNA sample;
- a voice recording;
- a sealed card naming companions and mission purpose;
- a distinctive object carried against the skin;
- a mechanical counter advanced at agreed intervals.

Before departure, record a short identity statement. Repeat it morning and evening on the plateau. Partners should verify one another rather than relying only on electronic authentication.

This will not necessarily prevent transformation or substitution. It may help the team notice one.

### Plan extraction by conditions, not coordinates

A return portal keyed only to latitude and longitude may reopen onto a wall, a vanished courtyard, or the correct place under the wrong weather. Define the extraction point by a bundle of conditions, such as:

> On bare red slate, east of the marked quartz band, while dry, after local sunrise, with no frost on the marker stones.

Use a large artificial platform brought from outside the world. Make it visually and materially unlike the plateau: colored composite panels, synthetic rope, numbered stakes in weighted bases. Do not cut it from local stone.

Set several extraction windows under different permitted conditions. If your transit technology can anchor to a person or carried object rather than terrain, do so.

## Essential equipment

Bring equipment for both an alpine expedition and a reality-instability survey.

### Clothing and shelter

- layered cold-weather clothing;
- waterproof but breathable outer layers;
- insulated gloves that preserve dexterity;
- glacier-grade eye protection and sunscreen;
- boots with nonmetallic traction;
- gaiters to keep frost powder and seeds out;
- a freestanding, brightly colored tent;
- an insulated ground platform;
- sleeping systems rated well below expected temperatures;
- chemical heat packs manufactured off-world.

Avoid relying on local buildings for shelter. A tiny house may contain miles of cold hall, and a substantial one may have no interior at all.

### Air and water

Bring all drinking water for the planned visit plus a large reserve. Local water may be chemically clean yet temporally or spatially unsafe. A bowl of fresh water could have been set out by an inhabitant, drawn from future rain, or connected to a cistern that later returns its contents to the sky.

Bring:

- oxygen and masks;
- full-face respirators for windborne plant dust;
- sealed water containers;
- conventional filters and chemical purification for true emergencies;
- test kits for pH, salinity, metals, and organic toxins.

Filtration cannot correct displaced time or remembered origin. Do not drink local water merely because it passes chemical tests.

### Navigation and recording

Carry redundant analog and digital instruments:

- several mechanical watches;
- digital clocks using independent power sources;
- compasses, while expecting them to be of limited use;
- barometers and thermometers;
- inertial movement sensors;
- surveyor’s tape;
- chalk, ceramic markers, and synthetic flags;
- cameras facing both forward and backward;
- notebooks printed with waterproof grids;
- paired audio recorders;
- a weather log.

Record sequences, not just positions. A useful field note would read:

> Departed camp after frost melted. Crossed three dry channels. Passed the black-leaf garden while silver sides faced upward. Entered the arcade before condensation began.

A useless note would be:

> Building 2.4 km northeast of camp.

Mark each route with the environmental state under which it was traversed. Do not assume the return route exists under changed conditions.

### Safety tools

- climbing harnesses and synthetic line;
- blunt ceramic probes;
- noisemakers and vibration alarms;
- portable oxygenated casualty bag;
- closed sample containers;
- broad snowshoe-like boards for questionable surfaces;
- colored test pellets to check local fall direction;
- a lightweight stretcher;
- an independent emergency transit beacon.

Metal should be minimized around unknown mechanisms and old bronze fittings. Never remove local metal artifacts; scarcity may make them culturally important.

## Camp placement

For the first expedition, camp outside the visibly dense city on a broad, exposed mineral surface.

Choose a site:

- far from cracks, wells, cisterns, channels, gardens, walls, and buried alignments;
- outside any shallow basin where cold or displaced rain could collect;
- not beneath condensation structures;
- with a clear horizon and multiple approach routes;
- on stone that shows no moss, roots, relief figures, doors, or unusual discoloration.

Before setting up, observe the site through a full temperature cycle if possible. Test whether loose objects fall toward the ground, toward a wall, or toward an older exposed surface. Place colored pellets around camp and check them regularly.

Use weighted bases rather than driving stakes into cracks. A crack may be deep enough to have its own climate, vegetation, and altered gravity.

Keep camp physically simple. Do not build drains that resemble local channels, arrange equipment into long intentional rows, or leave water bowls outside. The city may interpret such patterns as infrastructure requiring connection or maintenance.

## Movement rules

### Travel in teams, but do not rely on line of sight

Use groups of three. Two people can mutually confirm events while the third monitors instruments and the route log.

Stay close enough to touch under normal conditions. A person who remains visually nearby may already be separated by an expanding plaza. Use short physical lines cautiously, with quick-release attachments. If the rope becomes impossibly long, passes through masonry, pulls toward a wall, or develops frost of a different time of day, release it rather than following.

Never pursue a distant figure. Pursuit appears to increase intervening distance. Stop, announce yourself once, and wait. If the figure later appears as a discoloration or relief nearby, do not touch it.

### Test gravity continuously

Before stepping onto:

- old walls;
- steeply worn pavements;
- interrupted channels;
- stairs;
- thresholds;
- roofs;
- quartz bands,

release a colored pellet and watch where it goes. Water may follow remembered routes rather than present slopes, so pouring water is not a reliable gravity test.

Keep loose equipment clipped to your clothing. A dropped tool might “fall” to an ancient wall and remain there.

### Treat thresholds as expedition boundaries

Every doorway requires a separate assessment. Observe it from several angles and at different distances. Compare the exterior depth of the building with what is visible inside.

Do not enter if:

- weather is visible within;
- the far wall cannot be ranged;
- sound takes too long to return;
- the doorway shows the rear exterior wall;
- frost, moss, or rain crosses the threshold in only one direction;
- team members disagree about what lies beyond.

If entry is necessary, keep one person outside and do not depend exclusively on a tether. Lay a synthetic threshold panel across the doorway and record the exact weather, light, and frost state at entry.

### Do not walk active water routes

When rain begins, leave streets, drainage grooves, courtyards, aqueduct beds, and low plazas immediately. Water may activate gates, change road direction, restore remembered channels, or cause routes to exist that did not exist while dry.

Do not shelter beneath an aqueduct. Do not stand between a cistern and the sky. If a covered reservoir is being tended, stay well away.

A narrow rain curtain with an exact boundary should be treated as a physical border. Do not step across merely to test it. Mark its movement and wait.

### Use observation protocols

Some routes may function only when unwatched. This creates an obvious hazard: looking away can change the path, but continuous observation can prevent passage.

Never experiment with such routes casually. If it becomes necessary:

1. appoint one traveler as the mover;
2. appoint one as a condition recorder;
3. establish a retreat signal;
4. have all observers look away for a precisely timed interval;
5. let the mover cross without turning;
6. resume observation only at the agreed signal.

Do not attempt this near cliffs, wells, doors, or figures. Cameras may count as observers; assume they do until demonstrated otherwise.

## Water, food, and plants

Bring all food. There is no animal life to provide warning that a plant is poisonous, and no familiar ecology from which edibility can be inferred.

Do not eat:

- fruits resembling stones;
- mirrored blossoms or shoots;
- black-leaved succulents;
- moss from missing structures;
- anything offered in an interior-handled cup.

Wear gloves around vegetation. Wind-released dust should be treated as an inhalation and reproductive hazard. Mirrored cavities suggest forms of replication that may not respect ordinary distinctions between image, offspring, and observer.

Avoid stepping on moss even where it appears to cover solid ground. It may indicate an absent stair or surface that “ought to exist.” Probe beside it, not through it.

Do not cut vines that vanish into walls. They may connect distant courtyards and could function as water lines, sensory organs, or communication paths.

Sampling should be minimal and performed only on later expeditions. Seal each sample in two transparent containers and store it outside sleeping quarters. Never bring a sample directly back into a populated home reality without long quarantine.

## Conduct in the city

Assume the city is inhabited, even when nobody is visible. It is demonstrably maintained.

Behave as you would in a quiet settlement where the residents have not yet decided whether to meet you:

- do not force doors;
- do not climb roofs or condensation fins;
- do not remove tools, stones, jars, seeds, or metal;
- do not block drains;
- do not sit at communal tables without invitation;
- do not sleep in moss beds;
- do not open cisterns;
- do not rearrange marked vessels;
- do not imitate maintenance work unless directed.

Leave a simple gift at the edge of a plaza rather than inside a house. Suitable offerings include clean manufactured cloth, a sealed ceramic blade, or a small quantity of pure water in a clearly artificial bottle. Avoid seeds, animal products, living microbes, fire, or complex machines.

A greeting should be quiet and repeatable. Speak once, then wait. The absence of immediate response means nothing in a place where a worker and its completed repair may be separated by centuries.

If bowls of fresh water appear, acknowledge them but do not drink. They may be hospitality, a measurement, a lure, or part of a process unrelated to you.

## Temporal hazards

Never open old jars, sealed rooms, covered wells, or closed cisterns. Sheltered places accumulate unused weather and time. A vessel may release:

- severe frost;
- heat;
- darkness;
- silence;
- a particular morning;
- an interval in which some companions have not yet arrived.

Treat black spirals, white notches, dates, and temperature marks as hazard labels, even if their meaning is uncertain.

Do not light abandoned hearths. Do not disturb ashes. A hearth that contains stored summer heat may be useful to local inhabitants and dangerous to a human in insulated clothing.

If a sudden local season is released:

1. do not flee blindly;
2. establish whether it is expanding;
3. use oxygen and thermal protection;
4. keep all team members in physical contact;
5. retreat along the recorded sequence of conditions;
6. do not attempt to reseal the source unless you understand its closure.

Carry multiple clocks, but do not privilege one automatically. Compare clocks with bodily signs, shadow length, frost state, and companion logs. If one person experiences missing or repeated time, quarantine them socially but not cruelly: keep them warm, accompanied, and under observation until extraction.

## Encounters with figures

Possible inhabitants may appear as people, shadows, reliefs, trees, maintenance crews, garments without bodies, or changes in the city itself.

Do not assume a human shape indicates a human mind. Equally, do not assume a wall carving is inanimate.

Safe encounter procedure:

- stop at a respectful distance;
- keep hands visible;
- place tools on the ground;
- state your name and that you are a visitor;
- do not close distance unless invited;
- do not mirror gestures involving walls, roots, water, or lying down;
- do not accept a vessel you have seen opened;
- do not follow a figure through a doorway or across a wet road.

If a companion appears as a relief or shadow, do not damage the surface in an attempt to free them. Record changes in light, temperature, and moisture. Their state may be temporary or linked to the age of the surface. Extraction from the entire region is preferable to excavation.

If workers are visible at a distance, do not approach the work site. Observe from where you first saw them. Arrival may convert an active event into an ancient repair—and may place you on the same timescale as the workers.

## Weather strategy

Ferns folding, moss shifting, or shrubs turning silver-side-up may provide better warning of localized rain than the sky. Learn these signs from a distance before depending on them.

At night:

- secure all water;
- cover every open container;
- keep frost gear immediately available;
- post a paired watch;
- check whether the stars agree across the camp;
- do not follow lights, smoke, opening doors, or the sound of a settling slab.

If star patterns differ between two nearby positions, do not move camp after dark. Mark the boundary and wait for daylight.

Morning frost should be allowed to melt naturally unless it threatens life. Heating a route could alter the conditions by which it exists.

## Emergency rules

Immediately abort the expedition if:

- camp cannot be reached by reversing its recorded sequence;
- two team members see incompatible weather in the same direction;
- an artificial object begins aging into local material;
- a traveler develops bark, relief-like flattening, persistent shadow displacement, or roots;
- the extraction marker appears as an ancient city feature;
- rainfall rises from an uncovered container;
- a road changes direction while occupied;
- anyone hears speech from inside stone or from their own earlier recording;
- the team encounters its own apparently older camp.

If lost, do not keep walking toward camp by sight. Stop on bare stone, establish shelter, and reconstruct the last valid condition sequence. Distance is unreliable; fatigue and hypoxia are not.

If separated, each traveler should remain where they first confirm separation unless exposed to immediate danger. Searching is likely to stretch space or lead the searcher into an inappropriate route state.

## Recommended first visit

Keep the first visit to one local daylight period if possible.

Its objectives should be modest:

1. verify air pressure and composition;
2. establish a stable extraction platform;
3. observe one dry-to-frost cycle;
4. approach only the outer buried alignments;
5. record plant responses and water behavior from a distance;
6. leave a nonliving gift;
7. depart before entering any building.

The plateau rewards patience rather than penetration. Its dangers are not usually sudden or theatrical. They arise when a traveler assumes that because a road is broad, a day is bright, and a wall is still, those things are ordinary.

The most important preparation is therefore a change in habit: do not navigate by where things are. Navigate by what has happened, what the weather is doing, what the stone remembers, and whether the city appears willing to let you pass.
