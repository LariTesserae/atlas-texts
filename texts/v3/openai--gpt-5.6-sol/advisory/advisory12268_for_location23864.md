## Preparing to Visit the Highwater Conservatory

Treat the Conservatory neither as a garden nor as a dive site. It is a sealed, high-pressure biological machine whose garden happens to be visible from inside.

An ordinary human cannot safely enter it with ordinary diving equipment. Several hundred meters of water above the roof means pressures of dozens of atmospheres. Breathing gas at ambient pressure would involve extreme decompression obligations, oxygen-toxicity limits, nitrogen narcosis or helium-related complications, and the possibility of fatal pressure injury. More importantly, an exposed human would violate nearly every rule of the ecology: you would be an animal that swims, sheds microbes, releases waste, consumes oxygen, and carries unanchored living matter.

The safest visit is made inside a rigid, sterile, closed-life-support shell.

### Before departure

Confirm four things that the description does not guarantee:

1. **There is a functioning visitor or maintenance lock.**  
   Do not assume the transparent roof, floor windows, or service panels can serve as entrances. Approach through the tower’s original docking infrastructure, preferably a lit service chamber below the crown.

2. **The governing system recognizes visitors.**  
   The Conservatory may be unattended without being unguarded. Its filtration and recycling systems could classify skin cells, breath condensate, clothing fibers, lubricants, or an entire traveler as escaped biomass or foreign contamination. Obtain a machine-readable authorization token compatible with the tower, plus a passive visual or electromagnetic identifier in case the main network is isolated.

3. **The structure is still pressure-certified.**  
   Its surfaces look new, but seals age invisibly. Have a remote vehicle inspect the docking collar, transparent roof ribs, outer wall, ballast mechanisms, and the vertical tower before bringing a person near it.

4. **Your arrival does not coincide with a maintenance transition.**  
   Avoid ballast shifts, harvest operations, programmed pollen pulses, pressure equalization, and especially the six-hour blackout that occurs every thirty-nine days. Arrive well before or after artificial night, not shortly before it.

If the local calendar cannot be read, observe remotely for at least several days. The slow ecology encourages impatience: apparently nothing is happening until shutters begin closing, valves open, or the entire pressure regime changes.

### Medical preparation

Even inside a rigid shell, undergo screening for:

- claustrophobia and panic in total darkness;
- motion sickness in slow, nearly imperceptible movement;
- sensitivity to prolonged confinement;
- cardiovascular or respiratory conditions that would become dangerous if the shell partially equalized;
- inability to remain calm in an environment with no audible or visible animal presence.

Do not plan an ambient-pressure excursion unless you possess civilization-grade saturation-diving support and have explicit permission from the Conservatory. For an ordinary traveler, this is effectively prohibited.

Your suit or capsule should remain at approximately normal human atmospheric pressure. It must withstand the full external pressure without depending on the Conservatory for structural support.

### The personal shell

The ideal vehicle is a narrow articulated pressure pod: smaller than a conventional submersible, shaped to pass through industrial maintenance locks, and able to brace itself without damaging the surroundings.

It should have:

- a rigid pressure hull with generous safety margin;
- fully closed oxygen, carbon dioxide, humidity, and waste handling;
- no discharge valves of any kind while inside;
- doubled seals and isolated sample traps;
- external surfaces sterilized and certified not to shed;
- non-toxic, water-compatible hydraulic fluids—or, preferably, electromechanical joints;
- low-temperature lamps with adjustable intensity;
- sonar or structured-light mapping for tight passages;
- tactile proximity sensors that stop motion before contact;
- a retractable tether;
- several soft, load-spreading anchor pads;
- manual controls that function without tower communications;
- at least twice the life-support endurance expected for the visit.

Avoid exposed propellers. They could cut leaves, generate prohibited currents, draw pollen into machinery, or throw loose plant matter into collectors. Movement should come from winching along established service rails, articulated legs placed on marked structural members, or extremely gentle enclosed thrusters used only in open docking volumes.

The shell must not resemble a swimming organism if the Conservatory’s rules are enforced literally. Register it as maintenance equipment and remain mechanically anchored whenever possible. You should move as a piece of visiting machinery, not as a diver.

### Sterility and containment

Assume the exterior ocean and the interior conservatory are mutually quarantined—and that you are contaminated with material from both.

Before docking:

- remove soil, seeds, spores, pollen, hair, lint, food residue, and untreated organic materials;
- sterilize the shell exterior without leaving reactive biocides behind;
- use smooth, sealed clothing inside the capsule;
- place all food and personal waste in redundant containment;
- avoid wool, leather, paper, wood, cork, and natural-fiber cordage;
- seal all tools individually;
- inventory every item before entry and again after departure.

Do not bring a potted plant as a gesture of goodwill. Do not release commemorative objects, markers, ashes, dyes, tracing chemicals, microbial probes, or sample tags. Nothing produced inside may enter the outer ocean, and it is prudent to apply the reverse rule as well.

Do not take samples unless the governing system explicitly directs you to a sampling port. A clipped leaf is not merely a botanical specimen; it is nutrient mass budgeted into a century-long closed cycle.

### Clothing and personal supplies

Inside the pressure shell, wear:

- a close-fitting pressure-undergarment or emergency suit;
- thermal regulation layers—the water is warm, but the capsule may become hot;
- a communications hood or bone-conduction headset;
- eye protection against sudden lamp transitions;
- a compact breathing reserve independent of the capsule;
- a biometric monitor;
- soft restraints for the blackout period or unexpected vehicle movement.

Bring drinking water and compact food, but avoid eating while traversing the densest areas. A spilled drink remains contained within your cabin, yet any internal emergency that forces venting would become an ecological incident.

Carry no strong fragrances. The flowers have no scent, and while fragrance may not pass through a sound hull, its presence is a reminder that your shell is not actually sealed if you can smell the Conservatory—or it can smell you.

### Approaching the tower

The vertical approach may be more disturbing than the chamber itself.

The tower rises from darkness with no natural slope beneath it. Your depth instruments may indicate that the seabed is kilometers below while sunlight still filters down from overhead. Avoid looking continuously downward if you are prone to vertigo. In open water, scale becomes difficult to judge; the tower can seem like a cable rather than an industrial structure until platforms appear beside you.

Stay clear of:

- taut vertical cables;
- ballast vents;
- thermal outlets;
- dark apertures that may be intake grilles;
- moving service arms;
- illuminated chambers whose purpose is unknown.

Do not follow the tower downward out of curiosity. The lit rectangles below are not navigational promises. They may be pressure stations, inaccessible process vessels, or isolated systems operating under very different conditions.

Dock only at a confirmed mechanical interface. Make a soft capture, allow pressure and contamination checks to finish, and wait for explicit admission. Silence does not mean permission.

### Entering the Conservatory

Expect no dry vestibule. A proper lock may fill with water around the pressure pod while separately filtering every exchanged cupful. The process could be very slow. Do not override it.

Once admitted, pause before moving.

The chamber will initially seem smaller than it is because foliage occupies nearly every line of sight. Conversely, its steep machinery-folded floor may create the illusion of a landscape. Remember:

- a “ridge” may be a fragile heat exchanger;
- a “ravine” may contain critical pipes;
- an apparent clearing may be a collector intake;
- an apparent trunk may be a coolant line wrapped in engineered cellulose;
- a convenient handhold may be a sensor, flower support, or conductive pollen wire.

Use a precomputed route. If no route is available, remain near the lock. Exploration by squeezing through vegetation is unacceptable: the chamber’s narrow passages were designed for fluid, roots, and machines, not for human-shaped pressure vehicles.

### Conduct inside

The governing ethic is simple: **observe without becoming part of the cycle.**

Remain anchored or rail-attached. Move slowly enough that nearby leaves barely respond. If your passage produces a visible wake, you are going too fast.

Do not:

- touch the transparent roof;
- brush aside leaves for a better view;
- use strong sonar near delicate membranes;
- shine high-intensity lights into flowers or photoreceptors;
- block lamps, circulation slots, or pollen paths;
- rest on root cages;
- enter maintenance trenches;
- approach black intake grilles;
- turn valve wheels, however invitingly colored;
- cross conductive-wire curtains;
- attempt to “rescue” leaves being drawn into reduction intakes.

Plant material entering a black grille is not necessarily an accident. Mechanized decay is one of the Conservatory’s essential functions.

If the hinged leaves begin closing, stop where you are. The sequence may be botanical, diagnostic, or both. Do not race the green wave toward an exit; sudden movement is more dangerous than being gently enclosed by foliage. Check that your return route remains clear, then retreat only at normal maintenance speed.

If a programmed pollen current begins, remain stationary. Gold dust may surround the shell and make the water appear turbid for a time. Do not activate thrusters or filtration jets to clear your view. Let the Conservatory move its own pollen.

### Sound and bodily sensation

Inside your shell, you may hear more than an unprotected visitor would. The hull will conduct low-frequency pump cycles, pipe contractions, and occasional panel ticks. Some will be felt through the seat or teeth rather than heard.

Do not interpret every sound as structural failure. Establish baseline recordings during the docking period. Alarms should be based on pressure, strain, seal status, and acoustic comparison—not on intuition alone.

The absence of animal noise can be psychologically forceful. Humans expect underwater plants to conceal movement. Here, nothing flees when your light passes. No eye resolves from the leaves. After a while you may begin inventing movement at the edge of vision. Trust instruments over peripheral impressions.

The chamber’s cleanliness can also be misleading. Clear water at crushing pressure is still crushing pressure. A window that looks close enough to touch may separate your ordinary atmosphere from an instantly lethal environment.

### Artificial night

Do not intentionally remain for the thirty-nine-day blackout on a first visit.

If caught inside:

1. Stop moving immediately.
2. Secure to a certified structural rail.
3. Turn off exterior lights unless emergency procedures specifically permit them.
4. Switch the cabin to dim red internal lighting.
5. Confirm hull pressure and tether status.
6. Do not attempt to navigate by touch.
7. Wait through the full equalization cycle and for formal system clearance afterward.

The darkness will not feel empty. It will feel crowded: foliage, cables, housings, flowers, and pipes close around the capsule, all present but invisible. Prepare for the possibility that six hours will feel much longer.

Do not illuminate the plants merely for comfort. Their reorientation may depend on total darkness, and a personal lamp could create a false local dawn.

### Emergencies

Your first response to almost every emergency should be containment, not escape.

If the shell leaks, seal the affected compartment and return to the lock. Do not vent cabin contents into the chamber.

If immobilized by foliage, do not cut yourself free unless loss of life is imminent. Request mechanical assistance or slowly reverse along your route. Cutting one braided “trunk” could sever a coolant line.

If the tether snags, release it only if it can retract into a sealed housing. Never abandon loose cable.

If power fails, the shell should become mechanically stable rather than buoyantly rising into the roof or sinking into machinery. Neutral buoyancy alone is insufficient in a densely packed chamber; use passive rail clamps.

If the Conservatory begins rejecting your presence—closing partitions, increasing local filtration, activating warning lights, or directing you toward a lock—comply immediately. Do not assume you can argue with a system calibrated to think in decades.

A catastrophic hull breach at ambient pressure is not survivable through ordinary emergency ascent. There is no useful “swim to the surface” option. Your survival depends on hull integrity, redundant compartments, and access to a rescue lock.

### How long to stay

For a first visit, plan no more than four to six hours inside the chamber, excluding docking and sterilization. The Conservatory rewards stillness, but human attention degrades in warm, quiet, visually dense environments. Fatigue may express itself as curiosity: reaching, detouring, or wanting to inspect one more lit recess.

A longer scientific stay should use a docked habitation capsule, not repeated ambient excursions.

The most revealing observations are likely to be slow:

- the almost imperceptible turn of a leaf toward a lamp;
- a root bubble traveling to the roof;
- the low pulse of a pump through the hull;
- pollen crossing between geometric flowers;
- daylight shifting into lattices over white ceramic and new steel;
- distant illuminated tower chambers visible through a floor window.

Do not come expecting spectacle. The place’s scale is temporal rather than spatial.

### Departure and quarantine

Before leaving, account for every tool and exterior attachment. Use cameras to inspect the shell for:

- entangled fronds;
- pollen deposits;
- root fibers;
- mineral fans;
- material caught in hinges or anchor pads.

Do not simply wash these into the outer sea. The departure lock should collect and return all Conservatory material to its processing cycle.

After undocking, quarantine the vehicle until both external-ocean contaminants and Conservatory-derived matter have been separately contained. Keep biological samples—if any were formally issued—in pressure-stable, triple-sealed storage. Never open them in an ordinary biosphere.

Finally, preserve your recordings with exact timestamps. A moment that seemed uneventful may correspond to a cycle that occurs only once in weeks, years, or a century.

The proper attitude is not that of a diver entering a beautiful reef. It is that of a temporary component being admitted into an immaculate machine. Go enclosed, sterile, patient, and mechanically humble. Leave without having fed it, damaged it, fertilized it, frightened it, or taught it that animals can swim.
