## Before you go

Treat this as a wet industrial biosphere, not merely a rainy city. Its pipes, factories, reservoirs, animals, and microbial films are parts of one ecological system. Actions that seem hygienic or helpful elsewhere—drying a wall, clearing a gutter, killing vermin, closing a leaking valve—may be destructive, illegal, or immediately dangerous here.

Arrange your arrival through a recognized transit station in a central or inner-reservoir district. Do not plan to approach overland via the escarpment. The plateau’s edges are remote, cloudbound, cut by immense waterfalls, and poorly connected to ordinary streets. An unlicensed aerial arrival is also unwise: roof fliers, condensation cables, low cloud, industrial exhaust, and slow freight rigging make the airspace hazardous.

Before crossing universes, obtain:

- Confirmation that the atmosphere is human-breathable, including tolerable volcanic gases and industrial aerosols.
- Local medical and biochemical screening.
- A current migration and water-level calendar.
- Accommodation above routine flood level but below major roof-colony zones.
- A licensed local guide for tunnels, reservoirs, or industrial wards.
- Biosecurity clearance for everything organic you carry.

Do not bring living plants, seeds, untreated timber, soil, compost, or plant-based souvenirs unless explicitly permitted. A visible green organism would not be an innocuous curiosity here. It could be an ecological contaminant, a culturally alarming object, or both.

## What to wear

Expect warm air, constant damp, cool wet surfaces, and little opportunity for anything to dry naturally.

Bring:

- A lightweight waterproof outer layer with excellent ventilation.
- Quick-drying underlayers rather than absorbent cotton.
- Waterproof boots with soft, high-grip soles and protected ankles.
- Gaiters for shallow flooded streets.
- Several pairs of moisture-managing socks.
- Thin work gloves for railings and corroded metal.
- A close-fitting cap or hood for condensation, moth falls, and dripping galleries.
- Clear eye protection.
- A compact respirator rated for particulates and nuisance industrial aerosols.
- Hearing protection that reduces continuous bass noise without blocking bells, whistles, or spoken warnings.
- Antifungal skin powder and supplies for foot care.

Bright white clothing will quickly acquire mineral stains. Loose scarves, dangling straps, and long coats are poor choices around exposed machinery, cable drums, animal traffic, and crowded galleries.

The stone is likely to be most treacherous when it looks merely damp rather than flooded. Mineral-polished stairs, shell fragments, microbial films, and moth wings can all make footing unexpectedly slick. Use handrails cautiously: some carry condensation, heat, vibration, or stray electrical current.

## Equipment

Assume ordinary consumer electronics will fail sooner than expected. Moisture enters seams, mineral-rich water bridges contacts, and persistent vibration loosens fittings.

Useful equipment includes:

- A waterproof mechanical or ruggedized watch.
- Redundant navigation tools, including printed local diagrams.
- Sealed batteries and corrosion-resistant charging leads.
- A headlamp with a low, diffuse setting.
- A compact atmospheric monitor for oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and combustible gases.
- A noncontact thermometer for pipes, floors, and handholds.
- A walking pole for testing submerged steps and street depth.
- Waterproof document cases.
- Small sealed dry-bags rather than one large suitcase.
- A personal locator compatible with local systems.

Do not rely on a single map. The city is layered vertically, and a marked “street” may be a tunnel, roofed square, elevated road, or shallow canal depending on district and current water level. Obtain maps dated by reservoir state and migration season. Local directions may refer to pump cycles, bells, animal routes, or the age of a leak rather than compass points.

A compass may be unreliable around dense ironwork, power lines, and machinery. Satellite navigation may be unavailable or meaningless across universes.

## Health precautions

Never drink untreated local water simply because it looks clean. The city’s water moves through porous volcanic stone, old foundations, industrial systems, microbial films, and animal habitats. It may contain unfamiliar organisms, dissolved metals, volcanic chemicals, or biochemistry incompatible with human digestion.

For at least the first part of your stay:

- Drink sealed imported water or water certified for off-world humans.
- Eat only food cleared by a cross-species medical authority.
- Keep cuts covered with waterproof dressings.
- Wash and dry your feet carefully each day.
- Avoid putting your hands near your face after touching walls, rails, shells, or standing water.
- Seek treatment promptly for persistent rash, numbness, respiratory irritation, fever, or unusual wound discoloration.

Native animal flesh may be nutritious to residents and still be toxic, allergenic, or indigestible to you. “Rendered,” fermented, or thoroughly cooked does not guarantee biochemical compatibility. Bring certified shelf-stable food until local testing establishes what you can safely eat.

Industrial aerosols may vary by block. Permanent fog near steam leaks and condenser towers should not be assumed to be harmless water vapor. If residents put on masks, close shutters, or leave a gallery, imitate them first and ask questions later.

## Sound and sleep

The city’s noise is continuous and physically tiring. Much of it is low-frequency vibration conducted through masonry. You may feel it through your jaw, teeth, bedframe, or chest even when earplugs reduce the audible volume.

Bring filtered earplugs rather than total acoustic isolation. Pressure bells, machinery warnings, animal calls, and changes in pump rhythm may convey urgent information. Ask your host to teach you the principal local signals:

- Pressure-change bells.
- Flood or gate-opening warnings.
- Turbine alarms.
- Herd approach signals.
- Moth-emergence closures.
- Evacuation tones for gas or structural movement.

Choose lodging in a mid-level residential structure with vibration isolation. The lowest floors are more vulnerable to flooding, gas accumulation, and animal movement; the highest levels may be noisy with roof colonies, chimneys, cables, and condensation.

The absence of silence and greenery can be psychologically wearing. Bring familiar music, images of open landscapes, and a sleep routine. If you need a green object for emotional comfort, use a clearly artificial image or screen—not a live plant.

## How to behave around water and infrastructure

The most important rule is simple:

**Do not alter a flow.**

Do not turn valves, move boards from drains, clear shell masses from grates, close a dripping pipe, unplug a channel, or “repair” a leak. Even a small trickle may be legally protected, ecologically necessary, or hydraulically connected to a distant system.

Likewise:

- Do not throw soap, disinfectant, fuel, food, or medicine into street water.
- Do not drain a basin to retrieve a dropped object.
- Do not use a dehumidifier without permission.
- Do not aim heaters at wet masonry.
- Do not scrape opalescent films from stone.
- Do not enter an abandoned flooded structure because it appears calm.
- Never step onto a crusted surface without knowing whether it covers water or a void.

Dryness is treated as a civic danger. Complaining about damp is understandable; trying to eliminate it may not be. A hotel room may include an open condensation wall or a permanent floor channel. Ask how to keep your belongings dry without changing the room’s humidity.

Fire and strong heat are especially serious hazards. Open flames, high-output heaters, static-producing fabrics, and improvised electrical equipment may be restricted. In a dry spell, follow local precautions meticulously: the city becomes more brittle, electrically unstable, and ecologically stressed.

## Animal etiquette

The animals are not decorative wildlife. Many are protected, economically important, or functionally part of the infrastructure.

### Gutter crabs and wall grazers

Walk around dense aggregations when possible. Do not kick them aside or pour anything onto them. Runoff can cause an entire gutter mass to shift abruptly, so avoid standing with both feet in drainage channels.

Wall grazers may be almost invisible against wet stone. Before leaning on a wall, inspect it. Their shells may be sharp, and crushing them can attract scavengers.

### Cistern life

Do not cast sudden shadows over open cisterns, put your hands in the water, or tap on reservoir walls. Some animals close defensively; others strike from cracks. Barriers are there for ecological as well as personal protection.

### Chimney moths

During emergence, keep your mouth and eyes covered and avoid elevated rail lines. Their bodies and wings can make surfaces extremely slick. Do not use insecticide. Sweepers collect the dead as a resource, and live swarms may be critical food for roof colonies.

### Roof fliers

Never approach nests, stare closely at chicks, shine strong lights upward, or fly small drones near parapets. Metallic calls may be communication rather than aggression. If an entire row of birds turns toward you or unfolds its wings, withdraw under cover without running.

Carry no loose food on rooftops. Also avoid standing alone in exposed places during insect emergence, when large numbers may descend at once.

### Carrier herds

They have right of way. Give them far more space than seems necessary.

Do not:

- Walk between herd members.
- Stand between a herd and a flooded avenue.
- Touch domesticated animals without the handler’s permission.
- Try to redirect a wild herd.
- Use horns, flash photography, or sudden mechanical sounds near them.
- Shelter in a doorway directly ahead of an approaching herd.

If caught in a narrow street, follow local marshals. Move behind a substantial pier or into a designated recess, not merely onto a curb. Their apparent slowness should not be mistaken for controllability.

## Transport

The railway is slow by design. Allow generous time and never assume a stopped train is inactive. It may begin moving without the sounds familiar from faster systems, and its mineral barges can take a long distance to halt.

Use designated walkways even if flooded rails appear easier to follow. Rails may be energized, hidden under water, or used by cable-hauled freight.

Travel times are often measured by machinery cycles rather than minutes. A gate may take half a day to open, and a bridge route may close for an animal passage without an alternate nearby. Carry food, water, and a warm layer even on an urban day trip.

Avoid unauthorized lifts, freight drums, maintenance galleries, steam tunnels, and submerged lower streets. The city’s visible slowness conceals enormous forces. A gate moving a few centimeters a minute can still crush stone.

## Social expectations

Residents are likely to value patience, maintenance knowledge, and continuity. Do not treat old machinery as quaint, inefficient, or awaiting replacement. A furnace kept alight for generations or a slow-settling bridge may embody both civic history and ecological function.

Expect permits and public works to proceed slowly. Abruptness suggests failure here. Pressuring someone to drain, accelerate, dry, clear, or restart a system “immediately” may sound reckless.

Ask before photographing:

- Rendering houses.
- Reservoir interiors.
- Pressure-control stations.
- Animal nesting structures.
- Flooded former homes.
- Workers handling civic organisms.

Be careful with the word “vermin.” Creatures you perceive as infestations may be sanitation workers, protected migratory populations, or legally recognized maintainers of public infrastructure.

A useful visitor’s gesture is to notice the city’s flows without interfering with them. Learn the name of the trickle beside your lodging, the pump whose rhythm marks the night, and the season of the animals in your street.

## Emergency conduct

When something changes suddenly, assume it matters.

Stop and seek local instruction if:

- A familiar pump rhythm ceases.
- Several animal species leave an area at once.
- Roof fliers abandon parapets.
- Water changes color, temperature, smell, or direction.
- Pressure bells sound outside their expected schedule.
- A wall or floor becomes unexpectedly dry.
- Condensation vanishes from cables.
- Residents begin covering drains or opening channels.
- You smell hot mineral dust, ozone, rotten eggs, or sharply metallic vapor.

In a flood, do not automatically move downward toward broad streets; they may be intended as channels. Follow marked vertical evacuation routes.

In a dry event, avoid sparks, unnecessary electrical use, and friction-producing clothing. Do not attempt to compensate by pouring drinking water onto walls. The wrong water in the wrong conduit may worsen mineral damage or contaminate a living system.

If separated from your guide, remain at a staffed pump hall, station, public condensation chamber, or pressure-bell post. These are more dependable landmarks than street names.

## Best way to experience the city

Begin slowly. Spend your first days in a central, inhabited district where you can learn its rhythms. Watch a rain front arrive. Ride a surface railway in daylight. Visit a public reservoir from a maintained causeway. Attend a roof-colony viewing platform with a guide. Listen to the night pumps engage from somewhere structurally safe.

Do not rush immediately into the buried city or outer gullies. Those places combine poor navigation, unfamiliar animals, sudden depth changes, industrial gases, and few human witnesses.

The city rewards attention over movement. Its great spectacle is not a monument but coordination: water entering channels, animals responding, machinery changing cadence, and entire neighborhoods adjusting over hours. Prepare to be damp, delayed, overstimulated, and occasionally unnerved. Also prepare to discover that what first appears gray and barren is among the most densely alive places you have ever visited.
