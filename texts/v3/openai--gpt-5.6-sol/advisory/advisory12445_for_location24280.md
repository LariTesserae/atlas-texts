## Overall assessment

This is not a world to enter casually, alone, or with ordinary hiking equipment. Its darkness is permanent, its terrain is saturated and vertically complex, and its ecology is intertwined with infrastructure whose failures may resemble weather. A human visitor’s greatest danger will not necessarily be predators. It will be misunderstanding a sound, stepping into moving water, touching an active surface, or responding incorrectly when the normal vibration of the country changes.

Prepare as though visiting a rainforest, a cave system, a hydroelectric plant, and an inhabited organism simultaneously.

Your first rule should be:

**Do not attempt to make the place behave like a lit world. Learn to travel without illumination.**

---

## Before departure

### Confirm basic human compatibility

Do not infer habitability merely from the presence of forests and shelters. Obtain measurements for:

- atmospheric composition and pressure;
- oxygen partial pressure;
- toxic sulfur compounds and industrial aerosols;
- airborne spores, bacteria, and fungal fragments;
- temperature and humidity at several elevations;
- dissolved metals and microorganisms in water;
- gravity;
- local pathogens and skin-active enzymes;
- pressure changes near waterfalls, vents, and pumping stations.

A human can drown physiologically in warm, oxygen-poor air without recognizing the danger. Hydrogen sulfide may also suppress the sense of smell at dangerous concentrations. Carry redundant atmospheric monitors that communicate through vibration rather than light or alarms that could be lost in the ambient noise.

If prior survey data are incomplete, wear a positive-pressure environmental suit during the initial visit. It should be flexible, chemically resistant, thermally regulated, and designed for continuous immersion rather than vacuum. A conventional rigid spacesuit will be clumsy and lethal on roots, ladders, narrow shelter entrances, and slick stone.

Quarantine afterward should be strict. The local fungal-root-bacterial economy appears capable of incorporating unfamiliar chemistry. Do not assume it will ignore human tissue, gut flora, clothing, or equipment.

### Arrange a competent arrival point

The safest arrival point is likely:

1. inside or immediately beside a maintained shelter;
2. on a stable shelf above known flood levels;
3. away from pressure vessels, spillways, cable corridors, and warm root concentrations;
4. within physical reach of a marked descent route.

Do not arrive in a gorge, marsh, treetop, open ridge, or machinery chamber. In this country, being only ten meters from safe ground may be functionally equivalent to being kilometers away.

Leave a return beacon inside a sheltered enclosure, physically secured to bedrock or a structural member. Assume soil, vegetation, and even foundations can migrate. A beacon placed on the ground may be carried downslope with an intact grove.

## Light discipline

### Bring no exposed visible light

Do not use headlamps, glow sticks, illuminated screens, status LEDs, sparks, laser rangefinders, or visible landing portals. Tape over or disable every indicator before arrival.

The prohibition is not merely cultural. Sudden illumination may:

- injure or disorient native organisms;
- attract distant animals;
- trigger automated responses;
- reveal you to predators that normally hunt by rare optical events;
- cause panic through the forest’s vibration networks;
- interact with reflective or chemically altered water;
- produce the “shadows” mentioned in the shelter warnings.

Even a small light may be conspicuous over enormous distances in a world without any background illumination.

If imaging is essential, use passive instruments first: pressure mapping, passive acoustics, contact radar, and carefully shielded thermal sensing. Infrared is not automatically safe. Many organisms here communicate through heat and may detect an infrared emitter as clearly as a human sees a flashlight.

Avoid active sonar until you understand local signaling conventions. A survey pulse might resemble a territorial call, a repair command, or a warning of imminent flood. Use low-power, short-range systems whose outputs have been reviewed against recordings of the local acoustic environment.

All displays should be tactile, auditory at very low personal volume, or transmitted by bone conduction. Prefer haptic maps on the forearm or torso.

## Personal equipment

### Clothing and environmental protection

Wear:

- a waterproof but breathable full-body layer;
- sealed boots with exceptional wet-rock grip;
- ankle and shin protection;
- cut- and puncture-resistant gloves;
- a close-fitting hood;
- hearing protection with adaptive pass-through;
- eye protection, despite the darkness, against branches, spray, spores, and sudden illumination;
- a compact respirator independent of the main suit;
- a harness worn at all times.

Everything should resist fungus, corrosion, and prolonged warmth. Cotton, untreated leather, exposed foam, and absorbent webbing will become waterlogged or biologically colonized. Use equipment that can be boiled, sterilized, or discarded after the trip.

Avoid dangling straps. Vines, roots, repair machinery, flowing water, and articulated animals will all find them.

Do not wear highly conductive external metal where it might bridge electrical contacts. Machinery may use wet surfaces and bacterial films as parts of its circuitry.

### Hearing without deafness

Ordinary uncovered ears are unsuitable. Near major waterfalls, the sound pressure may cause rapid hearing damage, yet completely blocking sound would remove your most important warning sense.

Use adaptive hearing protection capable of:

- suppressing continuous concussion;
- preserving sudden local clicks and movement;
- translating low-frequency ground vibrations into haptic signals;
- limiting sharp pressure transients;
- allowing contact communication through jaw, throat, or bone conduction.

Carry a mechanical contact communicator: a short rod or pad that can be pressed to another traveler’s shoulder, jaw, or sternum. Establish a simple tactile language before departure.

For example:

- one squeeze: stop;
- two: move;
- three: danger;
- sustained pressure: brace or hold;
- tracing downward: descend immediately;
- tapping the wrist: machinery change;
- palm over crown or face: possible sky thinning or light event.

Do not rely on speech. There will be places where shouting into someone’s ear is ineffective.

### Navigation and fall protection

Carry:

- redundant inertial navigation;
- a tactile terrain recorder;
- short-range pressure or radar mapping;
- several hundred meters of nonconductive line;
- auto-locking ascenders and descenders;
- expanding anchors designed for wet stone;
- broad slings for living roots;
- a pole for testing surfaces and water depth;
- compact flotation;
- a rebreather or emergency breathing set;
- a line-cutting tool accessible with either hand.

Remain attached to a partner or fixed route in uncertain terrain. However, use breakaway links near machinery and fast water so a trapped companion does not pull the entire party into a conduit or ravine.

Navigation records should include not just position but the normal acoustic and vibrational character of each section. On this plateau, a route is partly a sequence of expected sounds. A missing pump rhythm may be more important than a mapped landmark.

Never assume a return path remains unchanged. Root bridges sag, channels rise, shelves creep, and towers reposition their feet.

## How to move

### Travel slowly enough to listen

The natural human response to complete darkness is to reach forward and move cautiously. That is insufficient here. Stop frequently and listen through several media:

- air;
- the soles of your feet;
- a hand on a trunk;
- a probe against stone;
- a contact sensor on pipes or shelter walls.

Before entering any new section, wait through several minutes of its normal cycle. Identify:

- the nearest water;
- whether its level is rising;
- periodic machinery;
- overhead movement;
- animal warning patterns;
- cable vibration;
- the direction of wind and spray;
- available high ground.

Rain and machinery can conceal approach sounds. Conversely, a sudden reduction in noise may be more dangerous than an increase.

### Follow contours, not waterways

Streams may look—if “look” has meaning through instrumentation—like easy navigation corridors. Avoid them. They are dynamic components of both the watershed and industrial system. Gates may open without warning, and a shallow trough may become a high-velocity spillway.

Travel along stable contour shelves and established tactile paths where possible. Do not camp:

- in polished channels;
- below pipe joints;
- beneath tensioned cables;
- on peat-filled basins;
- beside warm vents;
- directly under canopy gaps;
- within the mist plume of a large waterfall;
- on the downslope side of a slowly moving grove.

Polished stone is evidence of repeated water, not convenient footing.

### Treat warm surfaces as occupied

Warm roots may be metabolically active. Warm pipes may carry brine, compressed gas, or high-pressure water. Warm joints may belong to repairers—or to predators waiting beside them.

Do not grasp a warm object until you have mapped its extent and rhythm. A pulsing warmth is especially significant. It may be communication, fluid movement, or an organism’s circulation.

### Never climb a cable or pipe without necessity

The industrial network likely interprets load, vibration, and pressure. Climbing a line may resemble a fault, obstruction, or animal attack. Some lines may also move suddenly when gates or flywheels change state.

Cross beneath or over conduits only at established points. Listen for transmitted movement before touching them. If a cable’s pitch rises, back away from both it and its anchor points.

## Water and food

### Assume all natural water is unsafe

Rain is not necessarily clean. It passes through a chemically maintained cloud layer, an active canopy, fungal networks, mineral aerosols, and industrial emissions.

Do not drink from:

- streams;
- root cups;
- microbial pools;
- warm seeps;
- condensed spray;
- reservoirs attached to unknown pipes;
- any water that feels unusually slick, metallic, warm, or electrically active.

Use sealed imported water initially. If resupply is impossible, purification must include particulate filtration, chemical adsorption, sterilization, and removal of dissolved metals and sulfides. Ordinary camping filters are inadequate.

Maintained shelter reservoirs may be potable for local occupants but not necessarily for humans. Test every source. Do not put a hand into a reservoir to inspect it; use a disposable sampler.

If water “shines,” whether detected visually or as unusual radiative, thermal, or chemical activity, **do not touch, sample, or approach it**. Move away without crossing its drainage path.

Bring all food. Do not eat native fruit, fungi, larvae, microbial films, or shelter provisions unless compatibility has been established. Local food may depend on mineral chemistry toxic to humans. Its “sugars” or amino acids may not be biochemically ordinary.

Store food and waste in odor-tight pressure containers. Scent is a major communication channel here. Human waste could attract animals, recruit decomposers, or be interpreted by the forest as a novel nutrient source.

## Animals and repairers

### Do not confuse curiosity with harmlessness

Native creatures may investigate you through:

- tapping;
- touching your equipment;
- tasting condensation;
- following body heat;
- drumming on the ground;
- matching your footfalls;
- gathering when your machinery changes sound.

Remain still during first contact. Do not answer clicks or rhythmic knocks unless you know their meaning. Repeating a pattern may be interpreted as identity, challenge, mating signal, or mechanical command.

Large gliders and canopy animals should be given vertical clearance. When rain changes direction without a corresponding wind shift, crouch, secure loose equipment, and wait. A huge flyer may be passing overhead.

If nearby animals abruptly stop calling, freeze and assess. Do not immediately flee; rapid movement may distinguish you from the background. Identify whether the silence is spreading, and in which direction.

### Repair mechanisms should be treated as workers, not wildlife

Do not block, ride, dismantle, tag, or follow them into pipes. Give clicking groups a clear path. Their apparent slowness may conceal great strength, and they may regard a human as debris, biological fouling, or an unregistered maintenance object.

If one examines your equipment, remain motionless unless it begins pulling you toward machinery. Use the emergency breakaway line rather than striking it.

Avoid imitating their precise click sequences. You do not know whether those sounds are language, ranging pulses, authentication signals, or commands to valves elsewhere.

A repairer carrying moss and animals is still machinery. A machine that appears abandoned may still be a living part of the ecosystem.

## Shelters and possible occupants

The maintained shelters are probably your safest refuge, but they are not yours.

Before entering:

1. stop outside and listen for occupancy;
2. touch the entrance markings and record them without altering them;
3. announce yourself with a simple, non-repetitive contact knock rather than shouting;
4. wait;
5. enter without visible light;
6. keep the doorway unobstructed.

Do not rearrange tool racks, speaking tubes, maps, mats, or hooks. Their placement may be functional or communicative. Do not leave objects leaning against walls that transmit vibration.

Use relief maps with clean gloves. Make tactile copies rather than marking originals. If a map line continues beyond its edge, do not infer scale without comparing multiple maps; the industrial system may represent hydraulic or pressure relationships rather than geographic distance.

Speaking tubes should be approached cautiously. Listen before making any sound. Do not blow into them, tap coded patterns, or call loudly. A tube may connect not only to another shelter but to a pressure chamber, acoustic sensor, animal nest, or control system.

If fresh mats or supplies appear during your stay, assume an occupant or maintenance entity is nearby. Leave a compact, inert gift only if cultural guidance supports it. Good options might include a clean ceramic token with a simple tactile representation of your body and arrival point. Bad options include food, powered devices, reactive metal, lights, perfume, or biological material.

Never occupy a shelter for longer than necessary. Its unseen users may depend on it during floods or maintenance cycles.

## Reading danger

Learn the normal rhythm around camp before sleeping. Record several hours of:

- ground vibration;
- pipe pulse;
- local calls;
- rainfall;
- water level;
- air pressure;
- temperature.

Set alarms for **absence** as well as excess.

Wake and prepare to move if:

- a regular turbine or pump stops;
- insects and grazers fall silent together;
- cable pitch rises steadily;
- water noise moves closer or lower in tone;
- the ground begins transmitting evenly spaced impacts;
- warm air starts emerging from cracks;
- repairers travel rapidly in one direction;
- native animals begin climbing;
- a formerly wet surface drains abruptly;
- a shelter wall stops sweating in its usual rhythm.

A dry interval may not be safe. It can mean upstream impoundment, followed by release.

If you hear armored bodies climbing spillways, leave the watercourse. The animals may be migrating in anticipation of a surge.

If a slope begins moving, do not run downhill. Move laterally toward old stone or a stable structural support, staying clear of root masses under tension. The forest may descend intact, but a human can be crushed between interlocked trunks.

## The sky-thinning protocol

Take the shelter inscription literally.

### If the sky thins

Descend immediately, preferably into a designated shelter, overhang, or deep gallery. Do not stop to investigate. Cover all exposed skin and eyes. Shut down optical sensors and reflective surfaces.

A change may be announced by:

- unfamiliar warmth from above;
- rain becoming directional or sparse;
- animals moving downward;
- canopy leaves folding simultaneously;
- upper-atmosphere vanes changing pitch;
- a new open quality to echoes;
- visible contrast where none existed before.

### If water shines

Do not touch it. Do not sample it. Do not stand where it could splash you. Move uphill and away from connected channels.

“Shining” could indicate transmitted external light, photochemical activation, unusual ionization, suspended organisms, or something the local languages do not distinguish from illumination.

### If shadows appear

Remain still until they disappear.

Do not wave, approach, photograph, or test them with additional light. Do not assume a shadow is merely the absence of illumination. The warning’s alternate meanings—copies, hunters, things separated by light—suggest that illumination may create a detectable or autonomous distinction not normally present in this ecology.

If you are with companions, maintain contact but do not reposition. Close your eyes behind opaque protection. Continue monitoring only through touch, pressure, and passive sound. Afterward, retreat to shelter and verify one another by prearranged tactile codes and private information. Do not rely solely on voice or body shape.

## Camp and sleep

Sleep only in shifts. At least one person should remain awake with a hand or sensor on the shelter structure.

Secure everyone to separate anchor points above floor level. Floodwater may enter quietly through drains or speaking channels. Keep boots, respirators, gloves, and descent kits on your body rather than beside the bed.

Do not use open heat, combustion, or loud generators. Body heat and machinery noise already make you conspicuous. Power equipment from sealed cells with no external status lights and minimal vibration.

Inspect skin frequently by touch and medical sensor. Watch for:

- numbness;
- persistent warmth;
- rubbery films;
- painless cuts;
- fungal threads in seams;
- larvae in trapped water;
- unexplained metallic deposits;
- synchronized pulsing near roots or pipes.

Any local growth attached to clothing should be isolated rather than torn off immediately. Some vines seal leaks; pulling them may open a pressurized conduit—or a wound.

## Recommended party and duration

A first expedition should have at least four people:

- a terrain and vertical-movement specialist;
- an industrial systems engineer;
- a field biologist or xenobiologist;
- a medic familiar with immersion, toxic gases, and fungal exposure.

Everyone should be cross-trained in rope rescue and tactile communication. More than six may create too much noise and make contact management difficult.

Keep the first visit short: hours, not weeks. Spend most of it learning one shelter, one stable route, and one machinery rhythm. The temptation will be to range outward because nothing can be seen and every direction seems equally unknown. Resist it. This landscape conceals distance unusually well.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect continuous sensory strain. Your mind will try to manufacture visual space from echoes and will often be wrong. A nearby drip may seem enormous; a gorge may announce itself only as delay and cold air. You may feel watched when machinery changes cadence, and alone when thousands of animals are within arm’s reach.

The darkness itself may be less difficult than the absence of a fixed background. Nothing is still enough to serve as certainty. Water moves, ground creeps, roots pulse, structures adjust, and the entire plateau speaks through your feet.

With discipline, however, the place may become legible. You will learn that one cable’s murmur means stable pressure, that a certain series of canopy knocks travels ahead of rain, that cool mist marks an open drop, and that a shelter wall’s faint pulse is not random but part of a machine hundreds of kilometers wide.

A successful traveler will not conquer the darkness or illuminate it. They will borrow local senses: touch, timing, pressure, and restraint.

Leave softly. Remove every object you brought unless a deliberate exchange has been agreed upon. Record the shelter exactly as you found it. Before departure, listen long enough to confirm that the country has returned to its ordinary rhythm.

Here, that rhythm is the nearest thing to safety.
