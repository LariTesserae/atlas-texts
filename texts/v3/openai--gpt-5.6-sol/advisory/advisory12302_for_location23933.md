## Preparing to Visit the Cinder Conservatory

Treat the Conservatory as a combination of furnace, pressure vessel, chemical plant, cave system, and fragile botanical reserve. Its emptiness is deceptive. There may be no predators, but nearly every feature of the environment is capable of killing an unprotected human.

An ordinary traveler should not attempt the visit without a fully enclosed environmental suit, surface support, and several observed breathing cycles of the chamber.

### Before entering

Study the Conservatory remotely for at least two complete heat cycles. The chamber’s winds, pressure changes, and central release appear regular, but “regular” does not mean precisely timed. Place expendable sensors through the entrance crack to measure:

- Temperature at the surface, descent, streets, vents, and central pit
- Atmospheric pressure and the magnitude of its daily swing
- Gas composition, including oxygen, sulfur compounds, ammonia, hydrocarbons, carbon monoxide, and corrosive vapors
- Wind direction and speed during both phases of the breathing cycle
- Timing of seedpod discharges and the central diaphragm release
- Acoustic and infrasonic intensity
- Structural movement around the entrance

Do not assume the atmosphere is breathable merely because open flames and oxidative chemistry occur. It may contain no usable oxygen, or enough reactive gas to poison, anesthetize, burn, or dissolve human lung tissue within moments.

The best entry window is likely shortly after the central release, when the chamber is briefly still. Confirm this rather than relying on the apparent cycle. Leave well before the next reversal or pressure pulse.

## Essential equipment

### A sealed, heat-rated suit

The traveler needs a positive-pressure suit with:

- Independent breathing supply
- Full chemical isolation
- Active cooling that does not depend on outside evaporation
- Flexible joints and expansion sections
- Pressure compensation for rapid ambient changes
- Replaceable outer layers resistant to alkalis, sulfur compounds, hot oils, and abrasive salt
- A smooth faceplate with mechanical shutters or sacrificial transparent covers

The suit should be flexible rather than a rigid shell. The Conservatory destroys things that cannot bend, breathe, or equalize. Any rigid pressure vessel must be certified for the measured external pressure range and protected from impact and heat.

Human bodies contain abundant water. That is not a problem while the suit remains intact; it becomes a serious ecological and chemical event if the suit leaks. Sweat, exhaled moisture, coolant, blood, food, and waste must all remain contained. Carry no exposed water—not even a damp cleaning cloth.

Use redundant life support, but keep the excursion short. Closed-cycle systems are preferable, provided all heat and moisture remain captured.

### Hearing and vibration protection

The noise is not merely unpleasant. Reports amplified by the dome, resonant cords, vent roar, and thermal fractures may cause hearing loss, disorientation, panic, nausea, or injury through low-frequency pressure.

Use:

- Acoustic insulation built into the helmet
- Active hearing protection
- Limited, filtered environmental audio rather than an open microphone feed
- Visual or haptic alarms
- Text-based communications as a backup to speech
- A suit monitor for harmful vibration and pressure impulses

Earplugs alone are inadequate against infrasound and whole-body vibration.

### Eye, skin, and puncture protection

The needle-fine spores should be treated as hot, chemically active splinters. They may penetrate seals, clog radiators, abrade visors, or react with terrestrial materials. The outer suit should be puncture-resistant, and intakes should not exist at all.

Avoid flowering cups, swollen pods, and taut seed curtains. If leaves throughout the chamber begin folding simultaneously, interpret it as an environmental alarm and seek immediate shelter away from vents and the central pit.

### Navigation and access equipment

The entrance is narrow, concealed, and surrounded by a hollow salt crust. Do not land a heavy vehicle directly above it or anchor solely to nearby mineral scales. Establish anchors only after subsurface imaging.

Bring:

- Two independent descent lines
- Heat-resistant nonmetallic or protected anchors
- A powered ascender
- A physical guide line from the entrance to the deepest point of travel
- Beacons that function through mineral walls
- Inertial navigation, since ordinary radio and satellite positioning may fail underground
- A compact shelter capable of deflecting spores and falling flakes, though not intended for overnight use

Bridges, ladders, platforms, and roofs are not human-rated. They may be strong in the direction of habitual wind loading and fragile under a person’s weight. Never trust a structure because it has survived for centuries.

## Materials to avoid

Leave ordinary camping equipment behind. The Conservatory is hostile to:

- Exposed steel and common electronics
- Rubber seals not rated for hydrocarbons and sulfur
- Brittle plastics
- Wet batteries
- Adhesives that soften in heat
- Airtight consumer containers
- Loose fabric
- Open lubricants
- Anything that can shed fibers, droplets, or flakes

Favor ceramics, high-temperature composites, protected glass, mineral fabrics, and mechanically fastened components. Any necessary metal should be coated and monitored. Keep tools tethered: a dropped object may fall into a shaft, strike a catalytic flower, or become incorporated into the city’s slow self-repair.

Do not bring fire. No flare, match, combustion stove, or pyrotechnic marker should enter the chamber. Fire moves unpredictably in the heavy atmosphere and may travel downward along resinous growths.

## Conduct inside the Conservatory

### Walk as though the city is alive

Whether the vegetation built the city, inherited it, or merely maintains it is less important than the practical conclusion: architecture and organism cannot reliably be distinguished.

Do not:

- Cut fibers stretched between buildings
- Move wedges, counterweights, or grooved stones
- Clear moss from a doorway
- Break a resin seal
- Pull a root from masonry
- Turn a ceramic wheel “to see what it does”
- Enter sealed inward-facing structures
- Collect tools or plant samples
- Lean on a wall being braced by a fan-tree

A cord may be structural, musical, meteorological, reproductive, or all four. A loose stone may be a counterweight. A curtain may distribute spores or regulate pressure. The local systems operate slowly enough to look inert until disturbed.

### Stay away from the major hazards

Give wide clearance to:

- The central glass-lined pit
- Natural chimneys and any street aligned with them
- Black cracks exhaling vapor
- Taut seedpods
- Low depressions where blue fire or heavy gases may pool
- Hanging mineral skins
- Ladders and isolated platforms
- Recently sealed resin patches
- Roofs beneath the entrance crack

Never descend into the central pit. The diaphragm release could involve heat, pressure, toxic gas, flying grit, and a sudden upward current powerful enough to throw a person into the dome.

During any unexpected pressure change, get low behind a porous, established barrier—but not in a depression where dense gases collect. Clip into a secure line. Do not shelter in an airtight room.

### Keep the visit brief

The chamber’s danger is cumulative. Heat works through insulation, reactive vapors degrade seals, spores accumulate in joints, and repeated pressure cycling fatigues equipment. Plan the excursion in minutes or a few hours, not days.

Do not camp inside. Sleeping through a wind reversal would be exceptionally dangerous, and there is no safe local source of air, food, or water.

## Recommended itinerary

A first visit should be modest:

1. Descend immediately after a confirmed central release.
2. Pause near the entrance to verify suit pressure and communications.
3. Follow an outer circular street rather than crossing the central void.
4. Observe one garden district from several meters away.
5. Visit no enclosed house unless it has first been scanned and shown to have multiple exits.
6. Turn back after consuming no more than one-third of breathing and cooling reserves.
7. Be above the salt crust before the next major thermal transition.

The outer streets will provide more than enough to understand the place: silver leaves folding near vents, red filaments tightening around old masonry, ceramic vanes responding to currents, and buildings that seem half constructed and half grown.

## Emergency planning

A support team should remain outside but not directly over the hollow. They need the ability to retrieve an unconscious traveler by line without sending a second person into the chamber immediately.

Establish automatic retreat triggers for:

- Cooling system degradation
- Loss of suit pressure
- Unexpected gas penetration
- Rapid ambient pressure change
- Simultaneous plant closure
- Increasing infrasound
- Wind reversal
- Structural cracking near the entrance
- Loss of the guide line
- Any central-pit movement

If the suit is punctured, do not attempt a field repair beside vegetation. Cover the breach instantly, switch to emergency pressure, and ascend. If coolant or bodily fluid escapes, mark the location remotely but do not remain to clean it up while compromised.

A blocked entrance is the most serious contingency. Before descent, identify at least one possible secondary route with subsurface imaging, even if it would require excavation from above.

## Decontamination

Assume every exterior surface is coated with reactive dust, spores, oils, and salts.

Decontamination should occur in a sealed transition chamber and begin dry:

1. Immobilize loose particles with electrostatic or vacuum capture.
2. Remove sacrificial suit layers.
3. Collect all dust and broken material as potentially living and chemically hazardous.
4. Analyze samples before using any water-based wash.
5. Keep local material isolated from oxygen-rich, humid environments.

Do not casually expose spores to terrestrial air or water. A life form adapted to an anhydrous solvent system may die immediately—or react violently. Either outcome would be an ecological breach.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the greatest difficulty to be psychological rather than visual. The Conservatory offers few familiar signs of life. Nothing watches, cries, flees, or approaches. Yet the whole city responds around you: leaves turn, cords tighten, shutters rise, walls seal themselves, and unseen weights fall in the dark.

Your light will make the chamber seem smaller than it is. Beyond the beam will be immense quantities of hot, moving air. Sounds will arrive from misleading directions after circling the dome. A nearby crack may resemble distant thunder; a distant seedpod may sound like a weapon fired beside your head.

Then, if the cycle reaches its pause while you are present, everything may stop at once.

Do not let the silence tempt you toward the pit. Use it to leave.

The safest attitude is neither fear nor curiosity alone, but courtesy: enter lightly, touch nothing, assume every apparent ruin is still performing a function, and depart before the Conservatory draws its next breath.
