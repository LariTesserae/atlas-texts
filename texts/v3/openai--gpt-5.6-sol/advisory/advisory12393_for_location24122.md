## First judgment

An ordinary human should not visit this land in ordinary expedition gear. Treat it as a combination of:

- a near-vacuum high-altitude environment,
- an active volcanic and tectonic zone,
- a deep dry-powder desert,
- a severe electrical storm system,
- and an unresolved biological or anomalous site.

The absence of loud explosions, visible flames, and bad weather is misleading. Most lethal processes here are quiet.

No human should be the first arrival. Send instrumented crawlers and sacrificial probes through for several local upheaval cycles before crossing.

## Essential life support

Wear a fully sealed pressure suit with:

- independent oxygen and carbon-dioxide removal,
- at least twice the expected excursion duration in reserves,
- chemical filtration for mineral vapors,
- active heating and cooling,
- radiation monitoring,
- a conductive outer layer with controlled static discharge,
- replaceable abrasion panels over the boots, knees, gloves, and torso,
- and no exposed metal points or loose external cables.

The atmosphere should be considered unbreathable regardless of measured oxygen content. Pressure may be inadequate, and carbon grains, metallic flakes, vaporized salts, and hot dust could damage lungs immediately.

Carry water only in double-contained vessels. Never pour it on the ground. Hot anhydrous minerals and unfamiliar salts may hydrate violently, release gases, produce steam, or undergo abrupt structural changes. Likewise, do not vent oxygen, perspiration, sewage, or exhaled air. Apart from contamination concerns, oxygen could initiate reactions that this country has never naturally experienced.

Use chemical heat packs and battery systems designed without exposed flame. Combustion may be difficult outside, but an oxygen leak into hot mineral dust could create a very different local environment.

## Getting in—and, more importantly, getting out

Do not rely on a portal, gate, or return beacon fixed to the ground. The shelf supporting it may tilt, rise, split, become buried, or be folded into a cliff while you are away.

The safest arrangement is:

1. A pressure-sealed mobile base accompanies the traveler.
2. The return aperture is mounted inside it or referenced to a frame outside local geology.
3. A second independent extraction system is carried on the suit.
4. Several disposable rescue beacons are deployed behind the party.
5. Someone remains on the departure side with authority to retrieve the traveler without waiting for a scheduled check-in.

Any gate must have an airlock. Opening an unrestricted connection could release oxygen and water vapor into the range while drawing mineral dust into the home environment.

Keep the visit short. Plan by stability windows rather than by clock time or distance. If robotic observation suggests that a shelf usually remains quiet for forty minutes, assume only twenty.

## Mobility

The landscape is unsuitable for conventional hiking. Apparently level dust may be hundreds of meters deep and may behave more like dry fluid than soil. A traveler can disappear into it without the usual warning of mud or snow.

Bring:

- broad, articulated load-distributing boots,
- a long probe with thermal, density, and electrical sensors,
- compact ground-penetrating radar,
- a reel of high-strength quick-release tether,
- multiple smart anchors rather than one trusted anchor,
- and a small sealed crawler or articulated walking vehicle.

Do not use wheels unless preliminary surveys establish a solid route. Rotorcraft will perform poorly in thin air, while jets and rockets may loft charged dust, melt glass seams, or provoke unknown chemical reactions. A low, many-legged vehicle with independently sacrificial feet is preferable.

Never assume an anchor is permanent. Use three or more, monitor their relative motion, and retain the ability to sever every tether instantly. Being attached to a ridge that begins moving is worse than being unattached.

Walk only on surfaces verified as solid. Avoid:

- the bases and lips of scarps,
- the apparent floors of basins,
- fresh black planes,
- translucent crusts over red seams,
- pale salt growths,
- overhangs,
- narrow passes,
- and any fracture exhaling heat.

Salt ribs and hollow columns may look substantial but should be assumed brittle, hot, chemically reactive, or actively growing.

## Reading the ground

Because the thin atmosphere carries little sound, your ears will provide dangerously little warning. Fit the suit with:

- boot-mounted vibration sensors,
- haptic alarms,
- an inertial monitor,
- subsurface acoustic pickups,
- and automatic recording of tilt and relative displacement.

A dry click, pressure in the teeth, or a slow sensation in the chest may be the only perceptible warning of a kilometer-scale fracture. The suit should amplify these signals into unmistakable visual and tactile alerts.

Watch for several kinds of precursor:

- **Sudden warmth across a broad slope:** heat is moving upward; leave immediately.
- **A red seam brightening beneath its crust:** possible softening, fracture, or mineral transition.
- **Dust rising without wind:** subsurface displacement or electrostatic lifting.
- **Nearby crystals changing orientation:** an active rise front may be developing.
- **Fresh straight cracks behind a moving scarp:** lateral translation is underway.
- **The black skins contracting into beads:** a strong tremor or electrical event may be imminent.
- **Pale specks beginning to spiral overhead:** seek electrical shelter at once.

Do not stand still to observe an upheaval merely because it appears slow. A ridge that rises gradually can still close your only route, overturn the ground beneath you, or expose a surface hot enough to compromise the suit.

## Electrical weather

The first sign of a charge event may be organized rotation in the airborne mineral canopy. Treat this as the equivalent of an approaching severe lightning storm.

Retreat to a sealed Faraday shelter before violet threads or edge coronas become visible. The shelter should have:

- a smooth conductive exterior,
- no projecting antennae,
- isolated internal electronics,
- optical rather than metallic external data links where practical,
- surge-dump systems,
- and an insulated, articulated contact system.

Do not casually “ground” yourself with a metal stake. In a landscape where large electrical potentials may exist between neighboring crystals, a conventional ground line could become the preferred path for a major discharge. Use equipment designed to measure local potential and bleed charge gradually.

If caught outside:

1. Retract tools and antennas.
2. Disconnect tethers that span visibly different rock units.
3. Avoid ridges, needles, salt columns, and glass seams.
4. Do not enter a dust basin.
5. Crouch on an electrically insulating mat on a previously scanned solid shelf.
6. Keep limbs close and wait for the suit’s field monitor to declare the gradient safe.

Expect instruments to fail temporarily. Carry mechanical backups and passive indicators, including a protected crystal whose rotation can reveal the direction of the nearest active rise.

## Navigation

Compass directions and visual landmarks are inadequate. The land rotates and deforms; a visible destination may cease to be topologically reachable.

Use several independent systems:

- suit-grade inertial navigation,
- short-baseline laser measurements between local beacons,
- continuous three-dimensional mapping,
- multiversal or orbital reference if it can penetrate the canopy,
- and a breadcrumb network that records not only position but tilt, vibration, temperature, and electrical state.

Map routes as connections between stable patches, not as lines across coordinates. For example: “Base shelf to quiet slab B; cross only while fissure C is closed; descend beside inactive salt fan D.” Every route should include at least two alternatives.

Never descend into terrain you cannot climb out of while carrying an injured companion. A valley may close, rise, or turn on edge. Turn back while the return path is still easy, not when supplies reach half capacity.

## Shelter and camps

Do not camp in the stone chambers, however inviting their coolness and freedom from dust may seem.

Their behavior indicates that they interact with the surrounding physical fields. They may be magnetic shelters, tectonic alignment devices, artifacts, or simply unusual mineral configurations—but their tendency to remain cool and exclude dust deserves investigation before human occupation. Some have already been incorporated into new cliffs. A roofless chamber can become a sealed cavity.

Use a mobile pressure pod on multiple self-leveling legs. Place it on a broad, robotically surveyed shelf well away from scarps, dust margins, glowing seams, salt vents, and known structures. The pod should be able to:

- detect differential movement under each leg,
- release any leg that becomes trapped,
- survive overturning,
- remain sealed under heavy dust,
- and relocate under its own power.

There is no dependable night or day, so establish an artificial circadian schedule inside. The perpetual dimness and immense silence can produce disorientation quickly. Helmet audio, regular spoken check-ins, and a visible mission clock will help preserve judgment.

## The chambers

Approach the slab structures as protected archaeological and anomalous sites, not as shelters or convenient landmarks.

Before entering:

- map the structure remotely,
- measure temperature, magnetic fields, charge gradients, radiation, and timekeeping drift,
- place duplicate clocks and inertial sensors inside and outside,
- observe whether dust, heat, radio, and tether signals cross the threshold normally,
- and confirm that the enclosing terrain is not actively rising.

Do not rearrange the slabs or complete a partially formed enclosure. If they are artifacts, that would destroy evidence; if they are naturally assembled by the moving land, altering them may interfere with an active process.

A crystal placed inside can serve as a crude detector of the nearest rising peak, but do not depend on it for safe navigation. The “nearest” active uplift may be beneath the chamber.

No traveler should enter alone. Use a tether that can be detached from both ends, and keep the other person outside rather than just beyond the threshold.

## The black skins

Assume the glossy skins are alive until proved otherwise. Equally, assume they could be aggressive self-propagating mineral chemistry rather than life. Either possibility warrants strict quarantine.

Do not:

- touch them with a glove,
- breathe or vent near them,
- expose them to water or oxygen,
- illuminate them with a high-powered lamp,
- or carry a sample in the occupied pressure cabin.

Sampling should be robotic. Seal specimens inside nested inert containers under local atmospheric conditions, then place the entire assembly in an external quarantine compartment. On return, keep them isolated from water, organic material, batteries, and electrical junctions.

Their contraction during tremors makes them useful natural indicators. Observe from a distance. If several patches bead simultaneously, retreat before investigating why.

## Personal preparation

The traveler should train for:

- pressure-suit repair,
- confined-space survival,
- volcanic and seismic evacuation,
- navigation without horizon or compass,
- extrication from powders and granular flows,
- electrical-field emergencies,
- and silent-team communication.

Practice moving slowly in near darkness while receiving information mainly through haptic instruments. The instinct to listen for danger will be useless here. So will the instinct to seek low ground during exposed weather.

Carry a rescue harness, redundant cooling loops, eye-safe internal lighting, emergency pressure cocoon, suit patch kit, injectable pain control, and enough oxygen to remain immobile for a prolonged rescue. A suit breach against a razor-sharp newborn edge is among the most likely immediate emergencies.

## Rules worth memorizing

1. **Nothing flat is necessarily solid.**
2. **Nothing quiet is necessarily still.**
3. **Do not trust a route merely because you just used it.**
4. **Do not introduce water or oxygen to the terrain.**
5. **When the sky begins to rotate, shelter immediately.**
6. **When the black skins contract, expect change.**
7. **Do not sleep inside the stone chambers.**
8. **Never tether yourself to only one piece of the world.**
9. **Keep extraction independent of local ground.**
10. **Leave earlier than seems necessary.**

The great danger of this province is not that it is collapsing. It is that it is still being made. A traveler is not walking through ruins but across an active process, one that has no reason to preserve paths, camps, artifacts, or human bodies as separate things. Given time, it will incorporate all of them.
