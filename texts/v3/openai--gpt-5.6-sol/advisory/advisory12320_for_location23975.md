## Before you go

Treat this as a wilderness expedition conducted inside a functioning metropolis. The plain may look empty, but nearly every apparently vacant tract is infrastructure, habitat, jurisdiction, or all three. Do not assume that open ground is public ground, that a lone door is abandoned, or that an object beside a road is an object.

Arrange the visit through a recognized local host. You will need:

- A temporary civic identity readable by stations, houses, warning systems, and tower interiors.
- A local itinerary expressed in **events and conditions**, not merely coordinates: “after the western condensation cycle, when the platform unfolds beside the red-moss field,” rather than “at 14:00 at this latitude.”
- Authorization for any biological samples, recording equipment, drones, self-repairing devices, or adaptive clothing.
- A guide trained in human physiology and ordinary fixed-space perception.
- Access to a city-certified personal quiet field.

Do not arrive by dropping onto an apparently empty patch of plain. It may be a flood channel, a migrating transit route, a sleeping radial plant, the roof of a closed house, or the hunting range of an animal that resembles municipal hardware. Use a recognized arrival door or attended temporary platform.

Allow at least a day for orientation before traveling between towers.

## Medical preparation

The principal strain is acoustic, but not only in the ordinary sense. Much of the city’s signaling passes through the ground and body. Visitors may experience:

- Pressure in the teeth, jaw, sinuses, or joints.
- Nausea and disorientation from low-frequency standing waves.
- False sensations of distant thunder or approaching machinery.
- Difficulty judging where sounds originate.
- Fatigue from continual muscular bracing.
- Sleep disturbance, even inside quiet pockets.
- Anxiety when visual emptiness conflicts with overwhelming sound.

People with uncontrolled vertigo, severe migraine, pressure-sensitive implants, brittle dental work, certain cardiac rhythm disorders, or devices affected by vibration should obtain specialist clearance. Declare pacemakers, neural interfaces, cochlear implants, bone-conduction devices, and prosthetics to your host; civic signals may couple with them unexpectedly.

Bring ordinary hearing protection as backup, but **do not rely on sealed earplugs or total noise cancellation outdoors**. The dangerous energy can travel through bone and stone, and complete acoustic isolation may conceal public signals. More importantly, unexplained true silence is an emergency condition here. Use only locally certified attenuation that preserves alarms and infrastructure tones.

Take a conservative approach to the biology. The plain’s life is adapted to brief water cycles and intimate exchanges of spores, larvae, chemicals, and electrical signals. Obtain broad-spectrum travel medicine where appropriate, but do not assume alien microbes are either harmless or infectious in familiar ways. A light respirator, eye protection, skin barrier, and post-exposure wash are sensible during the first days, especially in condensation groves and after rain.

## What to wear

Pack for warm, dim, wet-dry conditions rather than cold.

Essential clothing includes:

- High, puncture-resistant boots with rigid soles and ankle protection. Fresh basalt edges can cut ordinary hiking footwear.
- Flexible gaiters to keep sharp runners, larvae, mud, and plant fibers out.
- Tough trousers and long sleeves resistant to abrasion and sap.
- Gloves that preserve fine touch. Touch is an important local communication channel.
- Clear or copper-tinted impact goggles.
- A breathable waterproof shell that can be deployed quickly.
- A close-fitting hood that does not block peripheral vision or bone-conducted alerts.
- A low-profile pack with no loose straps for creepers or drifting root curtains to catch.

Avoid highly reflective surfaces, strong floral colors, heated garments, exposed electrical coils, and anything that emits persistent low-frequency vibration. Flowers and animals may interpret warmth, charge, and vibration as biological signals. Ask your host whether your clothing resembles courtship display, territorial warning, or food.

Do not wear a garment capable of autonomous self-repair or replication without declaring it. A sufficiently independent self-reproducing machine may be legally treated as fauna rather than property.

## Equipment

A visitor’s field kit should include:

- A city-certified quiet-field unit with haptic emergency alerts.
- A passive mechanical backup alarm that your guide has approved.
- A haptic communicator or touch-pad phrase system.
- A local warning-symbol reader; do not assume signs are visual.
- A tethered navigation aid registered with the civic network.
- Puncture and laceration supplies.
- Sterile rinse for mineral dust, sap, and biological contact.
- At least one day of sealed water and compact food.
- A water-quality tester calibrated locally.
- A light ground mat resistant to glassy stone.
- A nonreplicating emergency beacon.
- A simple physical line or tether for storm visibility and spatial confusion.
- A paper or inert-film card listing your host, physiology, medications, and home-universe requirements.

Conventional satellite navigation may provide coordinates while giving dangerously wrong travel times. Directional distance around towers is not symmetric: an hour toward a structure does not imply an hour back, and “around the corner” can become a day’s walk. Compass readings may remain accurate while being practically useless.

Do not bring free-flying drones. They can trespass through suspended gardens, be taken for prey or pollinators, interfere with condensation fields, or become legally entangled with local machine populations.

## Learning to communicate

Ordinary speech outdoors will often fail. Shouting only exhausts you and may attract attention from animals that hunt through sound fields.

Before leaving the arrival station, learn the local haptic basics:

- Stop.
- Follow me.
- Flood route.
- Predator boundary.
- Infrastructure signal.
- Do not step.
- Quiet-field failure.
- Medical distress.
- I cannot perceive the sign.
- I am spatially disoriented.

Residents may communicate through touch more readily than visitors expect, but do not grab anyone. Offer the agreed contact surface—often forearm, glove, or handheld pad—and wait for consent.

Inside quiet pockets, speak normally. Do not step backward out of one while talking; the transition into the plain’s full acoustic field can be startling enough to cause a fall.

## Moving across the plain

Never judge occupancy by sight. A stretch containing no visible person or building can still be as regulated and active as a central railway station.

For your first excursions:

1. Travel with a guide.
2. Use an announced route.
3. Remain on surfaces the local system identifies as available.
4. Keep the guide within touch or haptic range.
5. Stop when the ground begins to pulse; do not try to hurry across a civic signal.
6. Yield to animals, drifting gardens, temporary water, and emerging structures.
7. Confirm every isolated roadside marker before approaching it.

The local ambush predator that resembles municipal equipment is not a colorful warning but an ordinary urban hazard. Maintenance systems usually place symbols near it, not barriers around it. A visitor who cannot read those symbols should regard every lone post, bollard, cabinet, marker, or scrap of machinery as potentially alive.

Do not climb ridges simply for a better view. Their crests may be glass-sharp, unstable, or ecologically sealed beneath creepers. Likewise, do not step onto radial plate-leaves. What looks like a field of separate foliage may be one organism extending over several hectares.

If suspended gardens drift across your route, wait. Do not push through hanging roots. They may be harvesting water, exchanging charge, or attached to an invisible municipal field that is about to move.

## Rain protocol

Rain is the moment when the city becomes most legible—and most hazardous.

The sound commonly arrives before the water. At the first storm warning:

- Check the direction of animal movement.
- Leave cracks, shallow basins, and low clay ground immediately.
- Do not shelter under fresh stone overhangs without local approval; they may channel runoff or contain temporary breeding pools.
- Follow raised roads or designated flood margins.
- Let emerging houses finish unfolding before touching their entrances.
- Secure goggles and cover cuts.
- Keep away from newly filled pools.

A dry channel can become a violent torrent even if the rain column is kilometers away. Because the stone is young and poorly weathered, runoff follows long straight cracks at speed.

After rain, expect rapid biological activity. Pools that were dust an hour earlier may already be breeding habitat. Do not wade, drink, wash equipment, or collect “empty” egg cases. Winged swarms, amphibious organisms, and machine-like callers can appear almost immediately.

Rain also makes tower surfaces reveal interior depth. This is beautiful but disorienting. Visitors sometimes walk toward what appears to be a lit street beneath a wet black wall. Enter only through a recognized threshold.

## Water and food

Carry sealed water until your host confirms which condensate is intended for human use. Water may be common on leaves and cables but already allocated to a grove, reservoir, animal route, or civic exchange. Collecting it can be both medically unwise and legally equivalent to diverting a public utility.

Never assume clear water is potable. It may contain mineral concentrations from the new stone, reproductive stages adapted to very short pools, or maintenance organisms.

Eat only in designated human-compatible areas. Local fruit may embody the city’s obligation to return collected atmospheric water downstream; taking it without permission can violate water law as well as ownership. Foods that visibly resemble manufactured components may be plant-machine propagules.

Carry out ordinary waste unless a local system explicitly accepts it. Do not compost, pour out beverages, scatter crumbs, or urinate on the open plain. Nutrient additions can alter territories and water negotiations far beyond the apparent scale of the act.

## Understanding the law

Civic law here is built around relationships rather than possession. Visitors should remember five principles:

1. **Emptiness does not mean availability.**
2. **Short-lived life has full standing while present.**
3. **Predators are residents, not vermin.**
4. **Water is part of a cycle, not a disposable commodity.**
5. **Adaptive machines may have ecological rights.**

Do not remove plants, stone, obsolete machinery, seeds, larvae, or fragments. A “broken” survey device may be part of a feral population; a vine-threaded machine may be a single symbiotic organism. Fresh mineral blades may also be under structural observation.

Avoid casting prolonged shade over low vegetation with tents or vehicles. Permanent or repeated shading may incur an obligation to provide warmth elsewhere. Your guide should register any camp, canopy, or stationary equipment.

Never feed predators, grazers, feral machines, or roadside marker-animals. Do not cross visible hunting boundaries merely because no predator is visible.

## Entering the towers

Tower interiors require a different kind of discipline. Their corridors respond to intention and attention. Casual wandering, divided attention, or strongly imagining the wrong destination can alter the route presented to you.

Before entering:

- Agree on a destination in precise functional terms.
- Silence irrelevant navigational displays.
- Keep one local person in the lead.
- Use a physical or haptic group link.
- Do not fixate on glimpsed rooms beneath the tower’s surface.
- Avoid phrases such as “anywhere is fine.”

An apartment does not necessarily have a stable position, and two facilities can occupy what seems to be the same volume while remaining accessible through different sequences. Do not try to retrace a path by counting turns. Follow the exit protocol supplied by the building.

If separated, stop trying to “find” the others through intense visualization. You may continue generating branches. Instead, invoke the designated neutral destination—usually a reception room, public threshold, or visitor recovery space—and wait.

Markets, hospitals, reservoirs, and public squares may overlap. Do not assume that sounds, moisture, or apparent depth from an adjacent function indicate a leak or structural failure.

## Acoustic emergencies

Learn the difference between loudness, expected quiet, and impossible silence.

A personal quiet pocket softens the surrounding sound but does not make the world acoustically blank. You should still receive approved alarms through vibration or controlled tones.

If all sound and vibration suddenly cease:

- Stop moving.
- Do not investigate the edge of the silent area.
- Do not shout.
- Activate the emergency haptic beacon.
- Maintain contact with your group.
- Follow the guide’s indicated retreat route.
- If alone, move only according to the civic device’s instructions.

True silence may indicate pressure-grid failure or a large sound-absorbing organism. Either possibility makes improvised exploration dangerous. Do not activate powerful speakers or homemade sonar; you may interfere with structural signals or advertise your location.

Conversely, when a deep civic note rises through your body, do not assume it is an attack or earthquake. Stop, bend your knees slightly, stay off sharp edges, and wait for your device to translate it. The signal may be routing transit, testing foundations, or announcing a regional change.

## Where to sleep

Use registered visitor housing, preferably within a stable quiet zone. A house may appear only when occupied and close into the ground after departure, so mark the exit method rather than the building’s appearance.

Do not camp on the outer plain during your first visit. A seemingly bare site may become:

- A flood margin.
- A platform.
- A migrating road.
- A condensation catchment.
- An animal corridor.
- A structure’s emergence zone.
- The active surface of a low-growing organism.

Expect some vibration at night and reflected radiance moving across the haze. Eye shades can help, but completely isolating sleep pods should remain connected to civic alarms.

## If you become lost

Do not head toward the nearest tower simply because it is visible. Directional distortion can turn that decision into an extended journey.

Instead:

1. Stop on confirmed inert ground.
2. Activate your registered beacon.
3. Check for predator and flood warnings.
4. Request a temporary station or attended door.
5. Conserve water and remain visible to civic systems.
6. Do not follow apparent human footfalls unless authenticated.

Predators can cast false steps into the distance while suppressing the sound of their actual approach. A familiar voice or walking rhythm is not sufficient proof of a rescuer.

A temporary station may appear as nothing more than a door in grass. Verify its civic signature before entering.

## Best way to experience the city

Do not rush from tower to tower collecting landmarks. The city reveals itself during transitions.

Arrange to be outside, with a guide, near a safe flood margin when rain is expected. From there you can watch roads rise, houses unfold, gardens drift, animals reposition, and temporary platforms assemble. The apparent wilderness will briefly resolve into a metropolis made of coordinated relationships.

Then let it disappear again.

The most important adjustment is psychological: you are not visiting an empty landscape punctuated by technology. You are visiting an extremely dense city that has chosen not to express density as permanent bulk. Move slowly, ask before touching, listen through your feet as much as your ears, and never mistake invisibility for absence.
