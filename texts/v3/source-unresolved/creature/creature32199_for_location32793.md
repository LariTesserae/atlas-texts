They are the inhabitants of the pockets—the pocket-dwellers, the hollow-keepers, the ones who have reached the end of their becoming and found it sufficient. They occupy every niche, every hollow carved into the living walls, every terrace where the gardens hold their pattern without growth or decay.

The builders are there. They stand in the doorways of the timber structures, their hands still curled around adzes that have rusted into their palms, their breath hanging in the bright columns like the dust motes—never settling, never dissipating. Their bodies are in the process of becoming architecture. Moss thick as velvet has claimed their shoulders; lichen spreads across their cheeks in fractal patterns identical to the liverworts on the stone. They do not breathe in and out, or if they do, the rhythm is so slow that the air remains unmoving, the condensation on their skin permanent, beaded and suspended. They are the infrastructure now, the load-bearing walls of flesh and fiber, integrated into the saturation of the place, indistinguishable from the hand-wrought beams they shaped.

Then there are the small ones. The frost-furred mammals—perhaps ermine, or some creature that once moved with the speed of thought—now curled in the storage niches, their fur tips permanently encrusted with ice crystals, their bodies soft and warm only in the pocket of heat that exists as memory, as potential. They do not sleep; they simply exist in a state of arrested animation, their hearts beating once per geological epoch, their eyes open and unblinking, reflecting the cold light without comprehension or desire.

The moths with wings like lichen cling to the wooden beams and the carved stone alike. Their wings are not folded but spread, displaying patterns that match the moss exactly—camouflage perfected over millennia of stillness. They do not flutter. They hang suspended in the shafts of light, their antennae catching the brightness, living statues of a metamorphosis that stopped mid-transformation, preserved in the amber of the cold.

The beetles, armored in iridescent blue, crawl along the pathways with a patience that makes movement indistinguishable from stillness. A beetle might advance one millimeter in an hour, its legs stepping over the grain of the wood with the delicacy of a clock hand moving through honey. They are the surveyors of the stasis, measuring the slow erosion of edges, the settling of forms into their own gravity, their armored shells clicking against stone with a sound so soft the moss absorbs it before it can become noise.

And the birds—silent, pale, nesting in the wooden eaves. Their nests are woven from moss and fiber, indistinguishable from the surrounding green, from the verdant saturation that claims every surface. They sit on eggs that will never hatch, or perhaps they have already hatched, and the chicks are part of the moss now, absorbed into the deep viridian, indistinguishable from the liverworts spreading in the corners where the light pools. They make no sound. Their throats are full of the silence that blankets everything, a silence so absolute it has weight, pressing down on the eardrums like deep water, like the weight of centuries held in suspension.

They are all of them the same kind of inhabitant: the preserved, the established, the finished. They do not eat, because the terraced gardens are in equilibrium, neither growing nor dying, the nutrients cycling in a closed loop that has long since stopped turning. They do not speak, because the moss has grown into their mouths, or because sound has simply ceased to be a possibility in this acoustic vacuum, in this world where the dampness muffles and the cold stills. They do not love or hate or hope; they simply *are*, in the way that stone is, in the way that the river-polished walls are—worn smooth by centuries of contact, but not eroded, merely... perfected.

They are the population of the frozen moment, packed tight against one another with no empty room between presences. Each occupies their cubic inch with absolute finality, their existence a single, unchanging note in a chord that has been sustained for millennia. There is no birth here, only the continuation of what was; no death, only the slow mineralization of flesh into the architecture, the gradual settling of bodies into the grain of the ancient wood until builder and built are one substance, moss and mammal and moth and stone all participating in the same geological patience.

They are the citizens of the stasis, living in the pocket of bright light, in the pocket of absolute silence, in the pocket of green that never grows but simply *is*—a congregation of beings who have reached the end of their story and found, in the iron-cold air, in the wet stone, in the unmoving brightness, a permanence that is indistinguishable from peace.
