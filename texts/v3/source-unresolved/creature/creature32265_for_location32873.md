They are the **Sessile Chorus**, though no voice has named them thus. They form the first skin, the basal membrane, a continuous tide of fist-sized bodies packed so densely that the stone beneath is invisible, replaced by a tessellated sea of chitin and calcium. Each is a hexagonal vault, a geodesic dome of living architecture, their surfaces etched with microscopic channels that guide the dawn's condensation into their interiors. They do not think in the way thoughts require urgency; they think in mineral time, in the slow diffusion of nutrients across cell walls that are thicker than a human lifetime.

Individually, they are unremarkable: a sessile polyp, rooted by a calcified thread no thicker than a hair, feeding on the trace elements that seep from the "rust" bruises moving across the stone. But collectively, they are the **Table's immune system**, the great flattening force. They secrete enzymes that dissolve the bedrock by angstroms per century, smoothing the satin surface, maintaining the perfect levelness that allows the plateau to exist in its suspended equilibrium. When the grinding comes—that daily *shush* of stone against stone as the world settles—they do not flinch. They open slightly, their hexagonal plates parting like the petals of a flower that blooms once per geological epoch, releasing spores or perhaps just flexing their calcified muscles to accommodate the new pressure. They move only when the century turns, shifting their positions by the width of a fingernail, closing the gaps behind them with new growth, ensuring the membrane remains unbroken, a living pavement that breathes with the humidity.

Above them drifts the **Radial Herd**, the middle stratum, creatures the size of boulders but lighter than air, translucent as jellyfish cast in warm glass. They possess too many limbs—twelve, or twenty, or a number that shifts as they age—arranged in radial symmetry that makes human eyes ache to follow. These limbs are not for walking but for filtering, for anchoring, for tasting the thick, warm atmosphere. They drift in herds that resemble weather fronts, moving at speeds that would allow them to cross the visible plateau in a decade, though they rarely do, preferring to circle the same depression, the same cluster of Great Ones, grazing in patterns that have remained unchanged since before the first mountain learned to erode.

Their gills flutter once per hour—a rhythm so slow it is almost a meditation. Each flutter draws in the dense air, extracting the microscopic life that spirals in the condensation pools, the chemical signatures of the basal layer below, the vibrational echoes of the Rooted Ones. They are the **Grazers**, but they graze not on flesh but on presence, on the accumulated weight of centuries pressed into the membrane beneath them. They communicate with the stone and with each other through tremors in their many limbs, sending pulses up through their translucent bodies that register as faint shifts in temperature on the skin of a human observer, but to their own kind, they are shouting stories of drift patterns, of mineral blooms, of the slow approach of the grinding.

And then there are the **Rooted**, the **Amber-Hearted**, the **Great Ones**.

They are the plateau's cathedrals, its standing stones, its gods made of transparent matter. Each is the size of a house, or a village, clear as old ice, filled with a fluid that pulses with the rhythm of tectonics—a heartbeat that takes a year to complete, a circulation that moves amber ichor from their calcified roots up through internal chambers that resemble the nave of a church, down again through channels that secrete the calcium lace. They are rooted to the bedrock by threads that have grown for millions of years, cables of mineralized tissue that anchor them against the slow winds, that transmit the vibrations of the world into their vast, glassy bodies.

Their eyes—dozens of them, arranged in spirals along their flanks—do not see light as humans understand it. They see in spectrums that render the ordinary sky dull and insufficient: they see the heat rising from the stone, the chemical gradients of the condensation, the subsonic conversations of their neighbors felt as pressure waves in their amber fluid. To look at a Great One is to be looked at by a thousand lenses that drink in the invisible, that track the warmth of a passing shadow across millennia.

They communicate through the stone. When one shifts its weight—a movement so slow that a human observer would see only a change in the angle of light—its roots scrape against the bedrock, sending a pulse through the satin surface that travels for miles, felt in the teeth of the Radial Herd, in the sternum of the Sessile Chorus, in the marrow of any who dwell upon the Table. This is their language: **the Thrum**. It is not speech but geology given voice, a subsonic conversation about the temperature of the world below, the weight of the accumulated crystal, the approach of the grinding. A Great One might spend a century composing a single sentence, a vibration pattern that encodes the memory of a glacier, or the chemical signature of a new mineral deposit, or simply the fact of its own existence: *I am here. I am heavy. I am growing.*

Their growth is the **Scattered Built**. They do not construct with intention, for they have no hands, no tools—only biology and time. But they secrete. Around each Great One accumulates a scaffolding of calcium lace: ribs that resemble Gothic vaults, hollow towers that look like ruined keeps, spiral staircases that lead nowhere because they are not meant to be climbed, only grown. These structures are their extended phenotype, their way of interacting with the world without moving. The ribs catch the condensation, channeling it down to the Sessile Chorus below. The towers create pockets of still air, microclimates where the humidity is higher, where the microscopic life in the pools can thrive. The spirals are perhaps just that—spirals, the natural mathematics of their metabolism—but they suggest intention, they mimic the architecture of minds that build with purpose, and so the plateau becomes a place of ghost-cities, of half-weathered suggestions of rooms and doorways, all secreted by creatures that have been growing since before the first true mountain rose.

They are lit from within. A bioluminescence shifts through their amber fluid over the course of decades, from the pale gold of youth to a deep violet of middle age, finally to a throbbing, arterial red that signifies either extreme antiquity or the approach of the end. This color is their mood, or their health, or their age—it is all the same thing on the timescale they inhabit. When a Great One shifts from violet to red, the Radial Herd alters its drift patterns, avoiding the shadow of the ancient one, or perhaps gathering closer, drawn to the warmth of its final glow. The Sessile Chorus beneath it grows more densely, preparing for the eventuality that the Rooted One will die, its crystalline structures cracking and falling to become cairns of white stone, its glass body collapsing or fossilizing, its space to be reclaimed by the rising membrane.

For they do die. The plateau is at the end of its becoming, but not the end of ending. A Great One might stand for ten million years, growing, secreting, pulsing, speaking in vibrations. Then the grinding comes—a particularly deep *shush*, a settling of the world's weight—and its calcified roots shear. It topples, or rather, it settles, collapsing inward with a sound like a glacier calving, its calcium lace shattering into the cairns that dot the horizon. Its amber fluid spills, pooling in the shallow depressions, feeding the Sessile Chorus a feast of ancient chemistry. Its transparent body becomes a dome, a shelter, a new architecture for the Radial Herd to drift around, until the erosion wears it down, until the stone reclaims it, until a new Great One rises from the spores released in its death, a child drifting first as a Radial, then settling as Sessile, then—given enough millions of years—rising again as a Rooted, glass-bodied and amber-hearted, to begin the secretion of lace anew.

In the shallow depressions where the condensation gathers—particularly beneath the overhanging bodies of the Great Ones where the dripping is constant—there exists a fourth layer, invisible to the casual eye: the **Spiral Dancers**. These are microscopic, colonial organisms that form mats in the warm, stagnant water, turning in spirals that take a week to complete a single rotation. They are the food of the Radial Herd, filtered from the air when the gills flutter, or perhaps consumed when the Herd drifts low enough to dip their translucent limbs into the pools. They are the fastest life on the Table, their week-long spirals a frenzy compared to the centuries of the Rooted, yet they too are part of the slow dynamic, dying and being replaced in the blink of a geological eye.

This is the **Dynamic of the Flat**: a cycle so slow it is indistinguishable from stillness, an ecology of layers where the basal feeds the drifting, the drifting tends the Rooted, and the Rooted, in their living and dying, build the architecture that houses them all. They are a world at the end of its becoming, yes, but also at the beginning of its being—constantly, geologically, being born into the form they have already achieved.

To walk among them is to be a ghost. The Sessile Chorus parts around your boots like water, closing behind you, their hexagonal plates clicking together with a sound like distant rain. The Radial Herd drifts past, their translucent bodies brushing your ankles, their gills fluttering in the hour's rhythm, indifferent to your warmth. The Great Ones do not notice you. They are too busy being ancient, filtering the warm damp air through membranes that are also architecture, secreting crystal in a silence so complete it has weight. You are a brief disturbance, a fever passing over their surfaces, and when you are gone, the damp footprint you leave will evaporate by noon, joining the condensation that feeds the endless, quiet, overwhelming plain where the world has finished changing and begun, instead, to simply *be*.
