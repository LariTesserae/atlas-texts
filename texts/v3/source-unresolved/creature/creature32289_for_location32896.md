The first inhabitants you meet are the ones who do not move: the **Thermoliths**. They carpet the continent-sized floor in a high-saturation reef of mineral life, their bodies warm to the touch, faintly phosphorescent with a glow the color of cooling embers and old bruises. They are not plants, though they occupy the niche of primary producers; they are geochemical engines given sessile form, drawing energy directly from the radiant heat of the planet’s unfinished cooling. Their surfaces are obsidian-smooth, chitinous, geometrically impossible—angles that should not exist in Euclidean stone yet do, because the physics here are *almost* right, not quite.

They communicate. In the profound darkness between their crystalline branches, light pulses in slow waves, a language of heat and photon emitted into the dry dark. When one Thermolith "speaks," its bioluminescence ripples outward in rust-colored tides, traveling through the dense, copper-tasting air until it reaches another, and the two synchronize, their glows beating in duet on a timescale of hours. This is their society: a conversation that spans the curving horizon, a network of slow thought rooted in the bedrock. They do not grow anymore—the geological *rigor mortis* has set in, a snapshot of formation frozen in the exact moment of its birth—but they remember. They hold the memory of their violent precipitation from the mantle in the lattice of their silicate matrices, glowing it out into the dark for others to read, a broadcast of being that requires no water, only heat and the slow accumulation of metallic ore.

Between these mineral forests drift the **mobile inhabitants**: the Drifters. They are the fauna proper, abundant and warm-blooded with internal fires rather than water. Imagine a centipede articulated not with flesh but with ceramic plates, overlapping scales of compressed mineral that click against each other in the thick atmosphere. They have too many joints, and those joints bend in directions that Euclidean geometry denies—sideways, backward, into angles that suggest they navigate a space with more dimensions than the one you occupy. They are buoyant in the gas saturated with sulfur vapor and metallic particulates, drifting like jellyfish through water, but the medium is air, and they are perfectly, absolutely dry. No moisture clings to their shells; they have never known H₂O as a solvent, only as a stranger concept they do not possess.

The Drifters do not age. They circulate through the same paths, generation after generation, in a perfect, frozen equilibrium—a dynamic of 0.3, never changing. They feed on the Thermoliths—not by consuming them, exactly, but by pressing against their warm surfaces and drawing off metallic exudations, the metabolic byproducts of the crystalline giants. Or perhaps they feed on each other in a closed loop of chemosynthesis that requires no sun, only heat and mineral catalysts drawn directly from the sharp, fresh rock. They leave no tracks, for the ground is too new, too *permanent* to record an impression; a footprint would be an insult to the stasis of the landscape, a violence against the snapshot.

And then there are the **Architects**.

The cairns of stacked stone, the alignments of sharp rocks pointing toward the dark ceiling, the small shelters built from fitted plates of shale—these are the work of the Drifters, or so it seems, though their technology is primitive, barely registering on any scale that measures intention. Yet the precision is uncanny. The stones are unweathered, freshly placed, yet they have stood there since before time began to move properly, arranged in patterns that suggest ritual or navigation. The Drifters place these markers to measure the slow tides of invisible gas that move through the cavern, thermal updrafts that rise in silent columns. They build shelters not against weather—there is no rain, no wind in the conventional sense—but against the psychological weight of the vastness, small assertions of *here* and *I* in a continent-sized hollow where the horizon curves upward into impossibility.

They leave tools half-buried in the scree: stone scrapers, awls of compressed mineral, cords of twisted fiber that do not rot because there is no moisture to rot them. The tools wait for hands that will never come to pick them up, because the Drifters themselves move in loops so perfect, so achronological, that they never quite arrive at the moment of use. They are builders who build for a future that does not exist, or for a past that never happened, arranging the sharp teeth of the buried god into geometry.

But there is another possibility: that the Architects are the Thermoliths themselves, in a phase of existence that has since fossilized into stillness. Perhaps, in the moment of their formation, before the stasis set in, they extended crystalline fingers into the dense air, stacking and aligning, building cairns as prayers to the absolute dark above. Now those structures remain, fresh and sharp and precise, while the Thermoliths have withdrawn into their slow, glowing contemplation, their communication reduced to pulses of light in the saturated silence.

Or perhaps the Architects are the Darkness itself—the impenetrable black that is the ceiling, a pressure, a weight, a texture like heat-haze but cold to the retina. They descend only as absence, leaving behind these stone arrangements as the only evidence that something intelligent once looked up from the floor and tried to touch the sky, even if that sky is merely the inside of a hollow world, a sphere the size of a planet.

In the end, the inhabitants are a triad of consciousness: the **Rooted** (Thermoliths), who think in light and heat and precipitate their thoughts into mineral form; the **Moving** (Drifters), who circulate in endless, ageless paths through the ceramic air, warm and jointed and bending wrong; and the **Remembered** (the structures), which hold the intention of builders who may be the same as the others, or may be ghosts of a creation event that happened yesterday and will last forever.

They are crowded—1.6 presences per volume, a high-saturation of life that has no use for photosynthesis, for chlorophyll, for the green tyranny of plants. They are the children of a chemistry that never learned to use water, existing in a metastable perfection where entropy does not increase, where the sharp basaltic needles do not dull, where the flake broken from the cliff hangs suspended, stopped, defying gravity’s slow insistence.

They are all warm, radiating heat that makes the dense air shimmer around them like a mirage. They are stable, complete, a closed system that has reached its final temperature. And they are waiting—not for time to pass, for it does not, but for something to change, for the stasis to break, for the hand that will never come to finally pick up the tool. In the silence, heavy and saturated and full, you can almost hear them breathing, though they have no lungs, only the slow exhalation of heat and rust-colored light into the dark, a billion dry organisms metabolizing the planet’s fever, dreaming in crystal, drifting through the mineral air of a world that is finished being born and has not yet begun to die.
