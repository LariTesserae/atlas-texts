The inhabitants are not made of cells, nor do they breathe air. They exist in the intervals—the spaces where water meets mineral, where shadow nearly forms but cannot hold, where time accumulates like sediment.

They are the **Lithovores**, or perhaps better, the **Slow Ones**. They dwell in the micro-channels between the packed stones, in the finger-width grooves that guide the persistent water downward. Each is a consciousness of mineral patience, having achieved a form of awareness through sheer duration. They feel the stone tick as it cools in the high-altitude dark; they taste the faint metallic tang of the condensation that weeps from the rock matrix. A single Lithovore might spend three hundred years feeling the gradual settling of one particular block, sensing the pressure of its neighbors as a kind of embrace, a constant, crushing intimacy. They do not move in the way of animals, but they *migrate*—shifting with the slow compaction of the structure, drifting downward as the mountain of stone compresses itself under its own impossible weight. They are the ones who remember the shape of the hands that placed them, though those hands are dust now, or perhaps they have become the copper that has not yet greened, the flint still sharp in forgotten crevices.

But the Lithovores are not alone. They share this architecture with the **Aqueans**—not fish, not microbes, but the water itself given distributed intention. Each droplet is a citizen, a traveler moving through the vertical city with glacial deliberation. An Aquean might spend forty days pooling in a shallow basin, feeling the insistent light press against its surface, evaporating slowly until it becomes mist, only to condense again on a higher wall, beginning the descent anew. They are a people of cycles, of memory carried in suspension. They know the geometry of every channel because they have worn some of them, over centuries, into smoothness. They communicate not through sound but through pressure, through the subtle chemistry of what they dissolve and what they leave behind—a language of mineral hunger and satisfaction spoken in the slow *plink* of one drop joining another.

There are also the **Builders Who Remained**. They are not ghosts in the human sense, but presences compressed into the tessellation itself. Perhaps they were the last of their kind, and rather than leave, they allowed themselves to be absorbed—their bodies becoming the mortar of intention, their nervous systems becoming the geometry of the channels. They exist now as the *almost* pattern, the irregularity that suggests a mind working through stone. When you stand in the bright silence, you are standing within their extended body. They are the ones who feel the water as blood, who experience the light as a touch against skin. They are still building, but each gesture takes a geological age: the placement of a single stone might be the work of ten generations of their slowed consciousness, passed down like a craft, or inherited like a genetic memory.

Together, these three—the stone-dwellers, the water-travelers, and the absorbed architects—form a single organism that the place itself sustains. The architecture is their lung, breathing moisture in and out. The light is their nervous system, too bright to look at directly, carrying sensation across every surface. They do not age as we do; they *accrete*. They are the erosion in its infancy, the fresh flakes of stone still littering the channels as new skin cells, the copper that has not yet oxidized as youth, as potential.

They are waiting. Not for anything to happen, but for the slow transformation to complete itself—for the water to wear the channels smooth, for the stone to settle into its final compression, for the light to bleach the last of the shadow from the deepest cistern. They are the custodians of a machine that runs on centuries, inhabitants of a world where a heartbeat is the filling of a basin, where a thought is the evaporation of a single droplet, and where to live is to persist, to sweat, to hold the water against gravity with the patience of mountains, forever young, forever waiting to become something else.
