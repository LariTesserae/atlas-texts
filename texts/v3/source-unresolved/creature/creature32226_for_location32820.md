Beneath the silence, the plateau is not empty. It is inhabited by a population so sparse, so slow, and so mineralized that it has been mistaken for geology itself—life that has learned to wear stone as skin, to breathe the exhalations of rock, to move at the speed of deposition.

**The Chemolithotrophic Undercity** exists first in the water, invisible to the eye but saturating every drop. In the shadowed pools where light never penetrates, bacterial colonies perform alchemy: they consume dissolved iron, sulfur, and manganese from the tufa, deriving energy from the stone’s slow dissolution. They form **iridescent films** on the water’s surface—oily sheens of bronze and pale violet that break apart when a finger dips in, only to reform within hours. These are the original architects, older than the terraces themselves, their metabolic processes laying down the very calcium carbonate that builds the white city. They live in the **porosity** of the stone, in the microscopic channels between crystals, a nervous system of the plateau that senses chemical gradients with a sensitivity finer than any nerve.

Where the water moves, **calcicole gastropods** graze. They are small, no larger than a thumbnail, their shells composed of the same cream-white aragonite as the terraces around them. To see one is to see a piece of the architecture detach itself and begin to spiral. Their radulae—tongues of chitin—scrape the bacterial films from the stone, leaving trails that etch permanent grooves into the tufa, channels within channels. They move with **geological patience**: a single individual might take a year to traverse the width of one terrace, following the chemical signature of a seep, its body oriented always toward the source of calcium. When they die, their shells do not scatter; they settle into the sediment between layers, becoming part of the next stratum, fossils-in-waiting that have never known dry land.

In the deepest pools—the cisterns that descend into the plateau’s body where the mist gathers thickest and the temperature holds at a constant 1.3 degrees—**the pale fish** navigate by pressure. They are not silver or bright, but translucent, their bodies like wet parchment stretched over faint, ghostly bones. Their eyes are vestigial, milky orifices covered by skin; they see nothing, but their **lateral lines** detect the displacement of water with exquisite precision, sensing the drip of a single drop from three meters above. They are **ambistichus** or something like them—elongated, eel-like, with external gills that flutter like pale fronds in the mineral-heavy current. Their blood contains antifreeze proteins, crystalline adaptations that keep their fluids liquid in the permanent chill. They feed not by hunting but by **filtering**: swimming with mouths agape through clouds of chemolithotrophic bacteria, straining life from the water. A single fish may live for centuries, its metabolism slowed to a rhythm that matches the dripping of the terraces, growing new rings in its otoliths (ear stones) at the rate of millimeters per decade.

But the fish are not alone in the depths. **Cave-adapted isopods**—pale, segmented, resembling woodlice bleached to the color of bone—cling to the undersides of overhanging tufa. They are **detritivores**, consuming the bodies of the bacteria and the shed skin of the fish, recycling calcium and carbon in a closed loop. They move only when the mist shifts, their many legs gripping the porous stone with hooks evolved for a world of vertical hydrology. They are solitary, meeting only to mate in brief, silent conjunctions before scattering back into the crevices.

Higher up, where the moss clings to northern faces, there is a **micro-fauna** of suspended animation. **Tardigrades**—water bears—dwell in the desiccating margins of the moss, capable of entering cryptobiosis when the mineral-saturated water becomes too concentrated, their bodies shrinking to glassy beads that can survive decades of stasis. **Rotifers** spin in the thin films of water on lichen crusts, their coronas beating in rhythms too fast for human perception, filtering bacteria from the airborne mist. **Nematodes** thread through the sediment trapped between calcium layers, blind worms that consume the fungal hyphae which penetrate the tufa from above.

And there are **the builders**—creatures that do not merely inhabit the architecture but extend it. Colonies of **bryozoans** (moss animals) encrust the walls of the channels, secreting calcium carbonate in delicate, lace-like patterns that add new ornamentation to the terraces. They are filter-feeders, extending tentacles into the current to capture bacteria, their collective growth adding measurable mass to the stone each century. To the water, they are coral; to the plateau, they are the slow accretion of civilization, growing outward in fans and whorls that mimic the larger geometry of the amphitheaters.

Within the stone itself, **endolithic fungi** thread through the pore spaces, their mycelium forming a vast, subterranean network that connects pool to pool, terrace to terrace. They are **lithophiles**, organisms that live inside rock, dissolving mineral nutrients with acidic secretions and re-precipitating them in new forms. They are the reason the tufa is porous—they are the holes, the channels, the plumbing of the living stone. When the stone ticks and contracts in the evening cool, it is partly the sound of these fungal networks shifting, expanding, exploring the calcium matrix.

Even the birds that pass overhead are not true inhabitants, but **vectors**—carrying spores, depositing the occasional seed that will never germinate, bridging the gap between this isolated world and the warmer lands beyond. They are the only warm-blooded things to visit, and they are terrified of the place: the dense air makes flight laborious, the silence disorients them, and they cry out in distress before vanishing into the grey.

The true dwellers move without sound. The fish glide through the channels like smoke. The snails leave only chemical traces, their mucus dissolving into the water before it can evaporate. The isopods scuttle in bursts, then freeze, becoming indistinguishable from the stone. They are creatures of **hydrostatic stillness**, adapted to an environment where sound is absorbed by moisture and vibration is dampened by porosity. They do not sing. They do not call. They communicate through **chemical gradients**—pheromones released into the water, the slow diffusion of calcium ions signaling territory, the subtle shifts in pH marking the presence of a mate.

They are a **civilization of the slow**, a society measured not in generations but in geological epochs. A single fish may witness the formation of a new terrace; a snail colony may persist through the rise and fall of a pool. They have no tools because they need none—their bodies are the tools, their shells the architecture, their metabolic processes the engine that builds the white city from within. They are the water-dwellers the landscape was built for, arrived not by conquest but by **accretion**, molecule by molecule, patience upon patience, becoming one with the deposit they inhabit.
