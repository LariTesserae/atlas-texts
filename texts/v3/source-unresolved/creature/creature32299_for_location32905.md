The Giants are the first inhabitants you encounter, though they do not turn to look at you. They stand twenty to thirty meters tall in the thin, bright air, spaced with brutal regularity across the valley—hundreds of meters apart, sparse as fence posts in an empty field. They are not trees but *architectures of plant*, resembling clubmosses the size of redwoods or stromatolitic structures grown from stacked layers of vegetal secretion. Their surfaces are honeycombed, weeping condensation in the cool eight-degree chill, a constant drip that darkens the peat at their bases. They are hollow, and the wind rushing across the high plateau finds their internal chambers and turns them into organ pipes, moaning through the resonance in a bass note that vibrates in your sternum—a physical weight of sound that makes conversation impossible. They photosynthesize furiously in the surgical light, the 2.9 brightness that bleaches the world to near-monochrome, converting that absolute illumination into slow growth: millimeters per year, a patience that reads as stillness. Each one is a vertical island, a solitary being in a sea of low green, standing alone in the glare that casts shadows black, sharp, and immediate.

Beneath them, and between them, lives the Mat. This is not soil but *vegetal substrate*—a continuous, spongy carpet of extremophile growth that has colonized the raw obsidian and pumice within decades of their cooling. Pale lichens, silvery mosses, and dense colonies of horsetail-like fronds form a thick, springy layer that smells of green sap and wet stone. It is a collective population, ten thousand generations compressed into peat that is still forming, still being constructed by the slow accumulation of organic matter. The bog breathes. It is the skin of the world, sealing over wounds when the ground shifts, knitting new growth over fissures that open with a hiss of steam. The water table sits at its surface; every step sinks into saturation, into the compressed bodies of the dead that support the living. It is a horizontal consciousness, vast and slow, feeding on the perched water, the sulfurous breath of the fumaroles, the intense light that strikes unfiltered from the thin sky.

Scattered through this vegetal expanse, sparse as the Giants themselves, are the human inhabitants. They are few—a few structures per square kilometer, concrete pump houses the size of garden sheds painted in faded industrial yellow, their exhausts venting steam into the thin air. They live in the shadow of the towers, in the roar of the wind and the machinery, adapted to the glare and the pressure against the eardrums. They operate the drilling rigs whose bits turn with agonizing slowness, boring into the emerging geology to tap geothermal heat or extract minerals that have not yet fully formed. They move along metal pipelines elevated on stilts to avoid the water table, carrying fluids from the depths of the young crust. They wear protection against the light, perhaps, or have grown accustomed to it; they endure the deafening, monolithic roar that never ceases, a duet of plants and machines in which the human voice is lost. They are transient caretakers, present but not numerous, maintaining the slow industry that moves with the patience of geological time.

And the machines themselves are inhabitants—massive, sluggish residents that generate the constant, overwhelming drone. The pumps thrum. The drills grind. The pneumatic pistons rise and fall with the patience of centuries, extracting slowly from the patient rock. They are anchored to the peat, part of the landscape now, as much residents as the Giants or the Mat. They form a duet with the vegetation: the plants grow slowly, adding millimeters per year; the machines extract slowly, tapping the heat and minerals of the young earth. Together they construct the world, while the Giants sing their bass note into the wind, and the Mat heals the wounds of the emerging geology, and the humans move between them, silent in the roar, under the surgical light of a sky that offers no mercy.
