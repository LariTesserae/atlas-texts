The inhabitants are the lithophytes, though to call them inhabitants is to compress a slowness that resists the word. They do not move across the peat; they accrete upon it. Each column stands eight meters high, a trunk of crystalline bark layered like geological time itself—ring upon ring of precipitated mineral, each layer a year’s patience. They are sparse, spaced across the black water and quaking peat at a density of 0.6, vast distances of silence between them, yet they form a congregation, a parliament of stone that has learned to breathe.

They are green. Impossibly, defiantly green, photosynthesizing in the diffuse, saturated light that filters down through the mist. Their “leaves” are mineral plates—fronds that unfurl in spirals at their crowns, each new spiral taking a month to complete its unfurling, each new ring of crystal a year to add itself to the trunk. They are life that has learned to precipitate itself from the chemistry of the basin, or stone that has learned to hunger. They drink not from the pools around their bases, but upward, defying gravity, drawing thermal water through capillary channels in their crystalline matrices, water that flows uphill along their trunks in thin, defiant rivulets toward the spiraling crowns.

They are listeners. Every frond, every column, leans at exactly fifteen degrees toward the center of the basin. This is not tropism toward light—the light here is diffuse, omnidirectional—but an orientation toward sound, or something like it. Beneath their roots, beneath the millennia of peat, the geothermal vents hum at a subsonic frequency, 0.2 of sound, vibrating through the ground, through the water, through the mineral bones of the lithophytes themselves. They are attuned to this vibration, bodies tilted toward its source as if eavesdropping on the earth’s slow heartbeat. The mist hangs in geometric columns around them, structured by their presence, as if the air has learned to crystallize in sympathy with their forms, breath held in lattice patterns.

There are no animals among them. Not a mosquito, not a bird, nothing that burrows or crawls or sings. This absence is not an emptiness but a presence, a negative space that makes the lushness of the lithophytes feel invasive, hungry, or perhaps sacred. They are the only permitted life in this room of the earth. The silence between them is total, broken only by the plink of water dripping from crystal frond to peat pool, and the slow, subsonic creak of mineral growth as thermal expansion shifts them in their beds.

Scattered around them, half-sunk in the acidic peat, are the tools of a visitation—flakes of obsidian, cores of worked flint, a spiral of seven standing stones now subsiding into the bog. These are the remnants of hands that once came to quarry the growing stone, to chip away at the lithophytes for their crystalline hearts, perhaps to make tools of their own, perhaps to offer them to the humming dark below. The lithophytes do not remember these hands individually, but they have absorbed them. The tools are weathering, patinating, sinking deeper each decade, becoming almost natural, their conchoidal fractures softening into the curves of erosion. The lithophytes grow around them, over them, through them, accreting the past into their mineral bodies.

The pools between the columns are mirrors too perfect, reflecting the lithophytes without distortion, but also reflecting something else—a memory. The water remembers the shape of the sky before the basin was dug, before the depression held its breath. The lithophytes stand in this memory, drinking the thermal dark, growing their slow rings, leaning toward the center where the peat is deepest and the hum is loudest. They exist at 0.6 dynamic speed: a gesture takes a month, a thought takes a century. They are ancient, established, in balance with the chemistry of the place. They are the basin’s true residents—patient, crystalline, listening, waiting for the slow breath of decades to turn again.
