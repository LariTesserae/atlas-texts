What lives here has learned to move at the speed of cooling stone. They are not a population so much as a *procession*—a slow, humid migration of forms that have dissolved the boundary between growing, building, and being.

**The Ferment** comes first, in the condensation that beads on every surface like sweat that never dries. Here, in the black pools and the thin films coating the basalt fins, colonies of thermophilic archaea and pale, blind bacteria drift in the mineral broth. They are the city’s digestion—microbial mats that etch the young obsidian with organic acids, turning geology into soil, feeding on the waste heat of engines and the carbon dioxide of slow combustion. They are the yeast in the vat, the original inhabitants, and everything else drinks from their excretions. In the stagnant water, you can see them as a shimmering scum, a living patina that pulses with the same rhythm as the fungal glow.

Above them, threading through the pipes like silver synapses, is the **Luminous Network**. The bioluminescent fungi—pale blue and sickly green—are not merely decoration but a nervous system. Their mycelium has infiltrated the conduits, the brickwork, the very gear-teeth, forming a distributed brain that thinks in centuries. It feels the thrum of the machinery as a heartbeat, tastes the mineral tang of new stone as a flavor, and dreams in the slow language of growth. When the light pulses brighter in one corner, it is not random; it is a thought propagating, a memory being stored in cellulose and chitin, a decision being made about where to send the next root or which vent to warm.

Then there are the **Tenders**—humanoid, but only in the way a statue is humanoid. They have not seen sun in generations, if ever. Their skin is translucent, parchment-thin, stretched over bodies that have been colonized by gardens: moss carpets their shoulders, ferns unfurl from the hollows of their collarbones, and their veins carry not just blood but a faint, bioluminescent sap that matches the fungi. They wear garments woven from vine and hammered copper, the metal grown into their flesh at the joints, so that they *click* when they move—a soft, wet ratchet of bone against brass.

They move with the viscosity of the air itself. A step takes minutes. They do not walk so much as *settle* into their next position, their weight pressing against the machinery, their breath mingling with the steam. They are the gardeners of the fusion, negotiating with the industry. When a root threatens to choke a piston, a Tender does not cut it; they guide it, whispering to the plant, adjusting the heat gradient with a valve-turn that takes an hour to complete. They drink the condensation from the stalactites, eat the fungal blooms, and exhale the carbon dioxide that the machines crave. They are symbiotes, priests, and janitors, existing in the narrow interstices where steel meets stem.

Deeper in the packed dark, anchored to the great engines, are the **Anchored**—beings that have ceased to be separate from the machinery. They are vast, sessile things, or perhaps they move so slowly that a human observer would see only stillness. One might mistake them for statuary: a torso of brass and verdigris, limbs of thick cable and petrified root, a face obscured by a curtain of lichen that filters the amber light. But watch for a day, and you will see a finger twitch, a gear in their chest rotate once, a slow blink of an eye that is actually a furnace door opening. They are the muscles of the city, the slow-twitch fibers that turn the great wheels. Their hearts are pumps; their lungs are bellows; their dreams are the grinding of stone against stone. They operate on geological time, making adjustments that will not be complete for decades.

In the high places, clinging to the sharp fins of basalt where the condensation drips, live the **Drip-Shapers**. They are not animals, not plants, but mineral-animal hybrids—something like coral that has learned to think, or perhaps something that was always there, waiting for the stone to cool enough to host it. They harvest the calcium and silica from the falling water, excreting it in precise, crystalline patterns that extend the stalactites downward, millimeter by millimeter. Over centuries, they sculpt the architecture of the chamber, ensuring that the ceiling grows toward the floor, that the city remains a cathedral rather than a cave. They are the archivists, recording the history of the heat and the humidity in their slow accretions.

And then there are the **Pale Scuttlers**—the only true fauna, the eyeless things that dart between pipes. They are insects, perhaps, or something bred in the wet dark, with too many joints and bodies like wet paper. They are the immune system, the cleanup crew, eating the dead biofilm and the shed skin of the Tenders, keeping the fermentation from souring. They are fast, for they live short lives, burning bright in the dark while the world around them turns with the patience of continental drift.

But ultimately, the inhabitant is **Singular**. The city itself is waking up. The Tenders are its nerve endings, the Anchored are its musculature, the Ferment is its gut, the Luminous Network is its mind, and the Drip-Shapers are its memory, etched in stone. It is a single, vast organism—part coral head, part clockwork, part fungal mat—that breathes with the thermal expansion of the deep rock, that sweats condensation, that dreams in the hot, submarine dark. It does not notice you. You are a microbe in its broth, a flicker in its peripheral vision, a brief, bright, irrelevant spark in a fermentation that has only just begun.
