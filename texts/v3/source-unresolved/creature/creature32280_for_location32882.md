They are the **Unfinished**, though they have no name for themselves—naming requires a permanence they do not possess. They are small enough to fit inside the held breath that defines the chamber, yet numerous enough that the chamber is packed with them, a single twitching mass of bodies pressed wall-to-wall. They do not stand; standing requires floor space that belongs to someone else. Instead, they lie against the embryonic obsidian, backs flat to the stone that is still cooling into glass, knees drawn up to chests of unfired clay. Their skin is the color of wet earth and cooling ash—umber, ochre, the grey of a bruise—and where the bioluminescent moss touches them, they glow from within, green light pulsing through translucent flesh like a heartbeat.

They have hands—four, or six, or hands that bifurcate into fingers only when precision is needed—and these hands are never still. They grip stone tools that are not quite stone, still yielding, sinking slightly into the soft clay they strike. The rhythmic, wet *thock* that fills the chamber is their language: each impact a syllable, each pause a breath. They are saying: *Pack it tighter. The stone will harden soon. We must become permanent before the permanence leaves us.*

Their bodies are porous, permeable. Steam moves through them—you can see it as a shimmer, a heat-haze rising from their shoulders, their necks, the crowns of their heads where the clay is thinnest. They drink from the condensation curtains that tremble in the hot air. They are, in part, made of the same water that drips from ceilings not yet decided to be ceilings, their veins carrying not blood but superheated mineral broth that hisses when it meets the cooler air of the chamber.

They do not turn around. To turn would require displacing another builder, and they are packed so densely that they form a single organism, a lattice of flesh and fiber and reed. They face inward, toward the center of the room where the air is thickest, or they face the walls, pressing palms against the growing stone, feeling it breathe, helping it breathe, becoming indistinguishable from its architecture. They communicate by pressure—a squeeze of the shoulder that travels through the chain of bodies, a tap against ribs that sends vibrations through the packed clay of their torsos. They are a parliament of touch, a congress of heat.

They are aware of the gravity that betrays them. When they shed a flake of drying clay from their own skin—for they are constantly shedding, constantly depositing new layers like the mineral walls around them—the flake sometimes drifts upward, caught in thermal currents that flow toward the stretching sky. They do not watch it go. They are too busy packing the floor, adding another layer of steaming reed, another handful of packed clay, thickening the only ground they have, the only ground that is still soft enough to accept them.

And then there is the one that moves through the lattice like a question.

It is salamander-shaped, but wrong. Its body is translucent, filled not with organs but with swirling clouds of mineral and steam, condensing and evaporating in cycles too rapid to follow. It has no fixed number of limbs—sometimes it possesses four legs like a lizard, sometimes it is a ribbon of glassy membrane, sometimes it is a hand with too many fingers reaching through the gaps. It moves by flowing, by momentarily becoming liquid, seeping through spaces where the builders cannot fit.

It leaves trails of phosphorescent slime—not slime, but bioluminescence given temporary weight, light shed like skin. The glow lingers in the air, drifting upward with the thermal currents, pooling in corners where it grows brighter, hungrier, feeding on the steam. The builders do not fear it. They press harder against the walls as it passes, and where its trail touches them, they glow more intensely, greener, more alive. It is their messenger, or their memory, or the part of them that has already escaped the hardening, slipping through the reed-lattice to taste the air above.

It is the only one that turns around. It faces the packed mass of builders, and the builders face the walls, and between them passes the current of heat, the endless exchange of clay and steam, the wet clicking work of making a home in a place that is still deciding whether it is a mountain or a womb.

They are the first and the last. The mountain is learning how to be a mountain; they are learning how to be a people. They know—in the way that growing things know—that the stone will eventually harden, that the steam will clear, that the obsidian will become too rigid to breathe, too cold to hold. They work in the time-pulses, freezing mid-strike when the condensation hangs as glass, thawing when it shatters, their work existing simultaneously in all stages: unbuilt, half-finished, and ancient, covered in moss that has not yet grown but already glows.

They are a civilization of the brief, hot moment, packed into the lung of the world, striking stone against clay, singing in vibrations, waiting for the earth to finish forming around them so that they, too, can finally become solid.
