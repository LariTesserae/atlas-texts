They are the Tenders, though they do not tend in the way a gardener tends—they maintain a machine that does not break, in a world that does not weather. They move through the steam in coveralls the color of rust and wet silt, the fabric fused to the skin beneath by the constant press of heat and moisture, the seams grown into the body like scars that never formed. Their faces are obscured by respirators that filter the iron-thick air, the filters never clogging because the particulate is held in perfect suspension, neither settling nor clearing.

They do not age. A Tender who has walked the gantries for what would be decades elsewhere shows no lines, no sag, no accumulation of memory in the form of scar tissue or sun damage. Their cells divide and die in exact proportion, a biological metronome matching the mechanical one. They have no childhood, no elders, no infants—only the perpetual present of the inspection route, the gauge check, the valve turn. They eat from the same algae farms that coat the concrete plinths, drink water that tastes of warm metal and silt, and sleep in hammocks that sway to the rhythm of the generators, waking to the same amber gloom, the same enveloping roar, the same level of water in the streets outside.

Their language has atrophied into vibration. They speak by tapping code against the pipes—three knocks for pressure, five for temperature—and the pipes carry their voices as a low thrum that merges with the drone of the hydraulics. When they must use words, their voices are hoarse, permanently saturated, speaking in loops: safety protocols repeated verbatim, the same jokes landing with the same flat affect, warnings about the water level that never changes, that never will change.

But they are not alone. The Works breathes with other lives, each locked into the same circular time.

In the pools that never rise or fall dwell the **Pool-Born**—frogs the size of a human fist, their skin iridescent with green-black algae, their throats pulsing in a chorus that harmonizes with the machinery. They do not metamorphose from tadpoles; they are born fully formed, or perhaps they bud from the silt itself, emerging already singing. Their population is a constant number, each death matched by a birth in the same moment, a transaction written in the steam, their croaks marking the hours that do not pass.

Above them, the **Winged Choir**—mosquitoes and midges in clouds so dense they obscure the sodium lamps, their wings beating at a frequency that adds a high, keening harmony to the industrial bass. They do not bite the Tenders; there is a truce, or perhaps the Tenders' blood is too warm, too saturated with the chemistry of the Works to interest them. The insects live and die in swarms that pulse with the heat, their life cycles compressed and repeated in the eternal twilight, never accumulating, never depleting.

On the sheet metal and in the hollows of the foundations scurry the **Gnawers**—rats that have grown sleek and numerous, their fur matted with the oily film of the condensation, their eyes reflecting the dim light like drops of black water. They are the cleaners, the recyclers, consuming the detritus of the Tenders and the decaying matter that somehow never accumulates in the hollows. They breed in the dark spaces between the pipes, their litters born and dying in the same breath, maintaining their numbers at a precise, teeming equilibrium. They watch the Tenders with unblinking eyes, recognizing them as part of the furniture, part of the landscape, another species in the heat.

And deepest in the system, in the pipes where the water runs hottest, there are the **Pressure-Born**—if they can be called alive. Colonies of thermophilic bacteria, perhaps, or something stranger: mineral formations that mimic life, crystalline structures that grow and slough off in cycles that match the machine's pulse. They are the immune system of the Works, the flora that never gets the chance to become forest, held in check by the very heat that sustains them.

But the true inhabitant, the one that encompasses all others, is **the Works itself**. The buildings are its ribs, the gantries its bones, the steam its breath, the constant roar its heartbeat. The Tenders are its antibodies, moving through its veins to check for blockages that never form. The rats are its gut flora. The frogs are its song. The Winged Choir is its cilia, filtering the air. It is a body without a single brain, or perhaps the brain is distributed—every gauge a neuron, every valve a synapse, every Tender a thought that repeats forever in the amber dark.

Here, to live is to be part of the loop. There are no newcomers, only iterations of those who have always been here. No departures, only the endless, circular turning of bodies and machines, locked in the wet, heavy, eternal now, where the only evolution is the perfect, unchanging balance between birth and death, between the creation of steam and its dissipation, between the Tender's hand on the valve and the valve's eternal, unchanging resistance.
