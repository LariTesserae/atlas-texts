The permanent residents are the salamanders—pale, eyeless, and patient. They are the inheritors of the doorways, the curators of the window ledges. One drifts through the central plaza, its alabaster body undulating with a rhythm slower than the settling silt. It is perhaps thirty centimeters long, its skin like wet porcelain, translucent enough to show the dark drift of its organs beneath. External gills frill from its neck, pale and feathery, waving in time with the kelp columns that grow from the rooftops. It does not hunt here; it gardens. With a snout like a blunt trowel, it pushes pale silt from the threshold of a two-story house, methodically, grain by grain, restoring the entrance to the dignity of openness. Later, it will ascend to a ledge encrusted with gold algae and graze, nibbling the growth with small, precise bites, keeping the aperture clear so that the green light may enter the interior chambers—not for its own sake, for it has no eyes, only the ability to sense pressure gradients and chemical traces, but for the kelp that grows within, for the minnows that shelter in the rafters, for the slow photosynthesis of minerals that is turning the black oak beams to stone.

These are the Keepers. They have no memory of the drowning, only the architecture. They know the forty structures as a human knows the rooms of a house: by touch, by the taste of the water in each street (each canal carries a different signature of dissolved mineral), by the resonance of the hum that vibrates through the dry-stone masonry. They are long-lived—perhaps centuries—matching the glacial time of the place. A single salamander may witness the silicification of a beam from timber to agate, watching the wood grain fill with quartz over decades that are mere moments in the cold, heavy water. They meet in the plaza, not in councils but in congregations, drifting in the black velvet of the deep silt, communicating through pulses of scent and the subtle shift of bioelectric fields that ripple through the stratified layers. They are the priests of the submerged liturgy, and their ritual is maintenance.

Then there are the others. The minnows—*Leuciscus* or their cold-adapted kin—are the village’s nervous system, the silver weather that moves through the streets. They do not live in one house; they flow through all of them, parting around the pillars of the signal tower, darting through the hollowed timber frames where the wood has become half-organic, half-geologic. They are transients in a village of permanence, yet they are essential. They feed on the plankton that drift in the milky turbidity of the water, and in turn, they are food for the newts. They move in clouds that shift and reform, a liquid intelligence that responds to the lenses of temperature and salinity, riding the invisible currents that pass through the stone streets. When a diver disturbs them, they scatter in silver explosions, but they always return, for they have no shore to flee to, only the architecture.

The giant water beetles are the archivists. They are prehistoric in appearance, armored in chitin that has darkened to the color of the petrifying wood—dense, almost mineral themselves. They crawl over the beams that are becoming stone, their legs finding purchase in the velvet-smooth masonry, antennae tapping the silicified grain. They seem to read the surface, perhaps consuming the bacteria that facilitate the mineralization, or perhaps simply traversing the library of the village’s slow transformation. They move at the speed of geology, one step taking a minute, and they do not hurry.

And the newts—bright-bellied, orange as embers against the green gloom—are the hunters, the enforcers of balance. They patrol the moss-carpeted walls, three centimeters thick, a sponge that drinks the water and exhales oxygen. They are more active than the salamanders, darting through the freshwater kelp forests, snapping at the shrimp that click in the shadows. They are the color of the sunset that once shone on this village before the waters rose, a memory of warmth in the 8-degree cold.

But these are merely the parts. The true inhabitant—the *whatever* that lives here—is the Village itself, the third thing that is neither the primitive hamlet nor the wild lake, but the symbiosis of both. It is a meta-organism, a cathedral that has learned to breathe. The stone houses are its bones, weathered to velvet smoothness, porous and resonant. The water is its blood, heavy and stratified, carrying the silt that is its memory. The kelp that grows from the rooftops in pale, translucent columns is its breath, reaching toward the light that filters through the duckweed mat above. The hum—the low-frequency drone that vibrates in the sternum more than the ear—is its heartbeat, the glacial melt moving through the hollowed timber and resonant stone chambers like blood through veins.

This entity has a consciousness that is distributed, not centralized. It feels the salamanders as its hands, gently clearing the doorways. It feels the minnows as its thoughts, darting and schooling through the canals. It feels the beetles as its slow, geological memory, crawling over the bones. It feels the newts as its immune system, hunting and balancing. It feels the divers as weather—a cold front, a pressure change—something that passes through and disturbs the silt before moving on, leaving only a trail of rising bubbles.

It is becoming. The wood turns to stone from the inside out, and the Village drinks this transformation, incorporating it into its body. The boundary between the built and the grown has dissolved; the houses are planters, and the streets are beds of waving weed. The inhabitants—salamander, fish, beetle, newt, and the emergent village-soul—are not separate from this transformation. They are the agents of it. They maintain the ruins not to preserve the past, but to facilitate the becoming. They are the gardeners of a metamorphosis, tending a city that is turning into a reef, a reef that is turning into a mountain, slowly, patiently, over centuries that are mere moments to the glacial lake that holds them.

In the deep silt of the plaza, where no light reaches and the darkness is a black velvet suggesting depth without end, the oldest salamander drifts, blind and white, dreaming the dreams of stone, while above, the kelp sways in currents that move only when you blink, and the hum continues, and the village breathes, and waits, and becomes.
