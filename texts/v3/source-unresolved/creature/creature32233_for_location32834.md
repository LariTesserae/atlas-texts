They are the **Unfixed**, though they do not call themselves that—names, they have learned, are anchors, and anchors drown in rising ground.

They stand taller than baseline humans, their spines elongated by generations of thin air, their chests barrel-wide to hold lungs that work like bellows. Their skin has gone the color of the stone around them: a pale, translucent gray, veined with blue where the blood moves close to the surface. They have developed a third eyelid, a milky membrane that sweeps across eyes the color of glacial ice—pale, depthless, adapted to the pewter light and the mineral glare. When they breathe, the air crystallizes instantly around their faces, hanging in jagged halos that shatter when they move. They wear the pelts of the shaggy, long-horned herds—the oily, dense coats—but altered, lined with tubing that siphons warmth from the geothermal seeps, so that steam rises from their shoulders like smokestacks in miniature.

They do not build. Instead, they *inhabit the pause*—the brief, fragile stillness between geological convulsions. Their dwellings are **vesicles**: inflatable sacs of bio-synthesized membrane, translucent amber, anchored to the fracturing stone by pneumatic grapples that bite into fissures. A camp might consist of fifty such bladders, clustered around a steaming vent like a clutch of eggs, glowing faintly from within as the inhabitants move inside. When the ground shifts—and it always shifts, cracking open new wounds, swallowing old ones—the vesicles deflate in minutes, folding into packs carried by the hydraulic harvesters or by the herds themselves. Nothing is left behind but the circular scars where the grapples bit into the rock, healing over with mineral crust within a season. They sleep on mats woven from the gray-green moss, huddled together for heat, listening to the stone groan as it rises millimeters in the night.

Their relationship with the machines is not one of mastery but of **drift**. The harvesters—those crab-like giants on hydraulic legs—are not tools so much as migratory beasts that the Unfixed have learned to ride. Some dwell permanently in the cabs, their bodies fused with the controls through neural ports, their skin grown calloused where it meets the metal. Others walk alongside the fleets, guiding them with low-frequency tones that vibrate through the thin air, directing the extraction arms toward fresh veins. The machines, in turn, carry the Unfixed when the land becomes too unstable to traverse on foot, their flat undersides skimming over the saturated flats while the people ride in open cages, wrapped in steam, watching the world change beneath them. When a vein exhausts itself, when the hydrothermal pressure shifts and the black pools begin to cool, the machines do not stop—they simply *walk* to the next deposit, and the Unfixed walk with them, or are carried, their vesicles packed tight against the harvesters' flanks like remoras on a shark.

They have no calendar, only **weather**—the weather of the earth. They know the moods of the vents. They can read the steam: a certain shiver in the vapor means a fissure is opening three kilometers east; a change in the mineral tang of the air means a pool is about to flash to boiling. The elders are the **Vein-Seers**, individuals whose eyes have been burned milky by the mineral mist, who navigate not by sight but by the heat on their skin, who can stand in the condensing twilight and point to a bare patch of stone and say, "Water there, in three days." Children are born in the moving camps, in the vesicles, and learn to walk not on solid ground but on the shifting, uncertain crust, their inner ears adapted to the constant micro-tremors of the rising mountains. They are called the Drift-Born, and they have never known the vertigo of stillness.

Their culture is a theology of **unbuilding**. Every evening, as the pewter light fades and the steam thickens, they perform the Rite of Release: they take apart their own shelters, fold their bedding, unplug their heating tubes, and stack their possessions into the packs that will be carried away. They do this voluntarily, before the ground forces them to, a daily surrender to impermanence. They believe that to cling to a place is to be crushed by it; that permanence is a kind of death, and that only in motion can they remain alive. Their history is not written but *exhaled*—stories carried in the steam, passed from mouth to mouth in the thin air, dissolving and reforming like the mist itself. They trade with the barge-platforms on the larger pools, exchanging the moss they harvest and the hides of the herds for filtered water and processed minerals, but the trade is secondary. What they truly harvest is the **transience**—the rare minerals before erosion scatters them, yes, but also the moments of warmth, the brief safety of a vent that has not yet shifted, the fragile, temporary right to exist in a place that is still deciding what it wants to be.

They are the only permanent thing in a world of flux: not because they stay, but because they have learned to *become* the flux. They are the human expression of the geological youth, rising with the mountains, cracking with the stone, weeping with the thermal springs. When the geysers erupt—wet, percussive *whumps* that send steam twenty meters into the gray—they do not flinch. They raise their faces to the scalding rain of minerals, and they breathe it in, crystallizing instantly, becoming part of the condensing soup, waiting for the ground to shift again so they can move with it.
