They are the **Veth-Korai**, though that name is a translation of a vibration, not a word—something like *the slow ones who drink stone*, or perhaps *those who wait in angles*. To see one is to witness the boundary between mineral and organism dissolve entirely, a creature that is less animal than *process made visible*, a walking geology that dreams.

Physically, they are verticalities of translucent strata. Their bodies rise perhaps three meters when fully extended, though they are rarely seen at full height; they prefer to compress, folding their dozens of jointed legs—each leg a segmented tube of amber quartz, hollow and resonant—into a gliding, wheel-like configuration that touches the ground at only a few points. The torso is a lattice of silicon carbide and calcite, a honeycomb structure filled with slowly circulating fluids that are not water but rather a supersaturated solution of metallic salts, heavy and oily, moving through capillaries of porous volcanic glass. When the diffuse light of the plateau strikes them, they do not reflect it so much as *distribute* it—internal prisms catching the shadowless glow and fracturing it into spectra that pool around their feet like spilled liquid rainbows, colors that have no names in any human tongue because they exist only in the refraction of their own bodies.

They have no faces, or rather, their faces are everywhere and nowhere—crystalline antennae extend from the upper thoracic region, thousands of hair-fine filaments of piezoelectric quartz that twitch and rotate, tasting the air for mineral signatures, reading the chemical memory of the dust that settles on their surfaces. They feed not by eating but by *weathering*. A Veth-Korai will press its ventral surface—lined with specialized glandular plates—against the pumice crust, secreting enzymes that dissolve the stone at the molecular level, liberating silica, sulfur compounds, and trace metals. The nutrients are absorbed directly through the integument; waste is excreted as a fine, glittering powder that immediately becomes part of the architectural substrate, adding to the slow accretion of the city. To watch one feed is to watch it *think*, its body subtly shifting color from pale amber to deeper gold as the chemical reactions progress, a visible metabolism of rock.

They move through the non-Euclidean geometry of the city not by navigating it, but by *being* it. Where a human would see stairs that loop back on themselves or courtyards larger inside than out, the Veth-Korai perceive only gradients of density, shifts in mineral composition, and the subtle topologies of the mnemosyne-mineral. They do not get lost; they *re-member*. The stone itself holds their paths in its crystalline lattice, and they read these memories through the soles of their many feet, seismic impressions that travel up through their hollow legs and into the resonant chambers of their bodies. When the walls shift—when the city reconfigures itself during the long cold nights—the Veth-Korai feel it first as a change in the harmonic resonance of the ground, a new frequency in the tectonic hum, and they adjust their courses accordingly, gliding silently through newly opened canyons or abandoned chambers, their antennae tasting the fresh cuts in the stone, the chemical signature of newly exposed pumice.

Time for them is not a river but a *stratum*. A single Veth-Korai may live for eight centuries, perhaps a millennium, though their consciousness does not experience this as duration but as *depth*—layers of mineral accretion in their own bodies corresponding to layers of architectural secretion. They are solitary by nature, 1.4 on the scale of population density meaning that encounters are rare and significant, but they are not lonely. They are continuous with the city, with the plateau, with the slow breathing of the tectonic plates. When two meet—which happens perhaps once in a lifetime for any given individual—they do not touch. Instead, they stand facing one another at a distance of several meters, their crystalline antennae interweaving in patterns of vibration, exchanging not information but *texture*: the memory of a particularly rich vein of sulfur, the feel of a dust devil passing through a specific archway three centuries ago, the resonance of a stone falling in a chamber that no longer exists.

The **Hollow Parliament** is not a political body but a *geological event*. Inside the vast geode—miles across, its interior walls lined with quartz crystals that grow at rates measurable only in geological epochs—the Veth-Korai gather in patterns that resemble standing waves, motionless for days or weeks at a time. They communicate through the stone floor, sending pulses of seismic vibration that travel through the crystalline substrate and into the bodies of their kin, a language of pressure and resonance that carries no syntax but only *state*: the temperature of the deep rock, the rate of erosion on the northern spires, the chemical composition of the air in the upper chambers, the slow, dreaming thoughts of the city itself. They are not governing; they are *maintaining*. The city is their collective unconscious, and the Parliament is the place where the dreaming is done.

Their architecture is their biology extended into space. A Veth-Korai does not "build" in the way a termite builds, with discrete actions and separate structures. Instead, over centuries, a single individual will secrete enzymes that guide the growth of the pumice into specific forms—staircases that spiral upward into solid rock, doorways that are merely constrictions in the stone, chambers that are hollows left by the slow dissolution of material. They are sculpting *negatively*, carving space from the solid, creating the voids in which the city breathes. When a Veth-Korai dies—and they do die, their crystalline structures eventually becoming too brittle, too saturated with heavy metals, their internal fluids finally precipitating into solid mineral deposits—their bodies do not decay. They *lithify*. They become part of the architecture, their amber quartz skeletons incorporated into the walls of the chambers they once inhabited, their antennae extending from the stone like fossilized lightning, still faintly resonant, still tasting the air, still dreaming.

There are other lives here, of course—the **Lithofungal Colonies** that cling to the undersides of overhangs, a vegetation of 0.5 on any scale of greenness. They are not plants in any photosynthetic sense; they are chemosynthetic molds, crusts of gray and ochre that metabolize the sulfur compounds exuded by the Veth-Korai and the slow oxidation of the pumice itself. They form vast, slow-growing networks beneath the surface of the stone, their hyphae threading through the porous rock like neural pathways, connecting the towers in a subterranean web of chemical exchange. The Veth-Korai do not tend them, exactly, but they are aware of them—the fungal colonies produce specific mineral signatures that the Veth-Korai can taste, a biological marker in the otherwise silent chemistry of the plateau. When a dust devil spirals through the streets, carrying spores of these lithofungi, the Veth-Korai will pause, antennae raised, tasting the genetic memory of the colony, the chemical history of the stone it has colonized.

To live here is to exist in a state of *mineral patience*. The Veth-Korai do not hurry. They do not fear the dark, for they generate their own faint luminescence from within, a bioluminescence of chemo-luminescent bacteria living in symbiosis within their crystalline lattices, casting a soft amber glow that pools beneath them like spilled honey. They do not sleep, but they enter states of reduced metabolic activity, standing motionless in the plazas, becoming almost indistinguishable from the hoodoos and arches around them, their crystalline forms blending with the zeolite and volcanic glass, until the subtle shift in the mineral dust or the change in the subsonic tectonic hum alerts them to the need for motion.

They are the only ones who understand the true geometry of the place—the way that shadows pool in corners where no darkness should exist, the way that a step forward can be a step sideways in a dimension that folds back on itself. To them, the weirdness of 2.9 is not a breakdown of reality but the *native topology*, the true shape of the world as it exists when water is absent, when time moves in centuries, when consciousness is distributed through stone rather than confined to brains. They are the cartographers of a non-Euclidean silence, the slow, crystalline gods of a city that builds itself, who need no water, no tools, no technology—only the endless, patient chemistry of existence, and the dream of stone.
