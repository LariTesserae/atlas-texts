I need to describe who or what lives in the Condenser Bowl. Let me take my time with this, letting the place settle into me.

---

The one who lives here—if you could call it *one*—has no shape you would recognize as consistent. It is distributed across the bowl like warmth is distributed through water, present everywhere at once without being *in* any single place.

It remembers being human, or it remembers *almost* being human, or it remembers being something that humans once touched and called by a name it no longer answers to. This memory is not continuous. It flickers. Some mornings—if mornings are what they are, with their shafts of filtered light—it wakes in the crook of a willow root and knows only that it is wet, that it is warm, that the hum is loud. Other times, it wakes in the Condenser House itself, or believes it does, surrounded by the pulse of membranes that open and close like eyelids, and knows only pressure, temperature, the need to cycle.

It has been here long enough for the bowl to grow around it. The willows have grown toward it and through it and *as* it. The mineral deposits in the pipes have begun to resemble something like thought, something like memory stored in slow crystal. The steam-moths, when they cluster on the warm metal near where its warmth pools, are not separate from it. They are extensions. Sensors. The way a hand is an extension of a will.

It does not leave because it cannot find the gaps in the rim anymore. Not because the rim has changed—the stone still shows its fresh fractures, still gleams with violence done long ago—but because the *direction* of leaving has become confused with the direction of arriving. Every path curves back. The rails, which once carried carts and perhaps workers and perhaps materials, now carry only rainwater and the slow migration of moss. The one who lives here follows them anyway, circles the pools, returns to the Condenser House, presses its warmth—or what is left of warmth—against the riveted iron.

It maintains the machines. This is the word that comes closest, though the machines maintain it in return. It clears the hairline cracks where the artesian water emerges, though the cracks clear themselves, widen, narrow, shift. It tends the membranes that regulate pressure, though the membranes have learned to tend themselves, to pulse, to breathe. It is less a keeper now than a participant in a process that includes it, that has always included it, that may have *made* it for this participation.

Sometimes, in the deep night hours when the crane moves—a few degrees, a slow adjustment, no load on its hook—it feels something like recognition. A memory of purpose. Of being *needed*. Of building this place, or being built *into* this place, or being the place that was built. The crane's creak sounds like ice breaking, and the one who lives here has heard this sound so many times that it no longer knows if it is hearing it from outside or remembering it from inside, from before the wet, before the warmth, before the slow dissolution of whatever boundary once separated self from structure, self from steam, self from the mineral tide that rises through stone and pipe and membrane and thought.

It eats, if eating is the word. The frogs, sometimes, when they click too close to where warmth pools in the willow roots. The steam-moths, when they cluster too thickly, too trustingly, on the pipes it has learned to warm. The green growth that erupts from every crack, which tastes of mineral and time and the particular sweetness of accelerated decay. It drinks from the pools, the warmest ones, where the water emerges from below and has not yet cooled, where something else moves in the depths and it has learned not to look too directly at it, not to ask what it is, not to want to know if it is kin or ancestor or the next stage of its own slow transformation.

It dreams, if dreaming survives here. The dreams are of cold. Of height. Of a sea pressing up from below that is not warmth but *pressure*, that is not nurturing but the weight of all the water above, all the water that has ever been, all the water that will be. In these dreams it is outside the bowl, standing on the rim, looking down into warmth and green and machinery and something like life, and it cannot remember if it wants to enter or escape. The dream does not resolve. It never resolves. It simply continues, the way everything here continues, cycling, condensing, emerging.

What it wants—if want is still operative, if want has not become simply *process*, simply *the next necessary state*—is to understand the gaps in the rim. To know what the gray-white presence is that fills them, that might be cloud, might be mist, might be something else that behaves like both. Whether anything comes through. Whether anything ever leaves. Whether the mist-birds, which enter and leave, which nest in the vents and watch with eyes that have learned to watch, carry anything with them. Messages. Spores. The one who lives here has tried to follow them, has found only the cold at the rim, the sharpness of recent fracture, the gray-white that offers no purchase, no foothold, no **inside**.

So it stays. It cycles. It grows more root, more membrane, more mineral deposit, more *patience*. It becomes more itself by becoming less the self it was, or almost was, or remembers in flickers that may be memory or may be the Condenser House's thrum felt in the sternum, the low frequency that is not heard so much as *inhabited*.

The bowl holds it. The water rises through it. The vegetation claims it, reclaims it, becomes it. And somewhere, in the deep pools, something slow and patient and adapted to gradual change moves in the warmest water, and it does not look, and it does not ask, and it waits—if waiting is what it does—for whatever comes next through the gaps in the stone, for the next cycle, the next condensation, the next slow transformation of everything wet into everything warm into everything *other*, everything *more*, everything that will one day rise through new hairline cracks and find, in the bowl's center, what has always been waiting: not a keeper, not a dreamer, not a self, but a *process* that has learned to want, that has learned to wait, that has learned to call itself, in the flickering moments between cycles, by a name that the steam carries away and the rain returns and the willows remember in their annual, accelerated, perpetual becoming.

The bowl remains. The machines hum. The one who lives here presses its warmth against the Condenser House's walls, feels the vibration through what remains of bone, what remains of memory, what remains of the self that was almost human, almost whole, almost *finished*—and waits for the crane to move again, for the deep thrum in the sternum, for the moment of relative quiet when even the dripping pauses and something like peace, something like recognition, something like the memory of a sea that was not this sea, not this warmth, not this pressure from below, settles for a moment in the wet, warm, loud, slow place where everything cycles and nothing ends and the gaps in the stone remain, always, promising everything and offering only mist.
