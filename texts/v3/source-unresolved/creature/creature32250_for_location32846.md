They are the Wet-People, though they possess no name for themselves that survives translation into dry tongues. They are the arborists of the cloud-floor, the surgeons of sap and the lashers of living root. Two hundred souls—perhaps fewer, perhaps more as the census shifts with births and drownings—dwelling in the hollows of three great elders whose true names are sung rather than spoken, melodies that change pitch as the bark expands and the heartwood recedes year by year.

Physically, they are creatures of verticality and saturation. They are slight and wiry, with the permanent forward stoop of those who spend their lives on inclined surfaces and swaying catwalks. Their skin is pale, almost luminous in the diffuse, mist-filtered light, mapped with faint, permanent colonies of lichen-green that have established themselves in the creases of elbows, behind ears, in the hollows of collarbones. They do not dry; they carry the atmosphere in their pores, a perpetual sheen of condensation that makes them gleam like wet stone. Their hair is dark, cropped short or bound in thick ropes of water-reed, often sprouting with small mosses that they do not bother to remove—they are gardeners of their own bodies. Their fingers are elongated and stained amber with tannins from the bark, calloused at the tips from gripping slick, algae-coated rungs. They wear no shoes; their feet are broad and prehensile, the soles thickened into leathery pads that grip wet wood, toes splaying to find purchase on vertical trunks where the sap runs in rivulets.

They are loud. In a place of 2.1 loudness, where the air itself roars with water and wings, their voices have evolved to cut through the churn. They speak in a dialect of projected shouts, ululating whistles, and staccato calls that carry across the platforms and down into the peat. A child’s laughter sounds like a tern’s alarm cry; an elder’s command rolls like the groan of timber in a squall. They communicate as much through touch—a steadying hand on the shoulder against the sway, a sharp tap on bark to signal structural fatigue, the brush of moss-felt cloaks as they pass in the narrow walkways—as through sound.

Their craft is maintenance made sacrament. They are the Keepers of the Green Architecture, practitioners of a technology that works *with* rot rather than against it. A man might spend his morning carving a new chamber into living heartwood, his stone adze ringing against wood that weeps sap into the wound, listening to the creak of fibers to know when the tree will tolerate the intrusion. Women and men and those between are the Lashers, weaving bridges from supple willow withies and supple vine, testing the tension of each knot against the constant wind, reinforcing joints with cairns of water-worn stone. They are the Aqueduct-Makers, hollowing logs with fire-hardened bone chisels to channel the endless runoff from roof-platform to root-mat, ensuring that the water flows where it must—down, always down, into the thirsty peat, into the fungal networks, into the bog below. Their iron is precious and rare, hammered into thin blades that rust within a season, so they rely on obsidian, flint, and the horns of the Slick-Coats they hunt, tools that sing against green wood without corroding in the perpetual damp.

They eat what the wetness provides: fungi in endless varieties—some growing in shelf-like brackets on the bark, others pushing up through the black peat in pale, luminous clusters that glow faintly in the gloom. They hunt the small deer-like creatures, the Slick-Coats, with bone-tipped spears, moving silently through the understory where the slick-coated herds graze on perpetual new growth. They gather midges by the handful, crushing them into protein-rich pastes; they tap the trees for syrup; they drink the mineral-thick water that collects in gourds, tasting of bark and stone and the green sweetness of algae. Their fires are perpetually smoky, fed with compressed peat that burns slow and wet and sulky, tended constantly by Fire-Keepers whose hands are blackened and whose eyes water from the damp smoke. A fire that dies means a hollow that floods and a family that shivers through the night.

Their society is organized around the ligneous architecture. Each great trunk is a clan, a lineage traced through the history of grafting—when a new branch is trained to bridge to another tree, a marriage is sealed; when a hollow is carved to receive a new family, a birth is anticipated. The eldest among them, the Root-Keepers, remember the original training of the saplings that are now buttressed towers wider than houses. They sit in the highest platforms, wrapped in thick layers of felted moss, their skin nearly the color of wet bark, their eyes milky with cataracts but their hands still sure with the memory of a thousand grafts. They direct the expansion of the settlement, deciding where new chambers can be hollowed without killing the host, where new walkways can span the gaps without snapping the living wood, reading the tree’s health in the color of the lichen, the pitch of its groan in the wind.

Children are born in the hollows, cradled in nests of peat and sword-fern. They learn to climb before they walk on the solid ground, scrambling up trunks on pegs and natural ridges, their small hands finding the slick algae handholds. A toddler’s first tool is a polished stone for scraping moss from a step; a youth’s rite of passage is the lashing of their first bridge, spanning a gap between two living towers, tested by their own weight and the sway of the storm-wind. They grow up with the constant motion of the settlement, their inner ears adapted to the rocking, their sense of balance attuned to surfaces that shift and breathe beneath their feet, the peat mat sighing methane with every step.

They possess no written language, only the language of the tree itself. Debts, births, deaths, and treaties are recorded in shallow cuts carved into bark—marks that swell and scar over within a season, leaving a topography of raised callus that tells the history of the clan. They sing to the trees, songs that mimic the groan of wood in high wind, believing—or simply knowing through long observation—that the trees respond to vibration, growing toward kindness, receding from cruelty. They are not farmers in any dry-world sense; they are symbiotes, pruning and shaping and harvesting, taking only what the tree can spare, giving back compost and care and the carbon of their breath, participating in the closed loop of saturation that defines their existence.

At night—though the cloud-floor knows no true dark, only a dimming of the perpetual grey—the settlement glows with bioluminescent fungi, pale green and cold blue, hanging in clusters from the undersides of platforms. The people huddle in their chambers, listening to the rain that never ceases, feeling the tree expand around them in the dark, growing, always growing. They are the consciousness of the place, the nervous system of a cathedral that breathes, the immune response of a wound that refuses to close. They are wet, and they are warm, and they are loud, and they are home.
