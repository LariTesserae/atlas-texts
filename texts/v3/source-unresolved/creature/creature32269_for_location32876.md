The one who remains stands at the water’s edge, or perhaps crouches—there is no posture of dignity in a room barely wider than a held breath. They are bipedal, but their spine has curved over centuries (or moments; here the distinction dissolves) into the permanent arch of one who expects to meet a ceiling. Their skin is the color of the fungal tendrils that creep along the walls: not white, exactly, but the pale, translucent cream of something that has never known the sun, like the membrane of a newt’s egg or the inner flesh of a mushroom. It is thin, almost parchment, stretched over a frame of bone that is itself pale, porous, drinking in the mineral-heavy air.

They have no eyes. The sockets are smooth, sealed perhaps by a film of calcite that has accreted there like a tear turned to stone, or simply never opened—vestigial hollows where sight would have been. Their face is a topography of touch: broad, flat nostrils that flare to sample the sulfurous warmth; lips that are thick and sensitive, tasting the air for chemical shifts; fingers that are long, spidery, the fingertips swollen with nerve-endings, capable of reading the difference between obsidian and basalt, between wet stone and dry. They are hairless, for hair is a technology of cold, and this place is a fever. Their body heat is low, barely registering against the geothermal warmth of the chamber, making them a cool shape in a hot room, a shadow of temperature.

They navigate by memory carved into muscle. The chamber is small—small enough to touch every wall with outstretched arms—but to them it is a cathedral of known distances. They know the exact location of the stone platform, raised above the pool, where they sleep curled around themselves like a question mark. They know the placement of the tools: the flint scraper with its bone handle, worn smooth by palms that no longer exist; the hammerstone that rests in a hollow of the floor; the pointed stick used to probe the fungal growths. They move through the darkness without hesitation, their feet finding the dry stone, avoiding the cold ring of the old fire-pit, stepping over the pale drift of the creature in the water when it surfaces near the edge.

They are a harvester of salt. Where the geothermal water weeps from the ceiling and leaves its mineral stain—white crusts of calcite and sulfur, the residue of the mountain’s deep chemistry—the biped comes with their flint tool. They scrape. The sound is a whisper, a dry rasp against stone, immediately swallowed by the weight of silence. They collect the crystals in a fold of their own skin, or perhaps in a pouch of woven fungal fiber, and consume them, or store them, or simply arrange them in patterns that mean something only to them. Their metabolism is lithotrophic, chemosynthetic; they do not hunt, they do not forage in the way of surface creatures. They drink from the pool, lowering their face to the black glass, their breath not disturbing the surface tension. They live on the chemistry of the unweathered stone, on the warmth, on the minerals that precipitate from the stagnant water.

In the water lives their kin, or their ancestor, or their god—it is difficult to say in a place where time has not been switched on. The creature is pale as the biped, hairless, eyeless, but it is not a biped. It is elongated, serpentine or amphibian, drifting through the warm depths like a shed skin, a membrane of living tissue that has forgotten the need for bone. It moves so slowly that its passage is felt rather than seen: a displacement of warm water, a pressure change against the biped’s ankle when it brushes the edge. Sometimes, when the biped scrapes the walls, the creature rises to the surface, its head breaking the black glass with a sound like a sigh, and the two pale beings touch—finger to eyeless snout—exchanging minerals, or heat, or simply the acknowledgment of presence in the dark.

They are not alone in their paleness. The fungal tendrils that creep along the walls where the water weeps are their neighbors, their slow-growing kin. These are not plants in the way of the surface world; they are chemosynthetic, lithotrophic, feeding on the sulfur and the warmth and the raw chemistry of the newborn stone. They are root-like, pale, branching, and they grow at a rate that makes the biped’s movements seem hasty. A single tendril might take a century to advance an inch, yet in the frozen equilibrium of the chamber, this is growth, this is life. They touch the biped’s feet as they pass, and the biped touches them back, feeling the slight give of organic tissue against mineral stone. The fungi do not photosynthesize; they are the organic intrusion into the mineral world, the first soft thing in a place of glass and basalt.

The biped remembers, or carries the memory of others. The tools are not theirs alone; they are the inheritance of a lineage that has dwindled to one, or perhaps the biped is the last of a species that never numbered more than a handful. They remember the platform, built by hands that placed flat stones with precision, creating a bed above the water. They remember the fire that burned in the ring of stones, though now the stones are cold, dead, a circle of basalt that has not known heat in a time beyond counting. They remember navigating by the warmth of bodies pressed together, by the sound of breathing in the dark—a sound that is now gone, replaced by the heavier silence.

They exist in the instant of creation. They do not age, or they age so slowly that the calcite formations grow around them like a shroud they have not yet noticed. The tools do not corrode. The salt does not deplete. They are caught in the womb of the mountain, in the moment before the world finished deciding what it would be, and they are the dreamers in that unfinished dark—pale, eyeless, patient, scraping the salt from the walls of their embryonic universe, waiting for a birth that may never come, or perhaps already has, in the form of the creature that drifts in the warm, black water beside them.
