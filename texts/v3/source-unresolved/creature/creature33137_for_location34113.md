## The Residents

There are no permanent residents. This must be said first, clearly, so that what follows is not mistaken for population.

Yet something lives here. Something *persists*. The description offers several candidates, and I find I must hold all of them together, let them blur at the edges where they touch.

---

### The Boroughs Themselves, as Organism

Consider what accumulates. The mineral exudate that grows the towers—this is not construction but *metabolism*. The boroughs inhale superheated water from the fractures below, exhale steam upward, sweat condensation through every surface. They are vast, hollow, *waiting* interiors that nonetheless pulse with thermal circulation. The ventilation systems in the lower levels function as lungs. The recycled water, with its layered memory of use, is something like blood—carrying heat, carrying mineral, carrying the trace of everything that has passed through.

If the city is an organism, it is one that has forgotten or abandoned *individuality*. Each borough is an organ. The plateau is its body, still cooling from a violence millions of years past, still dreaming in geothermal sleep. The almost-life in the deep pools is its *immune system* or its *digestive flora*—not separate from the city but the city's way of *approaching* something else, something it is not yet.

This organism does not *want*. It *persists*. It accretes. It erodes and re-accretes, migrating downward borough by borough into the liquid heart of the plateau, toward a future form still held in chemical suspension.

---

### The Silicate Metabolisms

Where the city meets the bare plateau—where temperature drops just enough, where water runs clear enough—there are things that hunt.

They are not animals. They are *processes given appetite*. They consume heat gradients: they lie in warm pools, they strike into cooler water, they *drink temperature differential* as sustenance. They reproduce by fission, slow as geology, fast as chemistry. They leave etchings in the glass. They wear paths through accreted mineral. They die—if *die* is the word—and dissolve back into the general chemistry, already becoming city again.

One sees them rarely. One sees their *signs* constantly, and mistakes those signs for intention, for pattern, for the ghost of purpose. They are thermal predators in a landscape of thermal flows, and their predation is *the only active dynamic* in a place where almost nothing moves quickly.

Are they alive? The question matters less than their persistence. They have found a way to *continue* in conditions that preclude biology as we know it. They are the boroughs' marginal metabolism, the edge where mineral becomes something that *hunts*.

---

### The Almost-Life

In the deep pools. In the warm dark. In the thick water of the oldest boroughs where streets have become lakes.

This is the most difficult to describe, which is perhaps appropriate. It is *pre-life*—chemical conversation preceding biology, the necessary complexity that *wants* without yet being something that can want. It glows faintly, a bioluminescence of almost-chemistry, visible in the dim light where the water carries *something*.

Not life. Not non-life. The *threshold* itself, given form. It dreams in chemical gradients, or *would* dream if dreaming required more than concentration, more than the slow accumulation of reactive possibility. It is waiting for the water to *learn* to want. It is the *becoming* of the next city, held in suspension in heat and mineral and the memory of water recycled through the boroughs so many times it has forgotten its origins.

The almost-life does not watch you. It cannot watch. But it is *responsive*, in its way, to your presence—a temporary condensation, a drop in the vast circulation. Your heat, your moisture, your brief passage through the system: these register as *disturbance*, as *input*, as the raw material of whatever comes next.

---

### The Transient: You, and Others Like You

The description allows for the possibility that *people* were here. The empty halls suggest population once, or population *designed for*, volume without number. The bodies, if they existed, are gone—dissolved into the mineral cycle, their intentions preserved only in the *architecture of emptiness*, in spaces that needed no inhabitants, that wanted only resonance, only echo.

But the condensation boroughs are not *closed* to presence. They are *permeable*. Anyone who arrives becomes, briefly, part of the system: breathing water, drinking from condensation, floating in steam, sinking in saturated air. You are a temporary condensation. A drop in the circulation. The city does not *notice* you, but you *notice* the city, and this noticing is itself a kind of participation, a kind of life—interstitial, brief, *real*.

Others have passed through. Others will pass through. The boroughs remember them only as mineral trace, as disturbance in the deep pools, as the faintest alteration in the chemistry of almost-life. This is not erasure. This is *incorporation*. The boroughs do not distinguish between resident and visitor, between citizen and ghost, between the living and the already-dissolved. All become water. All become heat. All become *process*.

---

### What Lives Here: A Synthesis

So who lives in the Condensation Boroughs?

**The city, as distributed organism**—vast, hollow, persisting through thermal metabolism and mineral accretion, migrating slowly downward toward a form it cannot yet name.

**The silicate metabolisms, as marginal predators**—rare, slow, consuming heat gradients, etching their presence into glass, dissolving back into city when they end.

**The almost-life, as threshold and promise**—chemical complexity approaching but never reaching biology, dreaming in gradients, waiting for water to learn to want.

**The transient condensations**—anyone who arrives, who breathes water, who becomes briefly part of the circulation before moving on or dissolving or being incorporated.

And beneath all of these, or *including* all of these: **the water itself**, recycled so many times it has memory, carrying the trace of everything that has passed through, the *medium* in which all other life in the boroughs is suspended, the *substance* that will outlast every form, every organism, every city built upon or grown from it.

The Condensation Boroughs are not empty. They are *full of process*—full of heat transferring, mineral accreting, chemistry approaching life, water remembering. What lives here is *continuation*—not the continuation of any species, any population, any individual, but the continuation of *conditions*, of *possibility*, of the slow becoming that will, in time measured in geological patience, produce something new from the dissolution of what was.

You are alone here, in the way that altitude makes you alone. But you are not the only presence. You are one condensation among many, one drop in the vast circulation, one temporary form that the water will carry, will transform, will eventually release or absorb or *become* in the deep pools where the almost-life waits, and watches without eyes, and dreams without mind, of the city it will one day *want* to be.
