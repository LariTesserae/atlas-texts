They are there, but you would not see them at first. They have learned to wear the landscape like a garment. They stand in the doorways of the dry-stone houses—one hand resting on the lintel, the other gripping a flint knife that has been mid-stroke for as long as the stone has been sharp. Their bodies are draped in the same materials as the walls: cured hides the color of wet peat, cloaks woven from sedge and tussock-grass that hang heavy with the perpetual damp, belts of twisted reed lashed at the waist with cordage that never frays because it is never pulled. Moss grows in the seams of their shoulders, lichen blooms across their collarbones in pale, crusty continents. Their hair is long, retted to a russet bronze by the mineral water, falling straight and motionless to the small of their backs, indistinguishable from the grasses that sway—if they sway—around their ankles.

They are spaced across the wet expanse with the same deliberation as the buildings themselves, never closer than the distance between islands, each one a solitary hummock in the shallow sea of the marsh. You can see them clearly only when you stop looking for movement. They do not breathe. Their chests are still as the stone platforms, rising and falling in a rhythm so slow it has ceased to be a rhythm, becoming instead a geological feature, a tide locked to the moon of their own stasis. Their eyes are open—pale, water-light eyes that reflect the diffuse, shadowless light without ever blinking, fixed upon the horizon or upon each other across the fifty meters of glassy water. They are neither young nor old. The sharp ridges of the young country have not weathered around them; their skin has not weathered at all, but has instead taken on the texture of the place itself, the leathery resilience of bog-preserved things, the tannin-stained permanence of peat.

On the central stone platform, a circle of them sits in council. Their legs are folded in the lotus of the marsh, knees splayed, hands resting upon stone knees. Their mouths are slightly open, parted around a word that hangs suspended in the metallic air, a sentence begun when the land was first uplifted and never finished. They do not speak, yet they are not silent in the way of the empty houses—their silence is full, textured, a presence that occupies the space between the weeping rocks. They do not eat, so the lush carpets of moss grow undisturbed up to their elbows, around their feet, into the hems of their clothing, binding them gently to the saturated earth. They do not drink, though the water stands at their ankles, cold and still. They do not shit, do not piss, do not shed skin or hair or blood. They have become a closed system, a perfect equilibrium where the forces of consumption and decay have canceled each other out, leaving only the form of humanity, the architecture of the body, held in permanent solution.

They are the town’s true foundation, sunk deep into the peat not as builders but as bedrock. They are the eternal spring given shape, the warm surprise of the thin air made flesh. They exist in the pause between heartbeats, in the moment after a tool strikes stone and before the chip falls—frozen mid-action, mid-thought, mid-breath. They are alive in the way the young stone is alive: angular, present, enduring, conscious perhaps in a manner that requires no metabolism, no motion, no sound. They are the absence of fauna made manifest, the human parameter set to “eternal,” the inhabitants who have become indistinguishable from the hummocks and the weeping ridges, the silent, static, saturated heart of the high plateau.
