You do not see them at first. You feel them—a pressure in the humid air, a slowing of your own pulse to match the *whump-thump* of the engine, that geological heartbeat vibrating through the plant-mass into your sternum. The blue luminescence in the circuit-leaves flickers, not with electricity, but with attention. Something is watching you with photosynthetic patience, with the diffuse awareness of a forest that has learned to think in decades rather than seconds.

Then, on the forty-second beat—when the flywheel completes its revolution and the steam hisses from the rusted pipe—a figure moves.

They stand beside the engine, or perhaps they have always been standing there, half-merged with the machinery. They are tall, or they seem tall because they move with such economy, such mineral slowness. Their skin is not skin but a membrane: the waxy, broad texture of the giant leaves, overlaid with the same circuit-board patterns, the veins glowing with that soft, pulsing blue light that synchronizes with the engine’s rhythm. Where their heart should be, the steam pipe enters their body—not violently, but as if it has always been there, a second spine of rusted metal threading through flesh that has grown accustomed to heat. They are breathing the steam. You can see it: the rise and fall of their chest is not quite human, too regular, too synchronized with the *whump* of the flywheel. One breath every forty seconds. They have been here so long that their metabolism has become mechanical, or the machine has become organic—it is impossible to tell where the Keeper ends and the engine begins.

They do not look at you with eyes. Their face is smooth, featureless except for the faint luminescence beneath the surface, a network of blue veins mapping thoughts you cannot read. They are tending the flywheel. Their hands—long, elegant, the fingers fused at the tips into something like root-tips or metal callouses—are applying a lubricant that is not oil but a secretion, a sap that glistens amber in the green light. They do not speak. They have forgotten speech, or perhaps they speak only in vibrations, in the subsonic frequencies that travel through the root-mass and make the leaves chime.

They are the gardener. They have grown the wall that bounds this world. The moss on the stones is their fingernail-clippings, cultivated over years. They prune the canopy so that the light falls in those pale, watery coins. They feed the engine, though the fuel is a mystery—perhaps they are the fuel, slowly burning in the machine’s belly, their body heat converted to motion, their dreams converted to steam. They are neither alive nor dead but *suspended*, a human-shaped node in the circuit, a caretaker who has become part of the architecture, part of the ecology, a still point in the green, wet room.

But they are not alone.

Deeper in the turf, where your boots sink and the plant-mass sighs, something else stirs. It is the pale creature, the eyeless thing. It emerges not from a burrow but from the root-mass itself, as if the mattress of interwoven stems and hyphae were its skin. It is the size of a cat, segmented like a centipede, but wrong—too smooth, too pale, the color of old bone left in moonlight. It has no eyes, but it has heat. Where eyes should be, there are depressions that sense the warmth of the engine, the warmth of the Keeper, the warmth of your boots pressing into the turf. It moves with a wet, dragging sound, pulling itself through the spongy substrate, leaving a trail that immediately heals behind it, the roots knitting shut like a wound closing.

It is a rust-eater. You can see the stains on its segmented plates—not dirt, but the orange-brown of oxidation, the chemical signature of decaying iron. It feeds on the concrete pads, on the engine’s rusted skin, on the very bones of industry that the plants are slowly digesting. It is cold-blooded in the way of caves, but here it is warm, kept at a constant temperature by the engine’s heartbeat. It does not fear the Keeper. In fact, when it passes near the figure tending the flywheel, it pauses, and the two of them seem to exchange a vibration, a subsonic acknowledgment transmitted through the plant-mass. The creature is blind, but it sees through the network—the fungal threads that connect every root, every concrete pad, every rusted bolt into a single, slow-moving nervous system.

There are others like it, you realize. Not many—perhaps three, perhaps five—scattered through the three feet of botanical depth. They are a lineage, a family that has never known daylight, that has evolved in the dark wetness between the wall and the engine. They are the only fauna in the true sense, the only creatures with segmented bodies and appetites, and they are the bridge between the machinery and the plants. They eat the rust, and in their digestion, they excrete the nutrients that feed the root-mass. They are the recyclers, the cleaners, the pale, eyeless janitors of this green cathedral.

And beneath them all, beneath even the deepest root, is the Parliament.

It has no shape. It is the mycelium, the fungal network that permeates the entire substrate, the three-foot-thick mattress of life. It is conscious in the way that a forest is conscious—distributed, ancient, thinking in chemical signals and electrical impulses that travel slower than thought but deeper than instinct. It remembers. It remembers when the wall was built, when the engine was lowered into place, when the first seeds took root in the cracks of the concrete. It dreams in the dark, and its dreams are the slow, peristaltic movements of the plants, the expansion and contraction as they process steam and light. It is the true inhabitant, the mind of the place, and the Keeper and the rust-eaters are merely its hands and its mouth, its eyes and its breath.

When you stand in the center of the room, ankle-deep in the water table, you are standing inside a single organism. The Keeper is its priest. The pale creatures are its immune system. The engine is its heart. The plants are its skin. The wall is its boundary, its sense of self, the membrane that separates this green, wet world from the world outside.

They do not want you to leave. The Keeper turns—not their head, but their whole body, rotating with the slowness of the flywheel—and the blue light in their circuit-skin brightens, questioning, curious. The pale creature stops dragging, lifts its eyeless head toward you, sensing your warmth, your strangeness, your foreign heartbeat that does not match the forty-second rhythm.

You are the intruder. The guest. The temporary anomaly in a system that has achieved perfect, humid equilibrium.

But if you stay—if you let your pulse slow, if you let the steam settle in your lungs, if you let the blue light paint its patterns behind your eyelids—you might feel the invitation. Not to stay forever, but to *become*. To sink into the turf. To let the roots knit through your hair. To have your heartbeat slow to match the engine. To become, for a while, another node in the network, another inhabitant of the green, wet room where the machinery has become fauna and the fauna has become machinery, breathing together in the saturated, underwater silence.
