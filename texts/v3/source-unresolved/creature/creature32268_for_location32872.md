What lives here is not a population so much as a *procession*—creatures that have grown slow enough to match the dripping, patient enough to become part of the geology they inhabit. They move through the saturated air like thoughts moving through water, each one a specialized organ in the body of the plateau.

**The Resonants** are the first you encounter, if you can call sitting still "encountering." They are the amphibians the size of dinner plates, their skin the color of wet slate and old moss, encrusted in places with calcite that has dried white as bone. They have no voices. Instead, each carries a distended throat-sac that pulses against the peat in rhythms too low for human ears, sending subsonic geometries through the black soil—maps of temperature, of mineral density, of the water table's slow rise and fall. They sit in the tea-colored pools with their eyes half-closed, filtering the peat through membranes in their flanks, their bodies acting as living sediment traps. When the mist thickens, they expand their sacs in unison, a wave of vibration that ripples across the hummocks, warning of pressure changes in the vents miles away. They are the barometers of the place, their bodies becoming more mineral than flesh with each passing decade, until the oldest of them look like geodes that happen to breathe.

Between the fern fronds move the **Wet-Walkers**, insects that have abandoned flight. Their wings are vestigial, heavy things that fold like damp leather against their backs, too saturated with condensation to buzz. Instead, they communicate through percussion—a wet, rhythmic clicking of mandibles against chitin, a language of taps and scrapes that sounds like a slow Geiger counter or a distant typewriter. They are harvesters, crawling the undersides of unfurling fiddleheads to drink the condensation that gathers there, their bodies gleaming with moisture. They carry spores rather than pollen, hitching rides on the mist itself, and when they die, their clicking ceases and their bodies sink into the peat to become nutrients for the next generation of ferns. They are the couriers of the closed loop, moving information and genetic material through the saturated dark.

Above them, perched on the hummocks, stand the **Glass-Eyed**. They are birds that have forgotten how to sing, possessing instead eyes that are not eyes at all but lenses—polished obsidian, volcanic glass that has grown in their sockets like pearls, focusing the diffuse grey light into something sharp and usable. They do not fly; the dense air makes flight inefficient, and besides, there is nowhere to go in a place that stretches to its own curvature. They stand on long, moss-cushioned legs, their bodies low and streamlined, feathers matted with mineral dust. They watch. They track the movement of the Wet-Walkers, the vibration of the Resonants, the shifting steam-plumes from the vents, their heads tilting with the mechanical precision of surveillance equipment. They are the monitoring system, the eyes of the factory that has forgotten it is being watched. When they feed, they swallow the Wet-Walkers whole, the clicking stopping abruptly in their gullets, and they digest slowly, for days, while the mist darkens their plumage further.

Around the geothermal smokestacks, where the steam rises in vertical pistons of white, live the **Scale-Crusted**. These are the thermophiles, reptilian or amphibian things whose hides have been replaced over centuries by precipitated minerals—copper-green scales, sulfur-yellow plates, crusts of silica that grow in geometric patterns across their backs. They bask in the superheated water, their bodies acting as living heat-exchangers, regulating the temperature of the vents by absorbing and radiating heat in slow cycles. They eat the mineral crusts directly, scraping copper and silica from the terraces with teeth that are themselves fossilized bone. They move only when the vents pulse, retreating into crevices when the steam cools, their metabolisms slowing to a near-stop. They are the maintenance crew, keeping the geological engine clear, their bodies gradually becoming part of the cooling towers they tend, until death is merely a continuation of their mineralization.

Beneath the moss, however, exists the true population: the **Hyphal Architects**. The fungal lace is not merely vegetation but a nervous system, a distributed intelligence that has colonized the ancient grid beneath the peat. The mycelium grows along the straight valleys, following the calcite veins and iron-oxide welds like circuitry, processing the dead and the dying with chemical precision. When the ferns fall, the fungi dissolve them within seasons; when the Glass-Eyed die, they consume them entirely, leaving only the obsidian lenses to sink into the black soil. In the deepest mist, the fungal fruiting bodies emerge—not mushrooms, but structures that suggest architecture: cubes, angles, repeating patterns that look like foundations or server racks. They are the memory of the place, storing information in chemical gradients, computing the hydrological cycle, remembering what the plateau was before it was a lung.

And finally, moving through the straight valleys with the inevitability of tectonic plates, are the **Foundation-Beasts**. Rarely seen, these are massive, elephantine or tortoise-like creatures whose hides have become complete ecosystems—moss carpets, miniature fern forests, even small pools of tea-colored water held in the folds of their mineral-crusted skin. They follow the grid-lines beneath the peat, the ancient roads too regular to be natural, their weight pressing the soil into the foundations below, maintaining the structural integrity of the hydrological engine. They are slow beyond comprehension, taking days to take a single step, their footsteps creating the hummocks, their bodies becoming islands in the mist. When they die, they do not decay quickly; they sink into the peat and mineralize, their bones becoming the bedrock for new growth, their moss gardens continuing to thrive for centuries after their hearts have stopped.

Together, they form a single, breathing organism: the Resonants filtering the water, the Wet-Walkers distributing the spores, the Glass-Eyed monitoring the system, the Scale-Crusted regulating the heat, the Hyphal Architects remembering and computing, and the Foundation-Beasts maintaining the structure. None of them hurry. None of them make a sound that travels through the thick air. They exist in the perpetual twilight, dripping with condensation, wearing the mineral stains of the rocks they inhabit, living inside a machine that breathes in cloud and exhales rain—each creature a gear in a factory that has forgotten it was ever built, now indistinguishable from the geology it maintains.
