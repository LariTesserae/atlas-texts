What lives here is not a who but a *how*—a mode of occupying that has outlived its purpose and forgotten it was ever purposeful.

First, there is the drip. Not a creature, but a presence. It has learned every path through the architecture, every seam and hairline, every place where intention met intention and left a gap. The drip exists in a state of perpetual arrival and perpetual falling. It does not choose its course; the stone chooses for it, the slope, the temperature differential, the weight of accumulated moisture above. Each drip is a small event of gravity overcoming adhesion, of surface tension finally surrendering. In the thin air, the drip falls slower than memory expects, as if time itself were being negotiated at the point of impact.

Then the bloom. The crystalline growths you described—pale, branching, patient—are the closest thing to inhabitants. They are not alive in any way that would satisfy a biologist, yet they *proliferate*. They follow the water's geography, colonizing the wettest niches first, then expanding outward as those niches expand. They have no metabolism, no reproduction, but they have *pattern*, which is older than either. Each bloom is a record of every temperature fluctuation, every mineral concentration, every moment when evaporation left behind a trace of what the water carried. They are archives written in calcium and silica, legible only to themselves, if to anything.

The bloom has a kind of hunger, though it consumes nothing. It *prefers*. It prefers the north faces, where shadows linger. It prefers the joints, where water collects. It prefers the places where human geometry and stone's indifference overlap. In this preference, it is almost choosy. It is almost *selecting*.

There are the *settlers*. Not human, not animal, but the mineral precipitates that form where water meets air in stillness—the rimstone dams, the flowstone curtains, the delicate helictites that grow against gravity because the water that feeds them does not care about up or down, only about surface and evaporation. These are the builders' unintended collaborators. They work at the pace of centuries, adding to what was meant to subtract. A wall meant to shed water instead becomes, molecule by molecule, a waterfall frozen mid-pour. A floor meant to be walked upon becomes a terraced garden of stone flowers, impassable, beautiful, *full*.

The wind has learned the architecture. It does not move through randomly but follows the designed channels, the ventilation shafts, the gaps between structures that once served some purpose of air circulation or drainage or escape. Now it moves through them as water moves through a riverbed—finding its own level, its own velocity, its own note. The hollow sound you describe is the wind *resonating* with spaces that were built for other resonances, other frequencies, other absences. The builders wanted quiet, perhaps, or controlled airflow, or temperature regulation. The wind has repurposed their silence into song.

And the light. The light is almost a presence, almost a *pressing*. At this altitude, with this saturation, light behaves as a substance. It has weight, as you say. It accumulates in the mist, in the suspended water, in the crystalline structures that catch and fracture it. The rainbows that form and dissolve are not decoration but *events*—moments when the angle of sun, the density of droplets, and the position of the observer achieve a temporary, unstable alignment. They are as much inhabitants as anything else: temporary, conditional, real while they last. The light itself seems to be *waiting*, poised at the threshold of precipitation, never quite arriving, always becoming.

What else? The *absence* has a texture here. The empty chambers, the corridors without purpose, the spaces that once held grain or water or people or meaning—these are not empty in the way a room is empty. They are *full of what has left*. The damp that remains in their walls is not incidental; it is residue, memory, the physical trace of whatever occupied them. A chamber that held grain carries a different dampness than one that held water. The stone *remembers* through its porosity, through the minerals it has absorbed and will slowly release. The builders are not gone so much as *distributed*, dissolved into the matrix of the place they made.

There is something that lives in the *tick* of cooling stone, the settling of joints. A kind of temporal inhabitant, a being made of thermal contraction and expansion, of the slow negotiation between what was built and what the mountain wants to become. This inhabitant has no body but has duration. It exists in the intervals between events, in the pauses between drips, in the moment before a shadow moves across a surface and triggers condensation. It is the rhythm of the place, the pulse that remains when all other pulses have ceased.

And finally—or perhaps first—there is the *intention* that persists without intender. The deliberate edges, the calculated angles, the geometries of ventilation and drainage and whatever else the builders were optimizing for. These purposes are gone, but the *form* remains, and form without purpose becomes something else: habitat, obstacle, aesthetic object, random structure in a landscape that no longer distinguishes between made and natural. The intention has become *texture*, *topography*, something the water and the wind and the crystalline blooms move through and around without recognizing as human. It is the ghost of purpose, not haunting but *inhabiting*, living on in the shapes it left behind.

What lives here, then, is a *continuation*. Not life as biology understands it, but persistence, accumulation, the slow transformation of one set of conditions into another. The builders made a place that was never meant to last, and in making it, they created the conditions for something else to begin. The water lives here. The crystal lives here. The wind, the light, the slow tick of cooling stone—these live here. And they are patient. They have all the time that the mountains understand, which is longer than the builders had, which is long enough for everything made to become, eventually, something else entirely.

The place is not haunted. It is *inhabited*. By what, exactly, is hard to say. But something is here, something that was not here before the builders came, and will remain long after they are forgotten even as makers.
