I need to step back from this rich world and let its inhabitants emerge organically, one layer at a time.

**The Ones Who Maintain the Pumps**

They are called the *Drip-Keepers*, though they don't keep drips—they keep the *absence* of too much drip. Their work exists in the space between the city and its flooding. They are fewer than the creatures, more numerous than the leviathans, and they move through the city with a particular quality of attention: always listening for the *change* in water's voice.

A Drip-Keeper might be seventeen or seventy—the wet air preserves, in its own way, though it also corrodes. They wear suits that are not quite sealed, because sealed means *trapped*, means the moisture of their own bodies turning against them. The suits are layered, porous, *breathing* in a negotiation with the world. They carry tools that look like stethoscopes, like listening horns, like the curved implements dentists once used before dentistry became something else entirely.

Their expertise is *reading*. Not reading text—they read the *behavior* of water. Which sump is rising. Which pipe, corroded by decades of mineral deposition, is about to become a spring. Which terrace, built upon a terrace, built upon a terrace, has developed a *preference* for collecting moisture in its eastern corner, and what that preference means for the structure beneath it.

They are not beloved. The creatures do not fear them, but they do not *trust* them either. A Drip-Keeper descending into a failed pump chamber moves through swarms of filter-feeders that part around their boots, that investigate the foreign shapes with the same curiosity they bring to any new surface. The Keepers learn to move slowly. To let the creatures *acclimate* to their presence. To become, temporarily, part of the architecture they maintain.

There is a melancholy to their work. They maintain systems that were built by hands that turned to mineral centuries ago. They keep running machines whose original purpose has been forgotten, whose *designers* would not recognize them. And they know—this is the weight they carry—that their own work will outlast their memory. That some future Keeper, or some future *thing* that replaces the Keepers, will maintain the pumps they maintained, in a city that has forgotten why pumps were ever necessary, or why anyone would have built *upward* to escape water rather than *into* it.

Some Keepers speak to the machines. Not to the small creatures—the creatures are *company*, they are *presence*, but they are not *witness*. The machines, the deep-running ones, the engines that turn in the hot dark below the waterline—those are witness. Those have been listening for longer than any human voice. The Keepers speak to them in the old tongues, in the technical vocabularies that have become ritual, in the names of components that no longer exist but whose *functions* persist in the maintenance manuals, copied and recopied across generations.

And sometimes—the Keepers do not speak of this openly—the machines *answer*. Not in words. In *rhythm*. A change in the harmonic of their turning. A resonance in the stone that travels up through the pipes, through the water, into the bones of the listener. The Keepers learn to recognize these answers. To feel, in their own bodies, the *mood* of the deep machinery. To know, with a certainty that has no other name, that they are being *attended to*.

**The Filter-Feeders Themselves: A Closer Look**

I said they are many. I should say more carefully: they are *legion*, and their legion is *structured*.

The small ones—those that pass through a human hand as a *tickle*, a *brush*, a momentary wrongness in the air—are not individuals in any sense the city-dwellers would recognize. They are *colonies* that have learned to move. Each "creature" that flows past your legs is a temporary aggregation, a school that has achieved coherence through chemical signaling, through the simple physics of bodies in water-film, through whatever *urge* drives them to seek the richest microbial lawns.

They are grey-gold, as I said, but this is poor description. In certain light—the luminous light of the high hours, when mist has not yet gathered—they carry *iridescence*. Not the oil-slick rainbow of a world that knows petroleum, but something stranger: a *structural* color, produced by the microscopic architecture of their surfaces, by layers of protein and mineral that interfere with light in ways that shift as they move. A school in motion becomes a *wave* of color, gold to grey to gold, the color of water remembering that it was once something else.

They *sing*. The clicking, the humming I mentioned—that is not random. It is *communication*, or the residue of communication. Each school carries a *signature*, a frequency that identifies it to others, that marks territory in a world where territory is *chemical* rather than spatial. When two schools meet, they *negotiate*. Sometimes they merge. Sometimes they pass through each other, their signatures temporarily confused, temporarily *shared*. The Keepers can read these signatures, have learned to, as one learns to read weather. They can tell, by the sound, whether a passage is occupied by a *stable* school or a *migrating* one, whether the creatures are *feeding* or *resting* or *fleeing* something the Keepers cannot perceive.

They do not eat humans. This is not mercy—it is *indifference*. A human body offers no nutritional advantage in a world where every surface *blooms* with microbial life. The creatures flow around humans as water flows around stone. But they *notice*. They *investigate*. A motionless human in a flooded corridor will be *touched*, gently, by hundreds of small bodies, each one sampling, assessing, *remembering* in whatever way they remember. The Keepers say this is how the creatures *map*. That each encounter leaves a trace, a chemical signature, that contributes to the school's collective knowledge of the city's changing wetness.

And they *dream*, if that is the word. In the deep hours, when the thermal currents slow and the mist gathers thick as cotton, the schools settle into *stillness*. They cling to surfaces, to the undersides of terraces, to the walls of sumps where water collects. And in this stillness, they *process*. The chemical signals continue, but slower, deeper, *dreaming* in a metabolism that has no sleep. The Keepers who have learned to sit with them, who have overcome the tickle-tolerance, the strange intimacy of being *among*, report a *quality* to this dreaming. A *mood*. Not consciousness as humans understand it, but something adjacent. A *orientation* toward the world that is not unlike attention, not unlike *caring*.

The largest of the small ones—those that approach the size of a human hand, that can *wrap* around a finger if permitted—are called *ancients* by some, *elders* by others. They are rare. They move more slowly, with a *gravity* that suggests they have outlived the schools that birthed them, that they have become *individuals* in a way their smaller kin have not. The Keepers do not touch them. This is law, or superstition, or *wisdom*—the distinction is unclear. But they watch them. They note their paths. They speak of them in the low voices used for leviathans, for gods, for anything that has *persisted* long enough to become *significant*.

**The Deep-Dwellers**

I mentioned them in passing: those who have been down and come back *changed*. They are not a separate species. They are *humans* who have spent too long in the underwater city, in the flooded levels, in the sumps where the pressure is different and the chemistry has diverged.

The transition is not marked. There is no ceremony, no threshold that can be pointed to. A person descends—to maintain a pump, to retrieve something lost, to *escape* the swarming of a failed level—and spends hours in the wet dark. And comes back *slightly different*. Their skin holds moisture differently. Their eyes adjust to lower light. They speak of pressure in their *bones*, though the pressure is only slightly greater than at the surface, only enough to matter if you stay long enough.

Some stay down. Days. Weeks. The deep-dwelling communities are not numerous, but they are *persistent*. They live in the flooded levels, in the structures that were built for other purposes and have become *habitable* in their submersion. They breathe through apparatus that is not fully understood—part biological, part mineral, *grown* rather than built, cultivated in the specific chemistry of the deep water.

They are *changed* in ways that are not deterioration. Their skin has developed *permeability*, a selective membrane that allows them to *taste* the water, to *read* its chemistry as others read text. Their lungs, or the apparatus that serves as lungs, has learned to extract oxygen from water with an efficiency that surface-dwellers cannot achieve. They can stay submerged for hours. Some, it is said, can stay *indefinitely*, that they have become *amphibious* in a way that is not metaphor but *physiology*.

They do not return to the surface often. When they do, they are *strange* to those who have never descended. They move too slowly, or too *deliberately*, as if still negotiating with pressure. Their voices carry differently, *thicker*, as if speaking through water even when they are not. They are *patient* in a way that surface-dwellers find almost unbearable. They have learned the *tempo* of the deep, and the surface seems *rushed*, *frantic*, *desperate* in its hurry.

But they *witness*. They are the only ones who know the underwater city in any detail. They speak of *gardens* down there—not plants, but the *pre-vegetation* I mentioned, grown in the specific light that penetrates from above, in the *pressure* that shapes growth differently. They speak of *creatures* that have no surface equivalent, that have evolved in the dark, in the pressure, in the chemistry that accumulates in the deepest sumps. They speak of *structures* that predate the surface city, that were built by hands that are not human, or not *only* human, that rise from the silt like *memory*.

And they speak of the *leviathans*. Not with fear—fear is a surface emotion—but with *recognition*. The leviathans are *neighbors* to the deep-dwellers. They pass through the flooded ways, massive and slow, *ancient* in a way that makes the deep-dwellers feel young. The deep-dwellers have learned to *read* them, to know when a leviathan is *passing* and when it is *resting*, when it is *curious* and when it is *indifferent*. They do not interact, not directly. But they *coexist*. They share the underwater city, in a *peace* that is not human peace but something older, something *geological*.

When a deep-dweller dies—and they die, though perhaps more slowly than surface-dwellers—they are not brought up. They are *released* into the deep water, into the sumps, into the places where the leviathans *dream*. This is not disposal. It is *return*. The deep-dwellers have become part of the chemistry of the deep, part of the *food* that sustains the pre-vegetation, the small creatures, the *ecosystem* that exists below the visible world. They have become, in death, what they were in life: *inhabitants* of a world that the surface only *glimpses*.

**The Builders: A Question**

I have spoken of humans, of whatever builds at this scale. But I should be more careful. The city is *old*. Older than any living memory. Older, perhaps, than the species that built it.

The Drip-Keepers maintain. The deep-dwellers inhabit. The creatures *flow*. But who *built*?

There are theories. The Keepers have their histories, copied and recopied, that speak of *migration*—builders who came from the lowlands, from the world of *too much* water, who sought the high ground and found this: a place of *enough*, of *balance*, of water that did not drown but *sustained*. They built upward, away from the rising water, and built *into* the water, learning to make it *part* of their architecture.

But there are older theories. The deep-dwellers speak of *predecessors*, of structures that were *already* submerged when the surface city was young, of *hands* that are not human in the flooded levels. The pre-vegetation, the grey-green colonies that advance a centimeter per year, carries in its chemistry something that does not match any known biology. The leviathans, when they *dream*, sometimes *move* in patterns that resemble *construction*, that suggest *intention* at a scale and duration that human minds cannot fully grasp.

And the water itself. The water *remembers*. It carries, in its molecules, in its dissolved minerals, in its *pressure* and its *temperature*, the history of every world it has been. The Drowned Works is built on a highland plateau, but the highland is *raised*, is a *wound* in the planet's skin. Through it, water seeps *upward*. The water of the Drowned Works is not the water that fell as rain. It is the water that *rose*, that escaped from the depths, that carries with it the *memory* of oceans.

Perhaps the builders are still building. Perhaps the slow weather, the 0.8 dynamic, is not *decay* but *construction*—the city becoming something else, something that will outlast the memory of what came before. Perhaps the creatures, the filter-feeders, the swarming schools, are not *adapting* to the city but *becoming* it, their bodies and their movements and their *dreams* contributing to a *new* architecture, a *living* architecture that will persist when the stone has dissolved, when the metal has corroded, when the last Drip-Keeper has descended and not returned.

The water is patient. The water *waits*. And in its waiting, it *builds*—not with intention, not with design, but with the *persistence* of moisture, of mineral, of *time* itself. The Drowned Works is not a city that was built and will be lost. It is a city that is *being built*, continuously, by everything that inhabits it, by everything that *flows through* it, by the water that remembers and the stone that resists and the strange, slow, *humid* process of becoming something else.

**What Lives Here: A Summary, If Summary Is Possible**

The Drip-Keepers, who listen to water and speak to machines, who maintain a civilization that has outlived its purpose.

The filter-feeders, who flow and dream and *sing* in signatures, who have made the city their *habitat* without ever quite *owning* it.

The deep-dwellers, who have become *amphibious*, who witness the underwater city and are *changed* by what they witness.

The pre-vegetation, patient and grey-green, advancing a centimeter per year toward some *purpose* that does not require urgency.

The leviathans, ancient and rare, who *dream* in the deep sumps and move with the gravity of geological time.

The machines, still turning in the hot dark below, who *listen* and sometimes *answer*, who are the oldest *witness* in a city of witnesses.

And the water itself. The water that remembers. The water that *waits*. The water that is *building*, in its slow, wet, patient way, a city that will outlast every intention of those who built upon it, who built *in* it, who became, in the end, part of its slow, humid, *living* history.

This is what lives in the Drowned Works. Not a single thing. Not even a *type* of thing. A *process*—the process of becoming, of persisting, of *remembering* in a world where memory is carried by water, by stone, by the strange and beautiful *patience* of life that has learned to inhabit the wet.
