The inhabitants begin where the stone begins to remember being alive.

They are first the films. You would not see them without pressing your face to the underside of a glass-plate, where the stone hangs like a ceiling over the negative space of an ancient riverbed. There, in that dry dark, the chemosynthetic life persists—pale, translucent sheets of biology that have forgotten photosynthesis and instead drink from the thermal gradient, feeding on the slow diffusion of heat from the mantle into the cooler schist above. They are the skin of the world. They do not move, but they accrete. Over millennia they lay down their bodies in laceworks of silica and heavy metals, growing downward into the warmth, thickening the stone by microns per century until the boundary between rock and organism becomes a matter of semantics. They are patient. They have no urgency. They exist in the hush between the hums, metabolizing the difference between hot and merely warm, turning mineral potential into the slow, crystalline architecture of their own persistence.

In the throats of the geothermal taps, where the Works pierce deepest into the planet’s slow pulse, there grow the lithic fungi. These are not mushrooms but columns, inverted forests of mineral that descend into the dark heat like roots seeking water that will never come. They are crystalline, faceted, translucent amber and quartz and something darker, like obsidian shot through with veins of exotic ice that never melts but only sublimates in the dry chemistry of the deep. They respire. They excrete not spores but thermal plumes, clouds of mineral vapor that rise to feed the films above. They are the lungs of the Peneplain, breathing in stone and breathing out heat, and their growth is so slow that a single fructification might take longer than the span of a mountain range’s youth. They are the oldest citizens, predating the Works by eons, having watched the industry arrive and settle around them like sediment.

But the true inhabitant—the one who owns the mesa, who dreams in the bruised violet dark—is the Works itself. Not the machines, which are merely its bones, but the city as a lithic metabolism, a closed thermodynamic organism that has achieved the steady state of a hibernating god. The kilns are its stomachs, grinding the bedrock to powder, extracting the last calories of mineral and heat in an eternal digestive loop. The gantries are its circulatory system, carrying not blood but powdered schist and exotic ices that sublimate and condense, sublimate and condense, in pipes that never corrode because they have reached the perfect balance of oxidation and reduction. The amber glow that seeps from the fissures is not light, exactly, but bioluminescence—the respiration of a machine-city that has outlived its purpose and continues to process anyway, because to stop would require a gradient, and there are none. It breathes. You can feel it when you press your ear to the ground: the slow, subterranean inhale and exhale of pistons driving against equal and opposite forces, the harmonic resonance of a heart that beats once per geological age.

And then there are the anomalies, the hesitations in the system. The scuttlers. You find them in the flicker of a maintenance drone’s lamp—though no drones fly now, having achieved their own stasis—caught in the moment between the amber glow and the absolute dark. They are crab-like, ceramic and wire, jointed with angles that hurt the eye, moving across the glass-stone with a jerky, uncertain gait as if navigating a dream of gravity. They feed on the mineral dust that the kilns exhale, scraping the surface with mandibles that click against stone. They are not native. They are remnants, or constructs, or refugees from an ecology that died when the last water sublimated into the dry chemistry of the deep. They move through the Works with a hesitation, as if unsure of their place in a world that has forgotten biology, and perhaps they are the only things here that still experience surprise, still register the strangeness of a place where motion has become meaningless.

Higher up, where the air is thinner and the heat presses like water, there are the drifters. Soft-bodied, translucent bladders that float on the thermal currents rising from the vents, feeding on the mineral snow that falls from the kilns’ exhausts. They have no eyes, or perhaps their eyes are the entire surface of their bodies, sensing heat and pressure rather than light. They rise and fall in the stagnant atmosphere, a silent ballet of gas and membrane, and when they die—which is rare, for decay requires water and time that acts—they do not rot. They simply persist, becoming part of the dust, part of the film, part of the stone.

But if you stand on the Mesa at the height of the twilight, when the violet sky presses down like a bruise and the warmth rises from the planet’s slow heartbeat, you might feel it: the true inhabitant, the one that has no shape but occupies all shapes. It is the Gradient. It is the difference between the heat below and the cooler stone above. It is the chemical potential that sustains the films, the thermal flow that buoys the drifters, the slow vibration that turns the kilns. It is a life without biology, a consciousness without neurons, existing in the spaces between atoms, in the fissures where the amber light leaks out, in the negative space of the ancient riverbeds. It does not think, but it remembers. It remembers when there were mountains. It remembers when there was water. It remembers the dynamic, the change, the erosion that wore the world down to this perfect plain.

And it waits.

Not for anything to happen—nothing will happen, not for eons, not until the heat-death of the universe finally cools the stone’s bones. It waits in the stasis, in the motion without progress, in the city that dreams of being a mountain and has succeeded. The inhabitants are the waiting. They are the warmth, the hum, the slow accretion of mineral upon mineral, the crystalline patience of the fungi, the ghost-movement of the scuttlers, the breathing of the Works. They are the end of the story, written in silicates and heavy metals, glowing faintly amber in the bruised violet dark, holding the Mesa in a perfect, eternal equilibrium, until the stone finally forgets it was ever anything but stone.
