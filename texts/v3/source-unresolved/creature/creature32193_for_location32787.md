What lives here begins with the eating. At the level of the scree, where the sharp young rock bleeds mineral salts into the mist, there are lithotrophs—bacteria and archaea that chew stone, secreting acids that soften the fracture faces into something approaching soil. They are invisible to the eye but visible in effect: a rust-colored film spreading across the black basalt, a greenish patina on the feldspar, the first hesitant crusts that will, in decades, become the valley’s first true dirt. Around them, in the perpetual condensation, colonies of slime-molds pulse with a slow, amoeboid intelligence, drifting across the stone in sheets of phosphorescent yellow, consuming the dead spores that rain down from above.

Above this foundation, the swarms. They are insects, but only in the way that a wave is water—recognizable in substance, alien in arrangement. Their wing-cases are chitinous plates that click together like castanets when they take flight, creating a white noise so dense it feels like pressure against the eardrums. They have too many legs—eight, ten, twelve—jointed in directions that suggest they are still deciding what to become. Their abdomens glow with cold fire, amber and sickly green, and when they darken the shafts of light, the valley becomes a strobe of illumination and shadow. They pollinate the fevered vegetation not with gentleness but with violence, ramming into the flowers, shaking pollen loose in explosive clouds.

The plants themselves are half-animal. The ferns do not merely grow; they *crawl*, their fronds unfurling with audible cracks while their rootlets grip and haul the bulk of the plant forward across the scree, millimeter by millimeter, in a slow migration toward the light. There are vines that hunt. They do not wait for prey to blunder into them; they extend sensitive tendrils that twitch at the heat of a passing body, then—over the course of hours—coil around the warmth, constricting, digesting. They move like sleeping animals, their motions so slow that a watcher might mistake them for stillness, until the realization dawns that the branch has shifted three inches while you blinked. Their leaves are broad and warm to the touch, radiating the fever-heat of their accelerated metabolism, and on their undersides, bioluminescent patches pulse in rhythms that mimic heartbeats, perhaps to attract the swarms, perhaps for no reason at all.

The "birds" are transitional nightmares. They have wings—membranous, veined, leathery—but they are attached to bodies that are too long, too segmented, like a lizard’s spine given flight. They are furred, that is the wrongness of them: patches of sleek, mammalian hair matting the reptilian scales along their flanks. Their eyes are lanterns, large and reflective, glowing with the same bioluminescence as the fungi, and when they move, they do not flap so much as *roar* upward in synchronized waves. Thousands of them lift from the canopy at once, their collective wing-beats a thunder that shakes the leaves, and they wheel through the blue-green twilight in patterns that suggest a hive-mind, a single organism temporarily disaggregated into bodies. They are hunting the swarms, their maws clicking, and their cries are not calls but *sonic weapons*, disorienting pressure waves that stun the insects before the birds descend to feed.

Then there are the herds. They are large—larger than any cow, perhaps larger than an elephant—but low to the ground, sprawling, with too many joints in their legs, folding and unfolding like the legs of a spider or a praying mantis. Their skin is translucent in patches, shimmering with internal phosphorescence, so that in the dimness they appear as drifting constellations of green and blue light, moving through the undergrowth in cyclic migrations that seem to follow the storms. They stampede not in panic but in ritual, their passage triggering landslides of soil and scree, their roar a geological bass note that vibrates in the chest. They eat the fast-growing vegetation, the ferns and the thorn-vines, and they are followed, always, by the parasites, the carrion-eaters, the things that wait in the shadows for the old and the injured to fall behind.

And there are the hominids. They are the ghosts, but they are real, flesh and bone and desperation. They move through the valley in a state of perpetual transit, never lingering, never building. They are adapted to the gloom—their eyes are large, black, and reflective, catching the bioluminescence and throwing it back in twin points of silver light. Their skin is marked with phosphorescent pigments, stripes and dots that glow faintly, allowing them to find each other in the saturated dark. They carry the stone tools—hammerstones, obsidian flakes, bone points—not as weapons of war but as extensions of their urgency. They use them to butcher the herd-beasts that fall behind, to dig for the tubers of the fast-growing plants, to crack open the mineral crusts for the salts within.

They are hunted as much as they hunt. The transitional forms stalk them—the reptilian things with too many joints that slither through the root-mats, the insectile things with fur that drop from the canopy on silken threads. The hominids move in loose bands, perhaps a dozen individuals, and their communication is not speech but signal—flashes of their phosphorescent markings, the striking of stone on stone to create sparks that cut through the roar. Their shouts, when they come, are swallowed by the cacophony, echoing off the knife-edge scarps and returning distorted, as if the valley itself is trying to speak back.

They do not sleep, not truly. There is no stillness here, no night that brings quiet. They rest in shifts, crouching in the lee of the singing rock, listening to the high crystalline chiming of the mineral veins, using it to orient themselves, to find the herds, to time their movements with the convective chaos of the storms. They are learning the valley as it learns itself—racing to understand the patterns of the mists, the migration of the glowing herds, the ripening of the fungal blooms. They are temporary, just as the tools are temporary, dropped in the mud when they break or dull, abandoned in the urgency of the next movement.

What lives here is a question without an answer, a process without a product. It is life in its most frantic adolescence, before the slowing, before the settling, before the death. The hominids, the herd-beasts, the furred flyers, the crawling plants, the stone-eating bacteria—all of them are screaming with the effort of becoming, colonizing a world that is still screaming itself into shape. They do not belong to the valley; they are the valley’s labor, its contractions, its afterbirth. They will change, or they will be washed away by the next storm, the next landslide, the next fissure that opens in the bedrock with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

To live here is to be in motion or to die. There are no elders, only the young and the desperate. There are no homes, only the next shelter from the rain. There is no future, only the immediate, pressing, luminous now—the glow of the fungi, the click of the swarms, the stampede of the herds, the shout of the hominids lost in the roar, all of it racing toward a maturity that may never come, because the earth is still too young, the storms too violent, and the life too loud to ever learn stillness.
