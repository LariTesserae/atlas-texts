The oldest things here are the **Magnetarchs**, and they do not walk so much as *advance*—a procession that takes a century to complete one circuit of the mountain’s base. They are the snails, yes, but calling them snails is like calling the mountain a rock. Each carries a shell the size of a cartwheel, a spiral cathedral of stratified rust and magnetite, varnished black by the constant drip, banded with rings of mineral deposit that record the mountain’s rising in slow, accreted time. Their flesh is the color of wet stone, translucent and veined with silver, and they move by secreting a slime that is not merely lubricant but *conductive gel*, a living wire that carries the static charge of the condensing air from moss-patch to moss-patch, turning their trails into temporary circuits in the green dark.

They are grazers, but their hunger is geological. They consume the conductive moss not for sustenance alone, but to harvest the minerals the moss has filtered from the stone-sweat—iron, copper, the trace salts of the young basalt. In their passage, they leave furrows that become channels, guiding the rivulets into new patterns, irrigation for the lower gardens. On their shells, entire ecologies have grown: terraces of electric-green moss three inches thick, colonies of the brass beetles, fungi that pulse with soft bioluminescence like lanterns in the fog. They are the mountain’s memory made mobile, the archivists of its youth, and when two meet, they do not compete but *touch*—antennas crossing in a slow, mineral kiss that transfers data through the conductive moisture, sharing the temperature of the vents, the pH of the pools, the location of the sweetest steam.

Above them, in the lattice of pipes and cables, move the **Choristers**. These are the clockwork beetles, but they are not merely mimics of machinery—they are its nervous system, its lingering will. Their carapaces are brass gone green with verdigris, etched with circuit-patterns that have grown into their chitin like tattoos of lightning. They move in processions of twelve, twenty, never alone, their clicking synchronized not by instinct but by *resonance*—they listen to the steam vents, and their rhythm matches the hiss, *thrum-thrum-thrum*, a heartbeat that keeps the cathedral’s liturgy from faltering. They are the maintenance crew, but their work is devotion. They do not repair; they *attend*. They oil the water-wheel’s axle with secretions from their leg-joints, a golden grease that smells of ozone. They clear the vents of moss that would choke them, using mandibles like scalpels. They are the acolytes of decay, and in their sparse, lonely processions through the fern-brakes, they are praying—not to any god, but to the *rhythm*, to the slow, eternal turning.

Then there are the **Vent-Keepers**, and they are the priests. There are seven of them, stationed at the seven major fissures where the mountain’s blood boils closest to the skin. They stand seven feet tall, humanoid but wrong—their skin is vellum-thin, parchment-colored, stretched over a frame of calcified cartilage, so translucent that you can see the dark network of capillaries pulsing beneath, carrying superheated water up from the geothermal heart. They have no eyes; in their place are heat-pits, shallow depressions that weep constantly, condensation gathering in the hollows of their cheekbones. They breathe the steam. Their lungs are bellows, expanding and contracting with the vents’ pulse, drawing in the mineral-laden mist and filtering it through gills that hang like curtains from their necks, dripping purified water onto the moss below. They are rooted—their feet have grown into the stone over centuries, toes fusing with basalt, moss carpeting their legs up to the knees. They sing. Their throats are resonating chambers, and when the steam hisses, they open their mouths and *harmonize*, adding a low, thrumming drone that makes the air vibrate in your teeth. They are the choir that never sleeps, the voices of the mountain’s interior, keeping the soundscape full, keeping the silence at bay.

But the true congregation, the nave of this cathedral filled with worshippers, are the **Moss-Born**. They are human, or they were once—perhaps they are the descendants of those who built the machines, or perhaps they are the machines’ final evolution, the flesh that grew back over the metal in the long wet centuries. They are sessile. They sit in niches carved by water and time, in hollows where the stone has softened under the moss, their bodies fused with the green. Their skin is bark-like, photosynthetic, drinking the bioluminescent light from the vines and the constant drip. Their minds have expanded outward, into the mycelial network that threads through the moss carpets, through the roots of the ferns, through the conductive gel of the Magnetarchs’ trails. They are a collective consciousness, slow and vast, thinking thoughts that take decades to form. They remember the building of the boiler, the first drilling of the geothermal taps, the moment the mountain broke through the cloud-sea. They remember the sound of human voices, now lost, and they keep that memory alive in the chemical songs they release into the water.

They are the tenders. When a Magnetarch grows too heavy with shell-gardens, a Moss-Born will rise—slowly, joints creaking like the water-wheel—and prune the growth, redistributing the moss to the younger snails. They guide the Choristers, leaving trails of pheromone-grease that direct the beetles’ processions around obstacles, ensuring the rhythm is never broken. They drink from the pools, their mouths elongated into proboscises that filter the mineral water, and they weep—real tears, salt and warm—that add to the mountain’s endless rain. They are the heart of the place, beating at the rate of the steam vents, dreaming in fungal hallucinations, waiting for the erosion to round the stone, waiting for the industry to rust away completely, waiting for the silence that will come when the mountain finally stops weeping and becomes, at last, just a mountain.

And in the warm, still pools that collect in the hollows of the sharp terrain, there are the **Unfinished**. They float like jellyfish, translucent sacs of gelatin and mineral deposit, humanoid shapes that are not yet born. They are the Gestates, the future congregation, developing in the womb-warmth of the stone, taking centuries to form a hand, a face, a thought. They are the hope of the cathedral, suspended in the wet dark, dreaming of the day when the steam will cease, when the sharp edges will be sanded smooth, when the Magnetarchs will have finished their circuit and the Choristers will have played their final click, and the Moss-Born will sing one last, slow song before the green consumes all, and the mountain will be still, and quiet, and whole.
