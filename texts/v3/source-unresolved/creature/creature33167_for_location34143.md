I need to describe who lives in the Condensation Works. The place itself is already so vividly realized—the people must emerge from it, shaped by its conditions, not merely placed within it.

---

## The Inhabitants of the Low Quarter

They do not have a name for themselves, or rather, they have too many. The condensation gives them *names* as it gives everything: a pump-operator who has lost three fingers to a corroded valve is called **Drip-Mouth**, because he speaks with a wet sound, because water collects in the hollow where his teeth used to be. A woman who farms sinter-borers in the eastern platforms is **She-Who-Feeds-Worms**, or **Mother-of-Calcium**, or simply **The Farmer**, title enough. Children are named for the hour they were born: **Near-Dawn**, **Deep-Heat**, **When-The-Pumps-Pauseed**. Names are *functional* here. They describe what you do, what the city has made of you, what you have made yourself into in return.

They are **small**, as the description notes, but not diminished—*concentrated*. The humidity compresses. The diet, heavy on mineral and protein and lacking in the dry starches that build height, settles them low to the sinter. They are **broad-shouldered**, **short-legged**, built for the crouch of walking beneath pipe-arches, for the balance required on slick mineral surfaces. Their **feet are wide**, the toes splayed, learned over generations to grip wet stone. Their **hands are large**, the fingers thick and perpetually wrinkled, *pruned* by constant moisture—not the soft wrinkles of a bath, but the deep creases of skin that has never known dryness, that has adapted to *function* in it, to find purchase, to hold tools, to catch mist-frogs, to feel the temperature of a pump housing and know from its sweat whether it will hold or fail.

Their **skin** is the color of the place: gray-white, the white of the scale, the gray of wet brick, with patches of **pink** where the heat has opened capillaries permanently, where the blood shows through skin that has grown thin with damp. They do not tan. There is no sun to tan them. Their skin simply *is*—moist, marked, carrying the stains of their work: rust from pipes, green from algae, the pale scars of sinter-borer bites, the dark patches where fungal spores have colonized a scratch, a cut, a place where the city has entered them and *stayed*.

They **smell**, because everyone smells here, but their smell is *specific*: the yeast of skin that never dries, the mineral of sweat evaporating and re-evaporating on their clothes, the particular **sweetness** of condensation that they have learned to recognize as *clean*, as *pure*, as the taste of survival. They smell of **moss** and **scale**, because they *are* moss and scale now, grown into their hair, their nails, the creases of their elbows where the wet collects and something *grows*. Some of the older ones have **white patches** in their beards, in their eyebrows—actual lichen, the city's own scale, cultivated on their bodies, harvested from their bodies, a symbiosis so complete that they cannot say where they end and the Quarter begins.

They **sweat** constantly. This is not discomfort. It is *communication*. The sweat on their brow tells others they are working, that they are hot, that they have been exerting themselves. A wet palm pressed to a dry one—rare, precious—is a greeting, a comfort, a *sharing*. They collect their own sweat in the folds of scale-roofing, feed it to the walls, participate in the cycle. The oldest among them have learned to **taste** their own sweat, to read it: salt means hydration, means survival; the absence of salt means danger, means the body is failing, means *drink*, *drink*, *drink* before the condensation itself turns against you.

### The Castes of Water

There is no government in the Low Quarter, not in any form that would be recognized. There is **order**, however, and order has its *shapes*.

The **Pump-Keepers** are closest to priests. They tend the heart of the city, the machines that move water against its will, that maintain the pressure differentials, that *are* the circulation. They are **pale**, paler than others, because they work in the shadow of the pipe-arches, in the cool damp of the lower levels where the pumps stand. They know the **sounds** of the machines as others know faces: the particular *hiss* of a seal beginning to fail, the *knock* that means cavitation, the *silence* that means power loss, *death*. They are **revered** and **feared**, because they can shut off the water, because they can *redirect* it, because in the Low Quarter, water is life and they hold the valves. They wear **rubber**, the precious stuff, harvested from the fungal networks, and their hands are always wet with the condensation of the machines they tend, with the sweat of their own exertion, with the *water* that is their god and their work and their sentence.

The **Tower-Climbers** are **young**, mostly, because the work kills. They scale the cooling towers, the great white columns, to clear the collection vanes, to repair the condensation plates, to *breathe* for the machines that cannot breathe for themselves. They are **lean**, wiry, with **strong arms** and **callused hands** and **eyes** that have learned to see through the steam, the mist, the permanent gray. They come down **changed**: their skin softer, somehow, from the steam; their hair white with mineral deposits; their **hearing** damaged by the *hum* of the towers, a constant tone that drowns all other sound. Some of them do not come down. The towers take them, as they take everything, as the Quarter takes everything, *eventually*. Their names are carved into the sinter at the tower bases, the only grave they will have.

The **Sinter-Farmers** work the ground itself, cultivating the worms that build the city, guiding their tunnels, harvesting them for food and for the mineral they process. They are **patient**, more patient than others, because sinter works on its own time, the time of geological slowness, of drops accumulating. They are **muscular**, from the digging, the hauling, the constant crouch of working at ground level. They know the **layers** of the sinter, can read its history in its striations, can predict where a tunnel will collapse, where the ground will hold, where the city is *growing* and where it is *dying*. They are **rich**, in the coin of the Quarter: they control the food supply, the protein, the *building* of the place itself. They live on the **highest habitable platforms**, above the worst of the damp, where they can watch the channels flow and *direct* them, shape the growth of their domain.

The **Scale-Harvesters** work the walls, the white lichen-industry, cutting sheets of the biological-mineral composite, processing it, applying it to new surfaces. They are **artists**, in the way that any craft becomes art when practiced in extremity. They know the **patterns** of growth, can encourage it, can *carve* it, can create walls that breathe in particular ways, that weep in particular places, that *insulate* against the cold or *conduct* the heat, depending on the need. They are **covered** in the stuff, their clothes stiff with scale, their hair white, their skin *textured* with patches where the lichen has taken hold and *stayed*. They are **slow**, deliberate in movement, because they have learned that the scale responds to pressure, to patience, to *time*. They are **mystics**, some say, because they work with something that is *alive*, that grows, that *chooses* where to grow, and they have learned to *negotiate*.

The **Frog-Catchers** and **Moth-Keepers** and **Algae-Tenders** are the **poor**, the **low**, the ones who work the living things that the city tolerates but does not *need*, not in the way it needs pumps and sinter and scale. They are **numerous**, because there are always more of them, because the mist-frogs breed faster than they can be caught, because the condensation-moths come to any light, any heat, *endlessly*. They live in the **lowest levels**, closest to the channels, where the water is warmest, where the air is thickest, where the sound of the frogs is *deafening*. They are **wild**, in the way that the Quarter allows wildness: they follow the *cycles* of the creatures, not the *schedules* of the city, they eat what they catch, they sleep when the frogs sleep, they *become* frog and moth themselves, in their dampness, in their noise, in their brief, desperate lives.

And then there are the **Others**, the ones who do not fit, who have *fallen* through the categories, who exist in the **spaces between**: the **leak-counters**, who find the drips, the seeps, the failures, and *report* them, and are paid in water-rations for their vigilance; the **dry-seekers**, who search for the rare places where the sinter is thick enough, the scale dense enough, to hold a pocket of *less* humidity, and sell access to them, sell *shelter* from the wet; the **memory-keepers**, who remember the world above the condensation, who tell stories of dry ground, of solid air, of *silence*, and are listened to, sometimes, and paid in food, in warmth, in the *attention* of those who have never known anything else.

### The Bodies of the Quarter

The people of the Low Quarter are **adapted** in ways that would be *deformity* anywhere else.

Their **lungs** are **larger**, more efficient, able to extract oxygen from air that is already half water. They **cough** constantly, a wet cough, the *clearing* of the passages, and the phlegm they produce is **thick**, **mineral-laden**, sometimes *cultivated*, used to feed the algae-farms, the fungal networks, returned to the cycle. They **breathe** differently: shallow, rapid, *always*, because deep breathing in this humidity is *work*, is *effort*, is *waste* of the energy they need to survive.

Their **ears** are **strange**: the eardrums thickened by constant pressure changes, the canals *narrowed* by the growth of fungal colonies that have found purchase in the perpetual damp. They hear **differently**—better at low frequencies, at the *thrum* of the pumps, at the *hum* of the mist-frogs, worse at high frequencies, at the *sharp* sounds that would warn of danger in a dry place. The oldest among them are *deaf* to human speech, can only hear the city, the machines, the *wet*.

Their **eyes** are **large**, the pupils perpetually dilated against the gray light, the irises *pale*, washed out, the color of old sinter. They see **poorly** in the diffuse light, but *well* in the dark, in the true night when the towers pause, when the dome of condensation thins, when the stars—*if there are stars*—might show through. They have learned to **navigate by sound**, by the *echo* of their footsteps on sinter, by the *resonance* of the pipes, by the *silence* of a sinter-hawk passing overhead.

Their **digestive systems** are **adapted** to the diet: the sinter-borers, rich in protein and mineral; the algae, the fungal fruiting bodies, the mist-frogs caught and cooked; the condensation itself, *distilled*, *pure*, the only water that does not make them sick. They **lack** the enzymes to process dry food, the grains, the meats of the world above. A sinter-farmer who eats bread becomes *ill*, swells with water her body cannot process, *dies* in the damp. They are **dependent**, utterly, on the Quarter, on its cycles, on its *wet*. They cannot leave. They do not *want* to leave. The world above is *myth*, is *death*, is the dry that would kill them as surely as the cold kills the mist-frogs.

Their **reproduction** is **strange**: the humidity *suppresses* fertility, the constant moisture confuses the signals, and so the birth rate is *low*, the population *stable*, held in balance by the high infant mortality, by the deaths of the tower-climbers, by the *consumption* of the damp, the slow poisoning of the body by the very water that sustains it. The women **carry** longer, the pregnancies extended, the babies born *small*, *wet*, *adapted* from the first breath. They are **rare**, the children, and **precious**, and **watched**, and when one dies—as many do, as the Quarter takes its due—the grief is *private*, *wet*, *silent*, because to wail is to waste water, to waste *sound*, to waste the *energy* that the living need to survive.

They **age** differently. The sinter accumulates in their joints, *calcifies* them, makes movement *painful*, *slow*. The scale grows on their skin, *thickens*, insulates them against the cold they will never feel. Their hearing fails, their sight dims, their lungs *fill* with the mineral they have breathed, and they **sink**, literally, into the sinter, become *part* of it, their bodies *recycled* into the ground they worked, the city they built, the *wet* that was their life. The oldest among them are **shapes**, barely human, *mounds* of scale and mineral and fungal growth, still *breathing*, still *sweating*, still *alive* in the way that the city is alive, and when they finally stop, they are *buried* in the sinter, become *foundation*, become *ground*, become *place*.

### The Life of the Quarter

They **wake** to the sound of the pumps, the *shift* of the towers, the beginning of the daily cycle. They do not use clocks—there are no clocks, not really, time is measured in *water*, in the flow of the channels, in the *drip* of condensation from the pipe-arches, in the *humidity* of the air, heavy or light, *breathable* or *thick*.

They **work** in the levels assigned to them, the Pump-Keepers in the damp dark of the lower pipes, the Tower-Climbers on the white heights, the Sinter-Farmers in the crunching mineral, the Scale-Harvesters on the weeping walls. They work in **shifts**, because the city never stops, because the condensation never ceases, because there is *always* water to move, heat to manage, *life* to maintain. They work **together**, in the way that people work together when the world is too loud for solitude, too wet for isolation, too *small* for privacy. They sing, sometimes, songs without words, *hums* that match the frequency of the pumps, that travel through the pipes, that *communicate* across distance, across the channels, across the *levels*.

They **eat** in the habitation levels, in spaces that are never dry, where the walls weep and the floors *tilt* toward drains. They eat **slowly**, because the food is *dense*, mineral-heavy, takes *work* to digest. They eat **together**, sharing plates, sharing *mouthfuls*, the wet contact of lips on shared spoons, the *intimacy* of survival. They drink the condensation, collected from the walls, *purified* by the city's endless recycling, *tasting* of metal and mineral and the *cleanliness* of distilled water. They drink **often**, because the humidity *deceives*, because the body loses water constantly through the skin, through the breath, and must *replace* it, must *participate* in the cycle, must *become* water again.

They **sleep** in hammocks, as the description says, strung between pipes, between walls, *above* the sinter, above the damp, above the *cold* that seeps up from the mineral ground. They sleep **lightly**, because the city is *loud*, because the mist-frogs are *singing*, because the pumps are *humming*, because to sleep deeply is to *miss* something, to *fail* to hear the warning, the shift, the *change* that might mean survival or death. They **dream** of dry places, of solid ground, of *silence*, and wake **weeping**, the condensation on their faces indistinguishable from tears, and *drink* it, *recycle* it, *return* it to the body that needs it.

They **love** in the wet, in the *dark*, in the spaces between the levels where the light is *least*, where the sound is *most* muffled, where they can *touch* without being *seen*, without being *heard*. Their love is **physical**, *urgent*, *brief*—because the Quarter takes everything, because there is no *privacy*, no * permanence*, no *promise* that the next cycle will not separate them, will not *kill* one or the other, will not *change* the ground beneath them, the walls around them, the *water* within them. They love **fast**, and **hard**, and **without hope**, because hope is *dry*, is *above*, is *elsewhere*, and they are *here*, in the wet, in the heat, in the *now* that is all they have, all they will ever have.

They **die** as they have lived: *wet*, *warm*, *loud*. The Pump-Keeper dies at his station, his hand on the valve, his body *sinking* into the damp floor, *becoming* part of the machine he served. The Tower-Climber dies on the height, falls or *jumped* or *taken* by the steam, and is *found*, days later, at the base, *white* with scale, *still* with the mineral of the towers, *returned* to the Quarter that made him. The Sinter-Farmer dies in his tunnel, the sinter *collapsing*, *burying* him, *preserving* him, *becoming* him in the slow accretion of the ground. The Frog-Catcher dies in the channel, the warm water *carrying* her downstream, *dissolving* her, *distributing* her, *recycling* her into the system that *fed* her, that *killed* her, that *is* her now, always, *forever*.

They do not **mourn** as others mourn. There are no funerals, no graves, no *markers* except the ones the city makes: the name carved in sinter, the patch of scale that grows differently where a body has rested, the *silence* in the chorus where a voice has been *removed*. They mourn by **working**, by *continuing*, by *participating* in the cycle that took their dead and *uses* them still, *transforms* them, *keeps* them *alive* in the wet, in the heat, in the *endless now* of the Low Quarter.

### The Children of Condensation

The children are **born** wet, *slimy*, *covered* in the white vernix of their adaptation, the *scale* that will protect them, that has already begun to *grow*. They are **washed** in condensation, *fed* on algae and fungal broth, *grown* in the humid dark of the habitation levels, never knowing dry, never knowing *solid*, never knowing the *light* that is not diffused, the *air* that is not heavy, the *silence* that is not filled with the sound of water, of machines, of *life*.

They **learn** by *doing*: the child of a Pump-Keeper learns the *sounds* of the machines, learns to *hear* the difference between *healthy* and *failing*, between *soon* and *now*, between *water* and *death*. The child of a Tower-Climber learns *height*, learns to *trust* the steam, to *read* the mist, to *climb* before she can *walk* properly, because the towers are *high* and the ground is *wet* and the city *needs* her. The child of a Sinter-Farmer learns *patience*, learns to *wait* for the worms to grow, to *guide* their tunnels, to *harvest* without *destroying*, to *build* with the *slow* mineral, the *patient* stone. The child of a Scale-Harvester learns *touch*, learns to *feel* the growth of the lichen, to *encourage* it, to *carve* it, to *become* one with the walls, the *breathing*, the *weeping* surfaces of the city.

They are **small**, smaller than the children of the dry world, because the humidity *compresses*, because the diet *lacks*, because the *weight* of the air *presses* them down, *settles* them into the sinter, *shapes* them into the *form* of the Quarter, the *low*, the *broad*, the *adapted*. They are **pale**, paler than their parents, because they have not yet *worked*, have not yet *sweated*, have not yet accumulated the *stains* of survival. They are **wet**, always wet, their skin *glistening*, their hair *dripping*, their clothes *clinging*, *molding* to their bodies, *growing* into them, *becoming* part of them.

They **play** in the channels, in the warm runoff, catching mist-frogs, chasing condensation-moths, *drowning* in the shallows and *laughing*, because the water is *warm*, is *safe*, is *home*. They play with the sinter-borers, the *worms* that build the city, feeding them, *guiding* them, learning the *taste* of the mineral, the *feel* of the ground *moving* beneath their feet. They play in the pipe-arches, in the *shadow*, in the *cool*, climbing on the rusted infrastructure, *listening* to the *rush* of water overhead, *feeling* the *vibration* of the pumps in their *chests*, *learning* the city as a body, as a *self*, as *them*.

They **fear** the night, the true night, when the towers pause, when the pumps stop, when the *silence* comes and the *dark* is *real*, not shadow but *absence*. They fear it because the mist-frogs *die* in it, the cold that the towers have held at bay *returns*, the channel water *cools*, and they have heard the stories, the *memories*, of the weekly silence, the *death* that comes with it, the *waiting* for the machines to *resume*, the *prayer* that the city will *breathe* again. They fear it because in the true night, the sinter-hawks *hunt* by sight, because the condensation-moths *die* in the cold, because the city *stops*, and the stopped city is *hungry*, is *dangerous*, is *not home* but *other*, *strange*, *unadapted*.

They **dream** of the world above. Not because they have *been* there—their parents have not been there, their parents' parents have not been there, the memory is *myth*, is *story*, is *hope*—but because the Pump-Keepers *speak* of it, the Tower-Climbers *describe* it, the memory-keepers *tell* of it, the dry, the solid, the *light*, the *silence*, the *sky* that is not a dome. They dream of it and **wake** weeping, and the weeping is *condensation*, is *water*, is *home*, and they *drink* it, and they *grow*, and they *become* the Quarter, as their parents became it, as their parents' parents became it, as the city *makes* them, *always*, *endlessly*, *now*.

### The Last Thing

There is a woman, old now, *ancient*, who remembers when the towers were *built*, when the Quarter was *new*, when the condensation was *engineered* rather than *inherited*. She is **called** The First Drip, though she was not literally first, though many women lived before her, worked before her, *adapted* before her. She is called this because she was *born* in the first year of the towers, in the first *summer* of the condensation, in the first *wet* that was *designed*, *controlled*, *made*.

She is **small**, smaller than the others, *shrunken* by age, by the mineral that has *filled* her joints, by the scale that has *covered* her skin, by the *weight* of the water she has breathed, drunk, *become*. She is **blind**, her eyes *milky* with cataracts grown in the diffuse light, in the *humidity*, in the *lack* of true darkness, true rest for the eyes. She is **deaf**, her ears *closed* by fungal growth, by mineral *deposits*, by the *silence* that has finally *come*, the silence of the body *failing*, the city *continuing* without her, *beyond* her, *after* her.

She **lives** in a hammock, strung between two pipes in one of the habitation levels, a place that is *warm*, that is *damp*, that is *hers*, that has been *hers* for longer than anyone else can remember. She **eats** what is brought to her: algae, fungal broth, the cooked meat of sinter-borers, the *condensation* that the others collect from the walls, *purify*, *offer* to her as they would offer *prayer*, *sacrifice*, *love*. She **drinks** it, *tastes* it, *remembers* the taste of the first water, the first *engineered* condensation, the first *drop* that fell from the first tower and *began* everything.

She **speaks**, sometimes, when the pumps are *loud* enough to *carry* her voice, when the mist-frogs are *singing* and her words join their *chorus*. She speaks of the *building*, of the *engineers* who came from above, who *designed* the towers, who *calculated* the differentials, who *knew* what they were making. She speaks of the **first workers**, the ones who *dug* the channels, who *raised* the pipes, who *died* in the first summers, before the adaptation, before the *learning*, before the Quarter was *home* rather than *experiment*. She speaks of the **first children**, born wet, born *strange*, born *adapted* or *dying*, the ones who *lived*, who *grew*, who *became* the people she is now, the *last* of them, the *memory* of them.

She speaks of the **dry**, the world above, the *myth* that is also *history*, the *past* that is also *future*, the *place* that *made* this place, that *abandoned* it, that *forgot* it, that *remembers* it only in *stories*, in *dreams*, in the *hope* that the condensation will *end*, that the towers will *fail*, that the *rain* will come, true rain, *falling*, *finished*, *gone*, and the Quarter will *dry*, will *end*, will *become* something else, something *new*, something *unknown*.

She does not **want** this. She says she does not want it, though her voice is *weak*, her words *fading*, her *meaning* uncertain. She wants the *wet*, the *heat*, the *sound*, the *now*, the *endless* cycle that has been her *life*, her *body*, her *self*. She wants the *condensation*, the *drip*, the *weeping* of the city, the *breathing* of the towers, the *humming* of the pumps, the *singing* of the frogs, the *silence* of the true night, the *return* of the machines, the *beginning* again, always *beginning*, never *ending*, never *finished*, never *dry*.

She **waits**, as they all wait, for the towers to cycle down, for the night to come, for the *dark*, the *silence*, the *cold* that is also *peace*, also *rest*, also *end*. She waits for it as she has waited for everything: *wet*, *patient*, *adapted*, *ready*. And when it comes, if it comes, she will *feel* it first—the change in pressure, the shift in sound, the *cooling* of the air, the *thinning* of the dome—and she will *smile*, her milky eyes *closed*, her deaf ears *hearing* nothing, her wrinkled face *wet* with condensation, with sweat, with *tears* that are also *water*, also *home*, also *her*, also *the Quarter*, also *everything*, also *now*.

And then she will *sleep*, the true sleep, the dry sleep, the *end* that is also *beginning*, and the Quarter will *continue*, will *breathe*, will *weep*, will *wait*, will *be*, *without* her, *with* her, *as* her, *forever*, *always*, *now*.

---

*The condensation drips. The pumps hum. The mist-frogs begin their evening chorus. The towers shift, almost imperceptibly, their permutation adjusted for the cooling night, the coming of the brief, precious dark. The city breathes. The city waits. The city is.*
