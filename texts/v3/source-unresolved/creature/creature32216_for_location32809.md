They are the Wiry, though they have many names in the tongues that barely exist here—the Reed-Binders, the Bone-Lashers, the Tier-Dwellers. They are small because the space is small; they are dense and compact because the air is thick, a soup they must swim through as much as breathe. Their fur is the blue-gray of lichen and distant ice, grown thick and sodden against a cold that does not bite but clings, a second skin of humidity that matches their own.

They build. But they do not build to last. They build to be unmade. Each morning—if morning can be said to exist in a place where the merciless white light never shifts—they rise from their huddles and begin the work of lashing gut around pale bone, weaving reed around reed, knowing with absolute certainty that by dusk the amber resin will have wept its tears, the joints will have loosened in the damp, and the wall they raised will begin its slow unraveling into mist. They do not mourn this. They have no word for grief in a world that has never known permanence. Instead, they have a gesture—a pressing of palm to wet stone—that means *returning*, that acknowledges the wall's transformation as completion rather than failure.

Their language is not spoken. The silence is too complete, the saturated air too absorbent; words would emerge damp and useless, dissolving before they reached another's ear. Instead, they communicate through pressure and vibration. A hand on the shoulder means *I am here, the space is narrow*. A brush of fur against fur means *the drifter approaches*. They have developed a tactile grammar as complex as any tongue, allowing them to navigate shoulder-width corridors without collision, to coordinate the raising of a bone-beam in the dimness, to feel the emotional state of a neighbor through the shared wall. Their tools are knapped flint for cutting, and antler for listening. They press the antler against stone and wood and feel the pulse of the place—the upward rush of memory-laden water, the slow crystallization of ice lace, the metabolic thrum of the gelatinous life that shares their walls.

Then there are the Drifters—the Translucent, the Curtains, the Calcium-Weavers. They are not animals, exactly, though they metabolize and die. They are not plants, though they photosynthesize in the flat white light and grow toward the moisture. They are the middle kingdom, the condensation given form and intent. Some are no larger than a fist, drifting through the lower tiers like bubbles of slow intention, secreting a calcium milk that dries to the hardness of bone, reinforcing the walls, caulking the gaps between reed and gut. They are the masons, working without hands, extruding living architecture from their own gelatinous bodies. Others are vast, hanging between the levels like living membranes, translucent curtains that filter the air, absorbing the excess humidity, their bodies rippling with peristalsis too slow to track. When they die—and they die often, their forms too fragile for the turbulence—they do not fall. They dissolve. They become mortar, they become the resin that weeps from the beams, they become the damp that clings to the builder's fur.

The relationship between the Wiry and the Drifters is symbiosis absolute. The builders provide the skeleton—the bone, the reed, the structural ambition—and the Drifters provide the flesh, the calcium, the living substance. But the Drifters are also the gardeners. They tend the pale lichens that cling to the north faces of the stone, the stubborn fungi that stain the water green. They carry spores in their transparent bodies, depositing them in the hollows where the rock weeps. And they are the memory. When a builder drinks from the upward-stream, defying gravity, they taste not only the future rain but the past—the accumulated dead of generations of Drifters, their calcium and their silence, recycled into the water, flowing up to be consumed again. Nothing is lost. Everything cycles through the soup.

The Wiry live in the tiers, stacked like the architecture itself. A family might occupy a volume no larger than a closet, walls touching walls, floor meeting ceiling in the next breath. Privacy is a concept they have forgotten, or perhaps they never developed it, for near-touching is the only possible state. They sleep curled against the wet stone or against the warm, pulsing bodies of the Drifters, using the gelatinous creatures as thermal batteries against the cold. Their young are born already small, already furred, already accustomed to the density. They learn to walk in the vertical spaces, to climb the bone-lattices, to move through the curtain-Drifters as one moves through water.

They have no fixed names for individuals. A person is identified by their current location, their current task, the Drifter they currently tend. You might be called *Tier-Three-North* in the morning, and by dusk, when the wall unravels and you are forced to migrate, you are *The Mist-Born* or *Unfinished* or *The One-Who-Waits-For-The-Shadow*. Time for them moves in eddies, not lines. They do not measure days by sunrise and sunset—the sun is constant, the light never varying—but by the cycles of build and unbuild, by the flow of the upward water, by the drift of the curtains. They are not impatient. They have no concept of permanence to chafe against. They exist in the eternal present of the Weeping, where the future rain is already falling upward, and the past is dissolving in the mortar.

And then there are the shadows. The flattened, merciless light casts shadows that move independently of their sources, sketching architectures that do not yet exist, walls that will be built tomorrow or perhaps were built yesterday. The Wiry believe these are the blueprints of the Drifters, or the ghosts of structures pressing backward through time. When a shadow falls across a builder's path, they pause. They touch the wall. They feel for the vibration of what is to come. They build toward the shadows, following the independent movements of the darkness as if reading a map written in negative space.

In the central chamber, where the air is thickest and the silence most profound, an elder might sit—if sitting is possible in the density—surrounded by the drift. The elder's fur is patchy now, thinning with age, revealing skin that is pale and translucent as the Drifters themselves. They are becoming one with the place. Soon, they will no longer leave the chamber. Soon, they will begin to secrete calcium. Soon, they will be part of the wall, part of the weeping resin, part of the upward-flowing memory. The young builders come to them, not to learn words (for the elder has long since stopped speaking, or perhaps never spoke, communicating only in pressure and touch), but to press the antler against the elder's chest, to feel the slow pulse of a heart that beats once per hour, synchronized with the drip of water, the growth of ice, the drift of the curtains.

The gelatinous fauna drift past, silent and absolute. They touch the elder's face with a cold that is not unkind, only inevitable. And the elder smiles, or what passes for a smile in a face that is gradually becoming architecture, and the city breathes around them—wet, bright, and slow—holding its inhabitants in suspension, neither living nor dying, but simply *being*, in the vertical marsh where water flows up and walls fall down and the light reveals everything while hiding nothing.
