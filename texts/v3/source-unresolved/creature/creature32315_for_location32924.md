They are not numerous in the way that numbers are counted in dry places. Here, population is measured by liters processed, by condensation harvested, by the weight of water a body can carry through the vertical dark. They move through the terraced mass like nutrients through a circulatory system—necessary, invisible, everywhere at once.

Their bodies have reached an equilibrium with the wet. Their skin is perpetually damp, not from exertion but from osmotic balance with the saturated air; it has developed a secondary membrane, a thin film of cultivated algae that buds in the creases of elbows, behind ears, along the spine—a living wetsuit that filters the metallic high-altitude air, that breathes for them when the atmosphere grows too thin. It is emerald and faintly phosphorescent, glowing in the perpetual blue-gray twilight, marking them as native, as belonging to the chemistry of the place. They are short, many of them, or hunched—adapted to the low ceilings, the terraced density, the constant necessity of ducking beneath pipes and fog-nets. Their feet are broad and splayed, gripping the sweating grating, their toes webbed slightly from generations of wetness.

They do not speak, or not in the way sound intends. The mist is a blanket; voices die within arm’s length, absorbed by the saturated air and the soft, dripping surfaces. Instead, they have developed a language of pressure and moisture. A palm pressed flat against a steam pipe transmits the subsonic thrum of the condensers, news from the lower levels, the heartbeat of the machinery. A touch on the shoulder—wet, insistent—means *I am here, the path is safe, the water is rising*. They have cultured bioluminescent patches on their wrists, throats, and temples—strains of algae harvested from the processing tanks—flashing slow codes in the gray. When they move through the mist, they are constellations, brief bright points that vanish and reappear three meters away.

They are the Keepers of the Cycle. Each tends a specific node in the endless loop of evaporation and precipitation. There are the Mist-Catchers, who climb the tallest towers to shake the fog-nets, letting the water sheet down in silver curtains into the aqueducts. There are the Distillers, who work in the steam-houses, faces obscured by respirators that filter the sharp air, hands perpetually scalded and cooled, tending the great condenser units that hum below the threshold of hearing. There are the Gardeners of the Vertical, who rappel down the hydroponic stacks on ropes of woven mycelium, pruning ferns, harvesting leafy greens, their bodies suspended in the dripping dark between seventeen levels of filtration. There are the Miners of the Mineral, who chip away at the calcium curtains and salt stalactites that grow slowly, measurably, over years—harvesting the deposits that would otherwise choke the drains, turning the city’s own accretion into resource.

They are ancient in their lineage. The settlement has been here long enough that no one remembers the original blueprints; the people have been here just as long. Their children are inoculated at birth with the symbiotic algae cultures, the moss budding in their skin folds before they draw their first breath. They dream in the rhythm of the dripping, in the systolic pulse of the thermal machinery. Their lungs have adapted to extract oxygen from the saturated atmosphere, to pull moisture and metal and ozone from the weeping air. They do not cough; they do not sneeze. They simply *are*, in a state of weathered balance, their bodies as much a part of the architecture as the rust blooming in streaks down the facades.

They are distributed, a nervous system spread through the anthill-density of the city. One person’s waste is another’s breakfast; one person’s maintenance is another’s survival. They are connected by the water that flows between them—open channels carrying jade-green algae films, pneumatic tubes hissing with capsules of compressed air and matter, electrified rails that carry carts of biomass between levels. The boundary has dissolved not merely between machine and organism, but between human and city. They are the city’s immune system, its digestive tract, its dreaming mind.

In the deepest levels, near the fermentation vats and the bio-reactors that bubble with engineered cultures, you might find the Old Ones. They have lived so long that the moss grows thick on their shoulders like a mantle; their hair has become a tangled ecosystem of lichens and the pale, fungus-colored moths that drift through the mist. Their eyes are milky with cataracts grown from mineral deposits; their skin is mapped with calcified rivulets that mirror the paths water has chosen on the terrace above. They sit motionless, monitoring the bubbling cultures, their chests rising and falling in time with the steam vents. They are the memory of the place. They do not speak. They only breathe, and the city breathes with them.

The fauna they share the space with are few and precious. The white moths, pale as fungus, flutter around the bioluminescent gardens; the people do not eat them, but they do not shoo them away, either. The spiders, fat and motionless in the corners of the hydroponic bays, are left offerings—a drop of nutrient solution, a fragment of bread left to mold and feed the cycle. The rats in the lower water channels, sleek with algae, are not vermin but indicators; their coats show the health of the water, the purity of the distillation. The people watch them, learn from them, let them be.

When they die, they do not leave. Their bodies are carried down to the bio-reactors, composted, fed to the algae, distilled into nutrient solution, pumped back up through the filtration towers to feed the ferns that feed the living. It is a closed loop, a perfect circle of wetness and decay and growth. They are the Condensation People, the Wet Ones, the Drippers—utterly, completely, irrevocably at home in the weeping, breathing, green machine of the high country.
