The roar is the first thing they know. Before sight—before there was sight to lose—there was the 1.6 kilohertz moan of the wind, funneled down the throat of the plateau, resonating in the limestone chamber until the stone itself becomes a speaker. The creatures do not hear it with ears, for they have none; they receive it through the soles of their many-jointed feet, through the thin membranes of their vertical slit-mouths pressed flush against the fluted walls, through the hollow chambers of their translucent ribs. The wind is their mother tongue, their calendar, their god. It tells them when the snow is falling on the roof above, when the sun has passed the zenith, when the great gales of the high plateau are scouring the scree into new configurations. They stand in rows—dense, shoulder-to-shoulder, a palisade of pale flesh—and they listen. Their bodies tremble in sympathetic vibration, a visible ripple passing through the mass like a pulse through deep water.

They are the *karst-dwellers*, though they have no name for themselves, nor for the place. They are a colony that thinks it is a crowd, a superorganism that has forgotten it is singular. To look at them is to see a carpet of meat, tens of thousands of pale, eyeless things packed into the sixty-meter span of the cavern, filling the volume from the chaos of fallen scree to the vaulted ceiling forty meters above. They do not walk so much as *percolate*. The mass shifts with a geological patience, a slow tide of translucent flesh that takes minutes to complete a single undulation. Their skin is thin as parchment, stretched tight over a framework of cartilage and bone that shows through in dark, branching lines. You can see the organs pumping—not a single heart, but a distributed system of dark, rhythmic sacs that move fluids through their bodies with a wet, peristaltic sigh. Their collective respiration is the wet undertone beneath the roar, a slow inhale-exhale that sounds like the tide of a sluggish, subterranean sea.

Individually, they are wrong. Humanoid in suggestion only. Their limbs possess too many joints—seven, eight, sometimes nine segments where four should suffice—allowing them to fold themselves into the irregular crevices between the limestone blocks, to wrap their bodies around the flutes of the walls, to conform to the scree with the flexibility of wet cloth. When one moves, it is a series of slow, deliberate articulations, each joint locking into place with a soft *click* of bone against bone. Their heads are elongated, narrow, eyeless. Where eyes should be, there are only smooth patches of pale skin, sealed over. They navigate not by sight but by pressure, by the chemical signature of the damp air, by the constant vibration of the wind transmitted through the stone. They know the location of every pool in the chamber, every dripping fissure, by the subtle changes in humidity that their skin detects.

Their mouths are vertical slits, lipless, that open not to speak but to *receive*. When pressed against the weeping walls, they filter the condensation that gathers in the flutes, drinking the mineral-laden water that has percolated down from the surface world. But they also press these slits to the stone to listen—to feel the deep, subsonic harmonics of the gale above, to taste the vibration of the mountain's slow uplift. The limestone is young, fractured, singing with the stress of its birth. They drink that song.

They possess a technology of the immediate. The young karst is sharp, full of fracture planes and conchoidal edges. The creatures knap it. Using hammerstones of harder flint—nodules they find in the scree or carry from the upper shaft—they strike the limestone, flaking it into scrapers, awls, crude hand-axes. These tools are hafted with gut and sinew, bound with the hair of the dead. They do not build. They do not raise walls or roofs. Their architecture is *density*. They pack themselves into the warmest hollows of the chamber, their bodies generating just enough metabolic heat to keep the stagnant pools from freezing entirely, to keep the rime from claiming the lower walls. Their bodies are the insulation, the structure, the furnace. The tools are for cutting the flesh of the dead, for scraping the mineral deposits from the walls to supplement their diet of condensation and the microbial films that grow in the dark pools, for opening the bodies of the deceased to feed the young.

Life here is a cycle of consumption. They breed in the deepest, warmest part of the mass, where the bodies press closest together. Gestation takes years. The young emerge small, almost larval, with even more joints than the adults, their skin nearly transparent, their organs visible as dark, rapid flutterings beneath the surface. They are passed through the mass, hand to many-handed thing, fed regurgitated minerals and the liquefied remains of the previous generation. They grow slowly, adding segments to their limbs, thickening their skin until it becomes tough enough to withstand the abrasion of the scree. They may live for decades, perhaps a century, their lives measured not in days but in the slow thawing and freezing of the rime on the walls, in the subtle shifts of the wind's song.

Death comes not from age alone, but from the mass itself. The scree shifts. The bodies press. A creature may be crushed in the slow, irresistible tide of the crowd, its bones snapping like dry twigs, its translucent flesh rupturing. Or it may sicken, its dark organs failing, its skin clouding with the opacity of decay. The dead are not mourned—there is no concept of mourning here—but they are *processed*. The living turn on them with their limestone tools, cutting the flesh into strips, sharing it among the rows. The bones are picked clean by smaller things: pale, many-legged scavengers that scuttle in the stagnant pools and the dark hollows between the scree, creatures that are not quite the same species, perhaps juveniles of a different form, or a symbiotic race that exists only in the interstices of the great mass. These scuttlers strip the bones in hours, leaving them white and clean, to be incorporated into the scree, to become part of the floor, to be ground down by the slow shifting of the crowd until they are dust, until they are limestone again.

They stand in rows, facing the walls, their slit-mouths pressed against the stone. They have been doing this for generations—perhaps for millennia, since the chamber first opened and the first condensation began to weep. They listen to the roar. They feel the mountain rising, millimeter by millimeter, beneath their feet, even as the wind scours it away above. They are the mountain's dream, its slow, wet, breathing thought. They do not know they are trapped. They do not know they are free. They simply *are*—a dense, saturated presence, a cathedral of flesh and stone and wind, existing in the absolute dark, drinking the sky's cold breath, listening to the scream of the gale, waiting for the slow, patient drips that will one day wear the limestone down to sand, and the mountain with it.

In the pools that reflect nothing, their pale forms are distorted when they lean to drink—faceless, many-jointed, wrong. The water drinks the condensation from the air, and they drink the water, and the wind roars down through the throat above, and the mass breathes, and the mountain dreams its sharp, young dreams, and time moves like the slow drip of water into darkness, vanishing before it reaches the bottom.
