They are the ones who do not move, or move only in the measure of stone and steam.

First, you notice the fungi. They are the elders here, though the chamber is young—younger than memory, fractured yesterday or the day before. They bloom from the tilted concrete platform, from the rusted gantry, from the junctions where pipes meet stone. Their caps are vast, dinner-plate wide, and they shine with a turquoise that has no business existing in this depth, in this dark. It is a light that does not illuminate so much as announce: *I am here. I persist.* They do not photosynthesize in the way surface plants do, drinking sun; they drink the electricity that leaks from the emergency conduits, the chemical fire of the vents, the minerals dissolved in the exhaled water. They are patient. A fungus here grows perhaps a millimeter in a season, yet it grows with the certainty of geology, laying down layer upon layer of calcite and chitin, becoming architecture. They are the settlers, the ones who claimed the industrial bones first, wrapping them in fibrous sheaths that pulse with the same slow rhythm as the machinery's dormant hum.

Then there are the algae. They live in the pools—the shallow, steaming collections of geothermal exhalation that gather where the floor dips. They form mats so dense they look like skin, like a membrane stretched across the heat. They are thermophilic, ravenous for the 60-degree water, drinking the sulfur and the heavy metals that would poison anything with a heartbeat. They do not swim; they drift, or perhaps they are moved by the slow convection of the hot air, by the invisible currents of steam that rise and fall in the chamber's breath. They are the color of old jade, of deep water, glowing faintly from within with the same bioluminescence as the fungi, a green-blue phosphorescence that turns the pools into lagoons of liquid starlight. They are the farmers of this place, converting heat into biomass, creating the soil from which the fungi rise.

Between them, or perhaps above them, sway the fronds. They are not kelp, though they resemble it—rubbery, pale, translucent in the amber and sickly green light of the conduits. They grow from the condensation, from the million suspended beads that cling to the ceiling and walls, drinking the drips before they fall. They hang like curtains in the steam columns, moving only when the heat rises, a slow undulation that makes the darkness seem to breathe. They are the dancers, the ones who catch the light and pass it on, who filter the photons from the industrial lamps through their rubbery flesh and scatter them in soft, diffused glows.

And everywhere, wrapping everything, are the sheaths. They are the most intimate of the inhabitants, the ones who have made the truce between the old world and the new. They are fibrous, glowing, organic cables that have colonized the rusted pipes, the steel gantry, the concrete. They do not merely grow on the metal; they grow *through* it, or perhaps the metal grows through them, a mineral-lichen hybrid that blurs the line between machine and organism. They pulse with a faint electrical conductivity, siphoning the dormant power from the geothermal tap below, conducting it like nerves, feeding it to the fungi and algae. They are the nervous system of this place, the synapses that connect the garden to the ghost of the factory.

There are no animals here, as you have heard. No grazers to eat the algae, no insects to pollinate the fungi, no worms to turn the mineral soil. The chemistry is too harsh, the ecosystem too new, a monoculture of autotrophs existing in a closed loop of heat and light and water. It is a garden without gardeners, yes, but more than that—it is a single organism, distributed across the chamber, thinking in the timescale of centuries. The fungi are its memory, storing the slow accumulation of mineral and light. The algae are its metabolism, converting raw geothermal energy into living matter. The fronds are its senses, feeling the steam, catching the photons. The sheaths are its circulatory system, moving energy and nutrients through the industrial skeleton.

It lives by waiting. It lives by glowing. It lives in the silence, broken only by the *tick* of cooling metal and the *plink* of water, which are not interruptions but rather the heartbeat of this place—the slow, geological pulse of something that has only just begun to be alive, and will continue, drop by drop, millimeter by millimeter, to become more itself in the dark.
