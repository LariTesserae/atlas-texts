They are not many. They are few, and vast, and slow—existing in a temporal register where a heartbeat is a millennium and a thought is the growth of stone.

The primary denizens are the **Arbor-Colonies**: each karst tower not a rock but a single, sprawling organism, a confederation of chemosynthetic bacteria that have built themselves a body of translucent calcium carbonate. They *are* the forest and the inhabitant simultaneously. Their “wood” is milky-amber, shot through with sap that pulses with pale bioluminescence—amber and violet—moving at the speed of growing mineral. They do not possess leaves so much as **calcified membranes**, thin sheets of living stone that flutter in thermal currents which take a century to pass. Their roots drink from the geothermal vents below, pulling heat upward into trunks that glow with an inner, womb-like fire. A single colony may span a spire thousands of meters high; its consciousness, if it can be called that, resides perhaps in the dense root-mass deep in the bruised-violet strata, processing chemical signals and pressure changes over decades. They alter the physics of the water around them, creating cathedral spaces where the liquid grows stiller, warmer, denser, as if the trees themselves are breathing the water into new states.

Then there are the **Drifting Geometries**—the only inhabitants that move with intention. They appear as translucent Platonic solids: icosahedrons, dodecahedrons, cubes the size of rooms, floating in the viridian middle depths. They are colonial organisms, ancient and rare, pulsing with soft, rhythmic light that expands and contracts like lungs made of glass. They navigate against the thermal currents, drawn by gradients of warmth and mineral density invisible to any eye. They leave no wake because they do not displace the water so much as *phase through* it, existing in a slight density-shift, occupying the same stratum yet a different memory of it. They may be the reproductive bodies of the Arbor-Colonies—seeds that have learned to swim—or they may be grazers, drifting from tower to tower to feed on the bacterial films that coat the translucent bark, fertilizing the stone with their waste. To encounter one is an event that might occur once in a subjective lifetime; they pass like slow comets through the greenish dim, indifferent to the towers, bound by geometries that have nothing to do with Euclidean space.

In the deepest grottoes, where the water is oldest and thick as honey, dwell the **Calcified Ancestors**—the First Forests. They are mineralized now, turned to stone by ten billion years of accretion, yet still growing, still adding layers of calcium carbonate like tree rings made of time. They resemble architecture—arches, columns, vaulted ceilings—but they are biological fossils, the ghost-structures of the earliest Arbor-Calcite. They do not move, but they *remember*. When a swimmer drifts past them, the water’s palimpsest shows superimposed images: the towers as they were when taller, the water shallower, the Drifting Geometries more numerous. These ancestors might be dormant intelligences, or simply the accumulated weight of the dead, their calcium carbonate shells still hosting dormant bacterial spores, waiting in the silence.

Beneath them all, in the sediment that snows upward from the vents, are the **Vent-Mats**—vast carpets of chemosynthetic bacteria chewing on sulfur and planetary heat. They are the digestive system of the world, the true architects who dream the mineral trees into being, whispering the instructions for growth through the water’s layered memory.

There are no predators here, no prey in any recognizable sense. The relationship is symbiotic, lithic, and patient. The Geometries may graze the Vent-Mats or the bark of the Arbor-Colonies; the Colonies may shelter the Geometries in their dense canopies, filtering the light that never brightens. They communicate not with sound—the water is too thick, the silence absolute—but through **bioluminescent code**: slow pulses of amber and violet light traveling through translucent wood and viscous strata, a language where a single sentence takes years to compose. They speak through **thermal signatures**, leaving trails of warmth that persist for decades in the layered water. They speak through **pressure**, the subtle flexing of branches as they grow millimeter by millimeter, a conversation conducted over millennia.

They are the inhabitants of a drowned continent, or perhaps the first citizens of a new one, growing upward into the liquid dome of the sky, sustained by the warmth of the planet’s core, silent as stone, alive as water, remembering every stratum of their existence and whispering nothing.
