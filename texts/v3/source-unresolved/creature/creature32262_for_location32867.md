They are the Hollow Ones. Not by name they call themselves, but by the architecture of their bodies—vessels of bone and membrane tuned to the acoustics of birth. They stand on the fractured limestone not as conquerors but as resonant chambers, their forms having grown to echo the saw-tooth angles of the ceiling, the sharp fins of stone that rise like frozen waves. Their feet are broad, leathery, split-toed things that grip the porcelain edges without shattering them—an adaptation compressed into a single geological instant, as if the mountain taught them how to step in the moment it tore itself open.

They are pale, but not with the pallor of the sun-deprived. Their skin is the color of wet limestone, of the crustose lichen that dusts the walls—blue-white, phosphorescent in their own right, gathering the faint fungal glow and returning it softened, diffused, as though their bodies are secondary light-sources. Their faces are not hollow; their *chests* are. When they speak, the sound does not originate in throats but in the cavernous hollows of their torsos, a drumming resonance that seems to come from the stone itself. They are walking echoes, each body a chamber that amplifies the world.

They move in patterns that never change. Not from stagnation, but because time here is not a river but a pool—black, still, reflecting nothing. Their routes are etched into the floor by generations of bare feet, yet they look ancient even now, worn smooth by the weight of an eternity that has not yet passed. They carry their young strapped to their backs in slings of woven fungus-fiber; the infants are already pale, already glowing faintly in the blue-white dark, and they do not cry but hum, matching the frequency of the dripping water, the wet breathing of the salamanders in the pools.

They are numerous—scattered like stars across the regional expanse—but each band is small, intimate, spaced at intervals that keep the caverns from feeling crowded. They do not build, because building implies permanence, and here permanence is the only heresy. They leave only the debris of existence: cracked shells of deep-water mollusks, the delicate bones of cave-swiftlets picked clean, the charred stubs of fire-drills that smoke perpetually in the corners of chambers. The patches of floor where they sleep are smoothed not by choice but by the pressure of bodies that have occupied that exact spot in the exact same position since yesterday—limestone yielding to flesh, polishing itself around their forms as if they were stones in a river, though no river flows here.

They are loud. Their voices rise in the perfect acoustics of the cathedral-grave, magnified by the high vaults, thrown back from wet stone in overlapping frequencies. A conversation between two of them sounds like a chorus; a warning becomes thunder. They harmonize without effort, their hollow chests tuning to one another across the chambers, their speech indistinguishable from the shrieks of the swiftlets that dart through the mist. All sound is one language here. They sing to the great salamanders, and the salamanders answer with wet, heavy respiration that vibrates in the black pools, reflecting nothing.

Their technology is the glint of chert—flakes sharp enough to cut the damp air, hafted with sinew and fungal glue. They use these tools to scrape the phosphorescent lichen from the walls, to harvest the glowing crust, to open the shells of creatures that live in the lightless water. But they do not keep their tools. They leave them where used, letting them become part of the debris, part of the landscape. They do not accumulate. They do not progress. They are a zero-sum equation of presence, a dynamic frozen at exactly 2.8.

What are they? Perhaps the dead who forgot to remain dead, resurrected into this new stone womb when the mountain collapsed. Perhaps the antibodies of the earth, formed in the wound of yesterday's violence. Perhaps simply life—aggressive, insistent, filling the hollow with noise and pale, glowing bodies. They do not age; they do not change; they do not grow or shrink. When one falls—if one falls—it does not rot. It simply stops, becoming part of the equilibrium, its hollow chest filling with mineral water, its skin becoming substrate for the next bloom of lichen, its bones joining the fractured floor.

They are the eternal present. The frozen moment. The life that swarms in overwhelming, sparse, loud, and absolutely unchanging abundance, inhabiting the grave that forgot it was a grave, roaring their presence into the stone that listens.
