They are the **Shapers**, first—tall, attenuated things that rise from the bone-colored stone like the yardangs themselves, as if the erosion that carved the fins had also carved them. They stand perhaps three meters high when they straighten from their work, but they are never still, never fully upright; they move in a constant, swaying motion, balancing against the pressure of the drone. Their skin is parchment—translucent, veined with maps of capillaries that pulse with the 130-decibel sub-bass vibrating through their feet, up through their legs, into their sternums. They have eyes, but the eyes are vestigial, milky, useless against the white-hot assault of light that bleaches the sky to violet bruising. They navigate by seismology, by touch. Their fingers—long, spindly, jointed wrong—drag across the limestone fins, reading the resonance of the stone, feeling the tectonic pulse of the grazers miles away as a dull thrum in their palms.

They carry their tools, always. Flakes of chert and flint, knapped in gestures that have not changed since before memory, since before the erosion stopped and the stasis began. But they do not build with these stones. They **tune**. A Shaper will kneel beside a hollowed organ-pipe of quartz-laced limestone and strike it, listening with the side of their face pressed to the rock, feeling the frequency travel up their arm and settle in their teeth. They chip away at the stone not to create a wall or a shelter, but to adjust the pitch of the plateau, to maintain the perfect, deafening chord that is the only history this world knows. Their language is percussion—taps and rhythms that travel for kilometers through the resonant bedrock, conversations that take hours to cross the distance between two solitary figures standing twenty kilometers apart.

They are nomadic, but their nomadism is orbital, geological. They follow the condensation lines—the invisible rivers of moisture that trace the 0.6 humidity of this frozen sea—walking paths so ancient they have become features of the landscape itself, shallow depressions worn into the stone by ten thousand years of identical footsteps. They do not accumulate. They do not hoard. A Shaper carries only what fits in the hollows of their hands: a scraper, a flake, a piece of sinew from the last grazer. When they die—and they do die, though time moves strangely here—they simply stop moving, becoming another punctuation mark, another vertical element in the horizontal vastness, slowly being eroded by the same processes that shaped the yardangs.

The **Grazers** are the mass against which the Shapers measure themselves. They are vast, many-legged, low-slung creatures that move across the empty stone like tectonic plates dreaming of drift. Each one might be the size of a house, a hill, a small island of flesh and chitin and patient, mineral metabolism. They have no eyes either, or eyes that have atrophied into light-sensors that serve only to detect the passage of clouds—though clouds are rare here. They feed on the crustose lichen, the pale ochre and ash-gray colonies that cling to the north faces of the stone waves, photosynthesizing in the brutal, heatless light with the slowest metabolism imaginable. A Grazer moves across a lichen colony and the colony takes a century to recover. The Grazers are the slow weather of this place, their passage felt before seen as a subsonic rumble that vibrates through the plateau, through the Shapers' feet, warning them to step aside, to let the mountain pass.

When a Grazer dies—or when the Shapers decide it is time—they butcher it with their stone tools. The work takes days. They work in silence, or rather, they work within the silence pockets, those paradoxical zones where the standing waves of the drone cancel themselves out and absolute quiet exists inside the screaming. In these pockets, a Shaper can hear their own heartbeat, can hear the scrape of flint against bone, can hear the distant, tectonic sigh of another Grazer settling into a new grazing ground. They do not eat quickly. The meat is tough, mineral-tough, and they process it slowly, drying it in the glare that bleaches color but does not warm, preserving it in the thin, cold air. They eat communally, though "communal" means gathering in a circle of perhaps three, four individuals, separated by kilometers of empty stone, tapping out the same rhythm in synchrony, sharing the meal through vibration and the occasional exchange of a dried strip of flesh carried across the plateau in the eternal, unchanging circuit.

Between the Shapers and the Grazers lives the **lichen**, the true foundation of this screaming world. It is not a plant, exactly, or not only a plant—it is a partnership of fungus and algae, a crust that grows at a rate so slow it is indistinguishable from stasis, yet it grows. It feeds the Grazers, which in turn disturb the stone, creating new surfaces for the lichen to colonize, a cycle so slow it might be a single breath held across epochs. There is also the **silver bunchgrass**, stiff and wire-like, rooting in the deepest fissures where the stone sweats, where the condensation collects in the sealed cavities—the obsidian eyes, the dark pools that never freeze, never evaporate, suspended in a state of liquid mercy. The grass drinks from these pockets, or touches them, or simply exists in their presence, a whisper of green against the bone-white stone.

But there is something else that lives here, something that lives in the **silence** and the **light**. The Shapers have no history, no progress, only the eternal present of the tone. They do not age, or they age so slowly that the concept is meaningless. They are born—if born is the word—from the stone itself, or from the water pockets, or from the vibration of the drone achieving a critical frequency that crystallizes into flesh. They do not reproduce in any biological sense; they are simply there, distributed like punctuation, appearing at intervals across the plateau as the resonance of the rock demands new voices in the choir.

They are attended by **echoes**—not ghosts, but resonant impressions. When a Shaper taps a message into the stone, the message persists, traveling in diminishing waves, circling the plateau for years, decades, centuries, until it fades into the general drone. Sometimes a Shaper will press their ear to a yardang and hear the tapping of a Shaper who lived before the stasis, before the erosion stopped, a voice from a time that no longer exists, speaking a language of percussion that has not changed because it never needed to. These are the only histories: the stone remembers, even if the Shapers do not.

And in the absolute silence of the cancellation pockets, where the sound cancels itself out and the light is still too bright to look at directly, the Shapers stand motionless, feeling the vibration of the plateau settle into a perfect, eternal equilibrium. They are tuning the world. They are the caretakers of the scream, the maintenance workers of a stasis so complete it has become a kind of life, a breathing, singing, screaming stasis that has finished becoming itself and now simply **is**, forever, in the deep violet sky, under the white-hot glare, in the teeth-aching, sternum-vibrating, thought-fragmenting drone of the Roof of the World.
