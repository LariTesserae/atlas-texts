They are not animals, not quite. Or rather, they are animals that have surrendered the distinction between body and environment, between creature and rock. They are the point where the mountain began to digest itself.

When you first see them, you see only the light: a thousand points of cold, greenish luminescence flickering in the absolute dark, like stars that have fallen and learned to crawl. They carpet the floor so densely that the volcanic glass beneath is invisible—a living rug of articulated plates and jointed limbs, breathing in unison, a single respiration of grinding and hissing. The air tastes of copper and ozone, thick with the dust they shed, a dust that is not dead skin but spent ore, the husks of minerals after their essence has been extracted.

Then you see one.

It is perhaps the size of a large dog, though the comparison fails immediately. Its body is a series of segments, each one a polygon of deposited mineral—black magnetite, flecked with pyrite’s fool’s gold, veined with quartz that catches the bioluminescence and throws it back in fractured rainbows. The plates are not grown like shell but quarried: the creature has extruded them from its own metabolism, precipitating calcium, iron, and sulfur from the stone it consumes until its back is a crust of geology, a mobile outcrop. Where the segments meet, the joints are not soft but geared, interlocking cusps of harder mineral that rotate with a sound like gravel in a cement mixer, slow and inevitable.

Its head is a grinding apparatus. Mandibles of sintered basalt rasp against each other, pulverizing the wall. Behind them, the mouth opens into a throat that glows with the dull red of the magma below—not fire, but chemical fire, the oxidation of sulfides in a furnace-chamber lined with extremophile archaea or perhaps abiotic catalysts that the creature cultivates within its gut like a garden. It breathes in the sulfur dioxide venting from the rock, and breathes out hydrogen sulfide and pure, elemental sulfur, which condenses on its flanks in yellow crystals.

The larger ones—the elders, or perhaps the fixed ones, though the concept of royalty is a mammalian imposition—are anchored to the walls. They do not move. They are rooted in the basalt, their bodies swollen with storage chambers full of refined metals: nuggets of native copper, threads of silver, spheres of quicksilver. The smaller ones swarm around them like worker bees, but they are not workers in any social sense. They are organs of a distributed body. A small one will approach an elder, regurgitate a pellet of ground ore into a waiting maw, and receive in return a fragment of purified metal, which it carries away to deposit in a growing vein, a line of wealth written into the wall.

They hang from the ceiling in clusters, not like bats but like stalactites that have decided to become animals. Their legs dangle, twitching occasionally, adjusting their grip on the glassy roof. When they let go, they do not fall but descend on silk threads of crystallized silica, spinning mineral fiber from spinnerets at their rear. They are building. The chamber is not a natural cavity but a structure, grown over centuries by the accumulation of their architecture—tunnels lined with smooth, polished ore, chambers floored with sorted gravel, walls encrusted with druses of amethyst and calcite that they have excreted like sweat, layer upon layer of biological mineralogy.

The light they emit is their language, or perhaps their nervous system made visible. It pulses in the abdomens, a bioluminescent organ that flickers with data: temperature gradients, chemical concentrations, the pressure of the colony shifting against the stone. When a thermal current shifts—when the magma below pulses hotter—the lights ripple across the mass in a wave of green fire, a signal that travels faster than their bodies can move. They know things. They know the temperature of the rock three meters down. They know where the richest sulfide deposits lie, the sweet spots where the glass is thin and the chemistry is volatile. They know, perhaps, that you are here, a warm, wet intrusion in their dry world, but they have no category for you. You are simply another thermal anomaly, soon to cool, soon to be ignored, soon to be mineral dust yourself.

You can feel them processing, if processing is the right word. It is not a mind but a computation, slow and geological. Each creature is a node in a network of chemical sensing and mechanical response. Together they are a factory the size of a room, refining the mountain’s blood—the magma—into tools, into structure, into pure metal that will one day, perhaps, be used to build something else, or simply to exist as wealth, as potential, as the concentrated essence of the earth. They take the raw, emerging rock and strip it. They concentrate the scattered atoms into veins that gleam, into nuggets that rest heavy in the palm, into a kind of memory of the stone’s original chaos, sorted and purified.

They do not age as you do. A single individual might live for centuries, its mineral plates thickening year by year until it becomes too heavy to move, at which point it cements itself to the wall and becomes part of the architecture, a pillar of living ore, still processing, still glowing, still grinding the stone with mandibles that never cease, a monument to the slow patience of heat.

The sound is the sound of industry without industry’s impatience. There is no rhythm, no shift-change, no purpose beyond the endless conversion. It is the sound of entropy being locally reversed, of chaos being sorted into lines of silver and copper, of thermal energy being harvested to do work against the grain of the world. It is the sound of a world being born, or perhaps of a world dying and being remade into something harder, something that will outlast the volcano that created it.

When you squeeze back out through the fissure, the dark closing behind you like a throat swallowing, you carry with you the dust in your lungs, the vibration settled deep in your sternum, the memory of a place where life has learned to eat stone and excrete treasure, where the boundary between the living and the mineral has dissolved into a single, grinding, glowing, endless process. They remain, packed tight in the heat, working in the dark, the mountain’s own slow machinery, refining the raw glass of the world into something pure, something that will endure when the fire below has cooled and the range has worn down to sediment.
