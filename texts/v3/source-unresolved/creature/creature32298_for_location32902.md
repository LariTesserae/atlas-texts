They are low to the ground, pressed close to the warm stone by three billion years of evolutionary patience. You would not recognize them as people at first—only as moving patches of the landscape, grey-green hummocks that shift between the doorways, indistinguishable from the moss colonies that carpet the streets. But they are there, and when they turn, you see the architecture of their faces: broad, flat, with eyes like wet river stones set deep in sockets that weep condensation. Their skin is not flesh in any conventional sense, but a living membrane, thick and leathery and photosynthetic, drinking the fog that settles on them each dawn, turning the dim light into slow, stored warmth. They wear nothing, for they are already clothed—in lichens that bloom across their shoulders in silver rosettes, in ferns that unfurl from the hollows of their elbows, in the same spongy moss that cushions the pavement beneath their feet. They are gardeners of their own bodies, pruning and tending, letting the vegetation grow thick on their backs until they look like walking hillocks of the vertical forest.

They move at the speed of geology. A single step might take a full minute to complete, the foot lifting, hovering, settling into a new depression worn into the stone by centuries of identical passage. They navigate the narrow lanes not by sight alone—though the twilight is dim—but by touch, pressing their palms against the living walls, feeling the vibrations of the city through the masonry. Sound here is strange: it carries far through the thin, warm air, yet feels muffled, as if heard through water. The inhabitants have adapted to this by speaking in frequencies so low they are felt in the sternum rather than heard, a subsonic thrumming that travels through the rock and the timber, through the bodies of the moss and the chitin of the small creatures. A conversation between two of them might span a season, each syllable a slow exhalation, each pause filled with the rustle of ferns and the distant chatter of the winged things.

They do not build so much as *curate*. The dry-stone walls are not constructed but grown into being, the stones fitted with the precision of a coral reef, lashed together with fibers that are themselves cultivated from the gnarled shrubs. They carve the drainage channels with tools of bone and flint, guiding the condensation that drips from the fog-catching fronds into cisterns that are really just hollows in the living rock, lined with wax-leafed plants that filter and store. They are a city at equilibrium, neither expanding nor decaying, and so their inhabitants have become a people at equilibrium—no births, no deaths in the way the world below understands them, only a slow turning, a recycling of cells and memory and moss. Each generation is the same as the last, learning the same paths, tending the same vertical gardens, wearing the same depressions into the stone floor of the mesa.

The small, quick creatures—the scaled and furred, the winged and many-legged—are not their pets, nor their livestock, though the people do harvest the honey-like secretions of the burrowing colonies, and though the winged ones sometimes carry messages in their beaks, knotted cords of fiber. They are neighbors in a shared verticality, the creatures nesting in the thatch and the eaves, the people sleeping in chambers carved from the warm geothermal rock, both drinking the same sky, both contributing to the perpetual, deafening chorus that fills the thin air. The people do not dominate this ecosystem; they have become indistinguishable from it, a neural network of slow consciousness spread across the stone, dreaming in millimeters per decade.

In the perpetual mild spring of the high mesa, they are utterly still and utterly alive—warm, wet with the fog's kiss but dry of any liquid they do not first condense from the air themselves. They are the final form of the settlement, the mesa’s own slow thoughts made manifest, ancient and primitive and urban in their absolute, unchanging solitude.
