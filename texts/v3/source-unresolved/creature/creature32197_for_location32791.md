They are small. That is the first thing—their stature, child-sized or slight, dwarfed not just by the ribs but by the very concept of verticality here. They move through the settlement like the bone-worms move through the peat: in slow, synchronized pulses, conserving heat, conserving motion. Their skin is the color of old parchment left too long in water, translucent and faintly blue-tinged, so that when they stand near the fungal shelves, they seem to glow from within with the same bioluminescent pulse. Their eyes are large, black, and lidless, or perhaps they have no eyes at all—instead, they navigate by the vibrations they feel through the soles of their feet, reading the settling of the corpse as others might read a map. The creature groans as it dies, millimeter by millimeter, and they feel it in their bones, adjusting their weight, shifting their step, a constant negotiation with gravity and decay.

They wear no metal, of course. Their clothing is woven from the shed wings of the rib-moths—those rice-paper membranes harvested after the insects have dissolved back into the moss—layered over garments of cured peat and sphagnum. They are barefoot, their feet broad and webbed, adapted to the spongy, sucking floor, leaving no prints in the peat that do not fill in immediately with moisture. Around their wrists and throats, they cultivate small gardens of lichen, living jewelry that maps the patterns of the bone onto their own bodies, a deliberate mimicry of the architecture.

Their hands are their technology. Long-fingered, pale, with nails that have grown thick and curved like the bone-tools they carry. Each inhabitant bears a kit of carved implements: awls made from the creature’s smaller bones, scrapers of splintered rib, needles of spine-shard. They do not speak loudly—the mist muffles, and silence is a form of respect here—but they communicate in a language of touch and pressure, of shared vibration through the walkway ropes. When they work, lashing a new span between ribs as the corpse shifts, they move as a single organism, passing tools hand to hand, their bare feet finding purchase on the weeping bone.

They are the thorax-folk, though they have no name for themselves that translates to anything like “people.” They are the inheritors of a dead god, or perhaps they are its final, fragmented thoughts given flesh. The walkways follow the grooves of fossilized nerves, the streets trace the ghost of a mind, and so the inhabitants live inside the memory of the creature’s consciousness. They do not worship it in temples; they worship it by tending. They clean the condensation from the bone. They harvest the bone-worms when the tides pulse too thickly, roasting them over the peat-fires until they crisp like pale noodles. They guide the growth of the moss, pruning it, feeding it, ensuring the raft of peat continues to swallow the sternum, to soften the sharp edges of the fractured keel. They are undertakers who refuse to leave the morgue, mourners who have made the coffin their home.

Their society is sparse, like the settlement itself. They live in the huts—woven reeds lined with insulating moss—but they also live in the vertical spaces, sleeping in hammocks of fiber rope strung between ribs, high above the worm-tides. They are not numerous; perhaps a few dozen, perhaps a few score, scattered across the vast chamber like stars in a dark sky. They meet in the plaza, where the smokeless fires burn, to share the warmth of the pools, to exchange tools, to perform the rituals of adjustment. When a rib cracks anew, when the settling of the corpse threatens a walkway, it is the elders who decide—the ones with the longest memory of the creature’s anatomy, the ones who can feel the difference between a settling groan and a final collapse. They are patient beyond human measure. They measure time in drips, in fungal growth, in the slow grinding of bone against bone.

Biologically, they are a closed loop with their environment, just as the moths are a mobile phase of the moss. They breathe the mist. Their lungs are large, sponge-like, filtering the spores and the moisture. They drink from the warm pools, though they must let the water cool first, as the heat is the lingering metabolism of the host and too much of it will burn their translucent skin. They are cold-blooded, or nearly so, their body temperature matching the damp chill of the air, saving energy for the work of survival. When they die—and they do, eventually, as all things in this chamber must—they do not bury their dead in the ground. There is no ground, only the raft of peat. Instead, they place the bodies in the highest tiers of the shelf fungi, where the bone is driest, and let the moss claim them, let the bone-worms process them, let them become part of the substrate, part of the architecture, part of the slow, vegetative reclamation. They believe—though belief is a soft word for what they know—that to die here is to become a pathway, a nerve, a memory in the bone.

They are not sad, exactly. They are the creature’s last immune response, or perhaps its dream as it dies. They are the quiet that follows the fall of something immense. In the plaza, at the center where the ribs converge toward the fossilized spine, an elder might sit with her moss-lantern, watching the rib-moths drift past like pale snow, listening to the creak of the settling wood, feeling through her webbed feet the slow, inevitable collapse of the world above. She does not pray. She adjusts her weight. She waits for the drip. She is home.
