They live in layers, in velocities of time that do not match yours. The smallest are the drift-kin, the microbial hordes that ride the green shafts of light like dust motes with intention, feeding on the minerals the pipes weep, reproducing in the warm suspension until they become weather, become the particulate snow that settles in the curves of the undulating floor. They are the room's memory, the sediment of ten thousand years of circulation, and they do not die so much as diffuse, becoming the medium through which everything else thinks and moves.

Then the gardens. The violet kelp stands in their micro-crevices, thick as wrists, swaying not to the pump's roar but to the deeper heartbeat of the planetary spire itself—a current older than the machinery, felt in the swaying fronds like a pulse in a wrist. They are bruised-purple and iridescent teal and luminous-white, photosynthesizing not from the sun but from the diffused high-altitude light filtered through the crystalline crown, growing in dense islands of jungle in an ocean of pressurized emptiness. The moss-mats pulse with peristaltic slowness, digesting the mineral rain that falls from the eroded walls, their surfaces hosting cities of the drift-kin, farming without hands, gardening without intent, simply *being* in their final, overgrown form.

Among the fronds, the flicker-fish dart—translucent, fingernail-sized, carrying their own lanterns in their bellies. They are not fish exactly, but light given temporary shape, bioluminescent ghosts that mate in spirals and die in minutes, their corpses feeding the moss below, their lives so brief they are nearly thoughts. They are the room's breath, the quick inhale and exhale of a system that has learned to metabolize its own machinery, darting through the fronds in schools that write equations in the water, visible only as a shimmering disturbance in the green gloom.

The crustaceans cling to the kelp stalks, beetle-sized, armored in a ceramic-metal composite that matches the walls, having grown shells indistinguishable from the architecture they inhabit. They click and chitter, scraping the mineral deposits with mandibles that have worn smooth by aeons of use, recording the chemistry of the water in their carapaces, a slow library written in calcium and ancient alloy. They are the archivists, the ones who remember what the walls have forgotten, who keep the blueprints in their genes rather than in stone.

And the walker—the amphibian with too many legs, salamander-slick, skimming across the curved walls near the ceiling where the water meets whatever thin pocket of air might exist in that highest curve. It leaves trails of mucus that become food, that become geology in miniature, a line of silver that disperses into the warm soup. It is the border-crosser, the one that moves between the liquid and the possible gas above, between the machine and the wild, between the constructed and the grown. It does not fear the pump's bass in its teeth; it has learned the rhythm, moving to it like a dancer to a drum that has been beating since before the world was young.

But the truest inhabitant is the machine itself—the boulder-sized heart that thrumms at the center, worn smooth and featureless by erosion, no longer recognizable as mechanism but as geology, as a kind of slow animal. It is not dead, though it has no moving parts you can see; it is simply *being* at a frequency too low for human hearing, a geological pulse that you feel in your molars. The pipes are its roots, thick as a man's torso, drinking from the depths of the spire, returning the water warmed and pressurized and dense, encrusted with the same violet vegetation that grows on the walls. It has forgotten its blueprints, forgotten that it was built, and has become instead a filter-feeder of planetary scale, digesting the world through its ceramic-metal gut, a stomach that runs on geological time.

Together they form the room's distributed consciousness: the drift-kin thinking in chemical gradients, the kelp thinking in the language of slow growth, the fish thinking in flashes of bioluminescence, the crustaceans thinking in the percussion of their clicking, the walker thinking in mucus-trails and the pressure of its many legs against curved stone, and the machine thinking in the deep, industrial bass that vibrates in the water and would vibrate in your teeth if you had teeth to feel it. They are the inhabitants of the end-state, the final erosion, the place where the world has stopped changing and simply persists, warm and loud and overgrown.

The water itself is inhabited—it is not empty, but full of the intention of these beings, the syrupy, dense, pressurized soup in which they drift and feed and pulse and wait. It is constructed water, processed by the spire, yes, but colonized, claimed, made alive by the things that swim through it. You are the intrusion, the temporary foreign body, the skin that feels the pressure; and they will outlast your presence as they have outlasted the erosion of the walls, as they have outlasted the blueprints, as they have outlasted the difference between the built and the grown, between the machine and the organism, between the ancient past and this eternal, breathing, submerged now. The pumps drone. The fish flicker. The moss pulses. And the room, in its final, perfect, spherical form, continues to live—not as a place, but as a being, ancient and industrial and total, dreaming in slow currents at the top of the world.
