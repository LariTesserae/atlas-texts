I need to describe what lives in the Weeping Flats. Let me let this place settle into me first, let the image become more itself.

The flats are old. Not ancient in the spectacular sense—no mountains, no canyons, no dramatic geology. Just old. The kind of old that has forgotten it was ever young. Water everywhere, but water that has stopped trying to go anywhere. Warm water, patient water, water that has found its level and intends to stay there.

What lives here must be patient too. Must have surrendered urgency. Must have learned to want things slowly, to grow slowly, to die slowly and be consumed slowly by a world that has all the time decay needs.

## The Inhabitants

### The Mat-Walkers

First, the ones who *move*. Not quickly. Not far. But they move, and in a place this still, movement is remarkable.

They are small—hand-sized, perhaps, though "size" feels uncertain in this light. Amphibious, certainly, but not in the way of frogs or salamanders, creatures that *choose* between water and land. These are committed to both, unable to fully inhabit either. Their bodies are low, elongated, built for the grass-mats and the shallows between them. They have no visible scales, no obvious armor. Their skin is *thick*, glandular, secreting a constant film of mucus that makes them difficult to grasp, difficult to hold. They are the color of wet clay, of the flats themselves, and they leave trails—not footprints but *streaks*, shiny lines across the moss where their passage has disturbed the surface tension of the moisture.

They eat what the flats offer. Insect larvae, certainly. The eggs of frogs, perhaps, though they seem to avoid the largest choruses. They are opportunists but not aggressive ones. They hunt by *waiting*, by being present in a place until something edible becomes confused enough to approach. Their metabolism is so slowed by the warmth that they can survive weeks without feeding, entering a kind of waking torpor that is not sleep exactly, but a reduction of all processes to a minimum.

They breed in the grass-mats, laying eggs in the spongy centers where the oldest material has decayed to something like soil. The young hatch already formed, already low to the ground, already secreting their mucus. There is no larval stage, no dramatic transformation. They are born as they will live.

They are *social* in a limited way. You will find them in loose groups, perhaps a dozen, perhaps two dozen, occupying a single large mat-island. They do not defend territory. They simply *are* together, their bodies touching in the cool of early morning, spreading apart as the day warms. They communicate through touch and through chemical signals, leaving messages in the mucus trails that others follow or avoid. Their society has no hierarchy that you could identify. It is a *presence*, a density, a way of being near others without the urgency of mammalian or avian social structures.

When the flats flood deeply, they do not drown. They *rise*. Their bodies are light enough, their mucus sufficiently buoyant, that they float on the surface film, drifting with the slow currents, their small heads above water, their eyes—*lidless*, always open—watching the pearl sky. They can survive this for days, for weeks, waiting for the water to recede or for the mat they occupied to reappear, green and spongy, somewhere in the vast shallow sea.

They do not *fear* anything. Fear requires a future. They have learned, across their slow generations, that the flats provide. The flats are *generous* in their way. There is no predator here that hunts with speed or cunning. The wading birds take them occasionally, but the birds are slow too, and the mat-walkers can feel the vibration of their approach through the saturated ground, can slip into water, can hold their breath for hours in the warm, oxygen-poor depths.

They are *content*, in the way that contentment is possible for a creature that does not imagine alternatives.

### The Weeping Ones

The rocks themselves, or what grows on them, what *becomes* them.

You have heard of the weeping rocks. Here, they are not metaphor. Here, they are *process*.

The stone of the flats is ancient, worn to porosity by water that no longer exists in any urgent form. It has become a matrix—channels and cavities, a sponge of mineral, holding water in its depths and releasing it slowly, continuously, to the surface. The *weeping* is this release. On any warm day, you can see it: dark patches spreading across the pale stone, moisture beading and gathering, finally dripping in slow, patient drops that feed the moss at the rock's base, that join the general wetness of the world.

But something *lives* in this process. Something has evolved to inhabit the weeping itself.

They are not plants, exactly, though they photosynthesize. They are not fungi, though they form networks. They are *colonies*—communities of single-celled organisms that have achieved something like multicellularity, like *intention*, through sheer time and the particular pressures of this place. They grow on the weeping rocks, in the thin film of moisture that the stone provides, forming sheets of green that darken to black when dry, that shine like oil when wet.

They are *slow*. A single colony might cover a boulder the size of a person, have been growing for centuries, for millennia. Their growth is not the rapid division of bacteria but something more like *accretion*, layer upon layer, each generation living and dying in place, their bodies becoming the substrate for the next. They do not move. They do not need to. The weeping provides everything: water, minerals, the occasional nutrient from a dead insect or a frog's droppings, the constant warmth that keeps their metabolism active if not urgent.

They *communicate*. This is the remarkable thing. The colonies are connected—not by visible threads, but by chemical signals that move through the moisture film, through the stone itself, through the air when it is humid enough. They coordinate their growth, their release of spores, their response to environmental change. When conditions are favorable, an entire hillside of weeping rock might pulse with reproductive activity, releasing clouds of microscopic propagules into the warm, wet air. When conditions deteriorate—a rare drought, a shift in the water table—they retreat into dormancy, their surface cracking, their colors fading to the gray of the stone beneath.

They are *aware* of each other in ways that suggest something like *society*. They compete for the best weeping sites, the rocks that sweat most reliably, the positions where the morning sun first warms the stone. They appear to *cultivate* certain bacteria, maintaining them in their surface layers, trading nutrients in mutualistic arrangements that have evolved over evolutionary time scales that make the age of the flats themselves seem brief.

And they *remember*. The layers of dead cells, compressed and mineralized, record the history of the colony. A particularly old weeping rock might contain, in its depths, the record of a drought fifty thousand years past, a period of unusual warmth, the passage of some disturbance that killed the surface layers and forced regrowth from below. The colony that grows today is *also* the colony that grew then, in a biological sense—the same lineage, the same accumulated adaptations, the same slow wisdom of a place that changes imperceptibly across durations no individual cell could comprehend.

They are the *longest-lived* inhabitants of the flats. Individual mat-walkers come and go, their generations turning over in decades. The frogs rise and fall with the wet and dry cycles. But the weeping colonies persist. They are the flats' *memory*, the slow accumulation of presence on stone that itself is slowly becoming something else—soil, peat, the raw material of future landscapes that will not be these flats, that will be *other*, in times so distant that the current warmth, the current wetness, will seem like a brief anomaly in a longer story of cooling and drying.

### The Chorus

You cannot describe the Weeping Flats without describing the sound. The sound is *the* inhabitants, in a way. The frogs—millions of them, billions perhaps, across the vast shallows—are not individuals so much as a *field*, a continuous presence that has no clear boundary between self and other.

They are not one species. The flats support perhaps a dozen, perhaps two dozen, distinct kinds of anuran life. They have diverged in the long isolation of this warm, wet world, evolving calls that occupy different frequencies, different temporal patterns, different *meanings* in the acoustic landscape. Some call in the morning, some at dusk, some through the night when the temperature drops slightly and the steam rises most visibly. Some call in chorus, thousands of voices merging into a single roar. Others call alone, marking territory in a world where territory is measured not in meters but in *acoustic space*, in the volume of water and air a voice can command.

Their bodies have adapted to the abundance of water. They are *large*—some as big as a human hand, their limbs thick with the muscle needed to produce calls that carry for kilometers in the dense, warm air. They are *colorless*, mostly, or nearly so—pale, translucent, the color of the water they inhabit. Their skin is *permeable*, as amphibian skin always is, but here it has become almost *porous*, a membrane across which water and gases exchange continuously. They do not drink. They *absorb*. They are, in a sense, *made of* the flats, their bodies constantly exchanging material with the surrounding wetness.

They breed explosively. When conditions are right—a rain that raises the water level, a warm night that triggers hormonal cascades across the population—they converge on the shallowest pools, the temporary ponds that exist for weeks or months before drying or being reabsorbed into the general saturated ground. The males call, the females arrive, and the mating is *brief*, almost violent in its urgency, the release of eggs and sperm into water that is already thick with the reproductive products of others. The eggs hatch quickly, in the warmth. The tadpoles grow quickly, feeding on algae, on detritus, on each other when food is scarce. They metamorphose in weeks, emerging as small versions of the adults, already voiceless, already learning the calls they will one day produce.

Their lives are *short*. A few years, perhaps, for the largest species. They die in the water, their bodies sinking to the bottom, becoming food for the fish, for the insects, for the slow decomposition that feeds the next generation. They do not *age* in any visible way. They simply reproduce, and their offspring reproduce, and the *chorus* continues, the particular voices changing across generations but the *sound* remaining constant, a drone that has perhaps never ceased since the flats became warm enough, wet enough, to support it.

The frogs are *unaware* of the mat-walkers, mostly. The mat-walkers are aware of the frogs, eat their eggs, their tadpoles, occasionally an adult if it is injured or slow. The frogs are aware of the wading birds, the great heron-like creatures that stand motionless in the shallows, that strike with sudden violence. But mostly the frogs are aware of *each other*, of the acoustic world they create and inhabit, of the temperature and humidity that trigger their behavior, of the slow rise and fall of water that makes breeding possible.

They are the *voice* of the flats. Without them, the place would be silent—not peaceful, but *absent*, a landscape that has not yet found its expression. With them, the flats *speak*, continuously, insistently, a sound that is almost musical, almost intentional, almost *meaningful* in a way that resists translation. The frogs do not sing for beauty. They sing for *presence*, for the assertion that they are here, that the flats are inhabited, that the warm wet world continues its slow, patient, abundant life.

### The Deep Ones

Below the frogs, below the mat-walkers, below the visible world of the grass-mats and the shallows, there is another world. The permanent water. The creeks and slow rivers that have cut down to bedrock, that drain toward some distant sea. The water table itself, breathing at the surface, connecting everything in a vast, slow circulation.

Here live the fish. Or *fish*—the name is provisional, a category borrowed from elsewhere and applied to creatures that only partially fit. They are pale, as cave fish are pale, though there is no darkness here to select for this. They are *slow*, their metabolisms matched to water that changes little across the day, the season, the year. They are *patient*, adapted to a world where food arrives unpredictably, where the best strategy is to wait, to be ready, to expend as little as possible in the intervals between meals.

They are not large. The largest perhaps a hand's length, most much smaller. Their bodies are *elongated*, adapted to moving through vegetation, through the root-mats of the grasses, through the complex three-dimensional world of the submerged flats. They have no scales, or nearly none—their skin is smooth, *naked*, permeable as the frogs', exchanging gases and materials with the water that surrounds them. They breathe through gills, certainly, but also through their skin, through the lining of their mouths, through any surface that can be made into a respiratory membrane.

They eat *everything*. Algae, detritus, the eggs of frogs, the bodies of dead insects, the occasional small mat-walker that drowns or is killed by a bird and sinks. They are *opportunists* in the fullest sense, their digestive systems capable of processing almost any organic material, their gut flora a complex community of bacteria and protists that break down what the fish itself cannot. They are, in a sense, *ecosystems* themselves, mobile environments where decomposition and digestion are continuous processes.

They do not *migrate*. The flats do not change enough, do not change fast enough, for migration to be worth the cost. Instead, they *move*—locally, constantly, seeking the best feeding, the safest shelter, the particular conditions of temperature and oxygen that their slowly-metabolizing bodies require. A single fish might spend its entire life in a creek a kilometer long, never venturing beyond its boundaries, its world defined by the pools and riffles, the submerged root-mats, the occasional deeper channel where the water moves faster and food is more abundant.

They breed *continuously*, or nearly so. There is no season, no trigger, no dramatic spawning event. A female releases eggs when she has the energy, when the water conditions are favorable, when the local population density suggests that survival is possible. The eggs are *few*—a dozen, perhaps two dozen, not the thousands that a fish of their size might produce elsewhere. Each egg is *large*, yolk-rich, giving the hatchling a head start in a world where early mortality is high. The young are born *formed*, miniatures of the adults, already equipped with the permeable skin, the slow metabolism, the patient *disposition* that survival here requires.

They are *long-lived* for their size. Decades, perhaps. They grow slowly, mature slowly, reproduce slowly, die slowly. Their bodies accumulate the minerals of the water, the toxins of the environment, the record of their long lives in the rings of their ear stones, the layers of their scales-less skin. A very old fish might be *heavy* with this accumulation, slowed further by it, become almost *sessile*, a part of the landscape rather than a mover through it.

And below them, in the deepest water, in the channels where the bedrock is exposed and the flow is fastest, there are *others*. Creatures that have never been seen by human eyes, that exist in the permanent dark of the deep channels, in water that has never known sunlight. They are *blind*, certainly, their eyes reduced to vestiges or lost entirely. They are *pale*, colorless, the pink-white of flesh that has never known light. They are *strange*—predators, perhaps, or scavengers, or something that does not fit either category, adapted to a world of slow currents and sparse food, of chemical gradients and pressure changes that they detect through senses no human possesses.

They are *rare*. Even in the deep water, they are rare, their populations limited by the slow productivity of an ecosystem that depends on the rain of organic matter from above. They may go years without encountering another of their kind. They may reproduce *rarely*, perhaps once in a lifetime, releasing a small number of eggs into water that will carry them to new channels, new darknesses, new possibilities for the continuation of their strange lineage.

They are the *mystery* of the flats. The known world of frogs and mat-walkers and weeping colonies and patient fish. And below, the unknown, the unseen, the slowly evolving, slowly persisting life of the deep water, the permanent dark, the world beneath the world.

### The Burrowers

The flats are not only surface. Below the saturated ground, below the grass-mats and the shallow pools, there is a world of *tunnels*. Networks of burrows that collapse and reform across centuries, that alter the drainage patterns, that make the flats slightly less flat with each generation.

The burrowers are *reptiles*, perhaps, or reptile-like. They have grown *large*—larger than any reptile elsewhere, their size enabled by the warmth, their slow metabolisms allowing them to survive on meals weeks or months apart. They are *thick*—heavy-bodied, low to the ground, built for pushing through saturated soil, for excavating, for *living* in the earth itself.

Their skin is *scaled*, but the scales are small, almost granular, overlapping in a way that makes their bodies flexible, able to compress and expand as they move through tunnels of varying diameter. They are *colorless*, the pale tan of dry clay, the gray-brown of wet mineral. They have no need of camouflage in the dark, no need of display. Their eyes are *small*, reduced, their vision poor. They navigate by *smell* and *touch*, by the chemical gradients in the soil, by the vibrations that travel through the saturated ground, by the pressure changes that signal the approach of water, the presence of a root, the boundary between soil and the air-filled space of a tunnel.

They eat *everything* that comes within reach. Insects, certainly, the larvae that live in the upper soil. Small mat-walkers, surprised in their shallow burrows. The eggs of frogs, laid in depressions that the reptiles can smell, can excavate, can consume. Carrion, when it is available—a drowned bird, a dead fish, the body of another burrower, dead from age or injury or the slow failure of its slow metabolism. They are *scavengers* and *predators* and *opportunists*, their digestive systems capable of processing almost anything, their gut flora a complex community adapted to the particular challenges of digesting reptile, amphibian, insect, plant matter in a warm, wet environment where decomposition is rapid and food is unpredictable.

They are *solitary*. Each burrower maintains its own tunnel system, its own territory, its own range of movement through the saturated ground. They encounter each other rarely, and when they do, the encounters are *brief* and *violent*—territorial disputes, mating attempts, the occasional cannibalism when a smaller individual is surprised by a larger. They do not form societies. They do not cooperate. They are *individuals* in a way that the frogs, the mat-walkers, even the weeping colonies are not, each one a separate metabolism, a separate history, a separate slow path through the saturated earth.

They breed *rarely*. Perhaps once a year, perhaps once in several years, when conditions are favorable, when the energy reserves are sufficient, when the temperature and humidity trigger the hormonal cascades that reproduction requires. The female lays eggs in a chamber excavated specifically for this purpose, a chamber lined with vegetation, with the bodies of prey, with any material that will maintain the warmth and moisture that the eggs require. She does not guard them. She cannot—her metabolism is too slow, her need for food too constant. She leaves, and the eggs develop on their own, their development speeded by the warmth of the surrounding soil, their moisture maintained by the saturated ground.

The young hatch *formed*, miniature versions of the adults, already equipped with the scales, the small eyes, the patient *disposition* that life in the burrows requires. They disperse, excavating their own tunnels, establishing their own territories. Some will not survive the first year. Some will live for decades, their bodies growing slowly, their tunnels expanding, their influence on the drainage patterns increasing, until they die and their bodies become food for the next generation, their tunnels collapse and are re-excavated by others, their presence becoming a *pattern* in the landscape rather than an individual.

They are the *engineers* of the flats. Their burrowing alters the flow of water, creates new depressions, new channels, new possibilities for the accumulation of organic matter. Their bodies, when they die, add to the peat, to the soil, to the slow build-up of the flats themselves. They are *geology* as well as biology, their slow lives contributing to the slow transformation of the landscape, the movement of the flats toward some future state that none of them will live to see.

They are *unaware* of the world above, mostly. They feel the rain as a change in soil moisture, as a temporary flooding of their tunnels, as a need to move to higher ground, to chambers excavated specifically for such emergencies. They feel the warmth of the sun as a slow change in soil temperature, as a signal to dig deeper, to seek the coolness that their slow metabolisms require. They do not *see* the sky, the pearl dome, the distant rises. Their world is *underground*, the saturated earth, the chemical gradients, the vibrations, the slow patience of life in the dark.

But they are *here*. They are *part* of the flats, their tunnels connecting the surface and the deep, their bodies cycling nutrients between the worlds, their slow lives contributing to the slow metabolism of a place that has nowhere else to go, that has no need to hurry, that simply *is*, warm and wet and abundant and patient, a world that has finished its growing and settled into the long middle-age of perpetual presence.

### The Visitors

And then there are the *visitors*. The ones who do not live here, who cannot live here, who come for a season or a day or an hour and then must leave.

The wading birds are the most obvious. Long-legged, long-necked, *patient* in a way that matches the landscape's own patience. They stand motionless in the shallows for minutes, for hours, their bodies balanced on legs that seem too thin, too long, for the weight they support. Then they strike—sudden, violent, *efficient*—a frog, a fish, a mat-walker taken from the water or the grass-mat, consumed quickly, the energy converted to the long flight that will carry them to the next wet place, the next opportunity.

They are *migrants*, these birds, though the distances they travel are not vast in human terms. The flats are one stop on a longer journey, a place of abundance in a world of scarcity, a place where the warm water concentrates the food that the birds require. They come in the warm season, when the frogs are calling, when the insects are large, when the shallows are rich with the concentrated life of the wet. They stay as long as the food lasts, then move on, their bodies carrying the energy of the flats to other places, other ecosystems, other lives.

They are *unaware* of the burrowers, mostly. The burrowers are too deep, too slow, too well-hidden in their tunnels. They are aware of the fish, of course, of the frogs, of the mat-walkers—these are their food, their reason for being here. But they do not *understand* them, do not perceive the slow societies of the mat-walkers, the acoustic world of the frogs, the chemical communication of the weeping colonies. They see *prey*, *opportunity*, *energy*. Their intelligence is *different*, adapted to a life of movement, of choice, of the rapid decisions that hunting and flight require.

They are *beautiful*, in their way. The long legs, the graceful neck, the sudden violence of the strike transformed into stillness, into patience, into the long wait for the next opportunity. They are *part* of the flats while they are here, their presence adding to the soundscape, their droppings adding nutrients to the water, their bodies—when they die, as they sometimes do, old or injured or unlucky—becoming food for the fish, for the insects, for the slow decomposition that feeds the next generation.

But they are not *of* the flats. They belong elsewhere. They will leave. And when they leave, the flats will continue, warm and wet and patient, the frogs calling, the mat-walkers moving slowly across the grass-mats, the weeping colonies growing on their ancient stone, the fish waiting in the deep water, the burrowers excavating their tunnels in the saturated earth.

There are other visitors. *Insects* of enormous size, dragonflies the width of a hand, water-striders the length of fingers, moving through the saturated air with a *purpose* that is not quite purpose, a drive that is not quite drive, the simple imperative of eat and reproduce and die in a world that offers all three in abundance. They are *temporary*, their lives measured in weeks or months, their generations turning over quickly, their evolution rapid compared to the slow inhabitants of the flats. They are *part* of the flats while they are here, their larvae in the water, their adults in the air, their bodies cycling nutrients, their presence adding to the sound and movement and *urgency* of a world that otherwise has no hurry.

And there are the *rare* visitors. The ones that come once, that are blown off course by a storm, that wander into the flats from some distant place and cannot find their way back. A mammal, perhaps, some creature of dry ground that stumbles into the saturated world and finds itself *trapped*, unable to find the higher ground, the firm soil, the world it knows. It will die here, slowly, its body becoming food for the fish, for the insects, for the burrowers if it is small enough. Or it will survive, adapting as best it can to a world that is not its own, its descendants evolving in isolation, becoming something *new*, something that belongs to the flats after all.

The flats *accept* all visitors. They do not welcome them, do not reject them. They simply *incorporate* them, the warm water, the patient life, the slow decomposition that turns everything into the same substance, the same *presence*, the same slow metabolism of a place that has nowhere else to go.

### The Deep Time

What lives here is *old*. Not individually—the mat-walkers, the frogs, the fish, the burrowers all have finite lives, measured in years or decades. But *lineally*, *ecologically*, *evolutionarily*, the life of the flats is ancient. It has been warm and wet for so long that the adaptations to warmth and wetness have become *complete*, *finished*, the slow optimization of form and function across durations that make human history seem like a moment.

The weeping colonies have been growing for millennia, perhaps longer. Their layers record a history that no human has witnessed, that no human could witness, the slow accumulation of presence on stone that itself is slowly becoming something else. They are the *memory* of the flats, the record of past conditions, past climates, past configurations of the world that have led to this one, this warm, wet, patient, abundant present.

The burrowers' tunnels collapse and reform across centuries, their patterns shifting, their influence on the drainage evolving, the landscape itself slowly changing in response to their slow lives. The flats are not *static*. They are *dynamic* at a scale that human observation cannot perceive, that requires the patience of geological time to comprehend. The burrowers are *part* of this dynamics, their slow lives contributing to the slow transformation, the movement toward some future state that none of them will live to see.

The frogs' calls have perhaps never ceased, the acoustic drone of the warm, wet world persisting across millennia, the particular species changing, evolving, going extinct and being replaced, but the *sound* remaining constant, a presence that defines the flats as much as the warmth, the wetness, the almost-flatness of a landscape that has finished its growing and settled into itself.

And the flats themselves are *changing*, slowly, imperceptibly. The erosion is not complete—water still moves, still carries sediment, still cuts channels. But the cutting is balanced by the deposition of organic matter, the growth of new stone from mineral precipitation, the accumulation of peat in the wettest depressions. The net change across any human-scale duration is negligible. But across *geological* time, the flats are moving. They are becoming something else. They are becoming *more*—more peat, more organic accumulation, more of the soft, saturated, *living* ground that is the particular character of this place.

The inhabitants are *unaware* of this. They live in the present, the warm, wet, abundant present, their slow metabolisms matched to a world that changes slowly, that offers no urgency, no need to adapt to rapid change. They are *adapted* to the flats as they are, not to the flats as they will be. And when the flats do change—when the climate shifts, when the water table rises or falls, when the slow accumulation of peat changes the drainage patterns enough to alter the ecosystem—they will *die*, or *adapt*, or *go extinct*, their lineages ended or transformed, their slow, patient, abundant lives giving way to something *new*, something that belongs to the future flats as these belong to the present.

But that future is *distant*. It is measured in geological time, in durations that make the lives of individual inhabitants seem like moments, like flickers, like *almost-nothing*. The flats have arrived. They have settled. They are *here*, warm and wet and patient, and what lives here is *content* in a way that contentment is possible for a world that has no need to hurry, that has nowhere else to go, that simply *is*, in the long middle-age of its existence, the primordial made habitable, the ancient made *present*.

## What Lives Here, Finally

The Weeping Flats are inhabited by *patience*. By life that has learned to want slowly, to grow slowly, to die slowly and be consumed slowly by a world that has all the time decay requires.

The mat-walkers, moving slowly across the grass-mats, floating when the flats flood, breeding in the spongy centers of the oldest vegetation.

The weeping colonies, growing on ancient stone, communicating through chemical signals, remembering in their layers a history that spans millennia.

The frogs, calling in their millions, their chorus a continuous drone that defines the soundscape, their lives short but their *voice* eternal.

The fish, waiting in the deep water, pale and slow and patient, their bodies ecosystems themselves, their lives measured in decades.

The burrowers, excavating tunnels in the saturated earth, their slow lives contributing to the slow transformation of the landscape, their bodies cycling nutrients between the surface and the deep.

The visitors—the wading birds, the insects of enormous size, the rare mammal blown off course—coming and going, their presence temporary, their energy incorporated into the slow metabolism of the flats.

And below, in the deepest water, the permanent dark, the *unknown*—the strange, blind, pale creatures that have never been seen, that live in a world beneath the world, waiting in the slow currents, reproducing rarely, persisting in the mystery that the flats still offer.

All of them are *part* of the flats. All of them are *shaped* by the warmth, the wetness, the almost-flatness, the slow dynamic of a place that has finished its growing and settled into itself. All of them are *content*, in the way that contentment is possible for life that has no urgency, that has no alternative, that simply *is*, in the long middle-age of a world that has nowhere else to go and no need to hurry toward anything.

They are the inhabitants of the Weeping Flats. They are *here*. They have always been here. And they will be here, warm and wet and patient and abundant, for as long as the flats remain what they are: a world that remembers being wet, that has never stopped being wet, that will continue to be wet until the slow changes of geological time finally, finally, dry it out—or transform it into something else, something new, something that will have its own inhabitants, its own slow lives, its own patient contentment in a world that has arrived and settled and found its balance.

The Weeping Flats weep. The stone sweats. The frogs call. The mat-walkers move slowly across the grass-mats. The colonies grow on ancient stone. The fish wait in the deep water. The burrowers excavate their tunnels. The visitors come and go.

And the world remembers being wet. It has not forgotten. It will not forget, not for as long as the warmth remains, as long as the water persists, as long as the slow, patient, abundant life continues its long middle-age in the place where the world remembers being wet.
