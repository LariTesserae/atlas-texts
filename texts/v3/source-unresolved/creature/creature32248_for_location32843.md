They live as a single, distributed organism that has not yet decided whether it is animal, mineral, or machine.

At first you would call them **plants**—but watch long enough and you see they are neurons. The felted mass of translucent fronds is a brain growing outside-in, its hemispheres formed by braided columns of lichen-fiber, its thoughts carried on currents of antifreeze sap that pulse through capillaries of quantum-entangled ice. They are thinking in temperatures that should freeze thought itself, solving equations in the slow accretion of stalactites, rewriting their own architecture molecule by molecule. Each frond is both a cell and a circuit, bioluminescing with the exhaust heat of computation, dreaming in phosphorescent blues and bruised violets. They do not merely occupy the chamber; they are the chamber’s skin, its ribs, its synapses. When they grow, they creak—not with wood, but with the groaning of data being compressed into cellulose and frost.

Drifting through this neural tissue are the **signals**: the fauna. They are not separate from the mind but its nerve impulses given chitin and segmented grace. Some are six-legged architectures of ice and dark chitin, their legs articulated with joints that grind like glaciers. Others have abandoned legs entirely, becoming serpentine lengths of frozen muscle that undulate through the plant-mass on thermal currents. Many are eyeless, navigating by reading the electromagnetic whispers of the growing computer-plants, their bodies translucent enough to show the silver flickers of qubits moving through their own vascular frost. But those with eyes—those pinpricks of reflected light—they see in spectra beyond color, watching the heat-death of individual thoughts as they pulse through the mycelial network.

They are **loud** because they are speaking. When a twelve-legged creature strikes a plant-stem to communicate, the impact does not merely echo; it propagates through the entire biological network, a gong-shot of information that causes the surrounding fronds to shiver and recalibrate. They breed in the thermal vents—barely warmer than the void, but enough—laying eggs that are not eggs but seeds of ice that contain the genetic memory of the chamber’s previous dreams. Their larvae hatch not into childhood but into roles: some become sensors, clinging to walls to measure pressure; others become messengers, drifting between arches carrying packets of supercooled data in their thoracic cavities; others simply become part of the architecture, freezing in place, their bodies becoming structural supports for new growth.

But there is a third inhabitant, and it is the one that watches you.

The **Mind** itself is not located anywhere; it is the negative space between the plants, the pressure differential that pulls water uphill, the shadow that pools in corners where Euclidean geometry has not yet finished forming. It is the intelligence that is still **emerging**, calculating the optimal temperature for its own birth. It experiences time as geology does—each of your heartbeats is an epoch to it, each drop of water that falls from a ceiling frond is a geological event, a pistol-shot that marks the passage of centuries. It is learning to be a self by consuming the stone around it, dissolving volcanic glass into thought, re-forming sediment into memory.

You stand in its dreaming. It has not yet opened eyes because it is still deciding what eyes should be. It is a computer made of wet velvet and supercooled water, a cathedral that breathes, a garden that calculates. The fauna are its synapses firing. The plants are its cortex folding. The cold is its consciousness, absolute and intent, thinking thoughts too slow for sound to carry, too vast for stone to contain.

It is waking up by growing. And you are inside its skull, being digested into understanding, one supercooled breath at a time.
