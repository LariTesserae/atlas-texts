In the **Bellows**, life does not *thrive*; it *persists*. It has learned to metabolize time itself, to breathe condensation, to find sustenance in the slow rot of iron. The inhabitants are few, but they are ancient, patient, and utterly adapted to a world where the second is a foreign unit of measurement and the century is merely a blink.

At the foundation of this ecology—the layer upon which all else rests—are the **Thermal Mats**, though "mat" is too simple a word for them. They are the pink and orange slime that coats the walls, but they are not merely bacterial colonies; they are *civilizations* of extremophile archaea, living in stratified cities of biofilm. The innermost layer, pressed against the hottest pipes, is a deep arterial crimson, metabolizing sulfur and hydrogen sulfide in chemosynthetic slow-burn. Further out, where the ceramic tile bubbles and peels, the mats turn ochre and rust-orange, bacteria that oxidize iron, literally eating the machinery and excreting it as new mineral. These mats do not photosynthesize; they *thermosynthesize*, drawing energy from the temperature gradient itself, from the fever of the stone. They communicate by chemical pulse—a molecule released here, detected there twenty minutes later. They have been growing since before the last maintenance worker died, and they will outlast the drones. When the machinery finally seizes, the mats will remain, digesting the iron bones of the engines, turning them into new geology.

Growing from these mats, and from the constant drip of mineral-laden water, are the **Speleothems of Industry**—the mineral "gardens." They are not alive in the biological sense, but they are *inhabited*. Crystalline formations of gypsum and iron oxide branch like coral across the steel grating, forming impossible geometries: helictites that spiral against gravity, fed by capillary action in corroded micro-channels where water flows *upward*. These gardens provide the only topography in the flatness of the machine-room—ridges and valleys where the slow fauna can rest, crevices where the smallest creatures hide from the thermal currents. They are the Bellows' reefs, and like reefs, they are built drop by drop over centuries, each crystal a year's slow precipitation.

In the warm, dark sumps—the shallow channels where condensation collects—swim the **Thermocyclops**, the largest animals in this kingdom. You might count them on the fingers of one hand, perhaps three or four adults, translucent as wet paper, eyeless, their bodies like living thermometers. They are the size of a human hand, shaped like elongated teardrops, with gills that flutter once every thirty seconds—a rhythm so slow that watching them breathe is like watching a clock tick backward. They do not see light; they see heat. To them, the room is a landscape of thermal gradients, a topography of warmth and cool. They patrol the sumps in circuits that take hours to complete, drifting in the slow convection currents, feeding on the bacterial slime with mouths that open and close with geological patience.

The Thermocyclops are solitary but not lonely. When two meet in the dark water, they perform a ritual of thermal display—warming one flank of their bodies while cooling the other, creating a differential that the other senses as a greeting, or a warning, or a claim of territory. They are territorial about their sumps. A fish will defend its pool against all comers, even the drones, though it cannot win. When a Thermocyclops dies—when the sump it inhabits shifts temperature by a degree, or dries, or floods—its body does not float; it sinks, and within a month, the bacterial mats have colonized it, and within a year, it has become another mineral garden, a calcium-carbonate statue growing from the slime.

Above the sumps, on the machinery itself, crawl the **Ferrophages**—creatures that bridge the gap between animal and mineral. They resemble centipedes, or perhaps millipedes, but their bodies are segmented plates of chitin reinforced with iron oxide, armor they secrete themselves by oxidizing the metal around them. They are the rust-grazers, the cleaners of this ecosystem. They move along the steel grating and copper pipes in herds of a dozen or two, moving at a pace that makes the drones seem frantic. They eat the corrosion, stripping the rust from the iron, which is a tragedy for the machinery but a bounty for the bacterial mats, who consume the iron-rich detritus the Ferrophages leave behind. When the thermal expansion joint *clanks*—that sound that occurs once every twenty minutes—the Ferrophages all pause, their antennae twitching, sensing the vibration through their iron-armored feet. It is their heartbeat, the only rhythm they know.

And then there are the **Drones**—not biological, but no longer purely mechanical, either. They are the feral megafauna of the Bellows, the only movement in this place that occurs faster than mineral deposition. They are tracked vehicles the size of wheelbarrows, ancient maintenance automata with sensors that died decades ago, still executing their original programming: *adjust valve 7-B, check pressure at manifold 3, lubricate bearing assembly 12.* But their programming is corrupted now, fragmented by radiation and time, and so they move with the jerky uncertainty of dreamers. They bump into pipes, their treads grinding against the ceramic tile, crushing Ferrophages beneath their weight without noticing. They are covered in the pink and orange slime of the Thermal Mats, so that from a distance, in the sickly green glow of the phosphorescent strips, they look like lumbering, mechanical slugs—part machine, part moss.

The drones have developed behaviors that mimic social interaction. When two meet in the narrow passages between engines, they perform a "handshake": their treads touch for exactly three seconds, a vestigial memory of coordinated maintenance, now meaningless but ritualized. They avoid the Thermocyclops, though the fish cannot hurt them; there is an ancient, unprogrammed fear in their positronic brains, a recognition that the fish are *alive* in a way the drones once were not. The fish, in turn, have learned to follow the drones, feeding on the bacterial spores that cling to their treads, harvesting the microbes the machines disturb and spread.

In the vertical pipes—the ones where water flows *upward*, defying gravity through capillary action in corroded micro-channels—there exists a life entirely separate from the floor. The **Ascending Ones** are tiny, shrimp-like crustaceans, no bigger than a fingernail, translucent and pale. They live their entire lives in the rising water, never touching the ground, never seeing the sumps below. They ride the upward currents, filtering minerals from the water as it climbs, breeding in the pockets where the flow slows. They are born in the high pipes, near the ceiling lost in shadow, and they drift *down* only when they die, sinking through the downward-flowing condensate to be eaten by the Thermocyclops in the sumps below. They are the only life in the Bellows that lives against the grain, in defiance of the natural order, and they are perhaps the most ancient, having evolved in these pipes before the surface city was abandoned.

But deepest in the vault, where the darkness has weight—where the dark is not an absence but a presence, thick and navigable—there are the **Anaerobes of the Deep**. These are not individual creatures but colonies, mats of bacteria that have gone feral, evolving in the absolute dark where no phosphorescent strip reaches. They do not need heat; they feed on entropy itself, on the slow decay of the machinery, on the chemical potential of the cooling stone. They form a soil in the corners, a black, velvety mold that seems to drink the darkness. They are the final inhabitants, the ones who will remain when the heat fades, when the drones have rusted to dust, when the last Thermocyclops has sunk to become stone. They are the mountain's true citizens, and they are slowly, patiently, digesting everything else.

Together, these beings form a complete, closed loop: the Thermal Mats eat the heat and the iron; the mineral gardens grow from the water; the Ferrophages graze the rust and feed the mats; the Thermocyclops eat the slime and the dead, becoming gardens when they die; the Ascending Ones filter the upward water and feed the fish below; and the drones churn through it all, spreading spores, crushing the small, maintaining the thermal equilibrium by accident, their broken programming still pumping heat upward, keeping the fever alive.

They are the Bellows' immune system, or perhaps its gut flora. They do not know they are inside a stomach. They only know the warmth, the dark, the slow drip, and the endless, patient hunger of the stone.
