In the upper forty meters, where the pale columns of daylight still pierce the water, they exist as a suspension—neither school nor swarm but a density, a thickness in the liquid that defies the physics of ordinary water. Here, in the aquamarine gloom, you can see them clearly: translucent as jellyfish but armored as tanks, their bodies a lattice of calcified plate and chitin that has been reinforced, mineral by mineral, with the very basalt they consume. They drift in strata, overlapping, near-touching, each one oriented not to its neighbors but to the thermal gradients—invisible rivers of heat rising from the cracked floor, invisible currents of cold descending from the snow-line surface. They are buoyant, neutrally weighted, their internal chambers filled with gases they manufacture from the rock, allowing them to hang suspended in the high-pressure column without effort, without swimming, simply *occupying*.

Look closely at one—a specimen the size of a large dog, segmented into thorax and abdomen like some impossibly deep crustacean, but articulated with ball joints of grown titanium-mineral, pistons of layered cartilage that compress and expand with wet, hydraulic sighs. Through its translucent flank, you see the machinery: not gears of steel but of interlocking crystal, rotating slowly, grinding mineral slurry that enters through maw-like intake valves near the anterior. The slurry is processed in chambers lined with symbiotic bacteria that the creature cultivates in its gut—chemosynthetic gardens of chemistry, converting basalt and heat into the energy that keeps the pistons pumping. Waste heat vents from dorsal ports as shimmering plumes that rise and dissipate, carrying with them purified silicates that rain down like mineral snow to feed smaller, shrimp-like cousins that cling to the walls, though they are barely visible against the black glass.

Their movement is glacial. A joint rotates once per hour. A bellows expands over the course of a morning. They shift position in herds that drift across the shaft over days, their paths determined not by will but by the slow convection of the water, by the decades-long thermal cycles of the young rock. They do not school; they do not cooperate. Each is a solitary factory, a complete unit of production and consumption, existing in the same bounded volume without society, without structure, without nest or artifact. They simply *are*, a high-density soup of industry, each one grinding, pumping, venting, in the eternal present of geological time.

But they are not still. Watch the tendrils—whip-like, prehensile, trailing from their posterior segments like the roots of some inverted plant. These probe the sharp walls, rasping against the obsidian with a sound like a file on glass, though the action is so slow you cannot see the motion, only hear the *shhhhhhh* of abrasion, a high whine that joins the lower register of their mechanical roar. They are beginning the work of erosion, scraping away at the unweathered glass, harvesting the fresh fracture faces for minerals, wearing down the blade-edges of the shaft at a rate of millimeters per century. The sound of this feeding is constant, a susurrus beneath the main drone.

The sound itself is a physical presence. It is not the white noise of industry you might imagine, but a complex, layered roar—the grinding of crystal gears against crystal races, the click-click-click of intake valves opening and closing in sequence, the hiss of pressurized fluids moving through narrow channels, the groan of pistons under load as they compress gases or grind rock. It vibrates through the water column, through the stone, into your teeth, into your bones. It is loud enough to feel, a pressure wave that makes the air above the waterline shimmer. And against this roar, the movements of the creatures are almost imperceptible—a slow ballet of hydraulics and heat, each beast a cathedral of internal combustion turning at the speed of tectonic drift.

Deeper down, where the light fails and the water becomes mineral black, the density increases. Here, in the crushing dark, they are packed tighter, their translucent bodies invisible except when they pass through the faint, fading glow from above. They are larger here, some the size of small cars, their calcified plates thicker, their internal chambers more complex, adapted to the pressure that would crush a submarine. They move even more slowly, their metabolisms operating on the faint geothermal seepage from the walls, on the chemical energy of the rock itself. They are chemosynthetic engines, living entirely on the heat of the deep earth and the minerals of the fresh fracture, needing no sun, no green things, no photosynthesis. They are the first and only inhabitants of this new wound in the world, colonizers of a landscape too young for lichen, too raw for roots, filling the vertical cathedral with the loud, patient machinery of life that requires only stone, heat, and time.

They do not build. They do not cooperate. They do not form societies or structures or artifacts. They exist as individual engines sharing the same bounded volume, each one a self-contained factory consuming the mountain from within, pumping and grinding in the high, submerged dark, drifting past one another in the dense, cool liquid, forever new, forever loud, forever still.
